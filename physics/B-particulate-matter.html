Physics HL · Theme B

B · The particulate nature of matter

Theme B asks: how do we understand matter at its smallest and largest scales? The winning move throughout this theme is the same: stop treating matter as continuous jelly and start counting particles. Temperature is the average kinetic energy of molecules. Pressure is molecules hitting walls. Current is charge carriers drifting. Entropy is the number of ways you can arrange those particles. Learn the counting argument and every equation in this theme follows.

B.1 Thermal transfers · B.2 Greenhouse effect · B.3 Gas laws · B.5 Circuits B.4 Thermodynamics — HL only ~12,000 words
Why this theme feels unfamiliar

Theme A was about one object whose position you can track. Theme B is about \(10^{23}\) objects you cannot track, so you switch to averages and statistics. That switch is the hard part, not the algebra. Whenever you get stuck in B, ask: "what are the particles doing, and what am I averaging?"

Temperature vs internal energy — the distinction the exam always tests

Temperature \(T\)
A measure of the average random kinetic energy of the particles. Unit K (or °C; the scale is the same size, the zero differs). It does not depend on how much substance you have.
Internal energy \(U\)
The total of the random kinetic energies plus the potential energies associated with the intermolecular bonds, of all the particles in the system. Unit J. It does depend on how much substance you have.
Common trap

A swimming pool at 20 °C has far more internal energy than a cup of tea at 80 °C, because there are vastly more molecules — even though the tea is at a higher temperature. "Heat" is also not a noun for stored energy: thermal energy transfers as heat; a body does not "contain heat". Use "internal energy" for what is stored and "heat" (or "energy transferred thermally") for what moves.

The Kelvin scale and the absolute zero argument

Convert with \(T(\text{K}) = \theta(°\text{C}) + 273.15\). Always convert to kelvin before using any gas law. Absolute zero (0 K) is the temperature at which the particles have minimum random kinetic energy — classically zero, though quantum mechanics means a real system retains a zero-point energy. The Celsius zero is just the freezing point of water at 1 atm; it has no fundamental significance.

Specific heat capacity

Intuition. Different materials need different amounts of energy to warm up by the same amount, because the energy gets shared differently — into molecular rotation and vibration as well as translation, or into stretching bonds. Water's specific heat capacity is unusually large (\(4180\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}\) approximately), which is why coastal climates are mild and why water is such a good coolant.

\[Q = mc\Delta T\]

\(Q\)
energy transferred thermally (J)
\(m\)
mass (kg)
\(c\)
specific heat capacity (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) — energy per unit mass per unit temperature change
\(\Delta T\)
temperature change (K — same size as °C, so a change in °C has the same number)

Also useful: heat capacity \(C = mc\) (J K⁻¹), and molar heat capacity (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹) when you count in moles.

How to use it — the calorimetry method
  1. Write "energy lost by hot body = energy gained by cold body + energy lost to surroundings".
  2. If the question says the calorimeter is well insulated, drop the last term.
  3. Use \(m_1c_1(T_1 - T_f) = m_2c_2(T_f - T_2)\), with \(T_f\) the final equilibrium temperature.
  4. Account for the calorimeter's own heat capacity if given (it is a third term \(C_{\text{cal}}\Delta T\)).
  5. Rearrange symbolically, then substitute. Units in kg and K.
Worked example 1 IB-style · 6 marks · mixing

Question. 0.30 kg of water at 15 °C is placed in a copper calorimeter of mass 0.20 kg. Steam at 100 °C is passed in until the temperature reaches 45 °C. Ignoring heat losses, determine the mass of steam condensed. (\(c_w = 4180\), \(c_{Cu} = 385\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}\), \(L_v = 2.26\times10^6\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\).)

  1. Energy gained by water: \(0.30\times4180\times30 = 37\,620\ \text{J}\).
  2. Energy gained by the copper: \(0.20\times385\times30 = 2310\ \text{J}\). Total gained \(= 39\,930\ \text{J}\).
  3. Energy lost per kg of steam = latent heat released on condensing + cooling from 100 to 45 °C: \(2.26\times10^6 + 4180(55) = 2.26\times10^6 + 2.30\times10^5 = 2.49\times10^6\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\).
  4. \(m = \dfrac{39\,930}{2.49\times10^6} = 0.016\ \text{kg}\).
Answer: \(1.6\times10^{-2}\ \text{kg}\) (16 g). The latent heat term dominates — over 90% of the energy comes from condensing, not from cooling. That is always true, and it is why steam burns are so severe.
Worked example 2 IB-style · 5 marks · electrical method

Question. A 12 V heater carrying 3.5 A heats 0.50 kg of a liquid for 200 s. The temperature rises from 21 °C to 47 °C. Determine the specific heat capacity of the liquid, and state one assumption.

  1. Energy supplied \(= VIt = 12\times3.5\times200 = 8400\ \text{J}\).
  2. \(c = \dfrac{Q}{m\Delta T} = \dfrac{8400}{0.50\times26} = 646\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}\).
  3. Assumption: all the electrical energy goes into the liquid, with no losses to the surroundings or to the container, and the liquid is uniformly heated (stirred).
Answer: \(650\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}\) (2 s.f.). In reality the container and surroundings absorb some energy, so the true value is lower than this — a good evaluation point for your IA.

Phase changes and latent heat

Intuition. During a phase change the temperature stops rising even though you keep adding energy. Where does it go? Into breaking (or forming) intermolecular bonds — changing the potential energy of the particles, not their average kinetic energy. That is why a temperature–time graph has flat plateaus at the melting and boiling points.

Specific latent heat of fusion \(L_f\)
Energy per unit mass to change a solid to a liquid at constant temperature (J kg⁻¹).
Specific latent heat of vaporisation \(L_v\)
Energy per unit mass to change a liquid to a gas at constant temperature.

Both: \(Q = mL\). Note the word specific means "per unit mass" — the data booklet lists values for water: \(L_f \approx 3.34\times10^5\), \(L_v \approx 2.26\times10^6\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\).

Common trap

Students write \(Q = mc\Delta T\) during a phase change. You cannot — \(\Delta T = 0\) but energy is being transferred. You need two separate terms: one \(mc\Delta T\) for each sloping section and one \(mL\) for each plateau. Sketch the heating curve first and count the sections.

Conduction, convection, thermal radiation

Conduction

Energy transferred by particles vibrating and passing energy to neighbours, plus (in metals) free electrons carrying energy. Needs a medium. Metals conduct well because of the free electrons; gases conduct badly because the molecules are far apart.

Convection

Energy transferred by the bulk movement of a fluid. Warm fluid expands, becomes less dense, and rises; cool fluid sinks. Needs a fluid and a gravity field. This is why a heater is placed near the floor.

Thermal radiation

Energy transferred by infrared electromagnetic waves. Needs no medium — it travels through a vacuum. All objects emit it; hotter objects emit more per unit area and at shorter wavelengths (see B.2 and E.5).

Linking question

B.1 links to A.3 energy: the "energy dissipated" in a mechanics problem is exactly the "internal energy increase" here — a falling object at terminal speed converts gravitational potential energy entirely into internal energy of the air. It also links to B.4: the first law \(Q = \Delta U + W\) is the formal statement of what "heating" does.

B.2 Greenhouse effect SL / HL

Black-body radiation and emissivity

Intuition. Everything glows. You glow in the infrared at about 10 µm; the Sun glows in the visible at about 0.5 µm. A black body is the ideal emitter and absorber: it absorbs 100% of incident radiation and, for a given temperature, emits the maximum possible intensity at every wavelength.

Black-body radiation
The electromagnetic radiation emitted by a perfect emitter. Its spectrum has a characteristic shape with a peak at a wavelength that depends only on temperature.
Emissivity \(e\)
A number from 0 to 1 comparing a real surface's emission to a black body's at the same temperature. A good absorber is a good emitter (\(e\) near 1: matt black); a poor absorber reflects and emits little (\(e\) near 0: shiny silver).
Albedo \(\alpha\)
The fraction of incident radiation reflected by a surface (0 to 1). Fresh snow has albedo ≈ 0.9, the ocean ≈ 0.06, and Earth's average ≈ 0.3.

Key laws you need (formally in E.5, but needed here):

\[P = e\sigma A T^4 \qquad \text{(Stefan–Boltzmann)} \qquad \lambda_{\max}T = 2.9\times10^{-3}\ \text{m K} \qquad \text{(Wien)}\]

where \(\sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-4}\) and \(T\) is in kelvin. The \(T^4\) dependence is ferocious: doubling the absolute temperature multiplies the emitted power by 16.

The solar constant and Earth's energy balance

The solar constant \(S \approx 1360\ \text{W m}^{-2}\) is the intensity of solar radiation at the top of Earth's atmosphere (at Earth's mean distance from the Sun, measured on a surface perpendicular to the rays).

Derivation Earth's average absorbed intensity and equilibrium temperature
  1. Earth intercepts radiation over its cross-sectional area \(\pi R^2\), not its surface area. So the total power received \(= S\pi R^2\).
  2. A fraction \(\alpha\) (the albedo) is reflected straight back, so the absorbed power \(= (1-\alpha)S\pi R^2\).
  3. Earth radiates from its whole surface \(4\pi R^2\), so the average absorbed intensity per unit surface area is \(I_{\text{abs}} = \dfrac{(1-\alpha)S\pi R^2}{4\pi R^2} = \dfrac{(1-\alpha)S}{4}\).
  4. With \(\alpha = 0.30\): \(I_{\text{abs}} = 0.70\times1360/4 = 238\ \text{W m}^{-2}\).
  5. In equilibrium, emission = absorption: \(\sigma T^4 = 238\), so \(T = \left(\dfrac{238}{5.67\times10^{-8}}\right)^{1/4} = 255\ \text{K} = -18\ ^\circ\text{C}\).

What it means: a bare Earth would be frozen. The actual mean surface temperature is about 288 K (+15 °C). The 33 K difference is the greenhouse effect — and it is natural and necessary.

Common trap

The factor of 4 is the single most-missed step. Earth absorbs over a disc (\(\pi R^2\)) but radiates from a sphere (\(4\pi R^2\)). If you use \(4\pi R^2\) for both you get \(I_{\text{abs}} = 59\ \text{W m}^{-2}\) and a temperature of 180 K — obviously wrong, and a good reason to sanity-check.

Greenhouse gases and infrared absorption

Intuition. The Sun is hot (~5800 K), so its radiation peaks in the visible; Earth's atmosphere is largely transparent to visible light. Earth is cool (~288 K), so it radiates in the infrared around 10 µm. Certain molecules — H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, and CFCs — have vibrational modes whose natural frequencies match infrared wavelengths, so they absorb Earth's outgoing IR and re-radiate it in all directions, including back down.

Carbon dioxide CO₂Combustion of fossil fuels, deforestation, cement productionLong atmospheric residence time (centuries); the main driver of the enhanced effect.

The enhanced greenhouse effect is the additional warming caused by human-induced increases in these concentrations. The honest physics statement for an exam: there is a well-established correlation between atmospheric CO₂ concentration and mean global temperature over at least the last 800 000 years (ice-core data), and there is a well-understood mechanism (IR absorption), but attributing a specific fraction of recent warming to a specific cause involves modelling with real uncertainties — discuss questions want you to name both the mechanism and the uncertainty.

Exam technique

When asked to "evaluate the evidence for the enhanced greenhouse effect", the markscheme wants: (1) the mechanism (IR absorption by greenhouse gases, re-radiation), (2) data (ice cores, the correlation, the rise in CO₂ from ~280 ppm pre-industrial to over 420 ppm), (3) a limitation (historical correlation does not prove causation; climate models have uncertainties in cloud feedback and aerosols; natural variability exists), and (4) a conclusion that weighs them. Four sentences, four marks.

Worked example 3 IB-style · 6 marks · energy balance with albedo

Question. A planet orbits a star and receives an intensity of 900 W m⁻² at its orbit. Its albedo is 0.20. Assuming it behaves as a black body, determine its equilibrium surface temperature.

  1. Average absorbed intensity \(= \dfrac{(1-\alpha)S}{4} = \dfrac{0.80\times900}{4} = 180\ \text{W m}^{-2}\).
  2. Equilibrium: \(\sigma T^4 = 180 \Rightarrow T^4 = \dfrac{180}{5.67\times10^{-8}} = 3.17\times10^9\).
  3. \(T = (3.17\times10^9)^{1/4} = 237\ \text{K}\). (Check: \(237^4 = 3.15\times10^9\) ✓.)
Answer: 237 K (\(-36\ ^\circ\text{C}\)). Lower albedo than Earth (0.20 vs 0.30) means more absorption, but the lower solar constant (900 vs 1360) dominates, so the planet is colder than Earth's 255 K effective temperature.
Worked example 4 IB-style · 7 marks · Wien + Stefan–Boltzmann for Earth and Sun

Question. The Sun has surface temperature 5800 K. (a) Determine the peak wavelength of its spectrum. (b) Earth radiates with a peak wavelength of 10.0 µm. Determine Earth's effective temperature. (c) Hence explain why the atmosphere is transparent to solar radiation but not to terrestrial radiation.

  1. (a) \(\lambda_{\max} = \dfrac{2.9\times10^{-3}}{5800} = 5.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 500\ \text{nm}\) — green light, i.e. the visible.
  2. (b) \(T = \dfrac{2.9\times10^{-3}}{10.0\times10^{-6}} = 290\ \text{K}\).
  3. (c) Solar radiation peaks in the visible, to which the main atmospheric gases (N₂, O₂) are transparent. Terrestrial radiation peaks around 10 µm in the infrared, which matches the vibrational modes of CO₂, H₂O and CH₄, so it is absorbed. Greenhouse gases therefore trap outgoing but not incoming radiation — that asymmetry is the whole mechanism.
Answer: (a) 500 nm, (b) 290 K, (c) the spectral separation of source and emitter is what makes a greenhouse effect possible at all.

It also links to C.2 electromagnetic waves: "infrared" is a wavelength band, and the whole mechanism is wavelength-selective absorption. B.3 Gas laws SL / HL

Pressure, amount of substance and the mole

Pressure \(p\)
\(p = F/A\), unit Pa (\(1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N m}^{-2}\)). Atmospheric pressure ≈ \(1.01\times10^5\) Pa.
Amount of substance \(n\)
Measured in mol. One mole contains \(N_A = 6.02\times10^{23}\) particles (Avogadro's constant). \(n = \dfrac{N}{N_A} = \dfrac{m}{M}\) where \(M\) is the molar mass.

Combine them and you get the ideal gas law:

\[pV = nRT = Nk_BT\]

\(R\)

\(k_B\)

When a fixed amount of gas changes state, use the combined form: \(\dfrac{p_1V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{p_2V_2}{T_2}\).

Common trap

Two in one: forgetting to convert °C to K, and forgetting that \(n\) must be constant for the combined law. If gas escapes, \(\dfrac{pV}{T}\) is not constant — go back to \(pV = nRT\) and track \(n\). Also: \(T\) in K, always. A change from 20 °C to 40 °C is 293 K → 313 K, a 7% increase, not a doubling.

Kinetic theory of an ideal gas

Intuition. Pressure is not a static push; it is the average effect of trillions of collisions per second with the walls. Each collision transfers momentum; force = rate of change of momentum; pressure = force/area. The whole of B.3 comes out of that one idea.

The assumptions of the ideal gas model — learn them, they are a 3-mark "state" question:

  1. The gas consists of a very large number of identical particles in constant random motion.
  2. The particles have negligible volume compared with the volume of the container.
  3. No intermolecular forces except during collisions.
  4. Collisions with each other and with the walls are perfectly elastic.
  5. The duration of a collision is negligible compared with the time between collisions.
  6. Newtonian mechanics applies.
Derivation \(pV = \tfrac13 Nm\langle c^2\rangle\) from momentum transfer
  1. Consider a cubical box of side \(L\) containing \(N\) particles, each of mass \(m\). Take one particle with velocity component \(v_x\) towards a wall perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis.
  2. It hits the wall and rebounds elastically, so its momentum changes from \(mv_x\) to \(-mv_x\): \(\Delta p = 2mv_x\).
  3. It travels to the opposite wall and back, a distance \(2L\), between collisions with this wall, so the time between collisions is \(2L/v_x\).
  4. Force on the wall from this particle \(= \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{2mv_x}{2L/v_x} = \dfrac{mv_x^2}{L}\).
  5. Summing over all \(N\) particles: \(F = \dfrac{m}{L}\sum v_x^2 = \dfrac{Nm\langle v_x^2\rangle}{L}\).
  6. By symmetry \(\langle v_x^2\rangle = \langle v_y^2\rangle = \langle v_z^2\rangle\), and \(c^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2\), so \(\langle c^2\rangle = 3\langle v_x^2\rangle\) and \(\langle v_x^2\rangle = \tfrac13\langle c^2\rangle\).
  7. Pressure \(p = \dfrac{F}{A} = \dfrac{Nm\langle c^2\rangle}{3L\cdot L^2} = \dfrac{Nm\langle c^2\rangle}{3V}\).
  8. Therefore \(pV = \tfrac13 Nm\langle c^2\rangle = \tfrac13 Nm\,c_{\text{rms}}^2\).

What it means: pressure depends on the number of particles, their mass, and their mean square speed — the average of the squares, not the square of the average. When it is wrong: real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature, precisely when assumptions 2 and 3 break down.

The bridge to temperature. Compare \(pV = \tfrac13 Nm\langle c^2\rangle\) with \(pV = Nk_BT\):

\[\tfrac12 m\langle c^2\rangle = \tfrac32 k_BT \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \bar{E}_k = \tfrac32 k_BT = \tfrac32 \frac{RT}{N_A}\]

So average molecular kinetic energy is proportional to absolute temperature, with \(\tfrac32 k_B\) as the constant. That is the deepest statement in this theme: temperature is molecular kinetic energy.

Common trap

\(c_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle c^2\rangle}\) is not the same as the mean speed \(\langle c\rangle\). The rms speed is always slightly larger. The distribution of speeds (Maxwell–Boltzmann) is skewed; if a question gives you a distribution and asks about "the most probable speed" and the "rms speed", they are different features of the same curve.

Worked example 5 IB-style · 6 marks · ideal gas + rms speed

Question. A cylinder contains 0.040 m³ of nitrogen gas at 300 K and \(2.0\times10^5\) Pa. The molar mass of nitrogen is 0.028 kg mol⁻¹. Determine (a) the amount of substance and (b) the rms speed of the molecules.

  1. (a) \(n = \dfrac{pV}{RT} = \dfrac{2.0\times10^5\times0.040}{8.31\times300} = \dfrac{8000}{2493} = 3.2\ \text{mol}\).
  2. (b) Mass of one molecule \(m = \dfrac{M}{N_A} = \dfrac{0.028}{6.02\times10^{23}} = 4.65\times10^{-26}\ \text{kg}\).
  3. \(\tfrac12 mc_{\text{rms}}^2 = \tfrac32 k_BT \Rightarrow c_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{3k_BT}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{3(1.38\times10^{-23})(300)}{4.65\times10^{-26}}} = \sqrt{2.67\times10^5} = 517\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
Answer: (a) 3.2 mol, (b) \(5.2\times10^2\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). Sanity check: the speed of sound in air at 300 K is about 350 m s⁻¹, and molecular speeds are somewhat larger than the speed of sound — consistent.
Worked example 6 IB-style · 7 marks · pressure from collisions directly

Question. A cubical container of side 0.50 m holds \(2.0\times10^{23}\) molecules, each of mass \(4.8\times10^{-26}\) kg, with rms speed 480 m s⁻¹. (a) Show that the pressure is about \(1.8\times10^4\) Pa. (b) The temperature is then doubled while the volume is held constant. Determine the new pressure and explain the change in terms of molecular motion.

  1. (a) \(p = \dfrac{Nmc_{\text{rms}}^2}{3V} = \dfrac{2.0\times10^{23}\times4.8\times10^{-26}\times(480)^2}{3\times0.125}\).
  2. Numerator: \(2.0\times10^{23}\times4.8\times10^{-26} = 9.6\times10^{-3}\); times \(2.304\times10^5 = 2212\). Denominator: 0.375. So \(p = 5900\ \text{Pa}\).
  3. (Note: with these numbers the pressure is \(5.9\times10^3\) Pa; a question phrased "show that … 1.8×10⁴ Pa" would use a smaller volume or larger \(N\) — always do the arithmetic yourself rather than forcing the printed value.)
  4. (b) At constant volume, \(p \propto T\), so doubling \(T\) doubles \(p\) to \(1.18\times10^4\ \text{Pa}\).
  5. Explanation: the mean square speed doubles, so each molecule delivers a larger momentum change per collision and collides more often (rate \(\propto c\)). The combined effect is \(p \propto c^2 \propto T\).
Answer: (a) \(5.9\times10^3\ \text{Pa}\), (b) \(1.2\times10^4\ \text{Pa}\), doubling because both the impulse per collision and the collision rate increase with speed.

B.4 Thermodynamics HL only

The first law and the sign convention that decides everything

Common trap — read this before anything else in B.4

The IB convention is: \(Q\) is the energy transferred to the gas; \(W\) is the work done by the gas. With that convention the first law is

\[Q = \Delta U + W\]

Energy in = increase in stored energy + energy out as work. If the gas is compressed, work is done on it, so \(W\) is negative. Say the words every time: "the gas is compressed, so work is done on the gas, so \(W \lt 0\)." Getting this sign right is worth 2–3 marks per paper.

\(Q\)
energy transferred thermally to the system (J), positive when heating the gas
\(\Delta U\)
change in internal energy (J)
\(W\)
work done by the gas (J), positive when the gas expands
Work at constant pressure
\(W = p\Delta V\); in general \(W = \int p\,dV\) = area under the p–V curve
Derivation Why \(W = p\Delta V\) and why it is the area under a p–V graph
  1. Imagine the gas in a cylinder with a frictionless piston of area \(A\). The gas exerts pressure \(p\), so the force on the piston is \(F = pA\).
  2. If the piston moves out by \(\Delta x\), the work done by the gas is \(W = F\Delta x = pA\Delta x\).
  3. But \(A\Delta x = \Delta V\), the change in volume, so \(W = p\Delta V\).
  4. For a process in which \(p\) varies, split the expansion into small steps: \(dW = p\,dV\), so \(W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} p\,dV\) — the area under the \(p\)–\(V\) curve.

What it means: expansion is work done by the gas (positive \(W\), it costs the gas internal energy); compression is work done on the gas (negative \(W\), it adds energy). When it is wrong: \(W = p\Delta V\) only holds at constant pressure. For isothermal or adiabatic processes \(p\) changes, so you integrate (or estimate the area).

The four processes on a p–V diagram

ProcessWhat is constantFirst law consequencep–V shape
Isobaric\(p\)\(W = p\Delta V\); \(Q = \Delta U + p\Delta V\). Heating causes both a temperature rise and expansion.horizontal line
Isovolumetric\(V\)\(W = 0\), so \(Q = \Delta U\). All the heat goes into internal energy.vertical line
Isothermal\(T\)\(\Delta U = 0\) (for an ideal gas), so \(Q = W\). All the heat becomes work. \(pV = \text{constant}\).\(p \propto 1/V\)

Why adiabatic is steeper

In an isothermal expansion heat flows in to keep \(T\) constant, so the pressure only falls because the volume grew. In an adiabatic expansion no heat flows in, but the gas still does work — it pays for it out of its internal energy, so \(T\) falls, which makes the pressure fall extra. Two reasons for the pressure to drop instead of one. That is why a bicycle pump gets hot when you compress air quickly (adiabatic compression) and why a spray can feels cold.

Worked example 7 IB-style · 7 marks · first law

Question. 2.0 mol of an ideal monatomic gas at 300 K expands isothermally from 0.020 m³ to 0.050 m³. (a) Calculate the work done by the gas. (b) State the change in internal energy. (c) Hence determine the energy transferred thermally to the gas.

  1. (a) For an isothermal process \(p = nRT/V\), so \(W = \int \dfrac{nRT}{V}dV = nRT\ln\left(\dfrac{V_2}{V_1}\right) = 2.0\times8.31\times300\times\ln(2.5)\).
  2. \(\ln 2.5 = 0.916\), so \(W = 4986\times0.916 = 4567\ \text{J} \approx 4.6\ \text{kJ}\).
  3. (b) For an ideal gas internal energy depends only on temperature. \(T\) is constant, so \(\Delta U = 0\).
  4. (c) \(Q = \Delta U + W = 0 + 4567 = 4.6\ \text{kJ}\) transferred to the gas. It is positive: the gas must absorb heat in order to do work without cooling.
Answer: \(W = 4.6\ \text{kJ}\), \(\Delta U = 0\), \(Q = +4.6\ \text{kJ}\). The sign is positive because the gas must be heated to sustain the expansion isothermally.
Worked example 8 IB-style · 8 marks · adiabatic compression

Question. A diesel engine compresses air adiabatically from 1.0 atm and 300 K to one-twentieth of its initial volume. Taking \(\gamma = 1.40\) for air, determine the final temperature and explain why the fuel ignites without a spark.

  1. For an adiabatic process \(TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{constant}\), so \(T_2 = T_1\left(\dfrac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{\gamma-1} = 300(20)^{0.40}\).
  2. \(20^{0.40} = e^{0.40\ln 20} = e^{0.40\times2.996} = e^{1.198} = 3.31\).
  3. \(T_2 = 300\times3.31 = 994\ \text{K} \approx 1000\ \text{K}\) (about 720 °C).
  4. The autoignition temperature of diesel is around 500 K, so an adiabatic compression to ~1000 K is comfortably above it: the fuel ignites on injection with no spark needed.
Answer: \(T_2 \approx 1000\ \text{K}\). This is why diesel engines have no spark plugs and why they need high compression ratios — the entire ignition mechanism is adiabatic heating (\(Q = 0\), so \(\Delta U = -W\) and the work done on the gas becomes internal energy).

Entropy and the second law

Intuition. Drop a drop of ink into water and it spreads; it never un-spreads. Not because un-spreading is impossible — every molecule could reverse — but because there are astronomically more ways to be spread out than concentrated. Entropy \(S\) counts those ways: \(S = k_B\ln W\) where \(W\) is the number of microscopic arrangements (microstates) consistent with the macroscopic state. Unit J K⁻¹.

For a reversible process at constant temperature: \(\Delta S = \dfrac{Q_{\text{rev}}}{T}\).

Second law (Clausius form)

Heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body. Energy is conserved either way, so the first law permits it — the second law forbids it. That is the point: the second law is not about how much energy but about which direction.

Second law (entropy form)

The total entropy of an isolated system never decreases: \(\Delta S_{\text{total}} \ge 0\), with equality only for a reversible process. Every real process increases the entropy of the universe.

Common trap

Entropy of the system can decrease — freezing water lowers the water's entropy, and that is fine. What can never decrease is the entropy of the universe (system + surroundings). Freezing water dumps latent heat into the room, and the room's entropy gain exceeds the water's entropy loss. Any answer that says "entropy always increases" without saying "of an isolated system / of the universe" is imprecise.

Worked example 9 IB-style · 6 marks · entropy change

Question. 0.50 kg of water at 0 °C is brought into contact with a large reservoir at 40 °C and warms to 40 °C. Determine (a) the entropy change of the water and (b) the entropy change of the reservoir. Comment on the total.

  1. (a) The water's temperature changes, so integrate: \(\Delta S = mc\ln\left(\dfrac{T_2}{T_1}\right) = 0.50\times4180\times\ln\left(\dfrac{313}{273}\right)\).
  2. \(\ln(1.1465) = 0.1367\), so \(\Delta S_{\text{water}} = 2090\times0.1367 = +286\ \text{J K}^{-1}\).
  3. (b) The reservoir stays at 313 K, so \(\Delta S_{\text{res}} = -\dfrac{Q}{T} = -\dfrac{mc\Delta T}{T} = -\dfrac{0.50\times4180\times40}{313} = -267\ \text{J K}^{-1}\).
  4. Total \(= 286 - 267 = +19\ \text{J K}^{-1} > 0\), consistent with the second law.
Answer: +286 J K⁻¹ (water), −267 J K⁻¹ (reservoir), total +19 J K⁻¹. The total is positive, as it must be — and the fact that it is small tells you the process is nearly reversible. A large temperature difference would give a larger positive total (more irreversible).

Heat engines and the Carnot cycle

Heat engine
A device that takes energy \(Q_H\) from a hot reservoir, converts part of it to work \(W\), and rejects the rest \(Q_C\) to a cold reservoir.
Thermal efficiency
\(\eta = \dfrac{W}{Q_H} = \dfrac{Q_H - Q_C}{Q_H} = 1 - \dfrac{Q_C}{Q_H}\)
Derivation The Carnot efficiency \(\eta = 1 - T_C/T_H\)
  1. The Carnot cycle is entirely reversible: isothermal expansion at \(T_H\), adiabatic expansion to \(T_C\), isothermal compression at \(T_C\), adiabatic compression back to \(T_H\).
  2. For a reversible isothermal step, \(Q = W = nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)\). So \(Q_H = nRT_H\ln\left(\dfrac{V_B}{V_A}\right)\) and \(Q_C = nRT_C\ln\left(\dfrac{V_C}{V_D}\right)\) (magnitude).
  3. For the two reversible adiabats, \(T_HV_B^{\gamma-1} = T_CV_C^{\gamma-1}\) and \(T_HV_A^{\gamma-1} = T_CV_D^{\gamma-1}\).
  4. Dividing these two equations: \(\dfrac{V_B}{V_A} = \dfrac{V_C}{V_D}\).
  5. Therefore \(\dfrac{Q_C}{Q_H} = \dfrac{T_C}{T_H}\), and \(\eta = 1 - \dfrac{Q_C}{Q_H} = 1 - \dfrac{T_C}{T_H}\).

What it means: the maximum possible efficiency of any heat engine depends only on the two reservoir temperatures, in kelvin. No amount of engineering beats it — that is the second law as a hard limit. When it is wrong: it is an upper bound for real engines, which always achieve less because of friction, turbulence and non-ideal heat transfer.

Worked example 10 IB-style · 7 marks · power station efficiency

Question. A fossil-fuel power station uses steam at 820 K and rejects heat to a river at 290 K. (a) Determine the maximum possible efficiency. (b) The station generates 500 MW.   4. Mass flow: \(\dot{m} = \dfrac{Q_C}{c\Delta T} = \dfrac{274\times10^6}{4180\times5.0} = 1.31\times10^4\ \text{kg s}^{-1}\).
Answer: (a) 65%, (b) at least 274 MW rejected, requiring \(1.3\times10^4\ \text{kg s}^{-1}\) (13 tonnes per second) of river water. A real station's efficiency would be nearer 40%, so the real heat rejection and water flow are considerably larger — which is why thermal pollution of rivers is a genuine environmental issue, and a strong point in an evaluate question.

B.5 Current and circuits SL / HL

Current, charge, potential difference and emf

Current \(I\)
\(I = \dfrac{\Delta q}{\Delta t}\), unit A (1 A = 1 C s⁻¹). Conventional current flows from + to −, i.e. the direction positive charge would move — even in a metal where the actual carriers are electrons moving the other way.
Potential difference \(V\)
\(V = \dfrac{W}{q}\), energy transferred per unit charge. Unit V (1 V = 1 J C⁻¹).
Electromotive force (emf) \(\varepsilon\)
The energy per unit charge supplied by a source: \(\varepsilon = \dfrac{W_{\text{source}}}{q}\). Despite the name it is a voltage, not a force. Unit V.
Charge
Unit C. The elementary charge \(e = 1.60\times10^{-19}\) C. It is quantised: \(q = ne\).
Common trap

emf is not the same as terminal potential difference. A real cell has internal resistance \(r\), so \(\varepsilon = V_{\text{terminal}} + Ir\), or \(V_{\text{terminal}} = \varepsilon - Ir\). The terminal pd drops as you draw more current. A question that says "a cell of emf 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω" is telling you the terminal pd is not 1.5 V when current flows.

Resistance, resistivity, Ohm's law

\[R = \frac{V}{I}, \qquad R = \frac{\rho L}{A}\]

Resistance \(R\)
Unit Ω. \(R = V/I\) is a definition; it is only Ohm's law when \(R\) is constant.
Resistivity \(\rho\)
Unit Ω m. A property of the material (and its temperature), independent of shape. Copper ≈ \(1.7\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\text{m}\).
Ohm's law
For a conductor at constant temperature, \(V \propto I\). The I–V graph is a straight line through the origin with gradient \(1/R\).

Electrical power and energy

\[P = VI = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}, \qquad W = VIt = Vq\]

Energy in the home is billed in kWh: \(1\ \text{kWh} = 3.6\times10^6\ \text{J}\). A 1000 W heater running for one hour uses 1 kWh.

Resistors in series and parallel

Series

Same current through each; pds add. \(R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + \dots\)

Parallel

Same pd across each; currents add. \(\dfrac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \dots\)

Derivation The parallel resistance formula from charge conservation
  1. Charge is conserved, so the current entering a junction equals the current leaving: \(I = I_1 + I_2\).
  2. The pd across each branch is the same, \(V\). So \(I_1 = V/R_1\), \(I_2 = V/R_2\), and \(I = V/R_{\text{eq}}\).
  3. \(\dfrac{V}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \dfrac{V}{R_1} + \dfrac{V}{R_2}\). Divide by \(V\): \(\dfrac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2}\).

What it means: adding a parallel branch always reduces the total resistance, because you have opened another route for the current. Check: two equal resistors \(R\) in parallel give \(R/2\) — never \(2R\).

Potential dividers and the practical circuit skills the IA needs

How to use it — sensor circuits and the IA
  1. The current is the same through both components: \(I = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{R_1+R_2}\).
  2. The output voltage across \(R_2\) is \(V_{\text{out}} = IR_2 = \varepsilon\dfrac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\).
  3. Decide the direction of the response: with an NTC thermistor in the \(R_2\) position, rising temperature lowers \(R_2\) and lowers \(V_{\text{out}}\). Put it in the \(R_1\) position and the response inverts.
  4. Choose \(R_1\) comparable in size to the sensor's resistance over your range — this maximises the sensitivity (the change in \(V_{\text{out}}\) per unit change in the measured quantity).
  5. Sensitivity trap: a very high or very low \(R_1\) makes \(V_{\text{out}}\) almost constant, giving you a flat dataset and a hopeless graph. This is the most common circuit-design mistake in IAs.
Worked example 11 IB-style · 6 marks · internal resistance

Question. A cell of emf 6.0 V and internal resistance 1.2 Ω is connected to a 4.8 Ω resistor. Determine (a) the current, (b) the terminal pd, (c) the power dissipated in the external resistor, and (d) the efficiency of the transfer of power to the external resistor.

  4. (d) Efficiency \(= \dfrac{P_{\text{external}}}{P_{\text{total}}} = \dfrac{I^2R}{I^2(R+r)} = \dfrac{R}{R+r} = \dfrac{4.8}{6.0} = 0.80\), i.e. 80%.
Answer: (a) 1.0 A, (b) 4.8 V, (c) 4.8 W, (d) 80%. Note the lost 20% is dissipated inside the cell, which is why batteries get warm under load. Maximum power transfer occurs when the load resistance equals the internal resistance — but then the efficiency is only 50%.

B.1 in depth — calorimetry, heat capacity and the error budget

Every calorimetry question in the IB boils down to one sentence: energy lost by the hot thing equals energy gained by the cold thing, provided nothing escapes. The exam tests whether you can write that sentence down with the right terms in it, and the IA tests whether you can make it true in a school laboratory. This section is about both, because each of them is where marks are actually lost.

Heat capacity versus specific heat capacity

Specific heat capacity \(c\) is a material property: the energy needed to raise the temperature of one kilogram by one kelvin, in \(\text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}\). Heat capacity \(C\) is an object property: the energy needed to raise the temperature of that particular object by one kelvin, in \(\text{J K}^{-1}\). They are related by

\[C = mc \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Delta Q = C\,\Delta T = mc\,\Delta T\]

Why the distinction matters: a copper calorimeter can, a beaker, a heating coil and a thermometer bulb all warm up along with the water you are measuring. Each of them absorbs energy that your \(mc\Delta T\) for the water alone does not account for. A careful experiment therefore writes

\[\Delta Q_{\text{supplied}} = \left(m_w c_w + C_{\text{cal}}\right)\Delta T\]

where \(C_{\text{cal}}\) is the heat capacity of the calorimeter and its fittings — a number determined in a separate calibration experiment. Neglecting it is the single biggest systematic error in school calorimetry, and it always biases your result in the same direction: the calculated \(c\) comes out too large, because some of the energy you think went into the water actually went into the metal.

Trap

Water's specific heat capacity is 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, but a typical 100 g aluminium calorimeter can has \(C = mc = 0.100 \times 900 = 90\ \text{J K}^{-1}\), which is 21% of the heat capacity of 100 g of water (\(418\ \text{J K}^{-1}\)). That is not a rounding error; it is a fifth of your signal. If the question gives you a calorimeter, use it.

The electrical method: measuring \(c\) without mixing anything

The cleanest way to measure a specific heat capacity is not to mix two substances but to supply a measured amount of electrical energy to a single substance and watch its temperature rise:

\[VIt = mc\,\Delta T \quad (\text{plus } C_{\text{cal}}\Delta T \text{ if you are being careful})\]

Here \(V\) is the pd across the heater, \(I\) the current in it and \(t\) the heating time, so \(VIt\) is the electrical energy delivered in joules. The method is attractive because \(V\), \(I\) and \(t\) can all be measured to better than 1%, whereas the temperature rise is usually the weak link.

How to run it
  1. Measure the mass of the liquid by weighing the calorimeter empty and then full; the difference is the liquid's mass. This avoids the error from liquid clinging inside a measuring cylinder.
  2. Start the stopwatch and the heater together, and record temperature every 30 s — before switch-on, during heating, and for several minutes after switch-off.
  3. Keep the current constant. A supply whose voltage drifts invalidates the assumption that the energy is simply \(V \times I \times t\); log the ammeter reading and average it if it moves.
  4. Stir continuously, and wait for the temperature to peak after switch-off before recording the final value — the element is hotter than the liquid and keeps delivering energy for a while.
Worked example 13 IB-style · 6 marks · Electrical calorimetry with a calorimeter correction

Question. An electrical heater is immersed in 0.150 kg of oil in a calorimeter of heat capacity 45 J K⁻¹. The heater operates at 12.0 V with a current of 2.4 A for 300 s. The temperature of the oil rises from 21.0 °C to 47.3 °C. Determine the specific heat capacity of the oil.

  1. Energy supplied by the heater: \(E = VIt = 12.0 \times 2.4 \times 300 = 8640\ \text{J}\).
  2. Temperature rise: \(\Delta T = 47.3 - 21.0 = 26.3\ \text{K}\). A rise of 26.3 °C is a rise of 26.3 K — the two scales have the same step size, so you never add 273 to a difference.
  3. Set up the balance: \(VIt = mc\,\Delta T + C_{\text{cal}}\Delta T\), so \(8640 = (0.150\,c + 45)(26.3)\).
  4. Rearrange: \(0.150c + 45 = 8640/26.3 = 328.5\), hence \(0.150c = 283.5\) and \(c = 1.89\times10^{3}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}\).
  5. Sanity check: oils typically have \(c \approx 2000\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}\), roughly half that of water, so a value just under 1900 is entirely plausible ✓. Ignoring the calorimeter would have given \(c = 2190\), an error of 16%.
Answer: \(c \approx 1.9\times10^{3}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}\); the calorimeter term is worth about 16% of the result and must not be dropped.

The cooling correction, and why you extrapolate backwards

A real calorimeter is never perfectly insulated. While the liquid is warming it is also losing energy to the room, and the rate of loss is proportional to the temperature excess above ambient (Newton's law of cooling). This means the temperature you read at the end is lower than the temperature you would have reached with perfect insulation, so \(\Delta T\) is underestimated and any \(c\) you calculate is too large.

The standard fix is graphical. You record temperature for several minutes after the heater is switched off, plot temperature against time, and draw a straight line of best fit through the cooling section. You then extrapolate that line backwards to the mid-point of the heating interval. The corrected temperature rise is the difference between the extrapolated line and the starting temperature.

Exam technique

If a data-based question gives you a temperature–time table and asks you to "determine a corrected value for the temperature rise", you are being asked for exactly this construction. State the method in words first — "extrapolate the post-heating cooling line back to the mid-point of heating" — then read the intercept off your graph. Marks are awarded for the method even if your intercept is a couple of degrees off.

B.2 in depth — planetary energy balance with an atmosphere

The bare-rock calculation of B.2 — absorb \((1-\alpha)S/4\), emit \(\sigma T^4\), solve for \(T\) — gives 255 K for Earth, while the measured mean surface temperature is about 288 K. That 33 K gap is the greenhouse effect, and the simplest possible model of it, the single-layer atmosphere model, is one of the most satisfying pieces of physics in the course because it gets the right answer from almost nothing.

Building the one-layer model

Make three assumptions, and be ready to state them if asked:

  1. The atmosphere is a single thin layer at a uniform temperature \(T_a\).
  2. It is transparent to short-wavelength solar radiation (visible and near-infrared) and opaque to long-wavelength terrestrial radiation.
  3. Each layer — surface and atmosphere — is in steady state, so energy in equals energy out for each.

Let \(S\) be the solar constant, \(\alpha\) the albedo and \(T_s\) the surface temperature. Work per unit area of surface, remembering that the incoming beam is spread over four times the collecting area:

\[\text{solar energy absorbed at the surface per unit area} = \frac{(1-\alpha)S}{4}\]

The surface radiates \(\sigma T_s^4\) upwards; all of it is absorbed by the atmosphere. The atmosphere radiates \(\sigma T_a^4\) from both faces — upwards to space and downwards back to the surface. Balancing each layer:

\[\text{Atmosphere: } \sigma T_s^4 = 2\sigma T_a^4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad T_s^4 = 2T_a^4\] \[\text{Surface: } \frac{(1-\alpha)S}{4} + \sigma T_a^4 = \sigma T_s^4\]

Substituting \(\sigma T_a^4 = \tfrac12 \sigma T_s^4\) into the surface balance gives the punchline:

\[\frac{(1-\alpha)S}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma T_s^4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma T_s^4 = \frac{(1-\alpha)S}{2}\]

Compared with the bare-rock result \(\sigma T^4 = (1-\alpha)S/4\), the right-hand side has doubled, so \(T_s\) is larger by a factor of \(2^{1/4} = 1.19\). With \(T_{\text{bare}} = 255\ \text{K}\) this predicts \(T_s = 303\ \text{K}\), which overshoots the real 288 K. The model is crude, but it captures the mechanism and the direction — and it is the same physics as an emergency blanket: one extra reflective layer roughly doubles the effective insulation.

Trap

The atmosphere emits both upwards and downwards, so it loses \(2\sigma T_a^4\) while receiving \(\sigma T_s^4\). Students who write \(\sigma T_s^4 = \sigma T_a^4\) have forgotten the second face, and they arrive at no greenhouse effect at all. Two faces, factor of two, factor of \(2^{1/4}\) on temperature.

Worked example 14 HL · 8 marks · One-layer model for a planet

Question. A planet receives a solar constant of \(S = 1600\ \text{W m}^{-2}\) and has albedo 0.40. (a) Determine its effective radiating temperature neglecting any atmosphere. (b) The planet has an atmosphere transparent to starlight and opaque to the planet's own infrared. Use the single-layer model to estimate the surface temperature. (c) Comment on the result.

  1. (a) Bare rock: \(\dfrac{(1-0.40)(1600)}{4} = 240\ \text{W m}^{-2}\). Set \(\sigma T^4 = 240\): \(T = (240/5.67\times10^{-8})^{1/4}\). Compute the ratio first, \(240/5.67\times10^{-8} = 4.23\times10^{9}\), then take the fourth root: \(T \approx 255\ \text{K}\) (about −18 °C).
  2. (b) With one layer: \(\sigma T_s^4 = \dfrac{(1-\alpha)S}{2} = \dfrac{0.60\times1600}{2} = 480\ \text{W m}^{-2}\). Then \(T_s = (480/5.67\times10^{-8})^{1/4} = (8.47\times10^{9})^{1/4} \approx 303\ \text{K}\) (about 30 °C).
  3. (c) Comment: the atmosphere raises the predicted surface temperature by 48 K. The model is unrealistic because it assumes a single isothermal layer that is perfectly opaque to all infrared. Real atmospheres are partly transparent in the infrared "window" and they transport energy upward by convection as well as radiation. Both effects make the real surface cooler than this estimate, which is why Earth sits at 288 K rather than the 303 K the model predicts.
Answer: (a) 255 K; (b) 303 K; (c) the one-layer model overestimates because it ignores the atmospheric window and convection.

Emissivity, the atmospheric window, and why Earth is not Venus

Real surfaces are not perfect black bodies. Emissivity \(e\) scales the Stefan–Boltzmann law, \(P = e\sigma A T^4\), with \(e = 1\) for a perfect emitter and \(e < 1\) for everything else. Dark, rough surfaces — soot, soil, water, vegetation — have \(e \approx 0.9\) to 0.98. Polished metals have \(e \approx 0.05\) to 0.2, which is why a shiny survival blanket works and why the walls of a vacuum flask are silvered.

Earth's effective emissivity in the infrared, measured from space, is about 0.61 — noticeably less than the ~0.95 of the surface itself. The difference is the atmospheric window: a band around 8–13 μm where the atmosphere is largely transparent, so some surface radiation escapes directly to space without being absorbed and re-radiated. Adding greenhouse gases closes the window part by part, which is why effective emissivity is not a constant of nature but depends on composition.

Exam technique

When a question gives you an emissivity you are expected to use it in \(P = e\sigma A T^4\), and you should also state the assumption that absorptivity equals emissivity (Kirchhoff's radiation law): a good absorber at a given wavelength is a good emitter at that wavelength. That is what licenses you to use the same number for absorbing incoming radiation and for emitting outgoing radiation.

Radiative forcing and climate sensitivity — what "discuss" questions want

Radiative forcing is the change in net downward radiative flux at the tropopause, in W m⁻², caused by some perturbation: doubling CO₂, a volcanic eruption, a change in solar output. It is the currency of climate physics because it lets you compare very different causes on one scale. Doubling CO₂ produces a forcing of about 3.7 W m⁻².

Climate sensitivity converts a forcing into a temperature change. The no-feedback value follows straight from differentiating \(\sigma T^4 = F\):

\[\frac{\Delta T}{T} = \frac{1}{4}\frac{\Delta F}{F} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta T \approx \frac{T\,\Delta F}{4F}\]

With \(T = 255\ \text{K}\), \(F = 240\ \text{W m}^{-2}\) and \(\Delta F = 3.7\ \text{W m}^{-2}\) this gives \(\Delta T \approx 1.0\ \text{K}\) per CO₂ doubling. Feedbacks multiply it: water vapour — a strong greenhouse gas whose atmospheric concentration rises with temperature — amplifies the warming; melting ice reduces albedo and amplifies it; increased cloud cover has both a cooling effect (albedo) and a warming effect (blanket) and is the largest single source of uncertainty. Assessed equilibrium climate sensitivity is around 3 K per doubling, roughly three times the bare no-feedback number.

Link

The \(\tfrac14\) in \(\Delta T/T = \tfrac14\,\Delta F/F\) is the same fourth root that makes temperature so insensitive to the solar constant. It is why a 0.1% change in solar output gives only about 0.07 K of warming — far too small to explain the observed warming since 1850, which is itself a standard exam argument against a solar explanation for modern climate change.

B.3 in depth — the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution and real gases

The kinetic model of B.3 gives you the average molecular kinetic energy, \(\bar{E}_k = \tfrac32 k_BT\). But averages hide the most interesting physics: at any instant some molecules are almost stationary while others move at several times the rms speed. The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution describes how the speeds are spread out, and it explains phenomena the average alone cannot — evaporation, reaction rates, and why a planet can lose its atmosphere.

Reading the distribution

Plot the number of molecules \(N(c)\) with speeds in a narrow band against speed \(c\). The curve starts at zero — essentially no molecule has exactly zero speed — rises to a single peak, and then has a long tail to the right. Three speeds are marked on it, and it is vital not to confuse them:

SpeedExpressionWhere it sitsUsed for
Most probable speed \(c_p\)\(\sqrt{2k_BT/m}\)At the peakDescribing the curve
Mean speed \(\langle c\rangle\)\(\sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m}\)Slightly right of the peakMean free path, diffusion
Rms speed \(c_{\text{rms}}\)\(\sqrt{3k_BT/m}\)Right of the meanKinetic theory: \(pV = \tfrac13 Nm\langle c^2\rangle\)

The ordering is always \(c_p < \langle c\rangle < c_{\text{rms}}\), with ratios \(1 : 1.13 : 1.22\). You do not need the numerical ratios, but you must know which of the three appears in \(pV = \tfrac13 Nm\langle c^2\rangle\): it is the rms speed, because that formula comes from averaging \(c^2\), not from averaging \(c\).

Trap

\(\langle c^2\rangle \neq \langle c\rangle^2\). The mean of the squares is not the square of the mean; they differ by the variance. When a question says "the mean square speed is 2.5 × 10⁵ m² s⁻²", take the square root to get the rms speed. Do not square anything, and do not divide by two.

What happens when you heat the gas

Raising \(T\) makes the peak move right, to higher speed, and down, because the distribution broadens so no single speed holds as large a share of the molecules. Because the curve is normalised — the area under it is the total number of molecules — it cannot simply move up; it must flatten as it spreads. The high-speed tail stretches dramatically.

That asymmetry is the key to three classic explanations:

  • Evaporation cools a liquid. Only molecules in the high-speed tail have enough energy to escape the surface. Removing them lowers the average kinetic energy of those left behind, so the temperature falls. Sweating works because of the shape of the tail, not because of the average.
  • Reaction rates rise steeply with temperature. An activation energy sits out in the tail. A modest rise in \(T\) can double or triple the fraction of molecules beyond it, even though the mean speed rises only as \(\sqrt{T}\).
  • Light gases escape from atmospheres. At the same temperature, hydrogen molecules move four times faster than oxygen molecules — mass ratio 1:16, so speed ratio 4:1 — and a far larger fraction of them exceed the escape speed. That is why Earth retains O₂ and N₂ but has essentially no free H₂.
Worked example 15 HL · 6 marks · The tail of the distribution

Question. Explain, using the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, why a small increase in temperature can produce a large increase in the rate of evaporation of a liquid, even though the mean molecular speed increases only slightly.

  1. The distribution of molecular speeds has a long tail at high speed. Only molecules whose speed exceeds the threshold corresponding to the energy needed to escape the surface can leave the liquid.
  2. The fraction that can escape is the area under the tail beyond that speed — a small sliver of the total area.
  3. When \(T\) rises the curve flattens and shifts right. The peak moves only as \(\sqrt{T}\), but the tail is exponential in character, so the area beyond a fixed high threshold grows far faster than the mean speed does.
  4. Hence a rise of a few kelvin can multiply the number of escaping molecules by a large factor, which is what we observe as a sharp increase in evaporation rate.
Answer: Escape selects the exponential tail, not the mean; the tail population grows much faster with \(T\) than \(\langle c\rangle \propto \sqrt{T}\) does.

When the ideal gas model breaks

The ideal gas rests on six assumptions, and each fails under a specific, predictable condition. Being able to name the failure mode is a standard 3-mark question.

High pressure

Molecules are pushed close together, so the volume of the molecules themselves is no longer negligible against the container volume. The gas becomes less compressible than \(pV = nRT\) predicts, because the free volume is smaller than the measured volume.

Low temperature

Molecules move slowly enough that intermolecular attraction matters. Attraction pulls molecules back as they approach a wall, so they deliver less momentum and the measured pressure is lower than the ideal prediction.

Near condensation

Attraction binds molecules into clusters; the gas liquefies and \(p\) becomes independent of \(V\) at constant \(T\), giving a horizontal isotherm. No smooth equation of ideal-gas form can describe that.

Very light gases at very low T

Quantum effects appear: the de Broglie wavelength of Theme E becomes comparable with the intermolecular spacing, and the classical counting of states fails. Helium stays liquid at atmospheric pressure down to absolute zero for this reason.

Quantitatively the fix is the van der Waals equation, which you may meet as extension material but are not required to derive:

\[\left(p + \frac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V - nb) = nRT\]

Read it as two corrections: \(an^2/V^2\) adds back the pressure lost to intermolecular attraction, and \(nb\) subtracts the volume actually occupied by the molecules themselves. Every term is a reminder of one broken assumption.

B.4 in depth — entropy calculations and real engine cycles

Entropy is the one idea in Theme B that students routinely memorise a sentence about without ever calculating anything. That is a missed opportunity: entropy sums are simple arithmetic, and doing one or two makes the second law feel like bookkeeping rather than philosophy.

Calculating an entropy change

The definition is

\[\Delta S = \frac{Q_{\text{rev}}}{T}\]

with \(Q_{\text{rev}}\) the energy transferred reversibly — that is, through an infinitesimal temperature difference — at absolute temperature \(T\). Two cases cover every IB question.

Case 1: an isothermal process, or a phase change. \(T\) is constant, so the definition applies directly. Melting 2.0 kg of ice at 273 K, with \(L_f = 3.3\times10^5\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\):

\[\Delta S = \frac{Q}{T} = \frac{2.0 \times 3.3\times10^5}{273} = 2.4\times10^3\ \text{J K}^{-1}\]

The value is positive: a liquid is more disordered than a solid, as it should be.

Case 2: heating or cooling through a temperature range. Now \(T\) changes, so you integrate. For a substance of heat capacity \(C\) heated from \(T_1\) to \(T_2\):

\[\Delta S = \int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{C\,dT}{T} = C\ln\!\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right) = mc\ln\!\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)\]

The logarithm is the fingerprint of all entropy work. It comes from the same place as the \(W = nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)\) of an isothermal gas process: in both cases you are summing a quantity divided by a changing denominator.

Trap

Temperatures in \(\ln(T_2/T_1)\) must be in kelvin, and the ratio must be dimensionless. Writing \(\ln(100\ ^\circ\text{C}/20\ ^\circ\text{C})\) is meaningless — converting both to kelvin gives \(\ln(373/293)\), a very different number.

Worked example 16 HL · 9 marks · Entropy change of the universe

Question. 1.0 kg of water at 80 °C is poured into 1.0 kg of water at 20 °C in an insulated container. (a) Determine the final temperature. (b) Determine the entropy change of the hot water, the cold water, and the universe. (c) Comment on the sign of your answer. Take \(c = 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}\).

  1. (a) Final temperature. Equal masses of the same substance, so the final temperature is the mean: \(T_f = 50\ ^\circ\text{C} = 323\ \text{K}\). (Doing it formally: \(mc(80-T) = mc(T-20)\) gives \(T = 50\ ^\circ\text{C}\).)
  2. (b) Hot water. It cools from 353 K to 323 K: \(\Delta S_{\text{hot}} = mc\ln(323/353) = 1.0(4200)\ln(0.9150) = 4200(-0.0888) = -373\ \text{J K}^{-1}\).
  3. Cold water. It warms from 293 K to 323 K: \(\Delta S_{\text{cold}} = mc\ln(323/293) = 4200\ln(1.1024) = 4200(0.0975) = +409\ \text{J K}^{-1}\).
  4. Universe. \(\Delta S_{\text{total}} = -373 + 409 = +36\ \text{J K}^{-1}\).
  5. (c) Comment. The total is positive, so the process is irreversible and spontaneous — which is exactly what we observe: hot and cold water mix by themselves and never unmix. Note that the hot water's entropy decreased; that is perfectly allowed. The second law constrains the total, not the individual parts.
Answer: (a) 323 K; (b) −373 J K⁻¹, +409 J K⁻¹, +36 J K⁻¹; (c) positive total entropy change confirms the process is irreversible.

The two statements of the second law, and why they are the same statement

The IB accepts either form, and the strongest answers give both:

Clausius form

Heat cannot spontaneously pass from a colder body to a hotter body. It is the everyday form: a cold spoon in hot tea warms up, and never the other way round.

Kelvin–Planck form

No device operating in a cycle can convert heat taken from a single reservoir entirely into work. Some energy must always be rejected to a colder reservoir.

They are equivalent, and the equivalence is itself a nice exam argument. Suppose you could violate Clausius — move heat \(Q\) from cold to hot with no other effect. Run a normal heat engine between the same two reservoirs; it takes \(Q_H\) from the hot reservoir, does work \(W\) and rejects \(Q_C\) to the cold one. Now use your Clausius-violator to pump that \(Q_C\) straight back up to the hot reservoir. The cold reservoir has had no net change, and the sole result of the combined machine is that heat \(Q_H - Q_C\) has been converted entirely into work — a Kelvin–Planck violation. So if one falls, both fall.

Exam technique

Questions about perpetual motion machines want you to name the specific law that forbids them. A machine that produces work with no energy input violates the first law. A machine that takes heat from the ocean and turns it entirely into work violates the second law — the energy is all accounted for, but you cannot convert heat completely into work without a colder sink. Identifying which one applies is usually the whole question.

Work from a p–V loop, and the cycles real engines use

On a \(p\)–\(V\) diagram, the work done in a process is \(W = \int p\,dV\), which is the area under the curve. For a closed cycle the net work done by the gas is the area enclosed by the loop, positive if the cycle is traversed clockwise (expansion at high pressure, compression at low pressure) and negative if anticlockwise. This single fact turns a lot of intimidating diagrams into an area calculation.

Worked example 17 HL · 7 marks · Area of a p–V cycle

Question. One mole of ideal gas is taken round the rectangular cycle A→B→C→D→A: A\((V_1, p_1)\) to B\((V_2, p_1)\) at constant pressure; B to C\((V_2, p_2)\) at constant volume; C to D\((V_1, p_2)\) at constant pressure; D back to A at constant volume, with \(V_2 > V_1\) and \(p_1 > p_2\). (a) Identify which stages involve work. (b) Show that the net work done per cycle is \((p_1-p_2)(V_2-V_1)\). (c) State the net work if the cycle is reversed.

  1. (a) Work requires a change of volume, so only the constant-pressure stages A→B and C→D do work. B→C and D→A are isochoric and do none.
  2. (b) A→B: \(W_{\text{AB}} = p_1(V_2-V_1)\), positive (expansion). C→D: \(W_{\text{CD}} = p_2(V_1-V_2) = -p_2(V_2-V_1)\), negative (compression). Net: \(W = p_1(V_2-V_1) - p_2(V_2-V_1) = (p_1-p_2)(V_2-V_1)\), which is exactly the area of the rectangle.
  3. (c) Reversed, the cycle runs anticlockwise and the net work is \(-(p_1-p_2)(V_2-V_1)\): work must be done on the gas. This reversed rectangle is a refrigerator — you pay work to move energy from low to high temperature.
Answer: (a) A→B and C→D only; (b) \(W = (p_1-p_2)(V_2-V_1)\); (c) the same magnitude with the opposite sign.

Real engines do not use the Carnot cycle, because the Carnot cycle's isothermal stages would have to be carried out infinitely slowly. Practical cycles replace them with fast, near-adiabatic strokes:

CycleFour strokesWhy it is usedTypical efficiency
Otto (petrol)adiabatic compression, isochoric heating (spark), adiabatic expansion, isochoric coolingCombustion is so fast the volume barely changes during it — hence constant volume25–30%
Dieseladiabatic compression, isobaric heating, adiabatic expansion, isochoric coolingFuel is injected over a longer period as the piston moves, so burning is at roughly constant pressure35–40%
Rankine (steam turbine)pump liquid, boil at constant pressure, expand through turbine, condense at constant pressureUses a phase change to absorb and reject huge amounts of energy at fixed temperature35–45%

All of them are bounded by the Carnot efficiency \(\eta = 1 - T_C/T_H\) evaluated at their extreme temperatures, and all of them fall short of it for the same reason: every real compression has friction, every real combustion is turbulent, and every real heat transfer happens across a finite temperature difference — and each of those generates entropy.

Why this matters

The "energy crisis" is not really about energy. The first law guarantees that energy is conserved, so in a strict sense we never run out of it. What we run out of is low-entropy energy — concentrated, ordered energy such as a fossil fuel or a uranium nucleus that can still be degraded to spread itself out. The second law says that once energy has spread out as low-temperature waste heat, no amount of clever engineering will gather it back without paying more elsewhere. Useful energy is consumed; energy itself is not.

B.5 in depth — Kirchhoff's laws, internal resistance and I–V characteristics

The potential-divider section of B.5 handles sensor circuits. But a large fraction of Paper 1 and 2 circuit questions do not yield to series/parallel reduction at all — you need two rules that work for any circuit, however tangled.

Kirchhoff's two laws

Current law (KCL): the sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum leaving it. This is charge conservation, plus the observation that charge does not pile up at a junction.

Voltage law (KVL): round any closed loop, the sum of the emfs equals the sum of the pd drops. This is energy conservation: a coulomb that goes all the way round a loop ends where it started, so the energy it gained at the sources must equal the energy it gave up in the resistors.

How to solve a two-loop circuit
  1. Mark a current in every branch, with an arrow. Do not worry about guessing the direction — if a current comes out negative, it simply flows the other way.
  2. Apply KCL at each junction to relate the branch currents; this usually reduces the number of unknowns immediately.
  3. Apply KVL to each independent loop, going round consistently: crossing a resistor with the current is a drop of \(IR\), against it is a rise; crossing a cell from − to + is a rise of \(\varepsilon\).
  4. Solve the simultaneous equations, then check: the pds round each loop should now balance, and the currents at each junction should now add up.
Worked example 18 HL · 8 marks · Two-loop circuit with opposing cells

Question. Two cells of emf 12 V and 6 V, each of negligible internal resistance, are connected as shown: the 12 V cell feeds a 4.0 Ω resistor in the top branch, the 6 V cell is in the middle branch, and a 2.0 Ω resistor is common to the right-hand side. The 12 V and 6 V cells oppose each other. Determine the current in the 6 V cell and state whether it is being charged or discharged.

  1. Define currents. Let \(I_1\) be the current from the 12 V cell and \(I_2\) the current from the 6 V cell, both flowing to the right. The current in the common 2.0 Ω resistor is then \(I_1 + I_2\) by KCL.
  2. Loop 1 (12 V cell, 4.0 Ω, 2.0 Ω): \(12 = 4I_1 + 2(I_1+I_2)\), so \(12 = 6I_1 + 2I_2\).
  3. Loop 2 (6 V cell, 2.0 Ω only): \(6 = 2(I_1+I_2)\), so \(I_1 + I_2 = 3\).
  4. Solve. From the second, \(I_2 = 3 - I_1\). Substituting: \(12 = 6I_1 + 2(3 - I_1) = 6I_1 + 6 - 2I_1 = 4I_1 + 6\), so \(4I_1 = 6\) and \(I_1 = 1.5\ \text{A}\). Then \(I_2 = 1.5\ \text{A}\).
  5. Interpret. Both currents are positive, so both flow as assumed — but the 6 V cell has current leaving its positive terminal, which means the 12 V cell is driving current through it. The 6 V cell is therefore being charged; it is a load, not a source.
Answer: 1.5 A flows from the 6 V cell's positive terminal, so it is being charged. Note the two currents are equal here because the arithmetic happened to balance — always check that your own numbers satisfy KCL before interpreting them.

Internal resistance: emf versus terminal pd

A real cell is an ideal emf \(\varepsilon\) in series with an internal resistance \(r\). When current \(I\) flows, some of the emf is "lost" driving current through the cell's own resistance:

\[\varepsilon = V_{\text{terminal}} + Ir \qquad\text{or}\qquad V_{\text{terminal}} = \varepsilon - Ir\]

Three consequences are examined repeatedly:

  • The terminal pd falls as the current rises. This is why car headlights dim when you operate the starter motor: the starter draws a huge current, \(Ir\) becomes large, and the pd available to everything else collapses.
  • The emf is the open-circuit pd. With \(I = 0\), \(V = \varepsilon\). So a voltmeter with very high resistance reads very nearly the emf — and a voltmeter with ordinary resistance does not, which matters when you measure a high-resistance source.
  • The short-circuit current is \(I_{\text{sc}} = \varepsilon/r\), which is the largest current the cell can deliver and is usually dangerously large.
Trap

Emf and terminal pd are not the same thing, and they are only equal when no current flows. Emf is the energy per unit charge supplied by the source (including inside the cell); terminal pd is the energy per unit charge delivered to the external circuit. Write \(\varepsilon\) and \(V\) differently in your working so you cannot confuse them.

Maximum power transfer

The power delivered to a load \(R\) is \(P = I^2R\) with \(I = \varepsilon/(R+r)\), so \(P = \varepsilon^2 R/(R+r)^2\). Differentiating with respect to \(R\) and setting the result to zero gives \(R = r\): maximum power is delivered when the load resistance equals the internal resistance. At that point \(P_{\max} = \varepsilon^2/4r\), and the efficiency is only 50% — half the power is dissipated inside the cell. Power stations deliberately work at the opposite extreme, \(R \gg r\), because they care about efficiency far more than about squeezing out the last watt.

I–V characteristics: reading the five graphs you must know

A component is ohmic if its current is proportional to the pd across it at constant temperature — a straight line through the origin on an \(I\)–\(V\) graph. Everything else is non-ohmic, and each non-ohmic component has a distinctive shape and a distinctive physical reason for it.

Asked to "state and explain" the shape of a filament lamp's I–V graph, give the chain in full: increasing \(V\) → increasing current → increased heating → greater amplitude of ion vibration → more frequent collisions for the drifting electrons → lower drift speed for the same field → higher resistance → the graph flattens. One link missing is one mark lost; the IB marks this chain link by link.

Measuring resistance without fooling yourself

The voltmeter–ammeter method has two wiring variants and they are not equivalent. With the ammeter in series with the resistor and the voltmeter across the resistor only, the ammeter reads the sum of the resistor current and the voltmeter current — so the deduced \(R = V/I\) is too small. This matters only when the voltmeter's resistance is not huge compared with \(R\), i.e. for large \(R\). Conversely, with the voltmeter across both resistor and ammeter, the voltmeter includes the ammeter's pd, so the deduced \(R\) is too large — which matters for small \(R\).

The clean way out for a low resistance is to use a four-terminal (Kelvin) connection: pass a known current through the outer pair of contacts and measure the pd on an inner pair. Since almost no current flows in the pd leads, their resistance is irrelevant. This is also the honest reason resistivity is a good IA topic: the technique itself is part of the physics.

For the resistivity experiment, \(R = \rho L/A\) predicts a straight line through the origin for \(R\) against \(L\), with gradient \(\rho/A\). Measure the diameter \(d\) with a micrometer at several points and several orientations (wire is rarely perfectly circular), so \(A = \pi d^2/4\) and the fractional uncertainty in \(A\) is twice that in \(d\). Keep the current small and switch off between readings — a wire that warms up during the experiment has a rising \(\rho\), and your graph will curve upward at large \(L\) where the resistance, and hence the heating, is greatest.

  1. [4 marks] 0.25 kg of water at 90 °C is mixed with 0.15 kg of water at 10 °C in an insulated container. Determine the final temperature.

    Show answer

    Energy lost by hot = energy gained by cold: \(0.25(4180)(90-T) = 0.15(4180)(T-10)\). The 4180 cancels: \(0.25(90-T) = 0.15(T-10)\), so \(22.5 - 0.25T = 0.15T - 1.5\), giving \(24 = 0.40T\) and \(T = 60\ ^\circ\text{C}\). Sanity check: the answer is between 10 and 90 and closer to 90 because there is more hot water ✓.

  2. [5 marks] Explain, in terms of molecular behaviour, why the temperature of a liquid stays constant while it is boiling even though energy is being supplied.

    Show answer

    Temperature measures the average random kinetic energy of the molecules. During boiling the energy supplied is used to do work against the intermolecular attractive forces, separating the molecules and increasing their potential energy, not their kinetic energy. Since the average kinetic energy is unchanged, the temperature is unchanged. The energy per unit mass required is the specific latent heat of vaporisation.

  3. [5 marks] A gas occupies 2.0 × 10⁻³ m³ at 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa and 300 K. It is compressed isothermally to 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³. Determine the final pressure and the work done on the gas.

    Show answer

    Isothermal: \(p_1V_1 = p_2V_2\), so \(p_2 = 1.0\times10^5\times2.0 = 2.0\times10^5\ \text{Pa}\).

    Work done by the gas: \(W = nRT\ln(V_2/V_1) = p_1V_1\ln(0.5)\) \(= (1.0\times10^5)(2.0\times10^{-3})(-0.693) = -139\ \text{J}\).

    The negative sign means work is done on the gas (it is being compressed), so the work done on it is 139 J. Since \(\Delta U = 0\) for an isothermal ideal-gas process, 139 J of heat must leave the gas — compression heats it unless you cool it.

  4. [6 marks] Calculate the rms speed of oxygen molecules at 400 K. Molar mass of O₂ = 0.032 kg mol⁻¹.

    Show answer

    Mass of one molecule \(m = 0.032/6.02\times10^{23} = 5.32\times10^{-26}\ \text{kg}\).

    \(c_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{3k_BT}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{3(1.38\times10^{-23})(400)}{5.32\times10^{-26}}} = \sqrt{3.11\times10^5} = 558\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

    Note this is lower than nitrogen at the same temperature would be, because the molecules are heavier — at equal \(T\), \(\tfrac12 mc^2\) is the same for all gases, so heavier molecules move slower.

  5. [6 marks] An ideal gas is taken round a cycle in which it absorbs 800 J of heat and does 300 J of work. (a) Determine the change in internal energy over the cycle. (b) Explain why your answer is expected.

    Show answer

    (a) \(\Delta U = Q - W = 800 - 300 = 500\ \text{J}\) increase for that part of the cycle.

    (b) Over a complete cycle the gas returns to its initial state, so the net change in internal energy must be zero (internal energy is a state function). Therefore over the whole cycle the net work done must equal the net heat absorbed. A positive 500 J here means this is only part of the cycle; other stages must remove 500 J.

  6. [7 marks] A heat engine operates between 600 K and 300 K. (a) Determine the maximum efficiency. (b) The engine performs 200 J of work per cycle. Determine the heat rejected per cycle. (c) Explain why a real engine cannot reach this efficiency.

    Show answer

    (a) \(\eta_{\max} = 1 - 300/600 = 0.50\), i.e. 50%.

    (b) \(Q_H = W/\eta = 200/0.50 = 400\ \text{J}\). \(Q_C = Q_H - W = 400 - 200 = 200\ \text{J}\). (Notice that at 50% efficiency half in, half out — as expected from the temperatures.)

    (c) A real engine has friction, turbulence, and heat transfer across finite temperature differences, all of which are irreversible and generate entropy. The Carnot efficiency assumes a fully reversible cycle, which is unattainable in practice, so real efficiencies are lower.

  7. [6 marks] Two resistors, 6.0 Ω and 3.0 Ω, are connected in parallel and then joined in series with a 4.0 Ω resistor to a 12 V supply of negligible internal resistance. Determine the current drawn from the supply and the power dissipated in the 3.0 Ω resistor.

  8. [7 marks] Discuss the energy balance of Earth and evaluate whether the enhanced greenhouse effect is well supported by evidence.

    Show answer

    Balance: Earth absorbs \((1-\alpha)S/4 = 238\ \text{W m}^{-2}\) on average and, in equilibrium, must emit the same. The corresponding effective temperature is 255 K, while the measured surface temperature is about 288 K; the 33 K difference is the natural greenhouse effect, caused by IR absorption and re-radiation by H₂O, CO₂ and CH₄.

    Evidence for enhancement: CO₂ has risen from ~280 ppm to over 420 ppm since the industrial revolution, from a source we can quantify (fossil fuel combustion); ice-core records show a close correlation between CO₂ and temperature over 800 000 years; the absorption mechanism is well-established laboratory physics; and the observed warming pattern (greater at night, greater in the Arctic, stratospheric cooling) matches the greenhouse signature rather than a solar-cause signature.

    Limitations: correlation is not proof of causation on its own; climate sensitivity depends on feedbacks (clouds, water vapour, ice albedo) that models handle with real uncertainty; and natural variability exists. Nonetheless the combination of mechanism, measurement and modelled attribution makes the enhanced greenhouse effect one of the best-supported results in applied physics.

  10. [6 marks] State the three assumptions of the single-layer atmosphere model and hence show that the surface must radiate \(\sigma T_s^4 = (1-\alpha)S/2\).

    Show answer

    Assumptions: (i) one thin atmospheric layer at uniform temperature \(T_a\); (ii) transparent to short-wave solar radiation, opaque to long-wave terrestrial radiation; (iii) steady state, so each layer's energy input equals its output.

    Atmosphere balance: it absorbs \(\sigma T_s^4\) from the surface and emits \(\sigma T_a^4\) from each of its two faces, so \(\sigma T_s^4 = 2\sigma T_a^4\) and \(\sigma T_a^4 = \tfrac12\sigma T_s^4\).

    Surface balance: \(\dfrac{(1-\alpha)S}{4} + \sigma T_a^4 = \sigma T_s^4\). Substituting the first result: \(\dfrac{(1-\alpha)S}{4} + \tfrac12\sigma T_s^4 = \sigma T_s^4\), hence \(\sigma T_s^4 = \dfrac{(1-\alpha)S}{2}\).

  11. [5 marks] Two gases, helium and argon, are at the same temperature. Explain, with reference to the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, which has the broader distribution of speeds and why.

    Show answer

    At equal temperature every gas has the same mean molecular kinetic energy, \(\tfrac32 k_BT\), so \(\tfrac12 m\langle c^2\rangle\) is the same for both. Helium atoms are much lighter (\(m\) smaller), so their speeds must be larger on average — \(c_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3k_BT/m}\) decreases with \(m\).

    A larger typical speed means the distribution is stretched out along the speed axis, so helium's curve is broader and flatter with its peak further right; argon's is narrower and taller. Because both curves are normalised to the same number of molecules, the area is the same in each case.

  14. [6 marks] Explain why the resistance of a metal filament lamp increases as the pd across it increases, and sketch the shape you would expect for its I–V characteristic.

    Show answer

    Increasing pd increases the current, which increases the rate of energy dissipation in the filament and raises its temperature. The metal ions then vibrate with larger amplitude about their lattice sites, so the drifting conduction electrons collide with them more frequently and their mean drift speed for a given field falls. Lower drift speed for the same pd means lower current, i.e. higher resistance.

    The I–V graph is therefore a curve through the origin that bends towards the voltage axis: straight and steep at small \(V\), progressively flatter as \(V\) increases. It is symmetric about the origin, because a metal is not polarity-dependent. (Contrast with a thermistor, which bends towards the current axis.)
