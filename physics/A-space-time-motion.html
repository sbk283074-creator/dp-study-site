A · Space, time and motion

Theme A asks: how do we describe and predict change? It runs from "a ball rolls down a slope" to "two observers disagree about whether two events happened at the same time". The thread is that the same three quantities — force, momentum, energy — describe motion at every scale, and that at high speed the stage on which motion happens (space and time themselves) stops being fixed. Get A.1–A.3 secure and half of Paper 2's quantitative work is already yours.

A.1 Kinematics SL / HL

Position, distance, displacement

Intuition. Distance is what your pedometer records; displacement is what a compass and a ruler would record between your start and finish. Walk 400 m around a running track and your distance is 400 m but your displacement is zero. Distance is a scalar (magnitude only); displacement \(s\) is a vector (magnitude and direction) and is defined as position relative to a stated reference point.

Common trap

Displacement must be given with a direction, or with a sign if you have defined a positive direction. "The displacement is 30 m" is incomplete; "30 m north" or "\(s = -30\) m taking north as positive" is complete. Similarly speed is scalar and velocity is the vector: \(v = \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}\).

Speed, velocity, acceleration

Instantaneous velocity \(v\)
\(v = \dfrac{ds}{dt}\) — the gradient of a displacement–time graph at a point. Unit m s⁻¹. It is a vector; its magnitude is the instantaneous speed.
Average velocity
\(\dfrac{\text{change in displacement}}{\text{time taken}} = \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}\). Note: displacement, not distance. A lap of the track gives average velocity 0 but average speed \(400/t\).
Acceleration \(a\)
\(a = \dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{d^2s}{dt^2}\) — the rate of change of velocity, unit m s⁻². Because velocity is a vector, acceleration covers three separate cases: speeding up, slowing down, and changing direction at constant speed (circular motion).
Common trap

"Deceleration" is not a sign. If you take the direction of motion as positive and an object is slowing down, then \(a\) is negative. If later it reverses and speeds up in the negative direction, \(a\) is still negative — and it is now speeding up. Never say "deceleration is negative acceleration"; say "the acceleration is opposite in direction to the velocity".

Exam technique

A Paper 1B data-based question often gives you a v–t graph from a video-analysis app and asks you to "determine the distance travelled". If the curve is a smooth curve rather than straight lines, do not use SUVAT — SUVAT needs constant acceleration. Instead count squares under the curve (or estimate with a triangle + rectangle), and state your method: "I estimated the area by dividing the region into 8 trapezia of width 0.5 s."

The SUVAT equations

Intuition. SUVAT is not five formulas to memorise — it is one situation (constant acceleration) described four different ways, each useful because it leaves out one quantity. Learn the derivation once from the area under a v–t graph and you can rebuild any of them in the exam.

\(s\)
displacement (m)
\(u\)
initial velocity (m s⁻¹)
\(v\)
final velocity (m s⁻¹)
\(a\)
constant acceleration (m s⁻²)
\(t\)
time (s)
Derivation All of SUVAT from the area under a velocity–time graph
  1. With constant acceleration the v–t graph is a straight line from \((0,u)\) to \((t,v)\). Its gradient is \(a\), so \(a = \dfrac{v-u}{t}\).
  2. Rearrange: \(v = u + at\). [Equation 1 — leaves out \(s\).]
  3. Displacement = area under the v–t graph. The area is a trapezium of parallel sides \(u\) and \(v\) and width \(t\): \(s = \dfrac{(u+v)}{2}\,t\). [Equation 2 — leaves out \(a\).]
  4. Substitute \(v = u + at\) into the trapezium area: \(s = \dfrac{(u + u + at)}{2}t = ut + \tfrac12 at^2\). [Equation 3 — leaves out \(v\).]
  5. From (1), \(t = \dfrac{v-u}{a}\). Substitute into (2): \(s = \dfrac{(u+v)}{2}\cdot\dfrac{(v-u)}{a} = \dfrac{v^2-u^2}{2a}\).
  6. Rearrange: \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\). [Equation 4 — leaves out \(t\).]
  7. Equivalently, eliminate \(u\): \(s = vt - \tfrac12 at^2\), useful when the final velocity is what you know.

What it means: SUVAT is geometry — gradient and area of a straight-line v–t graph. When it is the wrong tool: whenever \(a\) is not constant. Projectiles (constant \(g\), so fine), but not an object with drag, not SHM, not circular motion.

How to use it — the five-line SUVAT method
  1. Draw a sketch. Mark a positive direction and stick to it.
  2. Write the five symbols \(s, u, v, a, t\) in a row and fill in what you know, with signs and units.
  3. Put "?" against what you want and "×" against what you neither know nor want.
  4. Pick the equation that contains the three knowns and the target, i.e. the one that omits the "×".
  5. Substitute with units, calculate, then sanity-check the magnitude and the sign.
Worked example 1 IB-style · 4 marks · constant acceleration

Question. A train travelling at \(30\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) applies its brakes and decelerates uniformly at \(1.5\ \text{m s}^{-2}\). Determine (a) the time taken to stop and (b) the distance travelled while stopping.

  1. Take the direction of motion as positive: \(u = +30\), \(v = 0\), \(a = -1.5\), \(t = ?\), \(s = ?\).
  2. (a) Use \(v = u+at\): \(0 = 30 - 1.5t \Rightarrow t = \dfrac{30}{1.5} = 20\ \text{s}\).
  3. (b) Use \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\): \(0 = 30^2 + 2(-1.5)s \Rightarrow s = \dfrac{900}{3.0} = 300\ \text{m}\).
Answer: (a) \(20\ \text{s}\), (b) \(300\ \text{m}\). The acceleration is negative because it opposes the velocity — that is exactly why the object slows. Check with the average velocity: \(\frac{30+0}{2} = 15\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) for 20 s = 300 m. Consistent.
Worked example 2 IB-style · 6 marks · a two-stage problem with a graph

Question. A particle starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at \(2.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) for 6.0 s. It then travels at constant velocity for 10 s before decelerating uniformly to rest in 4.0 s. (a) Sketch a velocity–time graph. (b) Determine the total displacement. (c) Determine the average velocity.

  1. Stage 1: \(v = 0 + (2.0)(6.0) = 12\ \text{m s}^{-1}\); displacement \(= \frac12(12)(6.0) = 36\ \text{m}\).
  2. Stage 2: constant \(12\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) for 10 s; displacement \(= 12\times10 = 120\ \text{m}\).
  3. Stage 3: from 12 to 0 in 4.0 s; displacement \(= \frac{12+0}{2}\times4.0 = 24\ \text{m}\).
  4. Total displacement \(= 36 + 120 + 24 = 180\ \text{m}\). Total time \(= 6.0+10+4.0 = 20\ \text{s}\).
  5. Average velocity \(= \dfrac{180}{20} = 9.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
Answer: (b) \(180\ \text{m}\), (c) \(9.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). The total displacement is the total area under the v–t graph — a trapezium of parallel sides 10 s and 20 s and height 12 m s⁻¹ gives \(\frac{10+20}{2}\times12 = 180\) m, confirming the answer.

Projectile motion

Intuition. The one idea that makes projectiles easy: the horizontal and vertical motions are independent. Gravity only acts downwards, so it cannot change the horizontal velocity. A bullet fired horizontally and a bullet dropped from the same height hit the ground at the same instant. So you run two SUVAT calculations side by side, sharing only the time \(t\).

  • Horizontal: \(a_x = 0\), so \(v_x = u\cos\theta\) constant, and \(x = (u\cos\theta)t\).
  • Vertical: \(a_y = -g\) (taking up as positive), so \(v_y = u\sin\theta - gt\), \(y = (u\sin\theta)t - \tfrac12 gt^2\), \(v_y^2 = (u\sin\theta)^2 - 2gy\).
Derivation Range and maximum height of a projectile on level ground
  1. Vertical displacement returns to zero: \(0 = (u\sin\theta)t - \tfrac12 gt^2\), so \(t = 0\) (launch) or \(t = \dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g}\) = total time of flight \(T\).
  2. Range \(R = v_x T = (u\cos\theta)\cdot\dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g} = \dfrac{u^2(2\sin\theta\cos\theta)}{g} = \dfrac{u^2\sin 2\theta}{g}\).
  3. Maximum height: at the apex \(v_y = 0\), so \(0 = (u\sin\theta)^2 - 2gH\), giving \(H = \dfrac{u^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}\).
  4. Maximum range occurs when \(\sin 2\theta = 1\), i.e. \(2\theta = 90^\circ\), \(\theta = 45^\circ\).

What it means: range depends on \(u^2\) and on \(\sin 2\theta\), so 30° and 60° give the same range. When it is wrong: on level ground with no air resistance only. If launch and landing heights differ, go back to the two component equations.

Worked example 3 IB-style · 6 marks · projectile

Question. A ball is thrown with speed \(24\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) at \(35^\circ\) above the horizontal from ground level. Calculate (a) the maximum height and (b) the horizontal range. Assume \(g = 9.81\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) and no air resistance.

  1. Resolve: \(u_x = 24\cos 35^\circ = 19.7\ \text{m s}^{-1}\); \(u_y = 24\sin 35^\circ = 13.8\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
  2. (a) \(H = \dfrac{u_y^2}{2g} = \dfrac{(13.8)^2}{2(9.81)} = \dfrac{189.9}{19.62} = 9.7\ \text{m}\).
  3. (b) Time of flight from vertical motion: \(0 = 13.8t - 4.905t^2 \Rightarrow t = 2.81\ \text{s}\).
  4. Range \(= 19.7 \times 2.81 = 55.3\ \text{m}\). Check with \(u^2\sin 70^\circ/g = 576(0.940)/9.81 = 55.2\ \text{m}\).
Answer: (a) \(9.7\ \text{m}\), (b) \(55\ \text{m}\). Sanity check: at 45° the range would be \(24^2/9.81 = 58.7\) m, so 55 m at 35° is slightly less and plausible.
Worked example 4 IB-style · 7 marks · projectile landing at a different height

Question. A ball is projected horizontally at \(15\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) from the top of a cliff 44 m high. Determine (a) the time of flight and (b) the distance from the base of the cliff at which it lands.

  1. Vertical: \(u_y = 0\), \(a = -9.81\) (take up positive), \(s = -44\) m.
  2. \(s = ut + \tfrac12 at^2 \Rightarrow -44 = 0 - 4.905t^2 \Rightarrow t^2 = 8.97 \Rightarrow t = 3.0\ \text{s}\).
  3. Horizontal: \(x = 15 \times 3.0 = 45\ \text{m}\).
Answer: (a) \(3.0\ \text{s}\), (b) \(45\ \text{m}\). The vertical displacement is negative because the ball ends up below its launch point while up is positive — getting that sign right is worth a mark. Note the time of flight does not depend on the horizontal speed at all.

Fluid resistance and terminal speed

Intuition. Moving through a fluid means shoving fluid molecules out of the way, so the fluid pushes back. The faster you go, the more molecules per second you shove, so the resistive force grows with speed (roughly \(F_D \propto v\) at low speeds, \(F_D \propto v^2\) at the speeds you meet in the lab). A falling object therefore has weight down and drag up; drag grows as it speeds up; when drag equals weight the resultant force is zero, acceleration is zero, and it falls at constant terminal speed.

Derivation Terminal speed from force balance
  1. Resultant force on a falling sphere: \(F = mg - F_D\), with \(F_D = \tfrac12 C_D\rho A v^2\) (or \(F_D = bv\) for the linear model — the syllabus expects you to reason with whichever is given).
  2. Newton's second law: \(m\dfrac{dv}{dt} = mg - bv\) (linear model).
  3. At terminal speed, \(\dfrac{dv}{dt} = 0\), so \(mg = bv_t\) giving \(v_t = \dfrac{mg}{b}\).
  4. For the quadratic model: \(mg = \tfrac12 C_D\rho A v_t^2 \Rightarrow v_t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2mg}{C_D\rho A}}\).

What it means: terminal speed is reached asymptotically — the acceleration falls smoothly to zero, so a v–t graph curves over. When it is wrong: if the question says "assume air resistance is negligible", you ignore all of this and use SUVAT with \(a = g\).

Common trap

Three things students get wrong here. (1) Saying terminal velocity is zero — it is the acceleration that is zero. (2) Drawing the v–t graph reaching terminal speed and then turning sharply horizontal — the approach is a smooth curve. (3) Forgetting that on upward motion drag acts downwards, adding to weight, so the object decelerates faster than \(g\) on the way up.

Worked example 5 IB-style · 5 marks · terminal speed

Question. A sphere of mass 0.020 kg falls through air. The drag force is \(F_D = kv^2\) with \(k = 3.2\times10^{-4}\ \text{N s}^2\text{m}^{-2}\). (a) Calculate the terminal speed. (b) State the acceleration at the instant the speed is half the terminal speed.

  1. (a) At terminal speed \(mg = kv_t^2\), so \(v_t = \sqrt{mg/k} = \sqrt{(0.020)(9.81)/3.2\times10^{-4}} = \sqrt{613} = 24.8\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
  2. (b) At \(v = v_t/2 = 12.4\ \text{m s}^{-1}\): drag \(= k v^2 = 3.2\times10^{-4}(12.4)^2 = 0.049\ \text{N}\). Weight \(= 0.196\ \text{N}\). Resultant \(= 0.196-0.049 = 0.147\ \text{N}\) downwards.
  3. \(a = F/m = 0.147/0.020 = 7.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) downwards.
Answer: (a) \(25\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), (b) \(7.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) down. Check: at half the terminal speed the drag is a quarter of the weight, so the resultant is three-quarters of \(g\) = 7.36 m s⁻². The acceleration is less than \(g\) because drag opposes the weight.
Linking question

Terminal speed links A.1 to B.3 kinetic theory: the drag force exists because the object collides with molecules and transfers momentum to them, and the internal energy of the fluid rises by exactly the work done against drag. It also links to B.1: a falling object that reaches terminal speed stops gaining kinetic energy and instead warms the air.

A.2 Forces and momentum SL / HL

Newton's three laws

First law

An object remains at rest or moves with constant velocity unless acted on by a resultant external force. This is really a definition of what a force does: it changes velocity. It also defines an inertial frame.

Second law (IB form)

\(F = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\) — the resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum. When mass is constant this reduces to \(F = ma\). The IB wants the momentum form; use it.

Third law

If body A exerts a force on body B, then B exerts an equal and opposite force on A: same type, same magnitude, opposite direction, acting on different bodies.

Why the third law trips people up

The two forces in a third-law pair never cancel, because they act on different objects. A book resting on a table: the pair is (Earth pulls book) ↔ (book pulls Earth). The reason the book is in equilibrium is a different pair of forces on the same body: weight down and normal reaction up.

Free-body diagrams

Draw the object alone, as a dot or a box, and draw only forces acting on it, as arrows from its centre. Label each. The usual suspects: weight \(W = mg\) (down, from the centre of mass), normal reaction \(N\) (perpendicular to the surface), friction \(f\) (along the surface, opposing motion or attempted motion), tension \(T\) (along the string), upthrust/buoyancy, drag, applied force.

Common trap

Resolving the weight with the wrong angle. The angle between \(mg\) and the line perpendicular to the plane is \(\theta\), so the component parallel to the plane is \(mg\sin\theta\) and the component perpendicular is \(mg\cos\theta\). Check the limit: as \(\theta \to 0\) the down-slope component \(mg\sin\theta \to 0\) (a flat floor has no tendency to slide) — correct.

Friction and contact forces

Normal reaction \(N\)
The contact force perpendicular to the surfaces, arising from the compression of the surfaces at the atomic level. It is not always \(mg\) — on an incline it is \(mg\cos\theta\), and with a vertical component of an applied force it is different again.
Static friction
\(f_s \le \mu_s N\). It takes whatever value is needed up to a maximum; it is not always equal to \(\mu_s N\).
Kinetic (dynamic) friction
\(f_k = \mu_k N\), opposing the relative motion. Usually \(\mu_k \lt \mu_s\), which is why pushing a heavy box gets easier once it starts moving.
Worked example 6 IB-style · 6 marks · inclined plane with friction

Question. A 4.0 kg block rests on a rough plane inclined at \(25^\circ\) to the horizontal. The coefficient of static friction is 0.45. (a) Show that the block does not slide. (b) The plane is tilted until the block just begins to slide. Determine this angle.

  1. (a) Component down the plane: \(mg\sin 25^\circ = (4.0)(9.81)(0.423) = 16.6\ \text{N}\).
  2. Maximum friction: \(f_{\max} = \mu_s N = \mu_s mg\cos 25^\circ = 0.45(4.0)(9.81)(0.906) = 16.0\ \text{N}\).
  3. Since 16.0 < 16.6, friction cannot hold it — so the block does slide. (If the question says "show that it does not slide", check the numbers: with \(\mu_s = 0.50\), \(f_{\max} = 17.8\) N > 16.6 N, so it stays at rest with friction equal to 16.6 N, not 17.8 N.)
  4. (b) At the point of slipping, \(mg\sin\theta = \mu_s mg\cos\theta\), so \(\tan\theta = \mu_s = 0.45\) and \(\theta = 24.2^\circ\).
Answer: (b) \(\theta = 24^\circ\). Note the elegant result: \(\mu_s = \tan\theta_{\text{critical}}\). The mass cancels — the angle of repose is independent of mass, which is a good exam talking point.
Worked example 7 IB-style · 8 marks · two connected masses

Question. A 3.0 kg block on a smooth horizontal table is connected by a light inextensible string over a smooth pulley to a hanging 2.0 kg mass. Determine the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string.

  1. Both masses share the same acceleration magnitude \(a\) (inextensible string).
  2. For the 3.0 kg block (horizontal, smooth): \(T = 3.0a\).
  3. For the 2.0 kg mass (downwards positive): \(2.0g - T = 2.0a\).
  4. Add: \(2.0g = 5.0a \Rightarrow a = \dfrac{19.6}{5.0} = 3.92\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).
  5. \(T = 3.0 \times 3.92 = 11.8\ \text{N}\).
Answer: \(a = 3.9\ \text{m s}^{-2}\), \(T = 12\ \text{N}\). Sanity checks: \(a \lt g\) (the hanging mass is not in free fall because the string pulls up on it) ✓; \(T \lt 2.0g = 19.6\) N ✓ and \(T > 0\) ✓. The tension is not equal to the weight of the hanging mass — that is the single most common error in connected-body problems, because the hanging mass is accelerating.

Circular motion and centripetal force

Intuition. An object moving in a circle at constant speed is still accelerating, because its velocity direction keeps changing. The acceleration points to the centre — centripetal means "centre-seeking" — and has magnitude \(a = v^2/r = \omega^2 r\). The centripetal force is not a new force; it is the name we give to the resultant of real forces (tension, friction, gravity, normal reaction) that happens to point to the centre.

Derivation \(a = v^2/r\) from the geometry of a velocity vector triangle
  1. In time \(\Delta t\) the object moves through angle \(\Delta\theta\), so the arc length is \(\Delta s = r\Delta\theta\) and \(\Delta\theta = \omega\Delta t\).
  2. The velocity vector rotates through the same angle \(\Delta\theta\) while keeping constant magnitude \(v\). The tip of the velocity vector therefore traces a circle of radius \(v\), and the magnitude of the velocity change is \(|\Delta\mathbf{v}| \approx v\Delta\theta\) for small \(\Delta\theta\).
  3. \(a = \dfrac{|\Delta\mathbf{v}|}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{v\Delta\theta}{\Delta t} = v\omega\).
  4. Since \(v = \omega r\), \(a = v\left(\dfrac{v}{r}\right) = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r\).
  5. Direction: for small \(\Delta\theta\), \(\Delta\mathbf{v}\) points towards the centre.

What it means: the acceleration is perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes direction without changing speed and does no work. When it is wrong: if the speed is changing there is also a tangential acceleration; then the resultant is the vector sum and the motion is not "uniform circular motion".

How to use it — circular motion problems in four lines
  1. Draw the free-body diagram at the position asked about (top of the loop, bottom, side, banked track).
  2. Resolve along the radius, taking the direction towards the centre as positive.
  3. Write \(\sum F_{\text{towards centre}} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\).
  4. Never add "centrifugal force" to the diagram — there isn't one. If you are tempted, you are in the rotating frame, and the IB will not thank you.
Worked example 8 IB-style · 6 marks · banked track / conical pendulum

Question. A car of mass 1200 kg travels at 25 m s⁻¹ around a curve of radius 80 m on a horizontal road. (a) Calculate the minimum coefficient of friction needed. (b) The road is now banked at \(20^\circ\). Determine the speed at which no friction is needed.

  1. (a) Friction provides the centripetal force: \(\mu mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\), so \(\mu = \dfrac{v^2}{rg} = \dfrac{625}{80\times9.81} = 0.80\).
  2. (b) With no friction, resolving: vertical \(N\cos 20^\circ = mg\); radial \(N\sin 20^\circ = mv^2/r\).
  3. Divide: \(\tan 20^\circ = \dfrac{v^2}{rg} \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{rg\tan 20^\circ} = \sqrt{80\times9.81\times0.364} = \sqrt{285.7} = 16.9\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
Answer: (a) \(\mu = 0.80\) (a high value — this is a sharp, fast curve); (b) \(17\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) (\(\approx 61\) km h⁻¹). The mass cancelled in both parts: centripetal requirements depend on \(v\) and \(r\), never on mass.
Worked example 9 IB-style · 6 marks · vertical circle

Question. A bucket of water of total mass 5.0 kg is swung in a vertical circle of radius 1.2 m. (a) Determine the minimum speed at the top for the water to stay in the bucket. (b) Determine the tension in the handle at the bottom when the speed there is 8.0 m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) At the top, both weight and the contact force act towards the centre. Minimum speed: the contact force is zero, so \(mg = mv^2/r \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{gr} = \sqrt{9.81\times1.2} = 3.43\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
  2. (b) At the bottom, tension acts up (towards centre) and weight down (away): \(T - mg = mv^2/r\).
  3. \(T = m\left(g + \dfrac{v^2}{r}\right) = 5.0\left(9.81 + \dfrac{64}{1.2}\right) = 5.0(9.81 + 53.3) = 316\ \text{N}\).
Answer: (a) \(3.4\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), (b) \(320\ \text{N}\). Note the tension at the bottom is over six times the weight — that is why the bottom of a roller coaster loop feels so heavy, and it is why real loops are teardrop-shaped rather than circular.

Momentum and impulse

Momentum \(p = mv\)
Vector, unit kg m s⁻¹ (equivalently N s). "The quantity of motion" — how hard it is to stop something.
Impulse = \(F\Delta t = \Delta p\)
Unit N s. Equals the area under a force–time graph.
Derivation \(F = \Delta p/\Delta t\) is Newton's second law
  1. Newton originally stated his second law as: the rate of change of momentum of a body is proportional to the resultant force and takes place in the direction of the force.
  2. \(F = \dfrac{dp}{dt} = \dfrac{d(mv)}{dt}\).
  3. If \(m\) is constant: \(F = m\dfrac{dv}{dt} = ma\).
  4. For a finite time: \(F_{\text{avg}}\Delta t = \Delta p = m(v-u)\).

What it means: the momentum form is more general — it still works when mass changes (a rocket, a leaking sand truck). Why it matters in the exam: impact questions ask for the average force; you get it from \(F = \Delta p/\Delta t\), not from \(F = ma\), because you usually do not know \(a\) during a collision.

Conservation of momentum

Derivation Conservation of momentum from Newton's third law
  1. Two bodies A and B collide and exert forces on each other. By Newton's third law, \(F_{AB} = -F_{BA}\) at every instant.
  2. They interact for the same time \(\Delta t\), so the impulses are equal and opposite: \(F_{AB}\Delta t = -F_{BA}\Delta t\).
  3. Impulse = change in momentum, so \(\Delta p_A = -\Delta p_B\), hence \(\Delta p_A + \Delta p_B = 0\).
  4. Therefore \(p_{\text{total}}\) is unchanged: \(m_Au_A + m_Bu_B = m_Av_A + m_Bv_B\).

Condition: no external resultant force on the system. Internal forces cancel in pairs. When it is wrong: if there is significant external friction over the collision time, or an external impulse.

Common trap

Momentum is a vector, so you must choose a positive direction and use signs. In an explosion the initial momentum is zero, so \(m_Av_A = -m_Bv_B\) — the two momenta are equal and opposite, and not \(m_Av_A = m_Bv_B\). Students routinely lose two marks writing the explosion equation without the minus sign.

Worked example 10 IB-style · 6 marks · inelastic collision

Question. A 0.15 kg ball moving at 8.0 m s⁻¹ strikes a stationary 0.25 kg ball. After the collision the two balls stick together. (a) Determine their common speed. (b) Calculate the kinetic energy lost.

  1. Take the initial direction as positive. \(0.15(8.0) + 0.25(0) = (0.15+0.25)v\).
  2. \(1.2 = 0.40v \Rightarrow v = 3.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
  3. Initial KE \(= \tfrac12(0.15)(8.0)^2 = 4.8\ \text{J}\). Final KE \(= \tfrac12(0.40)(3.0)^2 = 1.8\ \text{J}\).
  4. Loss \(= 4.8 - 1.8 = 3.0\ \text{J}\) (63% of the initial KE becomes internal energy and sound).
Answer: (a) \(3.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), (b) 3.0 J lost. Momentum is conserved because the collision is fast (external friction negligible); kinetic energy is not conserved — that is the definition of an inelastic collision.
Worked example 11 IB-style · 8 marks · elastic collision, both unknowns

A.3 Work, energy and power SL / HL

Work done by a force

Intuition. Work measures how much energy a force transfers. Push a box sideways and you transfer nothing to its motion — the force is perpendicular to the displacement, so no work. That is why the definition includes \(\cos\theta\).

\[W = F s \cos\theta\]

where \(F\) is the magnitude of the force (N), \(s\) the displacement (m) of the point of application, and \(\theta\) the angle between the force and the displacement. Unit: J (1 J = 1 N m).

Common trap

The angle \(\theta\) is between the force and the displacement, not between the force and the horizontal. If you pull a sled with a rope at 30° to the horizontal and the sled moves horizontally, \(\theta = 30^\circ\) and \(W = Fs\cos 30^\circ\). If a crate slides down a slope while friction acts up it, \(\theta = 180^\circ\) and the work done by friction is \(-fs\) — negative, because friction removes energy. Always state that reason in words: markschemes award a mark for "negative because the force opposes the motion".

Worked example 12 IB-style · 5 marks · work at an angle

Question. A crate is pulled 12 m across a horizontal floor by a rope with tension 150 N at 40° to the horizontal. Friction is 90 N. Calculate (a) the work done by the tension, (b) the work done by friction, (c) the net work on the crate.

  1. (a) \(W_T = Fs\cos\theta = 150 \times 12 \times \cos 40^\circ = 150\times12\times0.766 = 1379\ \text{J}\).
  2. (b) Friction opposes motion (\(\theta = 180^\circ\)): \(W_f = 90\times12\times\cos 180^\circ = -1080\ \text{J}\).
  3. (c) Net work \(= 1379 - 1080 = 299\ \text{J}\) (weight and normal reaction do zero work, being perpendicular to the horizontal displacement).
Answer: (a) \(1.4\ \text{kJ}\), (b) \(-1.1\ \text{kJ}\), (c) \(0.30\ \text{kJ}\). The net work equals the change in kinetic energy — so the crate is speeding up, by 299 J worth.

Kinetic, gravitational and elastic potential energy

Derivation \(E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2\) from work and Newton's second law
  1. A constant resultant force \(F\) accelerates a mass \(m\) from rest to speed \(v\) over distance \(s\). The work done is \(W = Fs\).
  2. \(F = ma\) and \(v^2 = 2as\) (SUVAT with \(u = 0\)), so \(s = \dfrac{v^2}{2a}\).
  3. \(W = ma\cdot\dfrac{v^2}{2a} = \tfrac12 mv^2\).
  4. This is the energy stored in the motion, so \(E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2\).

What it means: doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy — which is why stopping distances scale with \(v^2\), not \(v\). When it is wrong: at speeds comparable with \(c\); use relativity (A.5).

Derivation \(\Delta E_p = mg\Delta h\) and \(E_e = \tfrac12 kx^2\)
  1. Lifting a mass \(m\) slowly through \(\Delta h\) requires an upward force equal to \(mg\). Work done \(= mg\Delta h\), which is stored as gravitational potential energy.
  2. This is change in potential energy only — \(E_p\) has no absolute value unless you choose a zero level. Always say "relative to the floor".
  3. For a spring obeying Hooke's law \(F = kx\), the force varies from 0 to \(kx\) as the extension grows from 0 to \(x\). Average force \(= \tfrac12 kx\), so work done \(= \tfrac12 kx \cdot x = \tfrac12 kx^2\).
  4. Equivalently, the area under an \(F\)–\(x\) graph (a triangle) \(= \tfrac12 Fx = \tfrac12 kx^2\).

What it means: the factor \(\tfrac12\) comes from the force increasing linearly — that is why a spring stores half of what a constant force of the same maximum value would.

Conservation of mechanical energy

If only conservative forces (gravity, spring) do work, then

\[E_k + E_p = \text{constant}, \qquad \tfrac12 mv^2 + mgh = \text{constant}\]

If friction or drag acts, mechanical energy is not conserved; instead \(W_{\text{non-conservative}} = \Delta E_{\text{mechanical}}\), which is negative for friction.

How to use it — the energy method vs SUVAT
  1. Use energy when the question involves speeds and heights but not times. A roller coaster loop, a pendulum, a falling diver, a spring launcher — all energy, no SUVAT.
  2. Use SUVAT when the question involves times or a constant acceleration.
  3. Energy questions are often faster because you never resolve vectors and never worry about the path shape — only the change in height.
Worked example 13 IB-style · 7 marks · energy with friction

Question. A 60 kg skier starts from rest at a height of 45 m and reaches the bottom at 22 m s⁻¹. (a) Calculate the energy dissipated. (b) Hence determine the average resistive force if the slope length is 320 m.

  1. Initial energy \(= mgh = 60\times9.81\times45 = 26\,500\ \text{J}\).
  2. Final KE \(= \tfrac12(60)(22)^2 = 14\,520\ \text{J}\).
  3. Dissipated \(= 26\,500 - 14\,520 = 11\,980\ \text{J} \approx 12\ \text{kJ}\).
  4. (b) Work done against resistance \(= F_{\text{avg}}\times 320 = 11\,980\), so \(F_{\text{avg}} = 37.4\ \text{N}\).
Answer: (a) \(12\ \text{kJ}\), (b) \(37\ \text{N}\). Check without friction the speed would be \(\sqrt{2\times9.81\times45} = 29.7\) m s⁻¹; 22 m s⁻¹ is lower, so energy was lost — consistent.
Worked example 14 IB-style · 8 marks · vertical circle with energy + forces

Question. A bead of mass 0.050 kg slides on a smooth vertical circular track of radius 0.80 m. It starts from rest at the top. Determine the normal reaction at the bottom of the circle.

  1. Height fallen from top to bottom \(= 2r = 1.60\) m. Energy: \(\tfrac12 mv^2 = mg(2r)\), so \(v^2 = 4gr = 4(9.81)(0.80) = 31.4\), \(v = 5.6\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
  2. At the bottom, towards the centre (upwards) is positive: \(N - mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\).
  3. \(N = m\left(g + \dfrac{v^2}{r}\right) = 0.050\left(9.81 + \dfrac{31.4}{0.80}\right) = 0.050(9.81 + 39.2) = 2.45\ \text{N}\).
Answer: \(2.5\ \text{N}\), i.e. five times the weight (0.49 N). This is a classic linking question: it uses A.3 energy to get the speed and then A.2 circular motion to get the force. Examiners love it because you cannot do it with one theme alone.

Power and efficiency

Power \(P = \dfrac{W}{t} = \dfrac{\Delta E}{t}\)
Rate of doing work, unit W (1 W = 1 J s⁻¹).
Power from force and velocity
\(P = Fv\) for a force \(F\) moving its point of application at speed \(v\) in the direction of the force (more generally \(P = Fv\cos\theta\)).
Efficiency
\(\eta = \dfrac{\text{useful energy output}}{\text{total energy input}} = \dfrac{\text{useful power out}}{\text{total power in}}\). Dimensionless, often ×100%.
Derivation \(P = Fv\)
  1. \(P = \dfrac{dW}{dt}\) and \(dW = F\,ds\) for a force along the displacement.
  2. \(P = F\dfrac{ds}{dt} = Fv\).
  3. For a car at constant speed on the level, the engine force equals the resistive force, so \(P = F_{\text{drag}}v\) — and since \(F_{\text{drag}}\propto v^2\), the power needed rises as \(v^3\).

What it means: doubling your motorway speed needs eight times the power. That is the whole argument for speed limits and fuel economy.

Question. A car of mass 1400 kg climbs a hill of gradient 1 in 12 at a constant 18 m s⁻¹. The resistive force is 700 N. (a) Calculate the minimum power output of the engine. (b) If the engine's efficiency is 28% and the fuel has energy density 44 MJ kg⁻¹, determine the fuel consumed per kilometre.

A.4 Rigid body mechanics HL only

Intuition. Everything so far treated objects as point masses. Now the shape matters. Pushing a door near the hinge barely opens it; pushing at the handle works — same force, different effect. Torque and rotational equilibrium

\[\tau = Fr\sin\theta\]

with \(F\) the force (N), \(r\) the distance from the pivot to the point of application (m), and \(\theta\) the angle between the force and the line from pivot to that point. Unit N m (note: not J, even though the dimensions match — torque is a vector thing, and examiners do comment).

Moment of a force = torque
The turning effect. Perpendicular distance \(d = r\sin\theta\), so \(\tau = Fd\).
Principle of moments
For an object in rotational equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments.
Centre of mass / centre of gravity
The single point at which the entire weight appears to act. For a uniform symmetric object it is the geometric centre.
A couple
Two equal, opposite, parallel forces whose lines of action do not coincide. Torque of a couple \(= Fd\) where \(d\) is the perpendicular separation. A couple produces rotation with no translation.
Derivation The principle of moments from \(\tau = I\alpha\)
  1. Rotational equilibrium means \(\alpha = 0\), so from \(\tau = I\alpha\) the net torque is zero.
  2. Taking anticlockwise as positive: \(\sum \tau = 0\), so \(\sum \tau_{\text{anticlockwise}} - \sum \tau_{\text{clockwise}} = 0\).
  3. Hence \(\sum \tau_{\text{anticlockwise}} = \sum \tau_{\text{clockwise}}\).
  4. For a body in complete equilibrium you also need \(\sum F = 0\) (no translation). Both conditions.

What it means: two independent conditions, so two families of equation. Trap: forgetting \(\sum F = 0\) — a body can have balanced moments and still be accelerating sideways.

Worked example 16 IB-style · 6 marks · moments

Question. A uniform beam of mass 20 kg and length 4.0 m is hinged at one end and held horizontal by a cable attached 3.0 m from the hinge at 40° to the beam. Determine the tension in the cable.

  1. Take moments about the hinge (this eliminates the unknown hinge reaction — the reason you always take moments about the pivot).
  2. Anticlockwise moment of the cable's vertical component: \(T\sin 40^\circ \times 3.0\). Only the vertical component turns the beam; the horizontal component acts along the beam and has zero moment.
  3. Clockwise moment of the weight acting at the centre (2.0 m from the hinge): \(20\times9.81\times2.0 = 392\ \text{N m}\).
  4. \(T(0.643)(3.0) = 392 \Rightarrow T = \dfrac{392}{1.93} = 203\ \text{N}\).
Answer: \(T = 200\ \text{N}\). Sanity check: if the cable were vertical, \(T = 392/3.0 = 131\) N; at 40° more tension is needed. ✓
Worked example 17 IB-style · 6 marks · tipping

Question. A uniform rectangular block of mass 5.0 kg, base 0.40 m wide and 1.2 m tall, stands on a rough floor. A horizontal force is applied at the top. (a) Determine the force needed to just start tipping. (b) State whether it tips or slides first if \(\mu_s = 0.60\).

  1. At the point of tipping the block pivots about the bottom edge; the normal reaction acts there, so take moments about that edge.
  2. Clockwise moment of the applied force: \(F \times 1.2\). Anticlockwise moment of the weight acting 0.20 m from the edge: \(5.0\times9.81\times0.20 = 9.81\ \text{N m}\).
  3. \(F(1.2) = 9.81 \Rightarrow F = 8.2\ \text{N}\).
  4. (b) Sliding begins at \(F = \mu_s mg = 0.60\times5.0\times9.81 = 29\ \text{N}\). Since 8.2 < 29, the block tips first.
Answer: (a) 8.2 N, (b) tips first. The comparison is the point of the question: whichever threshold is lower happens.

Moment of inertia

\[I = \sum_i m_i r_i^2 \quad \text{or} \quad I = \int r^2\,dm\]

Unit kg m². It is not just "how much mass" but "how far the mass is from the axis" — squared. The data booklet gives standard results: a point mass \(mr^2\); a hoop about its centre \(mr^2\); a solid cylinder or disc about its axis \(\tfrac12 mr^2\); a solid sphere about a diameter \(\tfrac25 mr^2\); a thin rod about its centre \(\tfrac1{12}ml^2\) and about its end \(\tfrac13 ml^2\).

Common trap

Two traps. (1) Using the wrong axis — \(I\) is meaningless without one, and the same object has different \(I\) about different axes (a rod about its end has four times the \(I\) it has about its centre). (2) Expecting a hollow and a solid cylinder of the same mass and radius to roll at the same rate. All the mass of a hoop is at radius \(r\) so \(I = mr^2\); a solid cylinder's mass is spread from 0 to \(r\) so \(I = \tfrac12 mr^2\). The solid one always wins a rolling race, and the mass cancels — the result is independent of both \(m\) and \(r\).

Newton's second law for rotation

Derivation \(\tau = I\alpha\) from \(F = ma\)
  1. Take a point mass \(m_i\) at distance \(r_i\) from the axis, with a tangential force \(F_i\).
  2. Tangential acceleration \(a_i = r_i\alpha\), so \(F_i = m_ir_i\alpha\).
  3. Torque on that mass: \(\tau_i = F_ir_i = m_ir_i^2\alpha\).
  4. Sum over all the masses: \(\sum\tau_i = \left(\sum m_ir_i^2\right)\alpha = I\alpha\).

What it means: \(I\) plays the role of "rotational mass". When it is wrong: if the axis is accelerating or not fixed, you must use the centre-of-mass form plus the parallel-axis ideas.

Angular momentum and its conservation

\[L = I\omega \qquad \text{and} \qquad \tau = \frac{dL}{dt}\]

If the net external torque is zero, \(L\) is constant: \(I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2\). This is why an ice skater spins faster when she pulls her arms in — \(I\) falls, so \(\omega\) must rise.

Worked example 18 IB-style · 7 marks · conservation of angular momentum

Question. A student sits on a rotating stool holding two 3.0 kg masses at 0.80 m from the axis, rotating at 1.5 rad s⁻¹. The student + stool has moment of inertia 4.0 kg m². She pulls the masses in to 0.25 m from the axis. (a) Determine the new angular velocity. (b) Explain the change in kinetic energy.

  1. Initial: \(I_i = 4.0 + 2(3.0)(0.80)^2 = 4.0 + 3.84 = 7.84\ \text{kg m}^2\).
  2. Final: \(I_f = 4.0 + 2(3.0)(0.25)^2 = 4.0 + 0.375 = 4.375\ \text{kg m}^2\).

  4. (b) \(E_i = \tfrac12(7.84)(1.5)^2 = 8.8\ \text{J}\); \(E_f = \tfrac12(4.375)(2.69)^2 = 15.8\ \text{J}\).

A.5 Galilean and special relativity HL only

Intuition. You already believe in relativity. On a train moving smoothly at 300 km h⁻¹ you can pour a cup of tea, drop a coin straight down, and play catch — everything behaves exactly as it does at rest. That is Galilean relativity, and it says: the laws of mechanics are the same in all inertial frames, so there is no experiment in mechanics that tells you whether you are moving uniformly or standing still.

Then came light. Maxwell's equations predicted a speed \(c = 3.00\times10^8\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) with no mention of who was measuring it. Every attempt (notably Michelson–Morley) to detect a change in the speed of light caused by Earth's motion failed. Einstein's move was radical: take the constancy of \(c\) as a postulate, and accept that space and time must bend to accommodate it.

Reference frames and Galilean transformations

Reference frame
A coordinate system plus clocks, used to measure positions and times of events.
Inertial frame
A frame in which Newton's first law holds — i.e. one that is not accelerating or rotating. A frame moving at constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is also inertial.
Event
Something that happens at a particular place at a particular time — a point in spacetime.

Galilean transformations between frame S and frame S′ moving at speed \(v\) along \(x\):

\[x' = x - vt, \qquad y'=y,\qquad z'=z,\qquad t' = t\]

Velocity addition: \(u' = u - v\). Time is absolute — everyone agrees on it. Newton's laws are invariant under this transformation, which is exactly why the train feels normal.

The two postulates of special relativity

Postulate 1 — the principle of relativity

The laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames of reference. There is no preferred frame; you cannot claim to be "really" at rest.

Postulate 2 — the invariance of \(c\)

The speed of light in a vacuum is the same value \(c\) for all inertial observers, regardless of the motion of the source or the observer.

Why this forces time itself to change

Suppose you are on a train and switch on a lamp in the middle of a carriage. You see the light reach the front and back simultaneously. Someone on the platform sees the train moving forward, so the back wall rushes to meet the light and the front wall runs away from it. Since both observers must measure the same \(c\), the platform observer sees the light reach the back first. Two events simultaneous for one observer are not simultaneous for the other. Relativity of simultaneity is not a paradox — it is the logical price of the second postulate.

The Lorentz factor and the Lorentz transformations

\[\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\dfrac{v^2}{c^2}}}\]

\(\gamma\) is dimensionless, \(\gamma \ge 1\), and it is the single number that controls everything: \(\gamma = 1\) at rest, \(\gamma \to \infty\) as \(v \to c\). At \(v = 0.6c\), \(\gamma = 1.25\); at \(v = 0.8c\), \(\gamma = 1.67\); at \(v = 0.99c\), \(\gamma = 7.09\).

The Lorentz transformations (S′ moving at \(v\) along \(x\)):

\[x' = \gamma(x - vt), \qquad t' = \gamma\left(t - \frac{vx}{c^2}\right), \qquad y'=y,\qquad z'=z\]

Common trap

Compare with the Galilean transformation \(t' = t\): the extra term \(-vx/c^2\) in \(t'\) is where all of the "weirdness" lives. It says that the time you assign to a distant event depends on where it happened and how fast you are moving. Students who memorise \(x' = \gamma(x-vt)\) but write \(t' = t\) get every simultaneity question wrong.

Time dilation and length contraction

Proper time \(\Delta t_0\)
The time interval between two events measured in the frame in which the events occur at the same place (a clock at rest relative to the events). It is the shortest measured interval.
Dilated time \(\Delta t\)
\(\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t_0\). Measured by an observer who sees the clock moving. Moving clocks run slow.
Proper length \(L_0\)
The length of an object measured in the frame in which the object is at rest. It is the longest measured length.
Contracted length \(L\)
\(L = \dfrac{L_0}{\gamma}\), measured in the direction of relative motion only. Dimensions perpendicular to the motion are unchanged.
Derivation Time dilation from a light clock
  1. Imagine a clock consisting of two mirrors separated by \(L_0\), with a light pulse bouncing between them. One "tick" is the round trip. In the clock's own frame the time is \(\Delta t_0 = 2L_0/c\).
  2. Now view the clock moving sideways at speed \(v\). The light must travel a longer, diagonal path. In time \(\Delta t\) the clock moves \(v\Delta t\) while the light travels \(c\Delta t\) along the diagonal.
  3. By Pythagoras, over half a tick: \(\left(\dfrac{c\Delta t}{2}\right)^2 = \left(\dfrac{v\Delta t}{2}\right)^2 + L_0^2\).
  4. Divide by \(c^2/4\): \((\Delta t)^2\left(1 - \dfrac{v^2}{c^2}\right) = \dfrac{4L_0^2}{c^2} = (\Delta t_0)^2\).
  5. So \(\Delta t = \dfrac{\Delta t_0}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}} = \gamma\,\Delta t_0\).

What it means: the moving clock's tick takes longer because the light has further to go — not because anything is "wrong" with the clock. When it is wrong: never use it without identifying whose clock; the moving observer notices nothing unusual about their own clock.

Worked example 20 IB-style · 5 marks · muons — the classic

Question. Muons are created in the upper atmosphere at a height of 10 km and travel downwards at \(0.995c\). The muon proper lifetime is \(2.2\ \mu\text{s}\). (a) Explain why, without relativity, very few muons would reach the ground. (b) Calculate the mean distance travelled in the Earth frame, taking time dilation into account.

  1. (a) Without relativity, distance \(= c \times \tau = 3.00\times10^8 \times 2.2\times10^{-6} = 660\ \text{m}\). This is far less than 10 km, so essentially no muons should survive to sea level — yet they are detected in large numbers.
  2. (b) \(\gamma = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-0.995^2}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-0.990025}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{0.009975}} = \dfrac{1}{0.09987} = 10.0\).
  3. Dilated lifetime \(= \gamma\tau = 10.0\times2.2 = 22\ \mu\text{s}\).
  4. Distance \(= 0.995\times3.00\times10^8\times22\times10^{-6} = 6.6\times10^3\ \text{m} = 6.6\ \text{km}\).
Answer: 6.6 km in the Earth frame — an order of magnitude more, which is why muons reach the ground. And now the same physics from the muon's frame: the muon's clock is normal, but the atmosphere is length-contracted to \(10/10.0 = 1.0\) km, so the muon only has 1 km to cross and survives. Both observers agree that the muon reaches the ground; they disagree about why. That is relativity working correctly, and examiners love it.
Worked example 21 IB-style · 6 marks · length contraction + the twin paradox

Question. A spacecraft of proper length 80 m travels past a space station at 0.60c. (a) Determine its length as measured by the station. (b) The spacecraft travels to a star 12 light-years away (as measured from Earth) at 0.60c and returns. Determine the age difference between the astronaut and an Earth observer on return.

  1. (a) \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.36} = 1/0.8 = 1.25\). Contracted length \(L = L_0/\gamma = 80/1.25 = 64\ \text{m}\).
  2. (b) Earth frame: distance 12 ly out and 12 ly back at 0.60c, so \(t = 24/0.60 = 40\) years pass on Earth.
  3. Astronaut's proper time: \(\Delta t_0 = \Delta t/\gamma = 40/1.25 = 32\) years.
  4. Difference \(= 40 - 32 = 8\) years. The astronaut is 8 years younger.
Answer: (a) 64 m, (b) the astronaut ages 32 years while Earth ages 40 — an 8-year difference. Why it is not a paradox: the situation is not symmetric. The astronaut accelerates, turns around, and changes inertial frame; the Earth observer stays in one frame. Only the astronaut's worldline is bent, and the bent one accumulates less proper time. If a question asks you to "resolve the twin paradox", that asymmetry is the entire answer.

Spacetime diagrams

A spacetime diagram plots \(ct\) (vertical) against \(x\) (horizontal). Using \(ct\) as the vertical axis means light rays travel at 45°. Key features you must be able to draw and read:

  • A stationary object's worldline is vertical; a moving object's is tilted away from vertical; light is at exactly 45°.
  • The steeper the worldline, the slower the object.
  • Light cone: the 45° lines from an event separate events that can be causally connected (inside the cone) from those that cannot (outside — "spacelike separated").
  • The \(x'\) axis of a moving observer is tilted relative to the \(x\) axis — that tilt is the relativity of simultaneity, drawn.
Common trap

Three reliable markschemes complaints. (1) Saying "moving clocks run slow" without saying as measured by whom — always name the frame. (2) Applying length contraction to the dimension perpendicular to the motion (it does not contract). (3) Using \(\gamma\) upside down: the proper time is the shortest and the proper length is the longest. If your "dilated" time came out smaller than the proper time, you divided instead of multiplying.

Linking question

Relativity links to E.2 matter waves through relativistic momentum \(p = \gamma m_0v\), and to D.3: particle accelerators must use relativistic momentum because particles reach speeds where \(\gamma \gg 1\). It also links to C.5 Doppler: the relativistic Doppler formula for light replaces the classical one when the source speed is a significant fraction of \(c\). And it links to E.3: in fission and fusion the mass "lost" is real and \(E = mc^2\) is the relativistic rest energy.

Practice

  1. [3 marks] A car accelerates uniformly from 12 m s⁻¹ to 28 m s⁻¹ over 160 m. Determine its acceleration.

    Show answer

    Use \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\): \(28^2 = 12^2 + 2a(160)\), so \(784 - 144 = 320a\), giving \(a = 640/320 = 2.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}\). Check: time \(= (28-12)/2.0 = 8.0\) s; average speed \(20\ \text{m s}^{-1}\times8.0 = 160\) m ✓.

  2. [4 marks] A stone is thrown horizontally at 20 m s⁻¹ from a cliff and lands 60 m from the base. Determine the height of the cliff.

    Show answer

    Horizontal: \(t = 60/20 = 3.0\) s. Vertical: \(h = \tfrac12 gt^2 = \tfrac12(9.81)(9.0) = 44\ \text{m}\). The horizontal and vertical motions share only the time — that is the whole method.

  3. [5 marks] Explain, with reference to the forces acting, why a falling raindrop reaches a constant speed, and describe how its acceleration changes during the fall.

    Show answer

    Two forces act: weight \(mg\) downwards (constant) and drag \(F_D\) upwards (increasing with speed). Initially \(v = 0\) so drag is zero and the acceleration is \(g\). As the speed increases the drag increases, so the resultant force \(mg - F_D\) falls and the acceleration decreases. Eventually \(F_D = mg\), the resultant force is zero, the acceleration is zero, and the drop continues at constant terminal speed. Note: the acceleration falls to zero; the velocity does not.

  4. [6 marks] A 0.20 kg ball moving at 6.0 m s⁻¹ rebounds from a wall at 4.0 m s⁻¹ after being in contact with it for 0.050 s. Calculate the average force exerted by the wall on the ball.

    Show answer

    Take the initial direction as positive: \(u = +6.0\), \(v = -4.0\). \(\Delta p = m(v-u) = 0.20(-4.0-6.0) = -2.0\ \text{kg m s}^{-1}\). \(F = \Delta p/\Delta t = -2.0/0.050 = -40\ \text{N}\). The magnitude is 40 N and the negative sign shows the force is directed away from the wall, i.e. opposite to the incoming velocity — as expected.

  5. [6 marks] A 1.5 kg trolley moving at 2.0 m s⁻¹ collides with a stationary 2.5 kg trolley and they stick together. (a) Determine the final speed. (b) Show that the collision is inelastic.

    Show answer

    (a) \(1.5(2.0) = (1.5+2.5)v \Rightarrow v = 3.0/4.0 = 0.75\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

    (b) KE before \(= \tfrac12(1.5)(4.0) = 3.0\) J; KE after \(= \tfrac12(4.0)(0.5625) = 1.13\) J. Since 1.13 < 3.0, kinetic energy is not conserved, so the collision is inelastic. (Momentum is still conserved: \(4.0\times0.75 = 3.0\) ✓.)

  6. [6 marks] A uniform rod of mass 2.0 kg and length 1.5 m is pivoted 0.30 m from one end. Determine the moment of inertia of the rod about the pivot, given \(I = \tfrac1{12}ml^2\) about its centre.

    Show answer

    About the centre: \(I_G = \tfrac1{12}(2.0)(1.5)^2 = 0.375\ \text{kg m}^2\). The pivot is \(0.75 - 0.30 = 0.45\) m from the centre. Using the parallel-axis theorem: \(I = I_G + md^2 = 0.375 + 2.0(0.45)^2 = 0.375 + 0.405 = 0.78\ \text{kg m}^2\).

  7. [6 marks] A particle travels at 0.90c. Determine (a) the Lorentz factor and (b) the distance it travels in the laboratory during one proper lifetime of \(2.2\ \mu\text{s}\).

    Show answer

    (a) \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.90^2} = 1/\sqrt{0.19} = 2.29\).

    (b) In the lab the lifetime is dilated: \(\Delta t = \gamma\Delta t_0 = 2.29\times2.2\times10^{-6} = 5.04\ \mu\text{s}\). Distance \(= 0.90\times3.00\times10^8\times5.04\times10^{-6} = 1.36\times10^3\ \text{m} \approx 1.4\ \text{km}\). Without relativity it would be 594 m — less than half.

  8. [8 marks] A spacecraft of proper length 120 m moves at 0.80c relative to a space station. (a) Determine its length as measured at the station. (b) The astronaut measures the journey to a star as taking 6.0 years. Determine the time measured at the station, and explain carefully why this is not a contradiction.

    Show answer

    (a) \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.64} = 1/0.6 = 1.67\). \(L = L_0/\gamma = 120/1.67 = 72\ \text{m}\).

    (b) The astronaut's 6.0 years is the proper time (the clock is at rest relative to the astronaut and both events — departure and arrival — occur at the astronaut's position). \(\Delta t = \gamma\Delta t_0 = 1.67\times6.0 = 10\) years at the station.

    Why it is consistent: in the station frame the astronaut's clock runs slow, so less time elapses for them; in the astronaut's frame it is the distance to the star that is contracted to \(L_0/\gamma\), so the journey takes less of their time. Both frames agree that the astronaut ages 6 years while the station records 10. There is no contradiction because "the time between departure and arrival" is not an invariant — only the spacetime interval is.
