Physics HL: how the course is built and how the marks are won

Physics HL is not five separate books stapled together. It is one story — stuff, energy, waves, fields, quanta — told five times at different scales, and the exam is deliberately built so that the hardest marks come from joining two parts of that story together. This page is the hub: what each theme contains, exactly how you are assessed, what each paper actually feels like in the room, the uncertainty arithmetic that quietly decides several grades' worth of marks, how to lay out an answer so a markscheme can pay you, and eight practice questions worked the way an examiner would. Every theme has its own page, and this one links to all of them — but it is written to be worth reading on its own.

Why this page is worth reading before the theme pages

There are two ways to be a strong HL physics student. The first is to know five themes separately: a mechanics list, a thermal list, a waves list, a fields list, a nuclear list, each with its own formulae and its own past-paper file. The second is to know one subject in which the same handful of quantities — energy, momentum, charge, wavelength, force — keep reappearing at different scales, and to be able to move between them on demand. Students who learn the first way find the extended-response questions in Paper 2 baffling, because those questions are almost never about one theme. Students who learn the second way find them hard but fair.

There is a blunter reason too. A large share of the marks you will lose over two years will not be lost because you did not know the physics. They will be lost to units, significant figures, a mis-set gradient, a percentage uncertainty you did not propagate, a "describe" answered as an "explain", and an answer written in a form the markscheme cannot match. Those are mechanical losses, and they are fixable in an afternoon. Sections 6, 7, 8 and 9 of this page are about exactly that, and they are the sections nobody revises.

1. What Physics HL actually is, in honest terms

Strip away the syllabus document and Physics HL is three things braided together.

First, a body of models — a point mass, an ideal gas, a perfect spring, a field, a photon, a nucleus. None of these exists. Each one is a decision about what to ignore: the point mass ignores rotation and size, the ideal gas ignores the volume of the molecules and the forces between them, the photon ignores everything wave-like about light. Almost every "explain why the experimental value is lower than the theoretical value" question in the entire course is answered by naming something a model ignored. That is not a side-issue; it is the intellectual core of the subject and it is examined constantly.

Second, a quantitative craft: choosing a relationship, estimating what the answer ought to be, measuring, propagating uncertainty, linearising a graph, extracting a constant from a gradient, and then judging honestly whether your number means anything. That is the skills strand and the IA, and it is examined in Paper 1B and all over Paper 2.

Third, and this is what separates HL from SL more than any topic list, a habit of carrying an argument across a scale change. Relativity is what happens to kinematics when speeds approach c. Thermodynamics is what happens to mechanics when you stop tracking individual particles and start counting them. Quantum physics is what happens to waves when the energy gets small enough to count. Induction is what happens to fields when they change in time. Every single piece of additional higher level material is an existing SL idea pushed until the SL version breaks — and then repaired. If you can name what broke, you can answer the question.

What people expect it to be

Harder sums. Students arrive expecting HL to be SL with bigger numbers and more formulae, and are surprised that the hardest exam questions often need no calculation at all — an eight-mark "Explain, with reference to the energy of the particles, why…" will defeat a student who can differentiate but cannot chain a cause to an effect.

What it really is

A course in modelling that happens to be checkable by measurement. The equations are the easy part and the data booklet gives you most of them. What is examined is whether you know when a model applies, what it leaves out, how to test it, and how to say precisely what your test showed.

Be honest about what is hard

Three things reliably hurt HL candidates, and none of them is "the maths is too difficult" — you are also taking Maths AA HL, so the calculus and the algebra are not your problem. (i) Theme D, and specifically the business of potential: it is a definition (energy per unit whatever), it is negative, it has an arbitrary zero, and half the cohort silently treats it as a force. (ii) Setting out a six-mark explanation under time, where accurate knowledge written as fact earns roughly half the marks no matter how much of it there is. (iii) Uncertainty and data handling, which appears in Paper 1B, all through Paper 2, and in three of the four IA criteria — and which most students never actually practise. Sections 6, 7 and 9 exist for those three.

2.2 Linking questions: the thing the new guide actually added

The old syllabus was twelve topics plus four options; you could pass it by revising each box separately. The current guide is built around linking, and examiners are instructed to set questions that cross theme boundaries. So the habit you are training is not "which formula goes with this question?" but "which two ideas is this question welding together?"

A + B — motion meets thermal

A falling mass drives a paddle wheel in an insulated beaker; a car brakes and its kinetic energy becomes internal energy in the discs. You use A.3 to get the energy and B.1 to convert it to a temperature rise. The bridge is Q = mcΔT with W or ΔEk on the left.

C + E — waves meet quanta

Double-slit interference proves light is a wave; the photoelectric effect proves it is not only a wave. The bridge is c = fλ and E = hf applied to the same radiation.

D + E — fields meet nuclei

Rutherford scattering is a charged particle moving in a Coulomb field (D.2) and it is the evidence for the nuclear atom (E.1). The bridge is energy conservation: ½mv² → kQq/r.

B + E — particles meet energy

Kinetic theory (B.3) and radioactivity (E.3) both work by counting enormous numbers of objects and using averages. The bridge is the mole, N = nNA, and the electronvolt as an energy unit.

How to use it — the four-step answer to any linking question
  1. Name both themes out loud in your first sentence. "This situation involves the transfer of gravitational potential energy (A.3) into internal energy of the water (B.1)." One mark is frequently available for simply showing that you see the link.
  2. Write the shared quantity. Nearly every link has one quantity that appears in both themes — energy, force, wavelength, charge, momentum. Write it down and give it a symbol.
  3. Write one equation from each theme containing that quantity, and combine them algebraically before substituting numbers. Combining first is what earns the method mark when your arithmetic then goes wrong.
  4. Finish with the consequence, not the number. "Because the specific heat capacity of water is large, the temperature rise is small, so the measurement is dominated by…" — that final clause is where the top-band marks live.
Common trap

The most expensive way to lose marks on a linking question is to answer only the half you recognise. If a question says "Explain, with reference to the energy of the particles…" and you write a perfect paragraph about forces with no energy in it, you score zero for the explanation even though the physics is right. The command term tells you which theme to use. Underline it before you write anything.

About "Paper 3" — read this before you trust any revision site

You will find plenty of material online that gives Physics HL a Paper 3 worth 20%, usually described as "experimental skills and investigation" or as the old options paper. Under the 2014 guide that was correct: HL had Paper 1 (1 h, multiple choice), Paper 2 (2 h 15) and Paper 3 (1 h 15 on the four options), plus the IA. Under the 2023 guide you are sitting, the options were abolished and their useful content was redistributed into the themes, and the external assessment was reduced to two papers: Paper 1, split into 1A (multiple choice) and 1B (data-based), and Paper 2. The official specimen set for first assessment 2025 is papers 1A, 1B and 2 only.

What this means practically: confirm against the current subject guide before you plan around any Paper 3 you read about. If your session does introduce one, it will be on the IB programme resource centre and your teachers will have it. Either way, do not revise from a pre-2023 options list — capacitors and rectification, particle physics and the standard model, thin-film interference and cosmology were all removed, and time spent on them is wasted.

Exam technique — the arithmetic of timing

HL Paper 2: 90 marks in 150 minutes = 1.67 minutes per mark (100 seconds). That is your unit of currency. A 2-mark "state" gets 200 seconds; a 6-mark "Explain, with reference to…" gets 10 minutes. Before you write, read the mark allocation and set yourself that budget — and when you go over it, stop and move on, because the next question is worth exactly the same per mark. HL Paper 1: 75 marks in 120 minutes is the same rate, but the multiple-choice part should be done faster than that, banking time for the data-based part where the marks are slower and richer.

In Paper 1A, if a question is still unclear after 60 seconds, eliminate two options, choose, flag it and move on. There is no negative marking: an unanswered question is a guaranteed zero, a guessed one from five options is worth 20%, and one from three remaining options is worth 33%.

4. What each paper is really like

4.1 Paper 1A — multiple choice, and how to cheat at it honestly

Paper 1A is not a knowledge test with a calculation attached; it is a discrimination test. In a well-made multiple-choice question the four wrong answers are not random — each one is the result of a specific, common mistake. Your job is to spot which mistake each distractor was built from, because that tells you what the question is really testing.

  1. Eliminate on units first. If the question asks for a force and two options are in N and three in J, you have removed three options without doing any physics.
  2. Eliminate on order of magnitude. Estimate to one significant figure before you look. The mass of a nucleus is ~10⁻²⁵ kg, not 10⁻¹⁵ kg; the energy of a visible photon is ~3 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, not 3 × 10⁻¹⁹ eV.
  3. Eliminate on sign and on limits. Does the quantity increase or decrease as the variable increases? What happens as r → ∞ or as t → 0? Two options usually die immediately.
  4. Only then calculate — and check your answer against the survivor rather than hunting for an exact match. A value within rounding of one option is that option.
Common trap

Doing the full calculation before looking at the options. In a question asking for the power dissipated in a resistor, you do not need three significant figures — you need to know whether the answer is 0.45 W or 4.5 W, and one line of working gets you there. Students who calculate everything run out of time in the data-based part, which is where the marks are slower and more valuable.

4.2 Paper 1B — the data-based question, with a model answer

This is the genuinely new component, and it is where prepared students gain the most ground on unprepared ones. You are given a dataset, a graph, or a description of an apparatus you have never used, and asked questions that increase in demand: read a value, calculate a derived quantity, tabulate it, plot it, find a gradient, estimate an uncertainty, state whether the data supports a claim. Nothing here is recall. All of it is the skills strand.

Worked example 1 Paper 1B · 8 marks · Calculator and data booklet · a light-intensity investigation

Data. A student points a small lamp at a light meter and records the intensity I at different distances d. She suspects that I = k/d². Her readings:

d / cm20.030.040.050.060.080.0
I / W m⁻²98432516116.2

(a) State and justify which quantities should be plotted to give a straight line if the relationship holds. [2]
(b) Complete a column of values of 1/d² in m⁻² for the first three distances. [2]
(c) The gradient of the resulting straight line is 0.039 W m⁻² · m². Determine k and state its units. [2]
(d) The smallest intensity reading has the largest percentage uncertainty. Suggest why, and state one improvement. [2]

  1. (a) Write the relationship in the form y = mx + c. The dependent quantity is I; the independent quantity is d, but it must be raised to power 1. So plot I on the vertical axis against 1/d² on the horizontal axis. The intercept should be zero, and the gradient is k. [2 marks: one for naming the axes, one for saying the intercept is zero / the gradient is k.]
  2. (b) Convert to metres first — this is where the marks are. d = 0.200 m → 1/d² = 25.0 m⁻²; d = 0.300 m → 1/d² = 11.1 m⁻²; d = 0.400 m → 1/d² = 6.25 m⁻². [2 marks: three correct values with units; the trap is leaving d in cm and getting 0.0025.]
  3. (c) Comparing I = k × (1/d²) with y = mx, the gradient m = k = 0.039. Units: the gradient is (vertical unit)/(horizontal unit) = (W m⁻²)/(m⁻²) = W. So k = 0.039 W — it is the power of the lamp going into the beam, which is a useful sanity check: 0.039 W is a plausible figure for a small lamp through a meter's aperture. [2 marks: value, and units derived from the axes rather than guessed.]
  4. (d) The meter's resolution is fixed in absolute terms (say ±1 W m⁻²), so as the reading falls, the fractional uncertainty ΔI/I rises: at I = 98 it is about 1%, but at I = 6.2 it is about 16%. Improvement: use a more sensitive meter for small readings, or take the readings over a smaller range of distances so that no reading is near the bottom of the scale, or average several readings at each distance. [2 marks: one for explaining that fixed absolute uncertainty gives growing fractional uncertainty, one for a concrete, realistic improvement.]
Answer: (a) I against 1/d², through the origin; (b) 25.0, 11.1, 6.25 m⁻²; (c) k = 0.039 W; (d) fixed absolute resolution over a falling reading, fixed by a more sensitive meter or a narrower range. Where the marks are: two of the eight are pure unit conversion, two are axis choice, and only two require any physics at all — this is typical of Paper 1B, and it is why the skills strand is worth more than its hours suggest.
How to use it — the Paper 1B routine
  1. Read the whole question before touching the data. The last part usually tells you what the first part is for, and it often gives away which graph to plot.
  2. Convert every quoted value to SI at the point you copy it into your own table. cm to m, mA to A, minutes to seconds. Do it once, in the table, and you will not forget it later.
  3. Add a column for the derived quantity, with a heading that has a symbol and a unit: 1/d² / m⁻² — never 1/d² alone. A column heading without a unit is a mark dropped.
  4. Work out the gradient's units before you calculate the gradient. It tells you what the gradient physically is, which is frequently the answer to the next part.
  5. Answer the final "state whether…" part with a comparison of numbers, not a feeling. "The percentage difference is 5%, which is smaller than the 8% percentage uncertainty, so the data are consistent with the relationship" scores; "it seems to fit quite well" does not.

4.3 Paper 2 — short answer and extended response

Paper 2 mixes one-mark recalls with structured multi-part calculations and, usually two or three times per paper, an extended-response question worth six to eight marks that crosses themes. The pattern within a calculation question is almost always the same and it is worth learning to recognise: part (a) asks you to state or show something that part (b) needs; part (b) calculates; part (c) asks you to comment, justify or deduce.

Three habits specific to Paper 2. First, use the given value even if you could not derive it — IB papers are designed so a failed "show that" does not cascade, and part (b) will accept the quoted number. Second, write the formula before substituting, every time, because the formula line is often where the first mark sits. Third, answer the "comment" part in a sentence containing a number: "the value is 12% lower than the accepted value, which is greater than the 5% uncertainty, so there is evidence of a systematic error, most likely…".

Exam technique — "Show that" is a gift, not a trap

When a question says "Show that the speed is 4.5 m s⁻¹", the examiner has given you the answer and is paying you for the route. Show every algebraic line, then the substitution, then the result to one more significant figure than the quoted value, then a brief comment that it agrees. Never write "clearly, it is 4.5" — the mark is the substitution, and handwriting the substitution takes fifteen seconds.

5. Uncertainties: the biggest single source of lost marks

If you want one topic from this page to be worth real money, it is this one. Uncertainty appears in Paper 1B, in every data question in Paper 2, and in three of the four IA criteria. It is also almost entirely mechanical: there are four rules, a table of them, and a fixed way of writing the answer. Once you can do it, you can do it under pressure, and most of your competitors cannot.

5.1 The vocabulary, precisely

Absolute uncertainty Δx
The uncertainty in the same units as the quantity: l = 0.640 ± 0.002 m. This is what you put on an error bar and in a table column.
Fractional uncertainty
Δx/x — a dimensionless ratio. Used for propagation, because it is the quantity that behaves simply when you multiply and divide.
Percentage uncertainty
(Δx/x) × 100%. The same thing in a friendlier form, and the form the markscheme usually asks for.
Precision
How tightly repeated measurements cluster. Improved by repeating and averaging, or by using a better-resolution instrument.
Accuracy
How close a measurement is to the true value. Limited by systematic error, which repeating cannot fix.
Resolution
The smallest change an instrument can display. For an analogue scale the uncertainty is typically ±half the smallest division; for a digital instrument, ±1 in the last displayed digit (unless the manual says otherwise).
Percentage difference
|accepted − experimental| / accepted × 100%. A completely different thing from percentage uncertainty: it compares you to a book value rather than describing your spread.
Why this distinction decides marks

The decisive sentence in an IA conclusion and in dozens of Paper 2 questions is a comparison: if your percentage difference is smaller than your percentage uncertainty, your result agrees with the accepted value and you have no systematic error worth reporting. If the difference is larger, you do — and you must name it and give its size. Students who quote only one of the two numbers cannot write that sentence, and the sentence is the mark.

5.2 The four propagation rules

OperationWhat you doExample
Addition or subtraction z = x ± y Add the absolute uncertainties: Δz = Δx + Δy (12.0 ± 0.2) − (8.0 ± 0.2) = 4.0 ± 0.4 cm — note the uncertainty doubles while the value halves, so the percentage uncertainty explodes.
Multiplication or division z = xy or z = x/y Add the percentage uncertainties: Δz/z = Δx/x + Δy/y If V has 2% and I has 3%, then R = V/I has 5%.
Power z = xn Multiply the percentage uncertainty by |n|: Δz/z = |n| · Δx/x r² has twice the percentage uncertainty of r; √l has half.
Logarithm z = ln x The absolute uncertainty in ln x equals the fractional uncertainty in x: Δ(ln x) = Δx/x On a decay plot of ln N against t, the error bars are ΔN/N — and they are all the same length, unlike the bars on N itself.
Derivation Why percentage uncertainties add for a product
  1. Let z = xy and increase both x and y by their uncertainties: z + Δz = (x + Δx)(y + Δy).
  2. Expand: z + Δz = xy + xΔy + yΔx + ΔxΔy.
  3. Subtract z = xy: Δz = xΔy + yΔx + ΔxΔy.
  4. The last term is the product of two small quantities, so it is negligible. Divide by z = xy: Δz/z = Δx/x + Δy/y. Fractional uncertainties add.
  5. The same argument with z = xn — or simply differentiating ln z = n ln x — gives Δz/z = |n| · Δx/x, and n = ½ recovers the square-root rule.

What it means: the quantity with the largest percentage uncertainty dominates, and squaring a quantity doubles its percentage uncertainty — so measuring a radius badly is twice as damaging as you think. When it is the wrong tool: for the difference of two nearly equal numbers, use absolute uncertainties; and for independent random uncertainties, a more rigorous treatment adds them in quadrature (√(Δx² + Δy²)) — but the IB convention at this level is to add them directly, so add them directly unless you are told otherwise.

How to use it — the three-line uncertainty routine
  1. Write the formula with the subject on the left, and identify every measured quantity in it. Constants with more significant figures than your data (π, g from the booklet, c, h) contribute nothing worth counting.
  2. Convert every measurement's absolute uncertainty into a percentage, in a column. Multiply by the power of that quantity in the formula.
  3. Add the percentages, convert back to an absolute uncertainty in the final answer, round the uncertainty to one significant figure, and round the answer to the same decimal place.
Worked example 2 5 marks · a product and a power: resistance from V and I

Question. A student measures V = 12.0 ± 0.1 V and I = 2.40 ± 0.05 A. Determine the resistance R and its absolute uncertainty.

  1. Formula: R = V/I. This is a division, so percentage uncertainties add.
  2. Percentage uncertainty in V: (0.1/12.0) × 100% = 0.83%.
  3. Percentage uncertainty in I: (0.05/2.40) × 100% = 2.08%.
  4. Percentage uncertainty in R: 0.83% + 2.08% = 2.91%. Notice that the current dominates by a factor of 2.5 — that is the instrument to improve, and saying so is the evaluation mark in an IA.
  5. Value: R = 12.0/2.40 = 5.00 Ω.
  6. Absolute uncertainty: 2.91% of 5.00 = 0.146 Ω, which rounds to 0.1 Ω at one significant figure.
Answer: R = 5.0 ± 0.1 Ω. Sanity check: 0.1 Ω in 5.0 Ω is 2%, consistent with the 2.9% we propagated before rounding. The dominant term is the current, so the single best improvement is an ammeter with better resolution — not more repeats.
Worked example 3 6 marks · a square root and a power: g from a pendulum

Question. A pendulum of length l = 0.800 ± 0.005 m is timed over 20 complete oscillations. The total time is 36.0 s and the student estimates the reaction-time uncertainty in starting and stopping the stopwatch as ±0.2 s each way. Determine g and its percentage uncertainty.

  1. Period: T = 36.0/20 = 1.80 s. The absolute uncertainty in the start and the stop each contribute, and the IB convention is to add them: Δt = 0.4 s over the whole 36.0 s, so ΔT = 0.4/20 = 0.02 s. Dividing by 20 is the whole point of timing many oscillations, and stating that is a markscheme line.
  2. Percentage uncertainty in T: (0.02/1.80) × 100% = 1.11%.
  3. Percentage uncertainty in l: (0.005/0.800) × 100% = 0.63%.
  4. Formula: from T = 2π√(l/g), rearrange to g = 4π²l/T². So g ∝ l (power 1) and g ∝ T⁻² (power 2).
  5. Percentage uncertainty in g: 0.63% + 2 × 1.11% = 2.85%. The period counts twice because it is squared — the classic error is to forget the factor of 2.
  6. Value: g = 4π²(0.800)/(1.80)² = 31.58/3.24 = 9.75 m s⁻².
  7. Absolute uncertainty: 2.85% of 9.75 = 0.28 m s⁻² → 0.3 m s⁻² to one significant figure.
Answer: g = 9.8 ± 0.3 m s⁻². The accepted value 9.81 m s⁻² lies inside this range, so the result agrees with the accepted value and there is no evidence of a systematic error. Note the two places marks are won: dividing the timing uncertainty by 20, and multiplying the period's percentage uncertainty by 2.
Worked example 4 4 marks · a difference: when absolute is the only safe route

Question. The mass of a boiling tube plus contents is 48.62 ± 0.01 g and the mass of the empty tube is 32.58 ± 0.01 g. Determine the mass of the contents and its percentage uncertainty. Hence explain why the uncertainty is so much larger in relative terms.

  1. This is a subtraction, so absolute uncertainties add: Δm = 0.01 + 0.01 = 0.02 g.
  2. Value: m = 48.62 − 32.58 = 16.04 g.
  3. Percentage uncertainty: (0.02/16.04) × 100% = 0.12%. Compare with the percentage uncertainty in each weighing, which is (0.01/48.62) × 100% ≈ 0.02%: the difference is six times less precise in relative terms, even though nothing got worse.
  4. The reason: the absolute uncertainty is set by the balance and does not shrink, while the quantity you are trying to determine is much smaller than either reading.
Answer: m = 16.04 ± 0.02 g, i.e. 0.12% — six times the relative uncertainty of either weighing. Design consequence: never determine a small quantity as the difference of two large ones if you can measure it directly. That single sentence is a top-band IA evaluation point.

5.3 Significant figures and the rounding rule

The convention the markscheme expects is simple enough to memorise in one line: round the uncertainty to one significant figure, then round the value to the same decimal place. So 9.75 ± 0.28 becomes 9.8 ± 0.3, and 5.00 ± 0.146 Ω becomes 5.0 ± 0.1 Ω. If the leading digit of the uncertainty is a 1 or a 2, examiners sometimes keep two significant figures (9.75 ± 0.28), which is also accepted — but one significant figure is never wrong.

For ordinary answers with no uncertainty quoted, match the least precise piece of data you were given: if the data are to two significant figures, give two. Give three only if the data support three. Writing v = 20.19987531 m s⁻¹ from data given to two significant figures is not precision, it is a claim you cannot support, and it can cost a mark.

Common trap

Rounding too early in a multi-step calculation. Keep at least one extra digit through the working and round only at the end; if you round T to 1.8 s and then square it, you have thrown away real precision and your final g will drift by more than your stated uncertainty. Related trap: writing the same number of decimal places as your calculator rather than as your uncertainty.

5.4 Error bars

  1. Every plotted point gets a bar in the direction of the quantity with the dominant uncertainty — usually the dependent axis. If both matter, plot both.
  2. The best-fit line must pass through every error bar (or very nearly all of them). If it cannot, either your uncertainties were underestimated or the relationship is not the one you assumed — and either is a legitimate, mark-worthy observation.
  3. To get the uncertainty in the gradient: draw the best-fit line, then the steepest and shallowest lines that still pass through all the error bars. Half the difference between those two gradients is the uncertainty in the gradient.
  4. A non-zero intercept is not automatically failure — it is evidence, and naming what physical effect would cause it is exactly the kind of comment the top bands reward.
Common trap

Writing "human error" as a source of error scores zero, and it is the single most common wasted sentence in the cohort. Writing "reaction time in starting and stopping the stopwatch is ±0.2 s on a 2.4 s measurement, i.e. 8%, which is larger than the 3% spread of the repeats, so it dominates; using a light gate would reduce it to below 0.5%" scores full marks. Specific, quantified, and followed by a concrete improvement. Three beats, one sentence each.

6. How to set out a physics answer

Physics markschemes are unusually generous about method and unusually strict about form. A correct number arrived at invisibly can score one mark out of three. A wrong number arrived at through a visible, correct method can score two out of three. The layout below is not a stylistic preference; it is the shape the markscheme is built to match.

How to use it — the five-line answer
  1. Write the formula in symbols. One mark, routinely. If the question supplies a diagram, add a sketch of it — free-body diagrams, ray diagrams and field-line sketches carry their own marks.
  2. Rearrange symbolically, before substituting anything. This lets the examiner follow your method even if your arithmetic fails, and it lets you see whether the answer should be large or small.
  3. Substitute values with units, in one line. a = (30 − 12)/4.0 = 4.5 m s⁻², not a trail of numbers across the page.
  4. Give the answer with a unit, to a sensible number of significant figures. No unit, no mark — this is stated explicitly in many markschemes.
  5. Add one sentence of sense-checking or interpretation. "The acceleration is negative, which is correct because the force opposes the motion." Cheap, and it is frequently the final mark.
Worked example 5 6 marks · where the marks are won and lost

Question. A block of mass 2.5 kg is released from rest on a rough slope inclined at 30° to the horizontal. The coefficient of dynamic friction is 0.20. (a) Show that the acceleration down the slope is about 3.2 m s⁻². [3] (b) Determine the speed after the block has moved 4.0 m. [3]

  1. Mark 1 — resolve the weight. Component down the slope: mg sin 30° = 2.5 × 9.81 × 0.5 = 12.26 N. Normal reaction: R = mg cos 30° = 2.5 × 9.81 × 0.866 = 21.2 N. A student who writes mg sin 30° but uses cos in the numbers loses this mark even though the idea is right.
  2. Mark 2 — the friction. F = μR = 0.20 × 21.2 = 4.25 N, acting up the slope. The most common lost mark in the whole of Theme A is using F = μmg instead of μR — on a slope those differ.
  3. Mark 3 — Newton's second law. ma = 12.26 − 4.25, so a = 8.01/2.5 = 3.20 m s⁻². Quoting it as 3.2 m s⁻² to match the "show that" is correct; quoting 3.204 m s⁻² and then writing "≈ 3.2" is better still.
  4. Marks 4 and 5 — part (b). Use v² = u² + 2as with u = 0: v = √(2 × 3.20 × 4.0) = √25.6 = 5.06 m s⁻¹. One mark for selecting the right equation, one for the substitution with units. Note the trap: reaching for v = u + at when you have not been given t — pick the equation whose variables you actually have.
  5. Mark 6 — the final answer. v = 5.1 m s⁻¹ to two significant figures, matching the 2.5 kg, 0.20 and 4.0 m in the question.
Answer: (a) 3.2 m s⁻²; (b) 5.1 m s⁻¹. Where students lose marks: using μmg for friction (mark 2), forgetting the unit on the final answer (mark 6), and answering (b) with four significant figures when the data support two. Where marks are safe: writing the resolved components as a labelled pair of lines with the trigonometry shown — that is mark 1 banked before any arithmetic happens.
Exam technique — the error carried forward rule is real

If part (a) gives you 3.2 m s⁻² and you got 4.1 m s⁻², use 3.2 in part (b). Marks for later parts are awarded for correct method using the stated value, and examiners annotate "ECF" — error carried forward — precisely so one slip does not destroy a whole question. Fighting to recover your own wrong number costs you the marks for part (b) as well as part (a).

7. The command terms

The command term tells you how much to write and how deep to go. Misreading it is the cheapest way to lose two marks, and unlike a content gap it is fixable in one sitting.

TermWhat it demandsHow much to write
StateGive a name, value or fact. No reasoning. One short sentence. No equation unless asked.
DefineGive the precise meaning, usually as a ratio or an equation. One sentence plus the defining equation with every symbol defined.
Determine / CalculateFind a numerical value. Formula, substitution with units, answer with units.
Show thatReach a stated result. The answer is given. Show every algebraic line. No marks for the final number — all of them are for method.
DeriveGet from one stated relationship to another by reasoning. Start from first principles, line by line, ending with a concluding statement.
ExplainGive a cause-and-effect chain. "Because X, therefore Y, which means Z." At least one link word per sentence.
DescribeSay what happens or what is observed. A sequence of events. No causes.
DeduceReach a conclusion from information given, not from memory. Short chain: "from the graph, …; therefore …".
EstimateAn order-of-magnitude answer. Show the round numbers you used. One significant figure is fine and expected.

7.1 Two weak answers and two strong ones, side by side

"Explain" — weak vs strong

Question [4 marks]: Explain why the temperature of a gas increases when it is compressed rapidly.

Weak — 1 or 2 of 4

"The gas gets hotter when it is compressed because the particles get closer together and hit each other more. Also work is done on the gas. This is why a bike pump gets warm."

Why it fails: the first sentence is a mechanism that is simply wrong — collisions between gas particles do not heat anything, they are elastic. "Work is done on the gas" is correct but asserted, not explained, and there is no link to internal energy or to temperature as a measure of mean kinetic energy.

Strong — 4 of 4

"Work is done on the gas by the piston, so energy is transferred to the gas: ΔU = W (with Q ≈ 0 because the compression is rapid, so there is no time for thermal transfer). Therefore the internal energy of the gas increases. Internal energy of an ideal gas is the total random kinetic energy of its molecules, so the mean molecular kinetic energy rises. Temperature is proportional to mean molecular kinetic energy, so the temperature rises."

Why it succeeds: four linked beats — work done, no time for heat transfer, internal energy is molecular kinetic energy, temperature measures it — each joined by "so" or "therefore". Every mark has a home.

"Justify" — weak vs strong

Question [3 marks]: A student obtains g = 9.4 ± 0.6 m s⁻². Justify whether this agrees with the accepted value of 9.81 m s⁻².

Weak — 0 or 1 of 3

"Yes, it agrees, because 9.4 is quite close to 9.81 and the experiment was done carefully."

Why it fails: "quite close" is not a criterion, and "done carefully" is not evidence. The command term is justify, which means the answer must contain the comparison that makes the decision.

Strong — 3 of 3

"The percentage difference from the accepted value is |9.81 − 9.4| / 9.81 × 100% = 4.2%. The percentage uncertainty is 0.6/9.4 × 100% = 6.4%. Since 4.2% < 6.4%, the accepted value lies within the range of the experimental result, so the two agree and there is no evidence of a systematic error."

Why it succeeds: it computes both numbers, compares them explicitly with an inequality, and states the conclusion the comparison licenses. That is what justify means in physics.

Common trap

Answering sketch as though it were plot, and vice versa. A sketch needs the shape, axes with quantities and units, and the key intercepts or asymptotes — a curve through the origin for v ∝ t, an exponential decay asymptote to zero for activity, a 1/r² tail for field strength. A plot needs plotted points from your data. If a question says "sketch" and you spend four minutes ruling a scale, you have wasted time and gained nothing; if it says "plot" and you draw a smooth curve through no points, you have lost the data marks entirely.

8. Graphs and data handling

Nearly every practical mark in the course runs through a graph, and the same four decisions decide whether the graph earns them: which quantity goes on which axis, what scale, whether the relationship has been linearised, and whether the gradient has been read correctly with its units.

8.1 The rules

  1. Independent variable on the horizontal axis — the one you chose to change. Dependent on the vertical.
  2. Label both axes with the quantity and the unit: T² / s² or T² (s²). Never T² alone, and never label the axis with the raw numbers 0.4, 1.6, 3.6.
  3. Choose a scale that uses more than half the paper in each direction, and use sensible steps (1, 2, 5 per large square), not awkward ones (3, 7).
  4. Plot points as small crosses or dots with circles, not as blobs — a blob two millimetres across is an unquantified uncertainty.
  5. Draw a best-fit straight line with a ruler, ignoring outliers, with roughly equal numbers of points on each side. Never join the dots. Never force the line through the origin unless the physics says it must.
  6. For the gradient, use two points on your line that are far apart — ideally more than half the length of the line — and not two of your data points. Write the coordinates beside them.
  7. Quote the gradient with units, derived from the axes.

8.2 Linearising: the five-line template

If a relationship is not of the form y = mx + c, make it so. This is the single most important practical skill in the course, and it is the same five lines every time.

Derivation Linearising T = 2π√(l/g) — the template for every practical
  1. Identify the variables: you choose l (independent), you measure T (dependent).
  2. Get the dependent quantity alone: it already is. Get the independent quantity to power 1: square both sides, T² = 4π²l/g.
  3. Write it in y = mx + c form: T² = (4π²/g) · l + 0.
  4. So plot T² vertically against l horizontally. The gradient is 4π²/g, and g = 4π²/gradient.
  5. Check the gradient's units: s² m⁻¹. Inverting it and multiplying by 4π² (dimensionless) gives m s⁻², which is right for g — a free error check before you touch a number.

What it means: the same five lines handle every relationship in the course. When it is the wrong tool: for exponential decay, take logarithms first, then linearise — N = N₀e⁻λt gives ln N = ln N₀ − λt, where the gradient is −λ.

Worked example 6 6 marks · linearise, read the gradient, extract a constant

Question. A student investigates how the period T of a mass on a spring depends on the mass m, and suspects T = 2π√(m/k). She plots T² against m and obtains a straight line passing through (0.10 kg, 0.16 s²) and (0.50 kg, 0.80 s²). Determine the spring constant k.

  1. Linearise: T² = (4π²/k) · m, so a plot of T² against m has gradient 4π²/k.
  2. Gradient: (0.80 − 0.16)/(0.50 − 0.10) = 0.64/0.40 = 1.6 s² kg⁻¹. Using two points far apart on the line is what keeps this accurate.
  3. Check the units: s² kg⁻¹, which is correct for 4π²/k since k is in N m⁻¹ = kg s⁻².
  4. Rearrange: k = 4π²/1.6 = 39.5/1.6 = 24.7 N m⁻¹.
  5. Round to two significant figures, matching the data: k = 25 N m⁻¹.
  6. Sanity check: a spring you can stretch 10 cm with 2.5 N is a soft spring, and 25 N m⁻¹ is soft. If you had divided by 2π instead of 4π² you would get 9.9 N m⁻¹, and if you had plotted T against √m the gradient would be 2π/√k — a third, equally valid, route to the same answer.
Answer: k = 25 N m⁻¹. The three marks that matter are the gradient with units, the correct rearrangement (4π², not 2π), and a final value quoted to the precision the data support.
Gradients, areas, and what they mean

Three areas are examined repeatedly, and each one is a definition in disguise: the area under a velocity–time graph is displacement (signed — below the axis counts negative); the area under a force–time graph is impulse, which equals the change in momentum; the area under a force–extension graph is the work done, which is the elastic strain energy stored. Gradients: displacement–time gives velocity, velocity–time gives acceleration, force–extension gives the spring constant, flux-linkage–time gives the negative of the induced emf. If you can recite those six pairings you can answer a whole family of questions without remembering anything else about them.

9. The internal assessment

Ten hours, 24 marks, 20% of your final grade — and the only component you control completely: you choose the question, you choose when to do the work, and you can iterate. It is marked by your teacher and moderated by the IB on four criteria of six marks each.

CriterionMarksWhat the top band looks like
Research design6 A focused, answerable research question; independent, dependent and controlled variables identified with reasons; a method detailed enough for a stranger to repeat; and a justified choice of range and instrument.
Data analysis6 Enough data, with repeats; uncertainty columns for every measured and derived quantity; correct propagation; a correct graph with error bars and a best-fit line.
Conclusion6 A conclusion that follows from the processed data, compared quantitatively with a literature value (percentage difference against percentage uncertainty), with the physics explained rather than asserted.
Evaluation6 Limitations that are specific and quantified, the single biggest one identified with its size, and improvements that are concrete and realistic.

9.1 Choosing a topic

The test for a good IA question is not "is it interesting?" but "can I get at least six data points with a measurable uncertainty in ten hours, and does it produce a graph I can linearise?" Anything that fails either half will cost you the data-analysis marks however well you write.

  1. Pick the relationship, not the apparatus. "How does the period of a pendulum depend on length?" is a question. "Investigate pendulums" is not, and it will show in your research-design mark.
  2. Check you can vary the independent variable over at least a factor of five. Six points across a narrow range give a gradient you cannot trust.
  3. Identify the largest uncertainty before you start, and design it out. If reaction time will dominate, use a light gate or video analysis. Saying "I chose a light gate because reaction time would have been 8% of my period" is a research-design sentence, not an evaluation one.
  4. Make sure there is a literature value to compare against. g, the resistivity of a metal, the refractive index of glass, Planck's constant from an LED threshold, the speed of sound. No literature value, no conclusion mark.
  5. Avoid the three reliable killers: anything needing vacuum or cryogenic conditions, anything where the effect is smaller than your uncertainty, and anything where you cannot control the temperature.

10. How physics connects to your other subjects

These are not morale-boosting platitudes. Each overlap below is a place where the same piece of work earns marks twice, and each is specific enough to act on.

10.1 Physics and Mathematics AA HL

You already have the calculus, so use it as a reading language rather than a technique. Four physics statements are derivatives, and recognising them turns a paragraph of waffle into one line: v = ds/dt (velocity is the gradient of a displacement–time graph), a = dv/dt, F = dp/dt (Newton's second law in its real form, which is why impulse is Δp), and ε = −d(NΦ)/dt (Faraday's law — emf is a rate of change of flux linkage, so a stationary coil in a steady field gives nothing, however strong the field).

The transfer the other way is just as real. Exponential decay in maths is N = N₀e⁻λt in physics; logarithms turn it into a straight line whose gradient is −λ. Worked once: a sample's activity falls from 800 Bq to 100 Bq in 12 hours, so 800e⁻¹²λ = 100, giving e¹²λ = 8, 12λ = ln 8 = 2.079, λ = 0.173 h⁻¹ and T₁/2 = ln 2 / λ = 0.693/0.173 = 4.0 h. Same mathematics, and the half-life is the answer to a physics question.

10.2 Physics and Computer Science HL

The overlap is data handling, and it is exact. Logging readings, computing derived columns, propagating uncertainty, fitting a line and reporting a gradient with its uncertainty is a small data-processing pipeline; in CS you would write it as a function per step, in physics you write it as a spreadsheet. If you can express the uncertainty propagation as one function applied down a column, you will stop making arithmetic errors in it — and you will have something concrete to say in a CS IA about handling real, messy measurements.

There is a second, quieter overlap: a field is an object with a value at every point in space, which is precisely the way you think about state in an object-oriented program. Students who find potential confusing often find it easier once they stop thinking of it as a number attached to an object and start thinking of it as a property of a location.

10.3 Physics and Chemistry

Shared experimental-design language (independent, dependent, controlled), shared uncertainty conventions, and a shared ten-hour collaborative sciences project. Beyond that, the real overlaps: the mole and Avogadro's constant, which you use in the kinetic theory of gases and which chemistry uses for everything; the ideal gas law pV = nRT, which is on both data booklets with the same R; specific heat capacity and calorimetry, which is the thermochemistry practical with different apparatus; and energy levels, which drive both atomic emission spectra in physics and flame tests and the periodic table in chemistry. If you are writing about spectra somewhere in the DP, write it once and use it twice.

Linking question

Why does a satellite in low orbit get hotter on the side facing the Earth as well as the side facing the Sun? Carry it through three themes: the centripetal force is supplied by gravity, so GMm/r² = mv²/r fixes the orbital speed; the satellite's gravitational potential energy Ep = −GMm/r is constant in a circular orbit, so no energy is being converted there; the temperature is set by a radiation balance — absorbed solar intensity plus Earth's infrared emission against the satellite's own emission σAT⁴. The quantity that transfers between themes is energy rate: from mechanical power P = Fv in A.3 to radiated power in B.2. No single-theme answer can earn full marks; the markscheme rewards the student who names the shared quantity once and then uses it in both contexts.

11. How to revise for physics

Two principles. Retrieval beats re-reading: closing the book and writing out a derivation, a set of definitions or a data-table from memory is worth several passes of highlighting. And interleaving beats blocking: doing mixed questions from three themes is harder and works better than doing twenty questions from one, because the exam is mixed and because the hard part is choosing the tool, not using it.

WhenWhatWhy this works
Weekly, in term

Short session 2: two or three past-paper questions on the current topic, marked against the markscheme with the missed line written out. Retrieval consolidates; markscheme-marking teaches you the shape of credit; the data exercise is the only way to make uncertainty fluent, and it is the skill that appears in every component.

End of each theme Write a one-page sheet: every formula, its conditions, its units, and one sentence on when it is the wrong tool. Then write the two or three linking questions for that theme. Six to eight weeks before the exams Two full papers per fortnight, timed, back to back where possible: Paper 1 then Paper 2. Mark strictly. Keep an error log. Stamina is a real component of a 4½-hour exam day, and it is trainable. Back-to-back papers also reproduce the actual conditions, which halves the shock.

Exam technique — the three-stage past-paper method

Stage 1: untimed, data booklet open, mark your own work with the markscheme and write out the line you missed. Stage 2: a whole paper, timed, no interruptions. Stage 3 — the one almost nobody does: collect every question you got wrong and sort them by theme pair. You will find your personal weak link, and it is almost always the same one (commonly B+D or C+E). Fix that one link and your grade moves more than any amount of extra content revision would move it.

How to use the data booklet
  1. Tab it once, physically. Six folds or sticky tabs: constants, Theme A, B, C, D, E. You should be able to open any theme's page in under three seconds, and you should practise doing so.
  2. Learn what is not in it. It gives T = 2π√(m/k) but not the derivation; s = λD/a but not the geometry; λ = h/p but not when to use it. What it cannot give you is judgement.
  3. Read the symbol list at the top of each section. Half of all "wrong formula" errors are really "wrong symbol" errors: ε is emf and E is electric field strength; r is both separation and internal resistance.
  4. Use it as a unit reference. If your derived expression has the wrong units, you have made an algebra slip — the constants page will tell you within seconds.
Common trap

The most expensive data-booklet mistake is misreading a formula's conditions. The booklet prints formulae without their caveats; the caveats are your job. Before substituting, say the condition out loud: constant pressure? v ≪ c? small angle? point mass? isothermal? If you cannot say it, you have not finished choosing the formula.

12. Practice

Answer these on paper, with units, before opening anything. The allocations are IB-style.

  1. [3 marks] Convert: (a) 650 nm to m; (b) 4.2 MeV to J; (c) 3.3 kΩ to Ω.

    Show the answer
    1. (a) 650 nm = 650 × 10⁻⁹ m = 6.50 × 10⁻⁷ m.
    2. (b) 4.2 MeV = 4.2 × 10⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 6.7 × 10⁻¹³ J. Two steps: mega → unit is 10⁶, then eV → J is 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹.
    3. (c) 3.3 kΩ = 3.3 × 10³ Ω = 3300 Ω.
    Answer: 6.50 × 10⁻⁷ m; 6.7 × 10⁻¹³ J; 3300 Ω. Why this is worth knowing: the single most common arithmetic error in the course is treating 1 MeV as 10⁻⁶ J instead of 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J. Convert to SI at the moment you copy a number into your own table, and you will never make it under pressure.

  2. [4 marks] A resistor has V = 8.0 ± 0.2 V across it and I = 0.40 ± 0.02 A through it. Determine its resistance and the absolute uncertainty in that resistance.

    Show the answer
    1. R = V/I: a division, so percentage uncertainties add.
    2. Percentage uncertainty in V: (0.2/8.0) × 100% = 2.5%.
    3. Percentage uncertainty in I: (0.02/0.40) × 100% = 5.0%.
    4. Percentage uncertainty in R: 2.5% + 5.0% = 7.5%.
    5. R = 8.0/0.40 = 20 Ω; absolute uncertainty = 0.075 × 20 = 1.5 Ω, which is 2 Ω to one significant figure.
    Answer: R = 20 ± 2 Ω. Why this is worth knowing: the current carries twice the percentage uncertainty of the voltage, so it is the quantity to improve — and "improve the ammeter" is a concrete evaluation point, whereas "be more careful" is worth nothing.

  3. [5 marks] The volume of a cylinder is V = πr²L. A student measures r = 12.0 ± 0.2 mm and L = 150 ± 1 mm. Determine the volume in mm³ and its percentage uncertainty.

    Show the answer
    1. V = π(12.0)²(150) = π × 144 × 150 = 6.79 × 10⁴ mm³ (67 900 mm³).
    2. Percentage uncertainty in r: (0.2/12.0) × 100% = 1.67%.
    3. r is squared, so its contribution is doubled: 2 × 1.67% = 3.33%.
    4. Percentage uncertainty in L: (1/150) × 100% = 0.67%.
    5. Total: 3.33% + 0.67% = 4.0%. Absolute uncertainty = 0.040 × 6.79 × 10⁴ = 2.7 × 10³ mm³.
    Answer: V = (6.8 ± 0.3) × 10⁴ mm³, i.e. 4.0%. Why this is worth knowing: the squared term contributes five times as much as the other measurement. Measuring a diameter carefully is worth far more than measuring a length carefully, and that asymmetry is the whole point of doing the propagation before you design the experiment.

  4. [5 marks] A student determines g = 9.4 ± 0.6 m s⁻². Calculate the percentage difference from the accepted value of 9.81 m s⁻², and state with a reason whether the result agrees with the accepted value.

    Show the answer
    1. Percentage difference = |9.81 − 9.4| / 9.81 × 100% = 0.41/9.81 × 100% = 4.2%.
    2. Percentage uncertainty = 0.6/9.4 × 100% = 6.4%.
    3. Compare: 4.2% < 6.4%, so the accepted value lies within the range 9.4 ± 0.6 — indeed 9.81 is inside [8.8, 10.0].
    4. Conclusion: the result does agree with the accepted value, and there is no evidence of a systematic error.
    5. Watch the direction of the comparison: a large uncertainty does not mean a good experiment, it means an inconclusive one. Saying "the uncertainty is large, so the experiment was poor" is a different and better point, and belongs in the evaluation, not the conclusion.
    Answer: 4.2% difference against 6.4% uncertainty; they agree. Why this is worth knowing: this comparison is the single most frequently tested idea in the data-handling part of the course, and it is the sentence that separates a 4 from a 6 in an IA conclusion.

  5. [5 marks] The intensity I of radiation from a point source varies with distance d as I = k/d². (a) State what should be plotted to obtain a straight line, and what the gradient equals. (b) The gradient is found to be 0.039 W. State the value of k and explain, in one sentence, how the units of the gradient were obtained.

    Show the answer
    1. (a) Plot I on the vertical axis against 1/d² on the horizontal axis. Comparing I = k × (1/d²) with y = mx + c, the gradient is k and the intercept is zero.
    2. (b) k = 0.039 W.
    3. The gradient's units are (vertical unit) ÷ (horizontal unit) = (W m⁻²) ÷ (m⁻²) = W. This is a sanity check as much as a unit: k is a power, so it must come out in watts.
    4. Common error to avoid: leaving d in centimetres when calculating 1/d², which gives a gradient wrong by a factor of 10⁴ and an answer no sanity check will rescue.
    Answer: I against 1/d²; k = 0.039 W, from (W m⁻²)/(m⁻²). Why this is worth knowing: deriving a constant's units from the axes is a transferable move — it works for gradients in every theme and it turns "what are the units?" from a guess into a calculation.

  6. [6 marks] A block of mass 3.0 kg slides down a rough slope inclined at 25° to the horizontal. The coefficient of dynamic friction is 0.15. Determine its acceleration down the slope, and state one assumption you have made.

    Show the answer
    1. Resolve the weight: down the slope mg sin 25° = 3.0 × 9.81 × 0.423 = 12.4 N; perpendicular to the slope mg cos 25° = 3.0 × 9.81 × 0.906 = 26.7 N.
    2. No acceleration perpendicular to the slope, so R = 26.7 N.
    3. Friction: F = μR = 0.15 × 26.7 = 4.0 N, up the slope.
    4. Newton's second law down the slope: ma = 12.4 − 4.0 = 8.4 N, so a = 8.4/3.0 = 2.8 m s⁻².
    5. Assumption: the slope is straight and rigid, the coefficient of friction is constant and independent of speed, and air resistance is negligible — any one of these stated earns the mark.
    6. Sanity check: with no friction the acceleration would be g sin 25° = 4.15 m s⁻²; friction must reduce it, and 2.8 is indeed less. Had you obtained more than 4.15, the error would be visible immediately.
    Answer: a = 2.8 m s⁻² down the slope. Why this is worth knowing: the friction is μR, and on a slope R = mg cos θ, not mg. Using μmg is the most common error in inclined-plane questions and it costs three of the six marks.

  7. [6 marks] A 2.0 kg mass falls through 1.5 m in a system that transfers 60% of the lost gravitational potential energy into 0.40 kg of water in an insulated container. The specific heat capacity of water is 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Calculate the rise in temperature of the water, and identify the one theme-A and one theme-B relationship you used.

    Show the answer
    1. Theme A: ΔEp = mgΔh = 2.0 × 9.81 × 1.5 = 29.4 J.
    2. Energy actually delivered to the water: 0.60 × 29.4 = 17.6 J.
    3. Theme B: Q = mcΔT, so ΔT = Q/(mc) = 17.6/(0.40 × 4180) = 17.6/1672 = 0.0105 K.
    4. ΔT = 0.011 K to two significant figures.
    5. Comment: a rise of one hundredth of a kelvin is far too small to measure with a school thermometer (−10 °C to 110 °C, resolution 1 °C), which is precisely why the real experiment uses a much larger falling mass, many repetitions, or an electrical method. That observation is the evaluation mark.
    Answer: ΔT = 0.011 K, using ΔEp = mgΔh (A.3) and Q = mcΔT (B.1). Why this is worth knowing: this is the archetypal A+B linking question — the shared quantity is energy, and the bridge is written down once and used in both themes.

  8. [5 marks] Estimate the number of photons emitted per second by a 60 W light bulb, assuming all the energy is emitted as light of wavelength 600 nm. State the two physics relationships you use and show the round numbers.

    Show the answer
    1. Energy of one photon: E = hc/λ. With h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and λ = 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ m:
    2. E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.0 × 10⁸)/(6.0 × 10⁻⁷) ≈ (2.0 × 10⁻²⁵)/(6.0 × 10⁻⁷) ≈ 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
    3. Photons per second: N = P/E = 60/(3.3 × 10⁻¹⁹) ≈ 1.8 × 10²⁰ s⁻¹.
    4. To one significant figure, as an estimate should be: ≈ 2 × 10²⁰ photons per second.
    5. Sanity check: a visible photon is a few times 10⁻¹⁹ J, so 60 J per second is a few times 10²⁰ photons. Anyone getting 10⁵ or 10⁴⁰ has made a power-of-ten slip, and spotting that is the skill an estimate question is testing.
    Answer: ≈ 2 × 10²⁰ photons per second, using E = hc/λ and N = P/E. Why this is worth knowing: estimate means one significant figure with the round numbers shown — not a precise calculation. Being able to move between energy per photon and total power is also the bridge between Theme C and Theme E.

Vocabulary
theme — one of the five content blocks (A–E) the syllabus is organised into, each opening with a guiding question.
guiding question — the deliberately broad question at the head of a theme that the sub-topics narrow down.
linking question — a question written into the syllabus that connects two themes; the basis of many Paper 2 extended responses.
additional higher level (AHL) material — the HL-only content: A.4, A.5, B.4, D.4, E.2 and the HL extensions inside other sub-topics.
data booklet — the booklet of constants, formulae and conversion factors provided in the exam; you cannot take your own annotated copy in.
data-based question — a Paper 1B question giving you an unfamiliar data set, graph or apparatus to analyse with no recall required.
external assessment — the examined components: Paper 1 (1A + 1B) and Paper 2, worth 80% at HL.
internal assessment (IA) — the scientific investigation: 10 hours, 24 marks, 20% of the grade, marked by your teacher and moderated by the IB.
scientific investigation — the formal name of the physics IA.
collaborative sciences project — the ten-hour interdisciplinary group 4 investigation; ungraded, and a free rehearsal for the IA.
moderation — the IB's check on your teacher's IA marks, using a sample of the cohort's work.
command term — the verb at the start of a question (state, explain, justify, sketch) that fixes the shape and depth of the answer.
show that — an instruction to reach a given result; every mark is for method, none for the final number.
error carried forward (ECF) — the markscheme convention of awarding later marks for correct method using a given value you failed to derive.
absolute uncertainty — the uncertainty in the same units as the quantity, e.g. 0.640 ± 0.002 m.
fractional uncertainty — the uncertainty as a fraction of the value, Δx/x; dimensionless.
percentage uncertainty — the fractional uncertainty × 100%; how well you know a value.
percentage difference — how far your value is from an accepted value, as a percentage of the accepted value.
propagation — working out the uncertainty in a quantity calculated from several measurements.
error bar — a line on a plotted point showing the range within which the true value probably lies.
best-fit line — the single straight line (or smooth curve) that best represents the trend, passing through almost every error bar.
outlier / anomaly — a point that does not follow the trend; you should identify it and say why, not silently delete it.
resolution — the smallest change an instrument can display; the source of most absolute uncertainties.
precision — how closely repeated measurements agree.
accuracy — how close a measurement is to the true value; limited by systematic error.
systematic error — an error that shifts every reading the same way; repeating does not remove it.
random error — an error that varies between readings; repeating and averaging reduces it.
zero error — an instrument that does not read zero when the quantity is zero; a systematic error.
linearise — to rewrite a relationship in the form y = mx + c so that it can be plotted as a straight line.
gradient — the slope of a graph, with units equal to (vertical unit)/(horizontal unit).
intercept — where a graph crosses an axis; often physically meaningful, sometimes a systematic error in disguise.
significant figures — the number of digits that carry meaning; round the uncertainty to one, then the value to match.
order of magnitude — the power of ten nearest a quantity; what an estimate question wants.
dimensional consistency — checking that both sides of an equation have the same combination of base units; a free error detector.
independent variable — the quantity you deliberately change.
dependent variable — the quantity you measure in response.
controlled variable — a quantity you hold constant so it cannot affect the outcome.
literature value — an accepted value from a data book or paper, used for comparison in a conclusion.
top-band — the highest mark range for a criterion; what a 5 or 6 out of 6 answer looks like.
