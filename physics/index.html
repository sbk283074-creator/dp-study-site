Physics HL · Course guide

Physics HL: how the course is organised

Physics HL is not five separate books stapled together. It is one story — stuff, energy, waves, fields, quanta — told five times at different scales, and the exam is deliberately built so that the hardest marks come from joining two parts of that story together. This page gives you the map, the assessment arithmetic, the data-booklet skills and the maths toolkit. Read it once properly, then come back to it whenever a topic starts feeling like a list of unrelated formulas.

The five themes

Everything in the course lives in one of five themes. Each theme opens with a guiding question — a deliberately big, unanswerable-in-one-sentence question that the theme then spends 40–60 hours narrowing down. The guiding question is not decoration: Paper 2 extended-response questions regularly quote its language back at you.

Why themes, not topics

The old syllabus was a list of twelve topics plus four options. You could pass it by revising each box separately. The current guide is built around linking: every theme contains a set of linking questions in the syllabus itself, and examiners are instructed to set questions that cross theme boundaries. So the mental habit you are training is not "which formula goes with this question?" but "which two ideas is this question welding together?"

What a linking question looks like, and how to answer it

A linking question gives you a situation that genuinely belongs to two themes at once. Some recurring combinations, all of which have appeared in specimen and recent material:

A + B — motion meets thermal

A falling mass drives a paddle wheel in an insulated beaker; a car brakes and its kinetic energy becomes internal energy of the discs. You use A.3 work–energy to get the energy and B.1 specific heat capacity to convert it into a temperature rise. The bridge is \(\Delta Q = mc\Delta T\) with \(W\) or \(\Delta E_k\) on the left.

C + E — waves meet quanta

Double-slit interference proves light is a wave; the photoelectric effect proves it is not only a wave. The bridge is \(c = f\lambda\) and \(E = hf\) used on the same radiation. Typical question: "Explain, with reference to both experiments, what is meant by wave–particle duality."

D + E — fields meet nuclei

Rutherford scattering is a charged particle moving in a Coulomb field (D.2) and it is the evidence for the nuclear atom (E.1). The bridge is energy conservation: \(\frac12 mv^2 \to kQq/r\).

B + E — particles meet energy

The kinetic theory of gases (B.3) and radioactivity (E.3) both work by counting enormous numbers of objects and using averages. The bridge is the mole, \(N = nN_A\), and the eV as an energy unit.

How to use it — the four-step answer to any linking question
  1. Name both themes out loud in your first sentence. "This situation involves the transfer of gravitational potential energy (A.3) into internal energy of the water (B.1)." One mark is frequently available for simply showing you see the link.
  2. Write the shared quantity. Almost every link has one quantity that appears in both themes — energy, force, wavelength, charge, momentum. Write it down and give it a symbol.
  3. Write one equation from each theme that contains that quantity. Then combine them algebraically before substituting numbers.
  4. Finish with the consequence, not the number. "Because the specific heat capacity of water is large, the temperature rise is small, so the measurement is dominated by…" — that last clause is where the top-band marks live.
Common trap

The most common way to lose marks on a linking question is to answer only the half you recognise. If a question says "Explain, with reference to the energy of the particles…", and you write a perfect paragraph about forces with no energy in it, you score zero for the explanation even if the physics is right. The command term tells you which theme to use. Underline it before you write.

Linking question

Why does a satellite in a low orbit (A.2, D.1) get hotter on the side facing the Earth as well as the side facing the Sun (B.2)? Carry the argument through three themes: the centripetal force is supplied by gravity, so \(GMm/r^2 = mv^2/r\) fixes the orbital speed; the satellite's gravitational potential energy \(E_p = -GMm/r\) is constant in a circular orbit, so no energy is being converted there; the temperature is set by a radiation balance — absorbed solar intensity plus Earth's infrared emission against the satellite's own emission \(\sigma A T^4\). The physics that transfers between themes is energy rate: from mechanical power \(P = Fv\) in A.3 to radiated power in B.2. Notice that no single-theme answer can earn full marks; the mark scheme rewards the student who names the shared quantity once and then uses it in both contexts.

Exam technique — the arithmetic of timing

HL Paper 2: 90 marks in 150 minutes = 1.67 minutes per mark. That is your unit of currency. A 2-mark "state" question gets 100 seconds; a 6-mark "Explain, with reference to…" gets 10 minutes. Before you start writing, read the mark allocation and set yourself that budget. Paper 1B is roughly the same rate. In Paper 1A, if a multiple-choice question is still unclear after 60 seconds, eliminate two options, guess, flag it and move on — an unanswered question is a guaranteed zero, a guessed one is worth 25%.

The experimental programme (HL)

60 hours total: 40 hours of practical work, 10 hours for the collaborative sciences project (shared across your science subjects, usually with the CS or chemistry class), and 10 hours for the scientific investigation — the IA itself. The IA is marked out of 24 on four criteria of 6 marks each: research design, data analysis, conclusion, evaluation. See F — Skills and IA for the full treatment of what a top-band answer looks like.

What was removed, what was added

Removed from the old syllabus: capacitors and rectification, particle physics and the standard model, thin-film interference, cosmology. Added: rigid body mechanics (A.4), Galilean and special relativity (A.5), thermodynamics (B.4), induction (D.4), quantum physics (E.2), astrophysics including Compton scattering. If you find old revision material online covering capacitors or the standard model, it is not your syllabus — do not spend time on it.

The data booklet: your most under-used asset

Every physics paper is sat with the data booklet. Students routinely ignore it, then lose marks they could have picked up for free. The booklet contains, in order:

  1. Physical constants — \(c\), \(G\), \(h\), \(e\), \(N_A\), \(R\), \(k_B\), \(\varepsilon_0\), \(\mu_0\), \(m_e\), \(m_p\), the Stefan–Boltzmann constant \(\sigma\), the solar constant, astronomical data (mass and radius of Earth, Sun, Moon; Earth–Sun distance).
  2. List of formulae for every theme — this is the part you must navigate at speed.
  3. Conversion factors — eV to J (\(1\,\text{eV} = 1.60\times10^{-19}\,\text{J}\)), u to MeV/\(c^2\), atmospheres to Pa, kWh to J, degrees to radians.
  4. The electromagnetic spectrum — wavelength/frequency/energy bands.
  5. Some astronomical and material data — e.g. specific heat capacities, latent heats, resistivities, refractive indices where needed.
How to use it — navigating under time pressure
  1. Index it once, physically. Before the exam season, put a sticky tab or fold on the corner of: constants page, Theme A, Theme B, Theme C, Theme D, Theme E. Six tabs. You should be able to open any theme's formula page in under three seconds.
  2. Learn what is NOT in it. The booklet gives \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\) but not the derivation. It gives \(s = \dfrac{ax}{D}\) but not the geometry. It gives \(\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p}\) but not when to use it. What the booklet cannot give you is judgement — that is what this site teaches.
  3. Read the symbol list at the top of each section. Half of "wrong formula" errors are actually "wrong symbol" errors: the booklet uses \(\varepsilon\) for emf and \(E\) for electric field strength, and uses \(r\) for both separation and internal resistance.
  4. Use it to check units. If you have "derived" something and the units do not work, you have made an algebra slip — the booklet's constants page is a fast unit reference.
Common trap

The single most expensive data-booklet mistake is misreading a formula's conditions. \(W = p\Delta V\) only applies at constant pressure. \(E_k = \frac12 mv^2\) only applies when \(v \ll c\). \(v = f\lambda\) works for any wave but \(f\) is fixed by the source, not the medium. Before substituting, say the condition out loud. The booklet prints formulas without their caveats; the caveats are your job.

The maths toolkit a physicist needs

You are also taking Maths AA HL, so the pure mathematics is not a problem for you. What is a problem is the set of small, unglamorous habits that physics markschemes punish: significant figures, prefixes, rearranging before substituting, and the difference between percentage difference and percentage uncertainty. Here they are once.

Rearranging first, substituting second

Never substitute numbers into an equation and then rearrange. Rearrange symbolically, then substitute in one line. It is faster, it lets the examiner award method marks even if your arithmetic fails, and it lets you see whether the answer should be big or small.

Worked example 1 Rearranging then substituting

Question. A car accelerates from rest at \(2.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) over a distance of 85 m. Determine its final speed.

  1. Choose the equation with \(u, a, s, v\): \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\), so \(v = \sqrt{u^2 + 2as}\).
  2. Rearrange symbolically: already done. Now substitute in one line: \(v = \sqrt{0^2 + 2(2.4)(85)} = \sqrt{408}\).
  3. \(v = 20.2\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). Rounded: \(v = 20\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) (2 s.f., matching the data).
Answer: \(20\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). Sanity check: 85 m at 2.4 m s⁻² — \(t = \sqrt{2s/a} = \sqrt{70.8} = 8.4\) s, and \(2.4 \times 8.4 = 20.2\). Consistent, so the answer is right.

Log rules (needed for pH-free but still exponential physics)

You need logs for: the decay law \(N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}\), the capacitor-free but still exponential attenuation \(I = I_0 e^{-\mu x}\), sound intensity level, and stellar magnitudes. The rules you must have automatic:

  1. \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\)
  2. \(\ln(a/b) = \ln a - \ln b\)
  3. \(\ln(a^n) = n\ln a\)
  4. If \(N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}\) then \(\ln N = \ln N_0 - \lambda t\), so plotting \(\ln N\) against \(t\) gives a straight line of gradient \(-\lambda\).

Straight-line graphs: \(y = mx + c\)

This is the single most important mathematical idea in the whole practical course. If a relationship is \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\), it is not linear in \(l\) — but \(T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{g}l\) is. So you plot \(T^2\) (vertical) against \(l\) (horizontal) and read the gradient \(m = 4\pi^2/g\), from which \(g = 4\pi^2/m\).

How to use it — linearising any relationship
  1. Write the relationship with the dependent quantity alone on the left.
  2. Identify the quantity you changed (independent) and aim to get it to power 1 on the right.
  3. Whatever is done to the independent quantity, do to the whole axis. If the right side has \(l\), plot against \(l\). If it has \(1/R\), plot against \(1/R\).
  4. The remaining cluster of constants is your gradient (and any additive constant is your intercept).
  5. Extract the physical quantity from the gradient, including units: gradient units = (y-unit)/(x-unit).
Derivation Linearising \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\) — the template for every practical you will do
  1. Identify the variables: you choose \(l\) (independent), you measure \(T\) (dependent).
  2. Get the dependent quantity alone: it already is. Get the independent quantity to power 1: square both sides, \(T^2 = 4\pi^2\dfrac{l}{g}\).
  3. Write it in \(y = mx + c\) form: \(\underbrace{T^2}_{y} = \underbrace{\left(\dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}\right)}_{m} \underbrace{l}_{x} + \underbrace{0}_{c}\).
  4. So plot \(T^2\) on the vertical axis and \(l\) on the horizontal. The gradient is \(4\pi^2/g\), and \(g = 4\pi^2/\text{gradient}\).
  5. Check the gradient's units: \(\text{s}^2/\text{m}\). Since \(g\) has units m s⁻², inverting the gradient gives the right units — a free error check.

What it means: the same five lines handle every relationship in the course. When it is the wrong tool: if the relationship is not a simple power law — for exponential decay take logarithms first, then linearise.

Trigonometry and vectors

\(\sin\theta = \text{opposite}/\text{hypotenuse}\), \(\cos\theta = \text{adjacent}/\text{hypotenuse}\), \(\tan\theta = \text{opposite}/\text{adjacent}\). For physics the useful instant-recall values are \(\sin 30^\circ = 0.5\), \(\cos 60^\circ = 0.5\), \(\sin 45^\circ = \cos 45^\circ = 0.707\), \(\sin 60^\circ = 0.866\), \(\cos 30^\circ = 0.866\). Resolving a vector \(F\) at angle \(\theta\) to the horizontal: horizontal component \(F\cos\theta\), vertical component \(F\sin\theta\). Always draw the triangle first — if you resolve with the wrong angle you get the complementary component, which is the classic inclined-plane error.

Calculus notions

You will not be asked to integrate hard functions, but you must read calculus statements fluently:

  • \(v = \dfrac{ds}{dt}\) — velocity is the gradient of a displacement–time graph.
  • \(a = \dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{d^2s}{dt^2}\) — acceleration is the gradient of a velocity–time graph.
  • \(F = \dfrac{dp}{dt}\) — Newton's second law in its real form.
  • \(\varepsilon = -\dfrac{d(N\Phi)}{dt}\) — Faraday's law: emf is the rate of change of flux linkage.
  • \(I = \dfrac{dq}{dt}\) — current is the rate of flow of charge.
  • \(\int v\,dt\) gives displacement — the signed area under a v–t graph.

Percentage difference vs percentage uncertainty

These are different things and the exam asks for both.

Percentage uncertainty
\(\frac{\Delta x}{x}\times 100\%\). How well you know a value. Comes from your instruments.
Percentage difference (percentage error)
\(\frac{|\text{literature} - \text{experimental}|}{\text{literature}}\times100\%\). How far your answer is from an accepted value. Comes from comparing to a data-book value.

The decisive test in an IA conclusion: if your percentage difference is smaller than your percentage uncertainty, your result agrees with the accepted value and you have no systematic error worth reporting. If the difference is bigger, you do — and you must name it.

Common trap

Writing "human error" as a source of error scores zero. Writing "reaction time in starting and stopping the stopwatch is ±0.2 s on a 2.4 s measurement, i.e. 8%, which is larger than the 3% spread of repeats, so it dominates the uncertainty; using a light gate would reduce it to below 0.5%" scores full marks. Specific, quantified, and followed by a concrete improvement.

Exam technique — "Show that" is a gift

When a question says "Show that the speed is 4.5 m s⁻¹", the examiner has given you the answer and is paying you for the route. Use the given value in your next part even if you could not reach it — IB papers are designed so that a failed "show that" does not cascade. And never write "clearly, it is 4.5" — write the substitution.

Check yourself

Practice

  1. [2 marks] State the weighting and duration of HL Paper 1 and Paper 2.

    Show answer

    Paper 1: 2 hours, 75 marks, 36% of the final grade (1A multiple choice + 1B data-based questions). Paper 2: 2 hours 30 minutes, 90 marks, 44%, short-answer and extended response covering SL core plus additional HL material.

  2. [3 marks] A student measures \(g = 9.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) with a percentage uncertainty of 6%. The accepted value is 9.81 m s⁻². Calculate the percentage difference and state whether the result agrees with the accepted value.

    Show answer

    Percentage difference \(= \dfrac{|9.81-9.4|}{9.81}\times100\% = \dfrac{0.41}{9.81}\times100\% = 4.2\%\). Since 4.2% < 6%, the accepted value lies within the uncertainty range of the experimental result, so the result does agree with the accepted value and there is no evidence of a systematic error.

  3. [4 marks] Show that a plot of \(T^2\) against \(l\) for a simple pendulum should be a straight line, and state the gradient in terms of \(g\).

    Show answer

    Starting from \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\). Square both sides: \(T^2 = 4\pi^2 \dfrac{l}{g} = \left(\dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}\right)l\). This has the form \(y = mx + c\) with \(y = T^2\), \(x = l\), gradient \(m = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}\) and intercept \(c = 0\). So the graph is a straight line through the origin with gradient \(4\pi^2/g\).

  4. [3 marks] Convert (a) 650 nm to m, (b) 4.2 MeV to J, (c) 3.3 kΩ to Ω.

    Show answer

    (a) \(650\ \text{nm} = 650\times10^{-9}\ \text{m} = 6.50\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}\).

    (b) \(4.2\ \text{MeV} = 4.2\times10^{6}\times1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} = 6.7\times10^{-13}\ \text{J}\).

    (c) \(3.3\ \text{k}\Omega = 3.3\times10^{3}\ \Omega = 3300\ \Omega\).

    Note the trap in (b): mega-eV → eV is \(10^6\), then eV → J is \(1.6\times10^{-19}\). Two steps.

  5. [4 marks] Identify two theme pairs that link Theme C to another theme, and for each name the quantity that forms the bridge.

    Show answer

    C + E: the bridge is photon energy and wavelength — \(c = f\lambda\) (C.2) combined with \(E = hf\) and \(E_1 - E_2 = hf\) (E.1), used in atomic spectra and in the photoelectric effect and de Broglie wavelength (E.2).

    C + A: the bridge is the sinusoidal function — simple harmonic motion \(x = x_0\sin(\omega t)\) (C.1) is the same mathematics as the AC generator output in D.4 and as any oscillation in A.4 rotational motion. Also C.4 resonance links to A.2 driving forces.

  6. [3 marks] Explain the difference in the answer expected from "describe" and from "explain", using the photoelectric effect as your example.

    Show answer

    "Describe what is observed when the frequency of the incident light is increased" expects the observation: below a threshold frequency no electrons are emitted however intense the light; above it, electrons are emitted and their maximum kinetic energy increases with frequency; the number emitted per second depends on intensity, not frequency.

    "Explain these observations" expects the causal chain: light consists of photons of energy \(E = hf\); one photon interacts with one electron; a fixed energy \(\phi\) is needed to escape; so \(hf = \phi + \tfrac12 mv_{\max}^2\), which explains both the threshold and the increase of \(KE_{\max}\) with \(f\). Describe = what; explain = why.

  7. [4 marks] A force–time graph for a collision is a triangle rising from 0 to 240 N in 0.10 s and falling back to 0 in a further 0.10 s. Determine the impulse and hence the change in momentum of the object.

    Show answer

    Impulse = area under the F–t graph = \(\frac12 \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac12 \times 0.20 \times 240 = 24\ \text{N s}\). Impulse equals change in momentum, so \(\Delta p = 24\ \text{kg m s}^{-1}\) (equivalently 24 N s). Note the base is the full 0.20 s triangle, not 0.10 s — a very common slip.

  8. [5 marks] Discuss why the removal of the "options" from the syllabus changes how you should revise compared with a student who took the pre-2023 syllabus.

    Show answer

    Under the old syllabus, four optional topics (e.g. relativity, optics, cosmology) could be studied in depth in isolation, and Paper 3 examined them separately — a student could specialise and avoid weak areas.

    Now, all HL students sit the same additional HL material (A.4, A.5, B.4, D.4, E.2 plus HL extensions), so there is no avoidance: every HL topic can appear in Paper 2. Furthermore, the assessment emphasises linking questions across themes, so the required skill is integration rather than specialisation.

    Practical consequence: revise by connection — for each topic, be able to name at least two other themes it links to and the shared quantity — rather than revising each topic as a sealed box.
