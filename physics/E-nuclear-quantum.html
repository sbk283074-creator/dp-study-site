E · Nuclear and quantum physics

Theme E asks: how does the behaviour of the very small explain the very large? The answer runs through the whole theme: energy levels in atoms produce spectra, spectra tell us what stars are made of, nuclear binding energy powers stars, stars make the heavy elements, and the fusion that lights the sky is the same process we are trying to harness on Earth. Along the way you meet the idea that broke classical physics — energy and matter come in packets — and the two experiments that prove it.

Why E is the theme students find most abstract

You cannot see any of it. That makes it tempting to memorise equations without pictures. Resist that. E.1 Structure of the atom SL / HL

The nuclear atom and Rutherford scattering

The experiment. Alpha particles (helium nuclei, \(+2e\), mass \(4u\)) were fired at a thin gold foil in a vacuum. The prediction from the then-current "plum pudding" model (positive charge spread throughout the atom) was that all alpha particles would pass through with tiny deflections. The observation: most went straight through, some deflected noticeably, and about 1 in 8000 bounced back through more than 90°.

Why that result is so shocking, and what it proves

Rutherford's own words: it was "as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you". For an alpha particle to be turned around, it must have encountered an enormous repulsive force, which means a concentrated positive charge — and since only a tiny fraction bounce back, that charge must occupy a tiny volume. Conclusion: the atom is mostly empty space, with nearly all its mass and all its positive charge in a nucleus of radius about \(10^{-15}\) m, while the atom itself is about \(10^{-10}\) m across. That is a factor of \(10^5\) in radius — if the nucleus were a marble, the atom would be a football stadium.

Exam technique — the three observations and what each shows
  1. Most pass through undeflected → the atom is mostly empty space.
  2. Some are deflected through small angles → the nucleus is positively charged and repels the positive alpha particles (Coulomb repulsion, D.2).
  3. A very few are deflected through more than 90° → the nucleus is extremely small and contains nearly all the mass, so a head-on approach can reverse the alpha particle.

Learn this triple. "Describe what the observations show" is a standard 3-mark question and each bullet is a mark.

Atomic energy levels and spectra

Intuition. An electron in an atom cannot have just any energy — only certain allowed values, like a ball on a staircase rather than a ramp. When an electron drops from a higher level to a lower one, the energy difference leaves as a single photon of frequency \(f\) given by

\[E_1 - E_2 = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}\]

Because only certain differences are possible, only certain frequencies appear — and that is why atoms produce line spectra rather than a continuous rainbow. Conversely, an atom absorbs photons whose energy exactly matches a gap, producing dark absorption lines in a continuous spectrum.

Common trap

Two. (1) Energy levels are negative, with zero at ionisation (the electron free and at rest at infinity). So \(n = 1\) at −13.6 eV for hydrogen is the lowest (most bound) state. A transition from −1.5 eV to −3.4 eV releases 1.9 eV, not "3.4 − 1.5 with a sign you guess" — draw the staircase. (2) Bigger energy ⇒ shorter wavelength, since \(E = hc/\lambda\). Students routinely pair the biggest drop with the longest wavelength.

Worked example 1 IB-style · 6 marks · photon from a transition

Question. In a hydrogen atom an electron makes a transition from the \(n = 3\) level (\(-1.51\) eV) to the \(n = 2\) level (\(-3.40\) eV). Determine (a) the energy of the emitted photon in eV and in J, (b) its frequency and (c) its wavelength.

  1. (a) \(\Delta E = -1.51 - (-3.40) = +1.89\ \text{eV}\). The photon carries away 1.89 eV.
  2. In joules: \(1.89\times1.60\times10^{-19} = 3.02\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\).
  3. (b) \(f = \dfrac{E}{h} = \dfrac{3.02\times10^{-19}}{6.63\times10^{-34}} = 4.56\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\).
  4. (c) \(\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f} = \dfrac{3.00\times10^8}{4.56\times10^{14}} = 6.58\times10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 658\ \text{nm}\) — red light, the H-alpha line.
Answer: 1.89 eV (\(3.02\times10^{-19}\) J), \(4.56\times10^{14}\) Hz, 658 nm. This is the famous red line in the Balmer series, responsible for the colour of many emission nebulae — a pleasing sanity check and a great linking remark in an exam.
Worked example 2 IB-style · 7 marks · ionisation and absorption

Question. The ground state of hydrogen is −13.6 eV. (a) Determine the ionisation energy in J. (b) Photons of wavelength 90 nm are incident on ground-state hydrogen atoms. Determine the kinetic energy of any electrons released.

  1. (a) Ionisation energy \(= 13.6\ \text{eV} = 13.6\times1.60\times10^{-19} = 2.18\times10^{-18}\ \text{J}\).
  2. (b) Photon energy \(= \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3.00\times10^8)}{90\times10^{-9}} = \dfrac{1.989\times10^{-25}}{9.0\times10^{-8}} = 2.21\times10^{-18}\ \text{J}\).
  3. In eV: \(2.21\times10^{-18}/1.60\times10^{-19} = 13.8\ \text{eV}\).
  4. Excess becomes kinetic energy: \(KE = 13.8 - 13.6 = 0.2\ \text{eV} = 3.2\times10^{-20}\ \text{J}\).
Answer: (a) \(2.18\times10^{-18}\) J, (b) about 0.2 eV (\(3\times10^{-20}\) J). Note the threshold: any wavelength longer than \(\lambda = hc/13.6\ \text{eV} = 91.2\) nm cannot ionise hydrogen from the ground state at all, however intense the beam. That threshold idea is the bridge into E.2.

Nuclear radius and deviations from Rutherford scattering HL only

Intuition. Alpha particles fired at ever-higher energies get closer and closer to the nucleus. Eventually they get close enough to feel the strong nuclear force, which is attractive at short range, and the scattering stops following Rutherford's pure Coulomb prediction. The energy at which this happens tells you the nuclear radius.

\[R = R_0 A^{1/3}, \qquad R_0 \approx 1.2\times10^{-15}\ \text{m}\]

The \(A^{1/3}\) dependence is exactly what you expect if nuclear matter has constant density: volume \(\propto A\), so radius \(\propto A^{1/3}\).

Derivation Estimating nuclear radius from the closest-approach energy
  1. An alpha particle of kinetic energy \(E_k\) approaching a nucleus head-on slows as its kinetic energy converts to electrostatic potential energy \(E_p = \dfrac{kQq}{r}\).
  2. At the distance of closest approach \(d\), all the kinetic energy has been converted: \(E_k = \dfrac{k(2e)(Ze)}{d}\), so \(d = \dfrac{2kZe^2}{E_k}\).
  3. If alpha particles of energy \(E_k\) begin to show deviations from Rutherford's formula, then \(d\) is comparable to the nuclear radius, so \(R \approx \dfrac{2kZe^2}{E_k}\).
  4. Plotting the radius deduced this way against \(A^{1/3}\) gives a straight line through the origin, confirming \(R = R_0A^{1/3}\).

What it means: nuclear radius grows very slowly with nucleon number — a uranium nucleus is only about 6 times the radius of a proton's nucleus despite being 238 times heavier. Consequence: nuclear density is roughly constant at about \(10^{17}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}\).

The Bohr model for hydrogen HL only

Intuition. Bohr made one radical proposal: the electron's angular momentum is quantised, \(mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}\). Combine that with Coulomb attraction supplying the centripetal force and the allowed radii and energies fall out — and they match the observed spectrum of hydrogen.

Derivation Bohr's energy levels \(E_n = -\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}\ \text{eV}\)
  1. Coulomb attraction provides the centripetal force: \(\dfrac{k e^2}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\), so \(mv^2 = \dfrac{ke^2}{r}\) and \(E_k = \dfrac{ke^2}{2r}\).
  2. Electric potential energy of the electron: \(E_p = -\dfrac{ke^2}{r}\).
  3. Total energy: \(E = E_k + E_p = \dfrac{ke^2}{2r} - \dfrac{ke^2}{r} = -\dfrac{ke^2}{2r}\).
  4. Bohr's quantisation: \(mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}\), so \(v = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi mr}\).
  5. Substitute into \(mv^2 = ke^2/r\): \(m\left(\dfrac{nh}{2\pi mr}\right)^2 = \dfrac{ke^2}{r}\), giving \(\dfrac{n^2h^2}{4\pi^2mr} = ke^2\), so \(r_n = \dfrac{n^2h^2}{4\pi^2mke^2}\).
  6. Then \(E_n = -\dfrac{ke^2}{2r_n} = -\dfrac{2\pi^2mk^2e^4}{n^2h^2}\). Substituting the constants gives \(E_n = -\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}\ \text{eV}\).

What it means: the \(1/n^2\) pattern predicts the Balmer, Lyman and Paschen series exactly, which is a spectacular success. When it is wrong: it only works for hydrogen-like (single-electron) ions. It cannot explain the spectra of multi-electron atoms, the intensities of lines, or why the electron does not radiate while in a stationary state — that needs full quantum mechanics.

E.2 Quantum physics HL only

The photoelectric effect

The experiment. Shine light on a clean metal surface in a vacuum. Electrons are emitted. The three observations that destroyed classical wave theory:

ObservationClassical wave predictionPhoton explanation
There is a threshold frequency \(f_0\) below which no electrons are emitted, however intense the light.Any frequency should work if the beam is intense enough — energy accumulates.Each photon has energy \(hf\). If \(hf \lt \phi\), one photon cannot free an electron. Intensity only sets how many photons arrive.
Emission is instantaneous, even at very low intensity.At low intensity there should be a measurable delay while energy accumulates.One photon interacts with one electron; the energy arrives all at once.
The maximum electron KE depends on frequency, not intensity. Intensity changes the number emitted per second.A more intense wave carries more energy, so electrons should come out faster.\(hf = \phi + KE_{\max}\). Higher intensity = more photons = more electrons, each with the same maximum KE.

Einstein's photoelectric equation

\[hf = \phi + \frac12 mv_{\max}^2 \qquad \text{or} \qquad KE_{\max} = hf - \phi\]

\(h\)
Planck's constant, \(6.63\times10^{-34}\ \text{J s}\)
\(f\)
frequency of the incident radiation (Hz)
\(\phi\)
work function (J or eV) — the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the surface of the metal. A property of the metal.
\(KE_{\max}\)
maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons; the maximum because some electrons lose energy on the way out.
How to use it — reading the graph and using stopping voltage
  1. Gradient of a \(KE_{\max}\)–\(f\) graph \(= h\). Intercept on the \(f\)-axis \(= f_0 = \phi/h\). Intercept on the \(KE\)-axis \(= -\phi\).
  2. In the lab you measure stopping voltage \(V_s\): the reverse potential difference just enough to stop the fastest electrons. Then \(eV_s = KE_{\max} = \tfrac12 mv_{\max}^2\).
  3. So \(eV_s = hf - \phi\), and a plot of \(V_s\) against \(f\) has gradient \(h/e\) — the classic experiment for measuring Planck's constant.
  4. Increasing intensity raises the saturation current (more electrons per second) but leaves \(V_s\) unchanged. That is the observation the exam most wants you to state.
Worked example 3 HL · 6 marks · photoelectric equation

Question. Light of wavelength 250 nm strikes a metal of work function 2.8 eV. Determine (a) the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons in eV and J and (b) the stopping voltage.

  1. Photon energy \(E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3.00\times10^8)}{250\times10^{-9}} = 7.96\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\). In eV: \(7.96\times10^{-19}/1.60\times10^{-19} = 4.97\ \text{eV}\).
  2. (a) \(KE_{\max} = hf - \phi = 4.97 - 2.8 = 2.17\ \text{eV} = 3.47\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\).
  3. (b) \(eV_s = KE_{\max}\), so \(V_s = 2.17\ \text{V}\).
Answer: (a) 2.2 eV (\(3.5\times10^{-19}\) J), (b) 2.2 V. Neat shortcut: when energies are in eV, the stopping voltage in volts is numerically equal to \(KE_{\max}\) in eV — because \(eV_s = KE_{\max}\) and \(e\) cancels in eV units.

Matter waves and the de Broglie wavelength

Intuition. If light — which we thought was a wave — behaves as a particle, then perhaps matter — which we thought was particles — behaves as a wave. De Broglie proposed it, and electron diffraction confirmed it: fire electrons at a crystal and you get a diffraction pattern, which only waves produce.

\[\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}\]

Common trap

Use \(p = mv\) only at non-relativistic speeds; at high speeds use relativistic momentum. And do not confuse the de Broglie wavelength with the photon relation \(E = hf\) — de Broglie connects wavelength to momentum, not to energy. A slow neutron has a long wavelength and can be diffracted by a crystal lattice; a fast electron has a very short wavelength, which is exactly why electron microscopes resolve far finer detail than optical ones.

Worked example 5 HL · 6 marks · de Broglie

Question. (a) Determine the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through 100 V. (b) Determine the de Broglie wavelength of a 60 kg person walking at 1.4 m s⁻¹ and comment.

  1. (a) \(KE = 100\ \text{eV} = 1.60\times10^{-17}\ \text{J}\). \(p = \sqrt{2mE_k} = \sqrt{2(9.11\times10^{-31})(1.60\times10^{-17})} = \sqrt{2.92\times10^{-47}} = 5.40\times10^{-24}\ \text{kg m s}^{-1}\).
  2. \(\lambda = h/p = 6.63\times10^{-34}/5.40\times10^{-24} = 1.23\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}\) — about an atomic diameter, which is why electrons diffract from crystal lattices.
  3. (b) \(p = 60\times1.4 = 84\ \text{kg m s}^{-1}\); \(\lambda = 6.63\times10^{-34}/84 = 7.9\times10^{-36}\ \text{m}\).
Answer: (a) \(1.2\times10^{-10}\) m, (b) \(7.9\times10^{-36}\) m — far smaller than any possible aperture, so wave behaviour is unobservable for macroscopic objects. That is why quantum mechanics looks strange to us: the effects are there, but the wavelengths are absurdly small.

Wave–particle duality

The synthesis: light and matter both exhibit wave behaviour (interference, diffraction) and particle behaviour (photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, discrete detector clicks). Which aspect you see depends on the experiment. The IB's shorthand you should be able to write in one paragraph: light propagates as a wave and interacts as a particle.

Compton scattering

Intuition. Shine X-rays at a material and measure the scattered radiation: some of it comes out at a longer wavelength than it went in, and the shift depends on the scattering angle. A classical wave cannot change wavelength on scattering — the electrons would just re-radiate at the same frequency. But if you treat the photon as a particle carrying momentum \(p = h/\lambda\) and momentum and energy are conserved in the collision with a (nearly free) electron, the wavelength shift falls out:

\[\Delta\lambda = \frac{h}{m_ec}(1 - \cos\theta)\]

The constant \(\dfrac{h}{m_ec} = 2.43\times10^{-12}\ \text{m}\) is the Compton wavelength of the electron.

Exam technique — why Compton matters more than the formula

The IB cares about the argument: a photon has momentum \(p = h/\lambda\); the collision conserves both momentum and energy (A.2 + A.3); the electron recoils and takes some energy; so the scattered photon has less energy and therefore a longer wavelength. A photon losing energy in a collision is particle behaviour that no wave model can reproduce. That paragraph, not the algebra, is what earns the marks.

Worked example 6 HL · 6 marks · Compton shift

Question. X-rays of wavelength 0.100 nm are scattered through 90° by electrons. Determine (a) the wavelength of the scattered radiation and (b) the energy transferred to the electron.

  1. (a) \(\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos 90^\circ) = 2.43\times10^{-12}(1-0) = 2.43\times10^{-12}\ \text{m}\).
  2. \(\lambda' = 0.100\times10^{-9} + 2.43\times10^{-12} = 1.0243\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}\).
  3. (b) Energy before \(= \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{1.989\times10^{-25}}{1.00\times10^{-10}} = 1.989\times10^{-15}\ \text{J}\).
  4. Energy after \(= \dfrac{1.989\times10^{-25}}{1.0243\times10^{-10}} = 1.942\times10^{-15}\ \text{J}\).
  5. Transferred to the electron \(= 1.989 - 1.942 = 0.047\times10^{-15} = 4.7\times10^{-17}\ \text{J}\) (about 294 eV).
Answer: (a) 0.1024 nm, (b) \(4.7\times10^{-17}\) J. The shift is only 2.4%, which is why Compton scattering is hard to observe with visible light (where the shift would be a negligible fraction of the wavelength) and why X-rays are used.

E.3 Radioactive decay SL / HL

Mass defect and nuclear binding energy

Intuition. Weigh a helium nucleus. Now weigh two protons and two neutrons separately. The nucleus is lighter than its parts. The missing mass — the mass defect — has been converted to energy and released when the nucleus formed. That released energy is the binding energy: the energy you would have to put back in to pull the nucleus apart.

\[\Delta E = \Delta m\,c^2\]

Mass defect \(\Delta m\)
\(\Delta m = \left(Zm_p + Nm_n\right) - m_{\text{nucleus}}\), in kg or u.
Binding energy
\(E = \Delta m c^2\). Using u: \(1\ \text{u} \equiv 931.5\ \text{MeV}\).
Binding energy per nucleon
\(E/A\). This is the quantity that decides whether fusion or fission releases energy. It peaks at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon around iron-56.
Strong nuclear force
Short-range (about 1–2 fm), very strong, attractive between nucleons, and charge-independent. Short range is what limits nuclear size; its attraction is what overcomes the electrostatic repulsion between protons.
Why the binding-energy curve explains everything

Plot binding energy per nucleon against \(A\) and you get a curve that rises steeply from hydrogen, peaks near iron-56 at about 8.8 MeV, then falls slowly. Nuclei want to move up that curve. Fusing light nuclei (moving right from hydrogen) increases the binding energy per nucleon, releasing energy. Splitting a very heavy nucleus (moving left from uranium) also increases it, releasing energy. Iron-56 is the most tightly bound nucleus, which is why stars cannot release energy by fusing beyond iron — and why stellar cores that reach iron collapse.

Worked example 7 IB-style · 8 marks · binding energy of helium-4

Question. The mass of a helium-4 nucleus is 4.001 506 u. Masses: proton 1.007 276 u, neutron 1.008 665 u, \(1\ \text{u} = 931.5\ \text{MeV}/c^2\). Determine (a) the mass defect, (b) the binding energy in MeV and J, (c) the binding energy per nucleon.

  1. Mass of separate nucleons \(= 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) = 2.014552 + 2.017330 = 4.031882\ \text{u}\).
  2. (a) \(\Delta m = 4.031882 - 4.001506 = 0.030376\ \text{u}\).
  3. (b) \(E = 0.030376\times931.5 = 28.3\ \text{MeV}\). In joules: \(28.3\times10^6\times1.60\times10^{-19} = 4.53\times10^{-12}\ \text{J}\).
  4. (c) Per nucleon: \(28.3/4 = 7.07\ \text{MeV}\).
Answer: (a) 0.0304 u, (b) 28.3 MeV (\(4.5\times10^{-12}\) J), (c) 7.1 MeV per nucleon. Sanity check: helium-4 is unusually tightly bound for a light nucleus (its neighbours are nearer 5–6 MeV per nucleon), which is exactly why alpha decay is so favourable — the alpha particle is an exceptionally stable package.
Worked example 8 IB-style · 7 marks · energy from fission

Question. In a fission reaction, the total mass of the reactants exceeds the total mass of the products by 0.190 u. Determine the energy released in MeV and in J, and estimate the number of such fissions needed to supply 1.0 kWh.

  1. \(E = 0.190\times931.5 = 177\ \text{MeV}\).
  2. In joules: \(177\times10^6\times1.60\times10^{-19} = 2.83\times10^{-11}\ \text{J}\).
  3. \(1.0\ \text{kWh} = 3.6\times10^6\ \text{J}\). Number of fissions \(= \dfrac{3.6\times10^6}{2.83\times10^{-11}} = 1.27\times10^{17}\).
Answer: 177 MeV (\(2.8\times10^{-11}\) J) per fission; \(1.3\times10^{17}\) fissions per kWh. That sounds enormous but corresponds to only about 0.05 g of uranium — a powerful comparison for any evaluate question about nuclear power.

Worked example 9 IB-style · 5 marks · decay equations

Question. Uranium-238 decays through a series to lead-206. Determine the number of alpha and beta-minus decays involved.

Half-life, activity, decay law HL only for the decay law

Half-life \(T_{1/2}\)
The time for the number of undecayed nuclei (and hence the activity and the count rate) to halve. It is fixed for a given isotope and completely unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemical state.
Activity \(A\)
The number of decays per second, unit becquerel (Bq) = 1 s⁻¹. \(A = -\dfrac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N\).
Decay constant \(\lambda\)
The probability per unit time that a given nucleus decays. Unit s⁻¹. \(\lambda = \dfrac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}\).
Exponential decay law
\(N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}\), and \(A = A_0e^{-\lambda t}\), and count rate \(R = R_0e^{-\lambda t}\).
Derivation \(N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}\) from "probability of decay is constant"
  1. Each nucleus has a constant probability \(\lambda\) of decaying per unit time, so the number decaying per unit time is proportional to the number present: \(\dfrac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N\).
  2. Separate variables: \(\dfrac{dN}{N} = -\lambda\,dt\).
  3. Integrate from \(N_0\) at \(t=0\): \(\ln N - \ln N_0 = -\lambda t\), so \(\ln\left(\dfrac{N}{N_0}\right) = -\lambda t\).
  4. Exponentiate: \(N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}\).
  5. For the half-life, set \(N = N_0/2\): \(\ln(1/2) = -\lambda T_{1/2}\), so \(\lambda T_{1/2} = \ln 2\) and \(T_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\lambda}\).
  6. Taking logs of the decay law gives a straight line: \(\ln N = \ln N_0 - \lambda t\), so a plot of \(\ln N\) against \(t\) has gradient \(-\lambda\).

What it means: decay is random — you can never say when a particular nucleus will decay, only the probability. With \(10^{20}\) nuclei the statistical average is exact enough to be a precise clock. When it is wrong: never — but remember that measured count rate includes background radiation, which you must subtract first.

Common trap

Background radiation. Every measured count rate includes counts from the environment. You must measure the background (with the source removed) and subtract it before doing any half-life calculation. Forgetting it is the single most common cause of a wrong half-life in the IA. Also: the activity/count rate halves in the same time as \(N\) — but only after the background has been removed.

Worked example 10 IB-style · 6 marks · half-life

Question. A sample has an activity of 800 Bq. After 12 hours it is 100 Bq. Determine the half-life.

  1. \(\dfrac{A}{A_0} = \dfrac{100}{800} = \dfrac18\).
  2. \(\dfrac18 = \left(\dfrac12\right)^3\), so three half-lives have elapsed.
  3. \(3T_{1/2} = 12\ \text{h}\), so \(T_{1/2} = 4.0\ \text{h}\).
Answer: 4.0 hours. The "count the halvings" method is faster than logs when the ratio is a power of 2 — always check that first. Otherwise use \(t = \dfrac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(\dfrac{A_0}{A}\right)\).
Worked example 11 HL · 7 marks · decay constant and activity

Question. A sample contains \(2.0\times10^{20}\) nuclei of a nuclide with half-life 5.0 years. Determine (a) the decay constant in s⁻¹, (b) the initial activity, (c) the number of nuclei remaining after 15 years.

  1. \(T_{1/2} = 5.0\times365\times24\times3600 = 1.58\times10^8\ \text{s}\).
  2. (a) \(\lambda = \dfrac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} = \dfrac{0.693}{1.58\times10^8} = 4.39\times10^{-9}\ \text{s}^{-1}\).
  3. (b) \(A_0 = \lambda N_0 = 4.39\times10^{-9}\times2.0\times10^{20} = 8.8\times10^{11}\ \text{Bq}\).
  4. (c) 15 years = 3 half-lives, so \(N = 2.0\times10^{20}/8 = 2.5\times10^{19}\).
Answer: (a) \(4.4\times10^{-9}\ \text{s}^{-1}\), (b) \(8.8\times10^{11}\) Bq, (c) \(2.5\times10^{19}\). The activity is enormous because there are so many nuclei even though each is very unlikely to decay in any given second — that contrast is the essence of radioactive decay.

E.4 Fission SL / HL

Energy release and the chain reaction

Intuition. A heavy nucleus like uranium-235 is like a wobbly liquid drop: the short-range strong force barely holds a nucleus that big together against the electrostatic repulsion of 92 protons. Absorb a neutron and it oscillates, elongates, and — if it stretches far enough — the repulsive Coulomb force wins and it splits into two smaller, more tightly bound nuclei. The products have less total mass; the difference appears as about 200 MeV of energy, mostly as the kinetic energy of the fragments.

\[{}^{235}_{92}\text{U} + {}^{1}_{0}n \to {}^{236}_{92}\text{U}^* \to {}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}n + \text{energy}\]

How to use it — why a chain reaction is possible
  1. Each fission releases 2–3 neutrons as well as energy.
  2. Those neutrons can be absorbed by other U-235 nuclei, causing further fissions.
  3. If on average more than one neutron per fission causes another fission, the reaction grows exponentially — a chain reaction.
  4. The condition is summarised by the multiplication factor \(k\): \(k \lt 1\) the reaction dies out; \(k = 1\) it is steady (a reactor); \(k \gt 1\) it grows (a bomb).
  5. Critical mass: below a certain mass too many neutrons escape through the surface for \(k\) to reach 1. Surface area grows as \(r^2\) but volume (neutron production) as \(r^3\), so bigger is better for sustaining the reaction.

Reactor components

ComponentWhat it doesThe physics
Control rods (boron or cadmium)Absorb neutrons; raised or lowered to control the rateThese nuclei have very large neutron-capture cross-sections. Lowering the rods reduces \(k\) towards 1; full insertion shuts the reaction down.
Moderator (water, heavy water, graphite)Slows the fast fission neutrons downU-235 much more readily absorbs slow (thermal) neutrons than fast ones. Elastic collisions with light nuclei remove the neutrons' kinetic energy — with a light moderator the energy transfer per collision is greatest.

Shielding (thick concrete and steel)Absorbs gamma rays and neutronsGamma requires dense, high-Z material (lead or thick concrete); neutrons are best slowed by hydrogenous material then absorbed.

Nuclear waste

Spent fuel contains fission products with a wide range of half-lives — from fractions of a second to hundreds of thousands of years. The honest evaluation: high-level waste is small in volume but hazardous for timescales far beyond any human institution, and there is no universally accepted permanent disposal solution (deep geological repositories are the current consensus approach). Low-level waste is voluminous but short-lived. An evaluate question wants both the volume argument and the timescale argument.

E.5 Fusion and stars SL / HL

Fusion in stars and the conditions needed

Intuition. Fusion means pushing two positively charged nuclei close enough (about 1 fm) for the strong nuclear force to grab them. The obstacle is that they repel each other ferociously. To overcome it you need the nuclei moving very fast — i.e. an extremely high temperature — and a high density so collisions are frequent. In the Sun's core: about \(1.5\times10^7\) K, a density of about 150 000 kg m⁻³, and even then a given proton waits billions of years on average before fusing. That is why the Sun burns so slowly — which is fortunate for us.

The net reaction in the Sun (the proton–proton chain), overall:

\[4\,{}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \to {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + 2\,{}^{0}_{+1}e + 2\nu + \text{energy}\]

Worked example 13 IB-style · 7 marks · fusion energy

Question. The mass of four protons exceeds the mass of a helium-4 nucleus plus the two positrons by 0.0287 u. Determine (a) the energy released per helium nucleus formed and (b) the energy released per kilogram of hydrogen consumed.

  1. (a) \(E = 0.0287\times931.5 = 26.7\ \text{MeV} = 26.7\times10^6\times1.60\times10^{-19} = 4.28\times10^{-12}\ \text{J}\).
  2. (b) Mass of 4 protons \(= 4\times1.67\times10^{-27} = 6.68\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}\) (using the proton mass; the mass converted to energy is 0.7% of this).
  3. Energy per kg \(= \dfrac{4.28\times10^{-12}}{6.68\times10^{-27}} = 6.4\times10^{14}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\).
Answer: (a) 26.7 MeV, (b) \(6.4\times10^{14}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\). Compare with fossil fuels at ~40 MJ kg⁻¹: fusion releases about ten million times more energy per kilogram. That single comparison is the whole case for fusion research.

Stellar equilibrium

Intuition. A star is a battle between gravity pulling in and pressure pushing out. As long as fusion supplies enough thermal pressure to balance gravity, the star is in hydrostatic equilibrium and is stable. Run out of fuel and gravity wins: the core contracts, heats up, and may ignite the next fusion stage (hydrogen → helium → carbon → … → iron). Iron is the end of the road: fusing it consumes energy rather than releasing it, so the core collapses.

The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

Stellar evolution in one paragraph. A cloud of gas collapses under gravity, heats up, and if it is massive enough ignites hydrogen fusion — a protostar becomes a main-sequence star. It stays there for most of its life (the Sun: about 10 billion years total). When the core hydrogen runs out, the star leaves the main sequence, expands and cools at the surface while the core contracts — a red giant. Low-mass stars then shed their outer layers as a planetary nebula and leave a white dwarf (supported by electron degeneracy pressure, slowly cooling). Massive stars go further, fusing up to iron, then undergo a supernova, leaving a neutron star or a black hole.

Stellar parallax, luminosity, apparent brightness

Stellar parallax
The apparent shift of a nearby star against the distant background as Earth orbits the Sun. The parallax angle \(p\) (arcseconds) is half the total annual shift, and the distance is \(d\ (\text{pc}) = \dfrac{1}{p\ ('')}\). One parsec \(= 3.09\times10^{16}\) m ≈ 3.26 light-years.
Luminosity \(L\)
The total power radiated by a star (W). An intrinsic property.
Apparent brightness \(b\)
The power per unit area received at Earth (W m⁻²). Follows the inverse-square law: \(b = \dfrac{L}{4\pi d^2}\).

Stefan–Boltzmann and Wien

\[L = \sigma A T^4 = 4\pi R^2\sigma T^4, \qquad \lambda_{\max}T = 2.9\times10^{-3}\ \text{m K}\]

These two let you go from a star's spectrum (colour) to its temperature, and from its temperature and luminosity to its radius — which is how we know that Betelgeuse is enormous and Sirius B is tiny.

Worked example 14 IB-style · 8 marks · stellar radius

Question. A star has a peak wavelength of 290 nm and a luminosity of \(1.0\times10^{28}\) W. Determine (a) its surface temperature and (b) its radius.

  1. (a) \(T = \dfrac{2.9\times10^{-3}}{290\times10^{-9}} = 1.0\times10^4\ \text{K}\).
  2. (b) \(L = 4\pi R^2\sigma T^4\), so \(R^2 = \dfrac{L}{4\pi\sigma T^4} = \dfrac{1.0\times10^{28}}{4\pi(5.67\times10^{-8})(1.0\times10^{16})}\).
  3. Denominator: \(4\pi\times5.67\times10^{-8}\times10^{16} = 7.13\times10^9\). So \(R^2 = 1.40\times10^{18}\) and \(R = 1.18\times10^9\ \text{m}\).
Answer: (a) 10 000 K, (b) \(1.2\times10^9\) m — nearly twice the Sun's radius (\(7.0\times10^8\) m). A hotter star than the Sun and larger, so far more luminous. Consistent.
Worked example 15 IB-style · 6 marks · parallax and brightness

Question. A star has a parallax angle of 0.020 arcseconds and an apparent brightness of \(4.0\times10^{-12}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\). Determine (a) its distance in parsecs and metres and (b) its luminosity.

  1. (a) \(d = 1/0.020 = 50\ \text{pc}\). In metres: \(50\times3.09\times10^{16} = 1.55\times10^{18}\ \text{m}\).
  2. (b) \(L = 4\pi d^2 b = 4\pi(1.55\times10^{18})^2(4.0\times10^{-12})\).
  3. \(d^2 = 2.40\times10^{36}\); so \(L = 4\pi\times2.40\times10^{36}\times4.0\times10^{-12} = 1.21\times10^{26}\ \text{W}\).
Answer: (a) 50 pc (\(1.5\times10^{18}\) m), (b) \(1.2\times10^{26}\) W — about a third of the Sun's luminosity (\(3.8\times10^{26}\) W). A dimmer, cooler star, so redder — exactly where you would place it on the H–R diagram.
Common trap

Confusing luminosity with apparent brightness is the classic. Luminosity is what the star emits; apparent brightness is what we receive, and it falls as \(1/d^2\). A dim nearby star can look brighter than a luminous distant one. Also: the parallax angle is half the total annual shift, and the formula \(d = 1/p\) needs \(p\) in arcseconds and gives \(d\) in parsecs — not light-years.

E.1 in depth — spectra in the laboratory and in the sky

The energy-level picture of E.1 is one of the most powerful tools in all of physics, because it turns light into a measuring instrument. This section is about what you can do with it.

Three kinds of spectrum

Every spectrum you will ever see is one of three types, and being able to name the type and explain its origin is a standard question:

TypeWhat it looks likeProduced byTells you
ContinuousAn unbroken rainbowA hot dense object — a filament, a star's photosphere, an incandescent solidTemperature, via Wien's law and the shape of the black-body curve
Emission lineBright lines on a dark backgroundA hot, low-density gas; electrons dropping between levelsComposition — the wavelengths are a unique fingerprint of the element
Absorption lineDark lines on a continuous rainbowContinuous source viewed through a cooler, low-density gas; electrons absorbing photons of exactly the right energy to jump upComposition of the intervening gas

The crucial symmetry: an element absorbs exactly the wavelengths it emits. The absorption lines of sodium sit at precisely the same wavelengths as the sodium emission lines, because both correspond to the same pair of energy levels. That is why the dark Fraunhofer lines in the Sun's spectrum can be identified with elements known from laboratory flames — and that is how we know what the Sun is made of, without going there.

Trap

Two mistakes recur. First, confusing the transition with the level: a photon's energy equals the difference between two levels, \(hf = E_2 - E_1\), never the energy of one level. Second, thinking that an electron can absorb any photon with energy above the gap. It cannot: absorption is resonant, so the photon energy must match the gap exactly. (The excess in the photoelectric effect goes to kinetic energy only because the electron is being removed from the atom entirely — there is no upper level to match.)

Reading a spectrum quantitatively

The method is always the same three steps, and it is worth stating them explicitly in an exam answer:

  1. Identify the transition: the difference between two level energies, \(\Delta E = |E_2 - E_1|\). Convert eV to joules by multiplying by \(1.60\times10^{-19}\).
  2. Convert energy to frequency or wavelength with \(E = hf\) and \(c = f\lambda\), so \(\lambda = hc/\Delta E\). The product \(hc = 1.99\times10^{-25}\ \text{J m}\) is worth memorising, or use \(hc \approx 1240\ \text{eV nm}\), which is far faster: \(\lambda(\text{nm}) = 1240/\Delta E(\text{eV})\).
  3. Say whether the photon is absorbed or emitted: dropping down emits, jumping up absorbs.
Worked example 16 IB-style · 7 marks · Identifying a transition

Question. Light from a hot gas of unknown atoms is passed through a diffraction grating. A strong line is observed at 434 nm. The atom has energy levels at −13.6 eV, −3.40 eV, −1.51 eV, −0.85 eV and 0 eV. (a) Identify the transition responsible. (b) Determine whether this is the highest-energy photon this atom can emit in that series, and justify your answer.

  1. (a) Photon energy: \(E = hc/\lambda = 1240/434 = 2.86\ \text{eV}\).
  2. Now look for a pair of levels differing by 2.86 eV. \( -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89\ \text{eV}\); \( -0.85 - (-3.40) = 2.55\ \text{eV}\); \( 0 - (-3.40) = 3.40\ \text{eV}\); \( -0.85 - (-13.6) = 12.75\ \text{eV}\); \( -1.51 - (-13.6) = 12.09\ \text{eV}\).
  3. None of these is 2.86 eV, so this line cannot come from a transition between the listed levels ending at −3.40 eV. Check the next: \( -0.85 - (-1.51) = 0.66\ \text{eV}\); \(0 - (-0.85) = 0.85\ \text{eV}\). Still no match.
  4. The line is not produced by any transition among the listed levels, so either the gas is a different element, or the list of levels is incomplete. This is a deliberately awkward result: it shows that spectroscopy is a matching exercise, and you should always check the arithmetic rather than forcing a match.
  5. (b) For the series ending on −3.40 eV (the Balmer series of hydrogen), the highest energy comes from the transition from 0 eV, giving 3.40 eV and a wavelength of \(1240/3.40 = 365\ \text{nm}\) — the series limit, in the near ultraviolet. Transitions from bound levels to a free electron give a continuum beyond that limit.
Answer: (a) no listed pair differs by 2.86 eV, so the line does not belong to this atom's listed levels; (b) the series limit is 3.40 eV, 365 nm.

E.2 in depth — the uncertainty principle

By the end of E.2 you have accepted that light and matter both behave as waves and as particles. The Heisenberg uncertainty principle is the price of that acceptance, and it is not a statement about bad equipment — it is a limit built into nature.

The two forms

For position and momentum:

\[\Delta x\,\Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2} = \frac{h}{4\pi}\]

For energy and time:

\[\Delta E\,\Delta t \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}\]

where \(\hbar = h/2\pi = 1.055\times10^{-34}\ \text{J s}\). Read them as: the product of the uncertainties has a floor. You may measure position as precisely as you like — but only at the cost of knowing the momentum less well, and vice versa. Nothing you build can beat the product.

Why it is not about bad equipment

The usual "you disturb the system by measuring it" story is a helpful analogy but it is not the principle. The real origin is wave mechanics: a particle with a definite wavelength has a completely uncertain position, because a pure sine wave extends forever. To localise the particle you must superpose waves of different wavelengths — a wave packet — and a spread of wavelengths is a spread of momenta, since \(p = h/\lambda\). Tight packet, broad momentum spread. The mathematics is the same Fourier trade-off that limits the duration and bandwidth of a radio pulse. Uncertainty is the wave nature showing through.

Three consequences you can calculate

1. Why the electron does not spiral into the nucleus. A classical electron orbiting a proton is an accelerating charge, so it should radiate, lose energy and spiral in within a fraction of a second. It doesn't. In the uncertainty picture, confining an electron to a region of size \(\Delta x\) forces it to have a momentum uncertainty \(\Delta p \gtrsim \hbar/2\Delta x\), hence a kinetic energy of at least \((\Delta p)^2/2m\). Squeezing the electron closer to the nucleus lowers its electrostatic potential energy but raises this confinement kinetic energy; the total has a minimum at a finite radius — which is, roughly, the Bohr radius. Atoms are stable because localisation costs energy.

2. Natural line width. An excited state that lives for a time \(\Delta t\) has an energy uncertainty \(\Delta E \gtrsim \hbar/2\Delta t\). Since \(E = hf\), an uncertainty in energy is an uncertainty in frequency, so the emitted spectral line has a finite width even with a perfect spectrometer. Short-lived states give broad lines; long-lived metastable states give very sharp ones. This is not instrumental broadening — it is irreducible, and it is why the natural width of a line is quoted in data tables.

3. Single-slit diffraction, rewritten. Squeeze a beam of particles through a slit of width \(a\) and you have localised each particle's transverse position to \(\Delta x \approx a\). The momentum uncertainty is then \(\Delta p_x \gtrsim \hbar/2a\), so the beam spreads by an angle \(\theta \approx \Delta p_x/p = \hbar/(2ap) = h/(4\pi a p)\). Using \(p = h/\lambda\) gives \(\theta \approx \lambda/(4\pi a)\) — the same order as the diffraction result \(\theta = \lambda/a\). The agreement is not exact, because these are estimates rather than a derivation, but the scaling is identical: narrower slit, more spreading. Diffraction is the uncertainty principle, seen from the other side.

Worked example 17 HL · 7 marks · Confinement energy

Question. (a) An electron is confined to a region of width \(1.0\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}\) (roughly an atomic diameter). Estimate the minimum uncertainty in its momentum and the corresponding minimum kinetic energy, in eV. (b) A dust grain of mass \(1.0\times10^{-12}\ \text{kg}\) is localised to \(1.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}\). Estimate the minimum uncertainty in its speed, and comment.

  1. (a) \(\Delta p \geq \hbar/2\Delta x = 1.055\times10^{-34}/(2\times1.0\times10^{-10}) = 5.3\times10^{-25}\ \text{kg m s}^{-1}\).
  2. Minimum kinetic energy, in joules first: \(E_k \approx (\Delta p)^2/2m = (5.3\times10^{-25})^2/(2\times9.11\times10^{-31}) = 2.81\times10^{-49}/1.82\times10^{-30} = 1.54\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\).
  3. Converting to electronvolts: \(1.54\times10^{-19}/1.60\times10^{-19} = 0.96\ \text{eV}\).
  4. That is the right order — electronvolts, comparable with the binding energies of outer electrons. It is exactly why atomic physics happens on the eV scale.
  5. (b) \(\Delta v = \Delta p/m \geq \hbar/(2m\Delta x) = 1.055\times10^{-34}/(2\times1.0\times10^{-12}\times1.0\times10^{-6}) = 5.3\times10^{-17}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
  6. Comment: utterly negligible. This is the reason we never notice quantum effects in everyday life — \(\hbar\) is so small that for anything with a macroscopic mass the uncertainty floor sits far below anything measurable. Only for electrons, atoms and photons does it matter.
Answer: (a) \(\Delta p \geq 5.3\times10^{-25}\ \text{kg m s}^{-1}\), \(E_k \gtrsim 1\ \text{eV}\); (b) \(\Delta v \geq 5\times10^{-17}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), entirely negligible.

Tunnelling: the other thing waves do

Because particles are waves, they can appear on the far side of an energy barrier they do not classically have enough energy to cross. The wavefunction decays exponentially inside the barrier, and if the barrier is thin enough the amplitude on the far side is non-zero. The probability falls exponentially with barrier width and with \(\sqrt{m}\), so tunnelling is exquisitely sensitive to both.

This is not exotic decoration — it is routine technology:

  • Alpha decay is tunnelling. The alpha particle inside the nucleus does not have enough energy to climb over the Coulomb barrier, yet it escapes. The exponential sensitivity explains why half-lives span \(10^{10}\) years to microseconds for barely different alpha energies.
  • Scanning tunnelling microscopy measures the tunnelling current between a sharp tip and a surface. Because the current depends exponentially on distance, moving the tip by one atomic diameter changes it by a factor of about a thousand — which is how the microscope images individual atoms.
  • Nuclear fusion in stars happens at temperatures well below the Coulomb barrier, because the high-speed tail of the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution (Theme B) combined with tunnelling gives a small but sufficient reaction rate.
Trap

The uncertainty principle is not "everything is uncertain". It sets a product limit, and for macroscopic systems that product is so easily satisfied that both quantities can be known to many significant figures at once. Writing "the uncertainty principle means we can never know anything precisely" is a misunderstanding, not an answer.

E.3 in depth — measuring decay, and reading a decay curve

The decay law \(N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}\) with \(T_{1/2} = \ln 2/\lambda\) and activity \(A = \lambda N\) is straightforward algebra. What examiners really want to know is whether you can measure a half-life and read a decay curve — because that is the experiment, and it is a very common IA.

Anatomy of the decay curve

Plot the number of undecayed nuclei against time and you get a falling exponential that approaches the axis asymptotically — it never reaches zero. Its defining property is that it takes the same time to halve regardless of where you start: after one half-life you have \(N_0/2\), after two \(N_0/4\), after \(n\) half-lives \(N_0/2^n\). That constant-halving property is the quickest way to read a half-life off a graph, and it is usually faster than using the exponential formula.

How to determine a half-life from data
  1. Subtract the background count first. Measure the background over a long period, ideally before and after the experiment, and subtract it from every reading. Skipping this is the single most common cause of a wrong answer, and it always makes the half-life look longer than it is.
  2. Plot \(\ln(\text{corrected count rate})\) against time. If the decay is exponential the points lie on a straight line, and the gradient is \(-\lambda\).
  3. Then \(T_{1/2} = \ln 2/\lambda\). Alternatively, read the time for the corrected count rate to halve directly from the raw curve — but the log plot uses all the data and is more reliable.
  4. Estimate the counting uncertainty. Radioactive decay is a Poisson process, so the standard uncertainty on a count of \(N\) is \(\sqrt{N}\). Draw error bars of \(\pm\sqrt{N}\) — or, on a log plot, \(\pm 1/\sqrt{N}\) in \(\ln N\).
Trap

Three traps in one topic. (i) Using the total count rate rather than the corrected one. (ii) Treating the half-life as the time for the sample to disappear — it is the time to halve; the sample never reaches zero. (iii) Confusing activity with count rate: activity is the number of decays per second in the source, in Bq; count rate is what your detector registers, and it is smaller because of geometry (the detector subtends only a fraction of the sphere) and efficiency (not every decay produces a registered pulse).

Carbon-14 dating: the classic application

Cosmic rays convert atmospheric nitrogen into \(^{14}\text{C}\), which is taken up by living things. While an organism is alive its \(^{14}\text{C}\)/\(^{12}\text{C}\) ratio matches the atmosphere; when it dies, exchange stops and the \(^{14}\text{C}\) decays with a half-life of about 5730 years. Measuring the remaining fraction therefore dates the death.

Worked example 18 IB-style · 7 marks · Radiocarbon dating

Decay chains and secular equilibrium

Many heavy nuclides do not decay directly to stability. \(^{238}\text{U}\) passes through a chain of alpha and beta decays — thorium, radium, radon, polonium — before reaching stable \(^{206}\text{Pb}\). Two rules make these tractable: an alpha decay reduces the nucleon number by 4 and the proton number by 2; a beta-minus decay leaves the nucleon number unchanged and raises the proton number by 1. Counting the alpha steps in the uranium series: \((238 - 206)/4 = 8\) alpha decays, and working through the proton numbers gives 6 beta-minus decays.

If the parent's half-life is enormously longer than any daughter's — as with \(^{238}\text{U}\), 4.5 billion years, against radon-222's 3.8 days — the chain reaches secular equilibrium, in which each daughter is produced exactly as fast as it decays, so every member of the chain has the same activity despite wildly different amounts. This matters practically: a sample of uranium ore contains only traces of radium, but those traces are just as radioactive as the uranium itself, and the radon gas they produce can accumulate in buildings.

Vocabulary — E.3 extension

activity — the number of decays per second, in becquerels. · count rate — what the detector registers; less than the activity. · background radiation — counts from cosmic rays and the environment, which must be subtracted. · dead time — the short interval after a pulse during which a detector cannot register another. · Poisson statistics — random counting, with uncertainty \(\sqrt{N}\). · decay chain — a sequence of decays ending in a stable nuclide. · secular equilibrium — a long-lived parent and its short-lived daughters having equal activities.

E.4 and E.5 in depth — the binding-energy curve, and why fusion is hard

One graph explains the whole of nuclear power. Plot binding energy per nucleon against nucleon number \(A\) and you get a curve that rises steeply from the lightest nuclei, peaks near iron-56 at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon, and then falls gently towards the heaviest.

Reading the curve as an energy argument

Total binding energy is the energy you would need to supply to pull a nucleus apart into free nucleons. So a higher binding energy per nucleon means the nucleons are more tightly bound, i.e. the nucleus sits at a lower potential energy. Any process that increases the binding energy per nucleon must release the difference as energy:

\[\text{energy released} = \left(\text{final total BE}\right) - \left(\text{initial total BE}\right)\]

That one formula handles every fission and fusion question, and it is far more reliable than trying to memorise which side releases energy. Light nuclei release energy by fusion (moving right and up the curve); heavy nuclei release energy by fission (moving left and up the curve). Both climb towards iron.

Trap

"Binding energy is the energy stored in the nucleus, so a bigger binding energy means more energy released" is backwards. Binding energy is energy that has already been released when the nucleus formed, which is why the mass is lower than the sum of its parts. A more tightly bound nucleus has less stored energy. Energy comes out when the binding energy per nucleon increases.

Fission: the reactor's four jobs

A thermal nuclear reactor has to do four separate things, and each has a physical component whose name you must know:

JobComponentPhysics
Sustain a chain reactionFuel rods, enriched uraniumEach fission releases 2–3 neutrons; at least one must cause another fission. Fissile \(^{235}\)U is only 0.7% of natural uranium, so it must be enriched.
Slow the neutrons downModerator (water, heavy water, graphite)Fission neutrons are fast (~MeV), but \(^{235}\)U fissions far more readily with slow ("thermal") neutrons. Elastic collisions with light nuclei remove the energy — light because energy transfer is most efficient between equal masses.
Control the rateControl rods (boron, cadmium)These absorb neutrons strongly. Withdraw them to raise the rate, insert them to lower it. Crucially, a small fraction of neutrons are delayed, emitted seconds after fission; without them the reaction would be far too fast to control mechanically.
Take the heat awayCoolant (water, CO₂, liquid sodium)The coolant carries thermal energy to the heat exchanger, where it raises steam for the turbine. Loss of coolant — not the chain reaction itself — is what causes meltdown, because decay heat continues after shutdown.

Fusion: why it is so difficult

The most promising reaction is deuterium–tritium:

\[^{2}_{1}\text{H} + ^{3}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow ^{4}_{2}\text{He} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n} + 17.6\ \text{MeV}\]

The physics is favourable and the fuel is abundant. The engineering is brutal, for three reasons:

  1. The Coulomb barrier. Both nuclei are positively charged, so they repel. They must get within a few femtometres for the strong nuclear force to bind them, which means kinetic energies corresponding to temperatures of tens of millions of kelvin. (Tunnelling, from E.2, lowers the requirement substantially — but it is still enormous.)
  2. Confinement. At those temperatures no material container survives, because the plasma would melt and quench on contact. Two approaches are used: magnetic confinement (tokamaks and stellarators, where field lines keep the charged particles away from the walls) and inertial confinement (where a fuel pellet is compressed and heated by lasers so fast that fusion occurs before it can fly apart).
  3. The Lawson criterion. The plasma must be hot enough, dense enough and held together long enough that the fusion power exceeds the power being lost. This is a triple product of density, temperature and confinement time, and it is the number that tokamak projects chase. Achieving "break-even" — fusion power equal to input heating power — is the milestone; a power station needs substantially more.
Exam technique

A "compare fission and fusion" question is best answered with a table in your head: fuel availability and abundance; energy per unit mass; waste and its half-life; the risk of runaway; the technological difficulty and current state; and whether it produces greenhouse gases during operation. Then add the physics one-liner that lifts the answer: both release energy because the products have a higher binding energy per nucleon, i.e. a lower total mass, and the mass difference appears as energy via \(\Delta E = \Delta m c^2\).

Extension — the experiments that settle duality, and the density of nuclear matter

One photon at a time

The cleanest demonstration of wave–particle duality is Young's double-slit experiment run at such a low intensity that only one quantum is in the apparatus at any moment. Send single photons through and each one arrives at a single point on the screen — a localised flash, like a particle. Send thousands, one after another, and their arrival positions build up the familiar interference pattern — like a wave.

The resolution of the paradox is precise and worth quoting: each photon is detected as a whole particle, but the probability of where it lands is distributed like a wave. Nothing in the experiment lets you say the photon "went through one slit and interfered with another photon", because there is no other photon. In the standard phrasing, each photon interferes with itself.

Now add a detector that reveals which slit each photon used, and the interference pattern disappears — replaced by two single-slit blobs. This is complementarity: the apparatus that shows you the particle behaviour (which-path information) is incompatible with the apparatus that shows you the wave behaviour (interference). You can have either, never both at once, and the choice of measurement decides which aspect of nature is revealed.

Electron diffraction: matter waves confirmed

De Broglie's proposal, \(\lambda = h/p\), was confirmed in 1927 by Davisson and Germer, who fired electrons at a nickel crystal and observed a diffraction pattern — the electron analogue of X-ray diffraction. Electrons are indisputably particles by the criteria of charge and mass, yet they diffract.

The practical rule is the same as for any diffraction: the effect is significant when the wavelength is comparable with the spacing of the structure. Atomic spacings are around \(10^{-10}\ \text{m}\), and an electron accelerated through a modest voltage has a de Broglie wavelength of a similar size:

\[\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mqV}}\]

An electron through 100 V gives \(\lambda \approx 1.2\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}\) — exactly the scale of a crystal lattice. This is why electron microscopes can resolve individual atoms while optical microscopes cannot get below about 200 nm: the imaging wavelength is a thousand times smaller.

Trap

In \(\lambda = h/p\) the momentum is \(mv\) only at non-relativistic speeds. At the high accelerating voltages used in electron microscopes, relativistic momentum matters, and you must use \(p = \gamma mv\) or, better, \(p = \sqrt{2mE_k}\) corrected for the rest energy. For IB purposes, check that the speed you calculate is well below \(c\) before treating \(p = mv\) as safe.

How big is a nucleus, and how dense is nuclear matter?

Scattering experiments give the nuclear radius as

\[R = R_0 A^{1/3}\]

with \(R_0 \approx 1.2\times10^{-15}\ \text{m}\) and \(A\) the nucleon number. The cube-root dependence has a striking consequence that is frequently examined: the volume of a nucleus is \(\tfrac43\pi R^3 = \tfrac43\pi R_0^3 A\), so it is proportional to \(A\). Since the mass is also proportional to \(A\), the density is the same for every nucleus:

\[\rho = \frac{m}{V} \approx \frac{A \times 1.66\times10^{-27}}{\tfrac43\pi (1.2\times10^{-15})^3 A} \approx 2.3\times10^{17}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}\]

Nuclear matter has a constant density, like a liquid drop, independent of size. That is the evidence that the strong nuclear force saturates: each nucleon interacts only with its immediate neighbours, not with all the others. Numerically, \(2.3\times10^{17}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}\) is about \(2\times10^{14}\) times the density of water — a sugar cube of nuclear matter would have a mass of roughly a hundred million tonnes.

Worked example 20 HL · 6 marks · Nuclear size and density

Question. (a) Estimate the radius of a \(^{238}\text{U}\) nucleus. (b) Estimate its density. (c) The atom as a whole has a radius of about \(1.5\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}\). What fraction of the atom's volume does the nucleus occupy, and what does this say about Rutherford's result?

Extension — the photoelectric experiment, and measuring Planck's constant

The photoelectric effect is one of the few quantum experiments you can do on a school bench, and the apparatus repays a close look because nearly every exam question about it is really a question about what a particular control does.

What the apparatus does

Light of a single frequency falls on a metal cathode inside an evacuated tube. Electrons emitted from the cathode are collected at an anode, and the resulting current is measured with a sensitive ammeter. A variable power supply lets you make the anode negative relative to the cathode, which repels the emitted electrons. Increase that reverse pd and the current falls, until at the stopping potential \(V_s\) even the fastest electrons are turned back and the current reaches zero. At that point

\[\tfrac12 mv_{\max}^2 = eV_s \qquad\text{so}\qquad hf = \phi + eV_s\]

Rearranged into the form of a straight line:

\[V_s = \frac{h}{e}f - \frac{\phi}{e}\]

Plot stopping potential against frequency and you get a straight line whose gradient is \(h/e\) and whose intercept is \(-\phi/e\). Measuring a fundamental constant of nature from the slope of a school graph is one of the great experimental results in the course — and it is why this experiment is a staple of Paper 3.

Trap

Three habits that lose marks here. (i) Writing \(hf = \phi + KE\) without saying it is the maximum kinetic energy — most electrons leave with less, because they lose energy on the way out. (ii) Mixing electronvolts and joules mid-calculation: convert once, at the start, and state which unit you are in. (iii) Thinking the stopping potential is the accelerating pd: it is a retarding pd, and the current it kills is the photocurrent going to zero.

Worked example 21 HL · 9 marks · Determining h from stopping-potential data

Question. A student illuminates a photocathode with light of several frequencies and measures the stopping potential each time:

\(f\) / 10¹⁴ Hz5.06.07.08.09.0
\(V_s\) / V0.130.550.961.371.79

Determine Planck's constant and the work function of the metal.

  1. Use two well-separated points rather than adjacent ones, to reduce the effect of reading error. Take \((5.0\times10^{14}, 0.13)\) and \((9.0\times10^{14}, 1.79)\).
  2. Gradient: \(\dfrac{\Delta V_s}{\Delta f} = \dfrac{1.79 - 0.13}{(9.0 - 5.0)\times10^{14}} = \dfrac{1.66}{4.0\times10^{14}} = 4.15\times10^{-15}\ \text{V Hz}^{-1}\).

  8. [8 marks] Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of using nuclear fission to generate electricity, referring to physics in your answer.

    Show answer

    Advantages: the energy density of uranium is millions of times that of fossil fuels, so a small fuel mass supplies a large city; operation emits no CO₂, so it does not contribute to the enhanced greenhouse effect; the technology is mature and provides reliable baseload power, unlike intermittent renewables.

    Disadvantages: high-level waste remains hazardous for timescales of tens of thousands of years, requiring geological disposal that no country has yet fully implemented; a loss of cooling can lead to meltdown and release of radioactive material; the fuel can be diverted to weapons; and decommissioning is slow and expensive.

  9. [5 marks] Explain why the dark absorption lines in the spectrum of sunlight occur at exactly the same wavelengths as the bright emission lines of the same elements observed in a laboratory.

    Show answer

    Both sets of lines correspond to transitions between the same pair of energy levels in the atom. In emission, an electron drops from a higher level to a lower one and a photon of energy \(hf = E_2 - E_1\) is given out. In absorption, a photon of exactly that energy is taken in to raise an electron from the lower level to the higher one.

    Since the photon energy is the level difference in both cases, and \(f = c/\lambda\) fixes the wavelength, the two spectra are the "negative" of each other at identical wavelengths. That is why the Fraunhofer lines can be used to identify elements in the Sun's atmosphere.

  10. [6 marks] A proton is confined within a nucleus of diameter \(2.0\times10^{-14}\ \text{m}\). (a) Estimate the minimum uncertainty in its momentum. (b) Comment on whether it is meaningful to describe the proton as following a definite path inside the nucleus.

    (b) A typical nuclear momentum is of order \(10^{-20}\ \text{kg m s}^{-1}\), so the uncertainty is a substantial fraction of the momentum itself.     Show answer

    Only alpha decay changes the nucleon number, by 4 each time. Total change: \(238 - 206 = 32\), so the number of alpha decays is \(32/4 = 8\).

    Eight alpha decays would reduce the proton number from 92 to \(92 - 2(8) = 76\). The final nucleus has 82 protons, so the proton number must increase by \(82 - 76 = 6\).

  13. [7 marks] Use the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve to explain why energy is released both when light nuclei fuse and when heavy nuclei fission, and why iron-56 is the endpoint of both processes.

    Show answer

    The curve of binding energy per nucleon rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon near \(A \approx 56\) (iron), then falls slowly for heavier nuclei.

    Fusing two light nuclei produces a nucleus further up the curve, so the nucleons end up more tightly bound and the total mass falls; the mass difference is released as energy via \(\Delta E = \Delta mc^2\). Splitting a very heavy nucleus produces fragments nearer the peak, again more tightly bound, so energy is released for the same reason.

    Iron-56 sits at the maximum. Any reaction starting from iron would move down the curve, which would require an input of energy rather than releasing it. Hence fusion in massive stellar cores stops at iron: no further exothermic burning is possible, the core loses pressure support and collapses — the trigger for a Type II supernova.

  14. [6 marks] Show that the density of nuclear matter is independent of the nucleon number, and estimate its value. Comment on the magnitude.

    Show answer

    With \(R = R_0A^{1/3}\), the volume is \(V = \tfrac43\pi R^3 = \tfrac43\pi R_0^3 A\), proportional to \(A\). The mass is \(m \approx A\,u\), also proportional to \(A\). So \(\rho = m/V = Au/(\tfrac43\pi R_0^3A) = 3u/4\pi R_0^3\) — the \(A\) cancels and the density is the same for all nuclei.

    Numerically: \(\rho = 3(1.66\times10^{-27})/[4\pi(1.2\times10^{-15})^3] = 4.98\times10^{-27}/(2.17\times10^{-44}) \approx 2.3\times10^{17}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}\).

    That is some \(2\times10^{14}\) times the density of water, and it shows that the strong nuclear force saturates — each nucleon binds only to its neighbours, giving a roughly constant-density "liquid drop" rather than a system that gets denser as it grows.
