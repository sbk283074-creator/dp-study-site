F · Skills and the scientific investigation

This is the 20% of your grade you control completely. The IA is not a test of how clever your apparatus is — it is a test of whether you can design a question you can actually answer, take data whose uncertainties you understand, process it honestly, and then tell the examiner precisely what you would do differently. Every mark comes from justified decisions: why this range, why this instrument, why this uncertainty dominates, why this improvement. This page turns all of that into a procedure.

Why most IAs lose marks — and it is not the physics

The overwhelming majority of lost IA marks are not lost because the physics was wrong. They are lost because the student never quantified anything. "The results were quite accurate" scores nothing. "The percentage difference from the literature value is 4.8%, which is smaller than the 6.1% uncertainty in the gradient, so the result agrees with the accepted value" scores full marks. Every section of this page ends with the sentence you should actually write.

Dimensional consistency: your free error-detector

Every equation you write must have the same dimensions on both sides. This is a check, not a proof — but it catches most algebra slips in seconds.

How to use it
  1. Replace every symbol with its base-unit dimensions.
  2. Simplify both sides.
  3. If they differ, your rearrangement is wrong.
  4. Also use it to find the units of a gradient: gradient units = (vertical-axis unit) ÷ (horizontal-axis unit). If your "gradient = g" claim has units of m s⁻², good; if not, something is wrong.
Worked example 1 3 marks · checking a claimed relationship

Question. A student claims the period of a pendulum is \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{g}{l}}\). Use dimensional analysis to show this is wrong, and give the correct form.

  1. Dimensions of \(g/l\): \(\dfrac{\text{m s}^{-2}}{\text{m}} = \text{s}^{-2}\).
  2. So the right-hand side has dimensions \(\sqrt{\text{s}^{-2}} = \text{s}^{-1}\), while \(T\) has dimensions of s. Not consistent.
  3. To get seconds you need \(\sqrt{l/g}\): \(\sqrt{\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{m s}^{-2}}} = \sqrt{\text{s}^2} = \text{s}\). ✓
  4. Correct form: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}\).
Answer: the student inverted the ratio. Dimensional analysis does not tell you the \(2\pi\), but it catches the inversion instantly — and this is exactly the check you should run on your own rearrangement in the IA.
Worked example 2 4 marks · units of a gradient

Question. For a spring, \(T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{k}m\). A student plots \(T^2\) (s²) against \(m\) (kg). State the units of the gradient and hence how to find \(k\), including its units.

  1. Gradient units \(= \dfrac{\text{s}^2}{\text{kg}} = \text{s}^2\text{kg}^{-1}\).
  2. Gradient \(= \dfrac{4\pi^2}{k}\), so \(k = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{\text{gradient}}\).
  3. Units of \(k\): \(\dfrac{1}{\text{s}^2\text{kg}^{-1}} = \text{kg s}^{-2} = \text{N m}^{-1}\). ✓ — the correct unit for a spring constant, confirming the analysis.
Answer: gradient in s² kg⁻¹; \(k = 4\pi^2/\text{gradient}\) in N m⁻¹. If your units had come out as N m⁻² you would know immediately that something was wrong.
Common trap

In the IA, the most common dimensional error is a mixed prefix: recording lengths in cm in one column and using them with a formula that needs m, or quoting a resistance in kΩ but substituting 3.3 into \(P = V^2/R\). Put the unit in every column header and convert to SI in the table itself. Write the header as "\(l\) / m" and the entry as 0.640, not "\(l\) / cm" with 64.0.

Uncertainty

Random vs systematic; precision vs accuracy

Random error

Causes scatter: readings sometimes high, sometimes low. Sources: reaction time, parallax, reading a fluctuating display, turbulence. Reduced by repeating and averaging. It shows up in your error bars.

Systematic error

Shifts every reading the same way. Sources: a zero error on a balance, a miscalibrated sensor, an unaccounted-for resistance, ignoring the end correction of a pipe. Not reduced by repeating. It shows up as a non-zero intercept or a percentage difference larger than your uncertainty.

Precision

How closely repeated measurements agree — the scatter. High precision = small random error. Nothing to do with being "right".

Accuracy

How close a measurement is to the true value. High accuracy = small systematic error.

Common trap

Saying an instrument is "precise to ±0.01 mm" when you mean the manufacturer's resolution. Precision is about your spread of readings; resolution is the smallest division. The uncertainty in a single reading from a digital instrument is usually taken as ±1 in the last digit (or ±half the smallest division for an analogue scale), but the uncertainty in a repeated measurement is better estimated from the spread of the repeats. Use whichever is larger — and say that you did.

Absolute, fractional and percentage uncertainty

Absolute uncertainty \(\Delta x\)
The uncertainty expressed in the same units as the quantity: \(l = 0.640 \pm 0.002\ \text{m}\).
Fractional uncertainty
\(\dfrac{\Delta x}{x}\) — dimensionless.
Percentage uncertainty
\(\dfrac{\Delta x}{x}\times100\%\).

Derivation Why percentage uncertainties add for a product, and why a power multiplies
  1. Take \(z = xy\). If \(x\) becomes \(x + \Delta x\) and \(y\) becomes \(y + \Delta y\), then \(z + \Delta z = (x+\Delta x)(y+\Delta y) = xy + x\Delta y + y\Delta x + \Delta x\Delta y\).
  2. Subtract \(z = xy\): \(\Delta z = x\Delta y + y\Delta x + \Delta x\Delta y\).
  3. The last term is the product of two small quantities, so it is negligible for small uncertainties. Hence \(\Delta z \approx x\Delta y + y\Delta x\).
  4. Divide by \(z = xy\): \(\dfrac{\Delta z}{z} = \dfrac{\Delta y}{y} + \dfrac{\Delta x}{x}\). So fractional uncertainties add.
  5. For a power, \(z = x^2\) is just the product rule with \(y = x\), giving \(\dfrac{\Delta z}{z} = 2\dfrac{\Delta x}{x}\). By the same argument \(z = x^n\) gives \(\dfrac{\Delta z}{z} = |n|\dfrac{\Delta x}{x}\), and \(z = \sqrt{x}\) (i.e. \(n = \tfrac12\)) gives half the fractional uncertainty.
  6. Note these are worst-case estimates: they assume all errors conspire against you. If the uncertainties are independent and random, adding them in quadrature (\(\sqrt{(\Delta x/x)^2 + (\Delta y/y)^2}\)) is statistically better — but the IB accepts and often expects simple addition.

What it means: the quantity with the largest percentage uncertainty dominates, and squaring a quantity doubles its percentage uncertainty. When it is wrong: for subtraction of two nearly equal numbers, where the absolute uncertainties add and the fractional uncertainty explodes — use absolute uncertainties there.

Derivation Why the gradient uncertainty propagates as the same percentage
  1. In the pendulum experiment \(g = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{m}\) where \(m\) is the gradient. \(4\pi^2\) is exact, so \(g \propto m^{-1}\).
  2. Using the power rule with \(n = -1\): \(\dfrac{\Delta g}{g} = |-1|\dfrac{\Delta m}{m} = \dfrac{\Delta m}{m}\).
  3. So the percentage uncertainty in \(g\) equals the percentage uncertainty in the gradient — no factor of 2, no square root.
  4. Had the relationship been \(g \propto m^2\) you would double it; had it been \(g \propto \sqrt m\) you would halve it. Always check the power before propagating.

What it means: improving your gradient by a factor of 2 improves your final constant by a factor of 2 (for a \(-1\) power). Use it in the evaluation: "reducing the timing uncertainty by half would halve the uncertainty in \(g\), from 6.2% to 3.1%."

Linking question

The skills here link to every theme at once. A pendulum IA is A.1 kinematics and C.1 SHM; a resistivity IA is B.5 circuits and D.2 fields; a spectroscopy IA is E.1 energy levels and C.3 diffraction gratings. The reason the IB weights the IA at 20% is precisely that it forces you to run a whole theme's worth of physics end-to-end — and the same linearising-and-error-bars skill is what Paper 1B's data-based questions test under time pressure.

How to use it — the general maximum–minimum method (works for everything)
  1. Calculate the quantity using the best values.
  2. Calculate it again using the most unfavourable combination of \(+\Delta\) and \(-\Delta\).
  3. The difference is your absolute uncertainty.
  4. This is slower than the rules but always defensible, and it is essential for trigonometric and complicated functions where the simple rules fail.
Worked example 3 5 marks · combining uncertainties

Question. A cylinder has diameter \(d = (24.0 \pm 0.2)\) mm and length \(L = (150 \pm 1)\) mm. Determine the volume and its percentage uncertainty.

  1. \(V = \dfrac{\pi d^2L}{4} = \dfrac{\pi(0.0240)^2(0.150)}{4} = \dfrac{\pi(5.76\times10^{-4})(0.150)}{4} = 6.79\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^3\).
  2. Percentage uncertainty in \(d\): \(\dfrac{0.2}{24.0}\times100\% = 0.83\%\). Since \(d\) is squared: \(2\times0.83 = 1.67\%\).
  3. Percentage uncertainty in \(L\): \(\dfrac{1}{150}\times100\% = 0.67\%\).
  4. Total: \(1.67 + 0.67 = 2.34\%\). So \(\Delta V = 0.0234\times6.79\times10^{-5} = 1.6\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3\).
Answer: \(V = (6.79 \pm 0.16)\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^3\) (2.3%). The diameter dominates because it is squared — so if you want a better volume, measure the diameter more carefully, not the length. That sentence is exactly the kind of targeted improvement the evaluation criterion rewards.
Worked example 4 6 marks · uncertainty in a logarithm

Question. In a radioactive decay experiment, a corrected count rate is \(N = 240 \pm 16\) counts per minute. Determine the value and absolute uncertainty of \(\ln N\).

  1. \(\ln 240 = 5.481\).
  2. Fractional uncertainty in \(N\): \(\dfrac{16}{240} = 0.0667\).
  3. Absolute uncertainty in \(\ln N\): \(\Delta(\ln N) = \dfrac{\Delta N}{N} = 0.0667\).
  4. So \(\ln N = 5.481 \pm 0.067\).
Answer: \(5.48 \pm 0.07\). Notice that the error bars on a \(\ln N\) plot are not all the same size — they grow as \(N\) falls, because \(\Delta N/N\) grows. This is a subtle point that impresses examiners and very few students mention.

Error bars and what they tell you

How to use it — drawing and reading error bars
  1. Draw a bar from \(x - \Delta x\) to \(x + \Delta x\) and similarly for \(y\), making a cross.
  2. The best-fit line must pass through every error bar (or, more precisely, through almost all of them). If it cannot, either your uncertainties are underestimated or the relationship is not the one you assumed.
  3. To get the uncertainty in the gradient: draw the best-fit line, then draw the steepest and shallowest lines that still pass through all the error bars. The uncertainty is half the difference between those two gradients.
  4. Do not draw the max/min lines through the origin unless theory says the intercept is zero.

Graphs and data analysis

Choosing axes and linearising

How to use it — the linearising recipe
  1. Write the relationship with the dependent quantity (the one you measured as the outcome) alone on the left.
  2. Identify the independent variable (the one you deliberately changed) and get it to power 1 on the right.
  3. Whatever operation is applied to it, apply to the whole axis: if the right side has \(l\), plot against \(l\); if it has \(1/R\), plot against \(1/R\); if it has \(T^2\), plot \(T^2\).
  4. Whatever multiplies it is the gradient; anything added is the intercept.
  5. Extract the physical constant from the gradient, and check the units of the gradient match.

Common trap

Two. (1) Putting the independent variable on the vertical axis "because it looks better" — the independent variable always goes on the horizontal axis (x), the dependent on the vertical (y). (2) Forcing the line through the origin when there is a real physical intercept. A non-zero intercept in a \(V\)–\(I\) graph is the emf; a non-zero intercept in a \(T^2\)–\(l\) graph is the end effect. Forcing it through zero throws away information you should be discussing.

Worked example 5 6 marks · gradient with uncertainty

Question. A student plots \(T^2\) against \(l\) for a pendulum and obtains a best-fit gradient of \(4.05\ \text{s}^2\text{m}^{-1}\). The steepest and shallowest acceptable lines have gradients 4.30 and 3.80. Determine \(g\) and its uncertainty.

  1. \(g = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{\text{gradient}} = \dfrac{39.48}{4.05} = 9.75\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).
  2. Uncertainty in gradient: \(\Delta m = \dfrac{4.30-3.80}{2} = 0.25\ \text{s}^2\text{m}^{-1}\); percentage uncertainty \(= \dfrac{0.25}{4.05}\times100\% = 6.2\%\).
  3. Since \(g \propto 1/\text{gradient}\), the percentage uncertainty in \(g\) is the same, 6.2%.
  4. \(\Delta g = 0.062\times9.75 = 0.60\ \text{m s}^{-2}\), so \(g = 9.8 \pm 0.6\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).
Answer: \(g = 9.8 \pm 0.6\ \text{m s}^{-2}\). The accepted value 9.81 lies inside this range, so the result agrees — state that explicitly. Note the percentage uncertainty in \(g\) equals that in the gradient because \(g\) is inversely proportional to it (a power of −1, so \(|n| = 1\)).

Question. The following data are obtained for a cell.

Exam technique — data-based questions in Paper 1B

Paper 1B gives you a dataset you have never seen. The routine is identical: read the axes and units, linearise if needed, find the gradient with its units, and interpret it. Two habits win marks: (1) use a large triangle for the gradient — at least half the line length; (2) quote the gradient with its units before you use it, because the follow-up question is usually "hence determine…" and the units will guide you.

The collaborative sciences project

10 hours, shared across your science subjects, not individually assessed in the physics grade — but it is 10 of the 60 prescribed hours, and it is the cheapest place to learn the skills the IA is marked on.

How to use it — get IA value out of the CSP
  1. Volunteer for the role that forces you to handle data: data logging, spreadsheet processing, or uncertainty analysis.
  2. Practise the exact habits the IA rewards: a variables table, repeats, uncertainty columns, error bars, a linearised graph.
  3. Keep your own record. If your project involves physics (renewable energy, water quality, materials testing), it may seed an IA research question — but note that the IA must be your own independent work.
  4. Use the interdisciplinary angle: the CSP's whole point is that one phenomenon can be approached from physics, chemistry, biology and CS. Write down the physics mechanism explicitly — it is excellent practice for the linking questions in Paper 2.

The scientific investigation (IA)

10 hours, 24 marks, 20% of your final grade. Four criteria, each marked out of 6:

CriterionMarksWhat it really asks
Research design6Is the question focused and answerable? Are the variables properly identified and controlled? Is the method detailed enough to repeat? Is the chosen range and instrument justified?
Data analysis6Is the data sufficient and correctly recorded with uncertainties? Is it processed correctly (including propagation)? Is the graph correct, with error bars and a best-fit line?
Conclusion6Does the conclusion follow from the processed data? Is it compared quantitatively with a literature value? Is the physics explained, not just asserted?
Evaluation6Are the limitations specific and quantified? Is the single biggest source of error identified with its size? Are the improvements concrete and realistic?

Choosing a research question you can actually answer in 10 hours

How to use it — the five tests for a good RQ
  1. It must be a relationship, not a fact. "How does the period of a pendulum depend on its length?" is good. "What is \(g\)?" is weak — it becomes a one-number answer with nothing to discuss.
  2. You must be able to get at least 6–8 data points across a wide enough range for a meaningful graph.
  3. The apparatus must exist in your school or be cheap and safe to obtain. Do not design a vacuum chamber.
  4. There must be a literature value to compare against — otherwise your conclusion criterion has nothing to bite on.
  5. Something must go interestingly wrong so you have a real systematic error to discuss. The best IAs have a genuine puzzle: a non-zero intercept, a small curvature, an unexpected dependence.
Common trap

Choosing an RQ that is too ambitious ("determine the universal gravitational constant \(G\)") or too trivial ("does the mass of a cart affect its acceleration down a slope"). The sweet spot is a standard relationship investigated with unusual care, where the value of your work comes from the rigour of the uncertainty analysis and the quality of the evaluation — not from novelty.

The hypothesis and the variables table

A good hypothesis states the expected relationship and the reason. Not "the period will increase with length" but:

"Theory predicts \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\), so a graph of \(T^2\) against \(l\) should be a straight line through the origin with gradient \(4\pi^2/g\). Using \(g = 9.81\ \text{m s}^{-2}\), the expected gradient is \(4.02\ \text{s}^2\text{m}^{-1}\). The measured gradient is expected to be slightly larger because the string will stretch under the bob's weight, reducing the effective length." Exam technique — the sentence that earns the research-design marks

For every controlled variable, write one sentence justifying the control: "The amplitude was kept below 10° so that the small-angle approximation \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) remains valid, since at 10° the error in this approximation is about 0.5%." That is a justified decision, and justified decisions are what the criterion rewards. Lists of variables without reasons score in the bottom band.

Apparatus and method detail

The test for the method section is simple: could a competent classmate reproduce your experiment exactly from what you wrote, without asking you anything? That means:

  • A labelled diagram or photograph of the actual setup (not a generic textbook picture).
  • Apparatus with ranges and precisions: "metre rule, ±1 mm", "digital stopwatch, ±0.01 s", "micrometer, ±0.01 mm".
  • The exact range and number of values of the independent variable, and why that range.
  • The number of repeats, and why.
  • Any safety or ethical points (lasers, heavy suspended masses, radioactive sources if your school permits sealed sources).

Common trap

Presenting raw data only, processing nothing, and expecting the analysis criterion to be satisfied. The table must show processed columns (means, derived quantities) and their propagated uncertainties. Show one worked example of the propagation in words beneath the table — "for the first row, \(\Delta(T^2)/T^2 = 2\Delta T/T = 2(0.003)/0.897 = 0.67\%\)" — then state that the rest were done the same way.

Processing and graphing

  1. Plot the linearised graph with sensible scales (use most of the page) and labelled axes with units.
  2. Add error bars to every point.
  3. Draw the best-fit line (or use software, but state that you used it and what method).
  4. Draw steepest/shallowest lines; quote gradient ± uncertainty with units.
  5. Extract the physical constant and propagate the uncertainty.

Conclusion: comparison to literature values

How to use it — the four-sentence conclusion that scores 6/6
  1. State the relationship you found, in words and as an equation with numbers. "\(T^2\) is directly proportional to \(l\): \(T^2 = (4.05\ \text{s}^2\text{m}^{-1})\,l - 0.01\ \text{s}^2\)."
  2. Give the physical constant with uncertainty. "Hence \(g = 9.75 \pm 0.60\ \text{m s}^{-2}\)."
  3. Compare quantitatively with the accepted value. "The percentage difference from 9.81 m s⁻² is 0.6%, which is far smaller than the 6.2% uncertainty, so my result agrees with the accepted value within experimental uncertainty."
  4. Interpret the intercept / any deviation physically. "The small negative intercept of −0.01 s² is within its own uncertainty of ±0.02 s², so it is not significant; had it been significant and negative it would suggest the measured lengths were systematically too large."

Evaluation: specific, quantified, actionable

What scores 0

"Human error." "The equipment wasn't very accurate." "I could use better apparatus." "Parallax error." These are unnamed, unquantified, and unactionable.

What scores 6

"The largest single contribution to the uncertainty in \(T\) is the reaction time in starting and stopping the stopwatch. Across 20 oscillations the spread of my three repeats was up to 0.16 s, i.e. ±0.08 s, which after dividing by 20 gives ±0.004 s on each period — a 0.4% uncertainty that dominates the 0.2% uncertainty in \(l\). Using a light gate at the equilibrium position would reduce this to under 0.05%, making the uncertainty in \(l\) the limiting factor instead."

How to use it — the evaluation template
  1. Name the error precisely ("reaction time in starting/stopping the stopwatch", not "human error").
  2. Say whether it is random or systematic, and which quantity it affects.
  3. Quantify it — a number with units, and a percentage of the measured value.
  4. Compare it with your other uncertainties and declare the single largest, with the numbers to justify the claim.
  5. Propose a specific improvement and state how much it would help (a number, an instrument, a technique).
  6. Identify at least one genuine systematic error separately, and explain what it would do to your graph (intercept? curvature? shift in gradient?).

Significant figures: the rules, and why they are a claim

Significant figures are not a formatting chore. Every digit you write down is a claim about how well you know something, and writing too many digits is a small lie: it asserts a precision your apparatus never had.

Counting significant figures

RuleExampleSignificant figures
All non-zero digits are significant4.873
Zeros between non-zero digits are significant4.073
Leading zeros are never significant0.00412
Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant4.103
Trailing zeros in a whole number are ambiguous — use standard form1500 → \(1.5\times10^3\)2
Exact numbers and defined constants have unlimited sf2 in \(C = 2\pi r\); 60 s in a minuteunlimited

The two calculation rules

Multiplying and dividing: quote the answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise input. So \(4.2 \times 1.63 = 6.846\), but 4.2 has only 2 sf, so the answer is \(6.8\).

Adding and subtracting: the rule is about decimal places, not significant figures. Quote the answer to the same number of decimal places as the least precise input. So \(12.4 + 0.83 = 13.23\) becomes \(13.2\), because 12.4 is known only to one decimal place. Notice the contrast: subtraction can destroy significant figures even when it preserves decimal places. If you subtract \(9.83 - 9.81 = 0.02\), you started with three sf and ended with one — and any uncertainty carried through is now a huge fraction of the result. That is the hidden danger in experiments that measure a small difference between two large readings.

Trap

Round once, at the end. If you round intermediate values and then continue calculating, the rounding errors accumulate and your final answer can be wrong in the last digit — or worse. Keep two or three "guard digits" in your calculator working and only round when you write the final answer.

Quoting a value with its uncertainty

The convention the IB expects is simple and worth memorising:

  1. Round the uncertainty to one significant figure. (Use two if the first digit is a 1 or 2, since rounding 0.14 to 0.1 throws away a lot — this is a widely accepted exception.)
  2. Round the value to the same decimal place as the uncertainty.
  3. State the units once, at the end.

So \(9.8137 \pm 0.2461\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) becomes \(9.8 \pm 0.2\ \text{m s}^{-2}\), and \(2.437 \pm 0.0182\ \text{s}\) becomes \(2.44 \pm 0.02\ \text{s}\). Writing \(9.8137 \pm 0.2\) is self-contradictory: you are claiming to know the fourth decimal while admitting you do not know the first.

Worked example 7 IB-style · 6 marks · Significant figures through a calculation

Question. A trolley of mass \((0.482 \pm 0.001)\ \text{kg}\) travels \((1.20 \pm 0.01)\ \text{m}\) in \((2.4 \pm 0.1)\ \text{s}\) from rest with constant acceleration. (a) Determine its acceleration, quoted correctly. (b) Determine the net force on it, quoted correctly.

  1. (a) \(s = \tfrac12 at^2\), so \(a = 2s/t^2 = 2(1.20)/(2.4)^2 = 2.40/5.76 = 0.41666\ldots\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).
  2. The inputs have 3 sf (\(s\)), 2 sf (\(t\)) and 4 sf (\(m\)); the limiting one is \(t\) at 2 sf, so the acceleration is \(0.42\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).
  3. Uncertainty in \(a\): fractional uncertainty in \(s\) is \(0.01/1.20 = 0.83\%\); in \(t\) it is \(0.1/2.4 = 4.2\%\), and \(t\) is squared so that contributes \(8.3\%\). Total \(9.2\%\), so \(\Delta a = 0.092 \times 0.417 = 0.038\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).
  4. Round the uncertainty to one sf: \(0.04\ \text{m s}^{-2}\). Match the decimal place: \(a = 0.42 \pm 0.04\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).
  5. (b) \(F = ma = 0.482 \times 0.4167 = 0.2008\ \text{N}\). Fractional uncertainty in \(m\) is \(0.001/0.482 = 0.21\%\), negligible next to the \(9.2\%\) in \(a\), so the total is about \(9.4\%\) and \(\Delta F = 0.019\ \text{N}\).
  6. Rounding: \(F = 0.20 \pm 0.02\ \text{N}\). The time measurement dominates the entire experiment — which is exactly the conclusion your evaluation should reach.
Answer: (a) \(0.42 \pm 0.04\ \text{m s}^{-2}\); (b) \(0.20 \pm 0.02\ \text{N}\). Timing is the limiting measurement.
Exam technique

Examiners rarely award a mark for significant figures alone, but they routinely withhold the final accuracy mark if an answer is given to an absurd number of digits or to fewer digits than the data supports. A safe habit that takes seconds: after every numerical answer, glance at the data and ask whether your last digit is meaningful. Three sf is almost always safe; two is right when the weakest input has two.

Error propagation through a whole investigation

The uncertainty rules are individually simple; the skill is chaining them through a real multi-step calculation and then saying which term matters most. Here is a full example, because this is precisely the reasoning your IA analysis needs.

Worked example 8 HL · 10 marks · Young modulus with full error propagation

Question. A copper wire of length \(L = (2.000 \pm 0.001)\ \text{m}\) and diameter \(d = (0.40 \pm 0.01)\ \text{mm}\) is loaded with a mass \(M = (1.00 \pm 0.01)\ \text{kg}\), and its extension is measured as \(\Delta L = (0.90 \pm 0.05)\ \text{mm}\). (a) Determine the Young modulus. (b) Determine the percentage uncertainty in the result. (c) State which measurement should be improved first, and why.

  1. Formula. \(E = \dfrac{FL}{A\,\Delta L} = \dfrac{4MgL}{\pi d^2 \Delta L}\).
  2. Substitute. \(F = 1.00 \times 9.81 = 9.81\ \text{N}\); \(A = \pi(0.40\times10^{-3})^2/4 = 1.257\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2\). \(E = \dfrac{9.81 \times 2.000}{1.257\times10^{-7} \times 0.90\times10^{-3}} = \dfrac{19.62}{1.131\times10^{-10}} = 1.73\times10^{11}\ \text{Pa}\).
  3. Fractional uncertainties. \(M\): \(0.01/1.00 = 1\%\). \(L\): \(0.001/2.000 = 0.05\%\). \(d\): \(0.01/0.40 = 2.5\%\), and \(d\) is squared, so \(5\%\). \(\Delta L\): \(0.05/0.90 = 5.6\%\). (The constants 4, \(\pi\) and \(g\) are exact or known far better than this.)
  4. Combine for a product/quotient: add the fractional uncertainties in quadrature if they are independent random uncertainties: \(\sqrt{1^2 + 0.05^2 + 5^2 + 5.6^2} = \sqrt{1 + 0.0025 + 25 + 31.4} = \sqrt{57.4} = 7.6\%\). (If you use the simpler worst-case rule of adding them, you get 11.7% — quote whichever method you are using, and be consistent.)
  5. Absolute uncertainty: \(0.076 \times 1.73\times10^{11} = 1.3\times10^{10}\ \text{Pa}\). So \(E = (1.7 \pm 0.1)\times10^{11}\ \text{Pa}\).
  6. (c) What to improve. The extension dominates at 5.6%, and the diameter is close behind at 5% of the result from a 2.5% measurement, because it is squared. Improving the length or the mass would be pointless — together they contribute under 1%. The right recommendation: use a longer wire (a larger \(\Delta L\) for the same load cuts the fractional uncertainty in proportion) and measure the diameter with a micrometer reading to 0.01 mm at several points, or use a thicker wire.
Answer: (a) \(1.7\times10^{11}\ \text{Pa}\); (b) about 8% (or 12% worst case); (c) improve the extension and the diameter — they contribute over 95% of the uncertainty.
Why this analysis is the whole point

Notice what the propagation told us: two of the four measurements barely matter at all. Without doing the propagation you would not know that, and you would waste your improvement budget on the wrong apparatus. A strong IA evaluation does not list every possible error — it ranks them, with numbers, and then addresses the top one. That ranking is worth marks in a way that a list never is.

Getting the uncertainty in the gradient

Draw the best-fit line. Then draw the steepest acceptable line and the shallowest acceptable line — the extremes that still pass through (or near) most of the error bars. The uncertainty in the gradient is half the difference between their gradients:

\[\Delta(\text{gradient}) = \frac{|\text{gradient}_{\text{steep}} - \text{gradient}_{\text{shallow}}|}{2}\]

Always compute gradients from a large triangle drawn on the line itself, never from two data points, and write the coordinates on the graph. Using two adjacent plotted points is the most common cause of a gradient that is out by a factor of two.

How to use the graph rather than individual values
  1. Plot all your data, with error bars.
  2. Draw the best-fit line and read the gradient, using a triangle at least half the length of the line.
  3. Compare the gradient with the physics: if the relationship predicts a gradient of \(4\pi^2/g\), say so and extract \(g\).
  4. Look at the intercept. A non-zero intercept when theory predicts zero is a systematic error, and it is often more informative than the gradient — a positive intercept on a length axis means you under-measured every length by a constant amount.
  5. Quote the gradient, its uncertainty, and the intercept, and then interpret all three.
Vocabulary — data handling

significant figures — the digits that carry meaning about precision. · guard digit — an extra digit kept during working to avoid rounding error. · absolute / fractional / percentage uncertainty — the uncertainty in units, as a fraction, and as a percentage. · quadrature — combining independent uncertainties as the square root of the sum of squares. · line of best fit — the single line that best represents the trend. · outlier — a data point far from the trend, which must be explained rather than discarded. · intercept — where the line crosses an axis; often reveals a systematic error.

The four criteria, level by level

Each criterion is marked out of 6, and the marks come in bands: roughly, 1–2 for an attempt, 3–4 for a competent but incomplete treatment, and 5–6 for work that is complete, quantitative and reflective. Knowing what separates the bands is worth more than any amount of extra data.

Trap

A limitation is not the same as an error. A limitation is something your design cannot overcome — you cannot measure the bob's centre of mass to better than a millimetre with a metre rule, and no amount of repetition helps. An error is a specific deviation with a mechanism: reaction time in starting the stopwatch, a parallax error in reading the rule, a zero offset on the ammeter. Evaluations that only list limitations miss the quantified-error marks; evaluations that only list "mistakes" miss the design-reflection marks. You need both, and you need to say which is which.

Writing a conclusion and evaluation that score

A five-sentence conclusion that always works

When you are stuck, write these five sentences in this order. Each one maps to something the criterion asks for.

  1. State the relationship quantitatively. "The data show that \(T^2\) is directly proportional to \(l\), with a best-fit gradient of \(4.10\ \text{s}^2\ \text{m}^{-1} \pm 0.06\)."
  2. Convert it into the physics. "Since \(T^2 = (4\pi^2/g)l\), this gives \(g = 9.63 \pm 0.14\ \text{m s}^{-2}\)."
  3. Compare with the accepted value. "The accepted value is 9.81 m s⁻², a percentage difference of 1.8%."
  4. Judge the agreement. "The percentage uncertainty is 1.5%, so the accepted value lies just outside my uncertainty range and the result is marginally lower than expected."
  5. Explain the physics. "The most likely cause is that the amplitude was not kept small: the isochronous approximation \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) fails above about 10°, which lengthens the period and therefore reduces the calculated value of \(g\) — consistent with the direction of the discrepancy."

Sentence 5 is the one most students omit, and it is the one that separates the top band from the middle. A conclusion that states a number is a calculation; a conclusion that explains why the number came out as it did is physics.

Worked example 9 IA-style · 6 marks · Rewriting a conclusion

Question. Here is a student's conclusion: "The experiment worked well. My value of the spring constant was 21.4 N/m, which is close to the true value of 20 N/m. The graph was a straight line, showing that Hooke's law is correct." Rewrite it to score in the top band, given that the uncertainty in the gradient was 4%.

  1. Quantify the comparison. Percentage difference \(= |21.4 - 20.0|/20.0 \times 100\% = 7.0\%\).
  2. Compare with the uncertainty. 7.0% > 4%, so the accepted value lies outside the uncertainty range — the result does not agree within experimental uncertainty, despite looking "close".
  3. Rewrite. "The extension was proportional to the load up to about 6 N, giving \(k = 21.4 \pm 0.9\ \text{N m}^{-1}\) from the gradient of the linear region. This is 7.0% above the manufacturer's value of 20.0 N m⁻¹, which is larger than my 4% uncertainty, so the discrepancy is significant and cannot be attributed to random error. A systematic cause is available: the ruler was clamped so that its zero was 2 mm below the spring's unloaded end, making every extension 2 mm too large. Correcting for this reduces the gradient by about 4%, bringing the result to roughly 20.6 N m⁻¹ and within the uncertainty of the accepted value. Beyond 6 N the data curve away from the line, indicating that the elastic limit was being approached — a limitation of the model rather than of the measurements."
  4. Note what the rewrite adds: a number for the comparison, a judgement about significance, a named systematic cause with a size, a corrected value, and a comment on where the model stops applying.
Answer: A 7.0% difference against a 4% uncertainty is significant; a 2 mm zero error explains most of it, and the deviation above 6 N marks the elastic limit.

The four-part structure for every error you discuss

Every error in your evaluation should follow the same four beats. This structure is what "specific, quantified, actionable" actually means:

  1. Identify the mechanism. Not "human error" but "the reaction time in starting and stopping the stopwatch at the extremes of the swing, where the bob is momentarily stationary and the instant is poorly defined".
  2. Quantify it. "Across five repeats the total time for 20 oscillations spanned 0.16 s, so the uncertainty in the period is ±0.004 s, a fractional uncertainty of 0.4%."
  3. Say what it does to the result. "This propagates into a 0.8% uncertainty in \(g\), since \(g \propto 1/T^2\), making it the dominant random contribution."
  4. Fix it, with the expected magnitude. What a strong evaluation says about the conclusion

The final paragraph should return to the research question and state how much confidence the data support. Three levels of claim are available, and choosing the right one is a mark in itself:

  • Strong agreement: the percentage difference is smaller than the percentage uncertainty, so the accepted value lies inside the error bars. You may say the hypothesis is supported and the accepted value is confirmed within experimental uncertainty.
  • Marginal: the difference is roughly equal to the uncertainty. Say the result is consistent with the accepted value but not precise enough to confirm it, and identify what would need to improve.
  • Disagreement: the difference exceeds the uncertainty. There is evidence of a systematic error; you must propose a specific mechanism, with a size that accounts for at least most of the gap, and ideally check it against something else in your data — an intercept, a curvature, a trend in the residuals.

Notice that the third case is not a disaster. An IA that finds a 6% discrepancy, explains it with a quantified systematic effect, and confirms the explanation from the intercept is a better investigation than one that gets lucky and has nothing to say. Examiners reward the analysis, not the luck.

Extension — descriptive statistics for repeated measurements

Most school investigations quote a mean and a range. That is usually enough, but knowing the two statistics beyond them lets you choose the right uncertainty for a repeated measurement — and tells you when repeating is pointless.

Mean, spread, standard deviation, standard error

Take \(n\) repeated measurements \(x_i\). The mean is

\[\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\]

The standard deviation measures how widely the individual readings scatter about the mean:

\[s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n-1}}\]

The standard error of the mean measures how well you know the mean itself:

\[\text{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\]

The distinction matters enormously and is rarely made in school write-ups. The standard deviation describes your readings: about two-thirds of them should lie within one \(s\) of the mean. The standard error describes your result: it is the uncertainty you should quote on the mean. Crucially, the standard error falls as \(1/\sqrt{n}\), so to halve your uncertainty you need four times as many repeats — a fact worth knowing before you plan to take fifty readings.

Trap

Do not quote the standard deviation as the uncertainty in the mean. If your ten readings of a period have \(s = 0.03\ \text{s}\), the uncertainty in the mean is \(0.03/\sqrt{10} = 0.009\ \text{s}\), three times smaller. Quoting \(s\) overstates your uncertainty (which is safe but pessimistic); quoting \(s/\sqrt{n/2}\) or dividing by the wrong thing is just wrong. State which one you are using — "the standard error of the mean of five readings" — and the moderator can follow you.

Three ways to get an uncertainty, and when each is right

MethodHowUse when
Instrument resolution± half the smallest division (or ±1 division for a digital display, conservatively)You take a single reading — the commonest case
Half-range of repeats\(\pm\frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}\)You have 3–5 repeats; quick, conservative and perfectly acceptable at this level
Standard error of the mean\(s/\sqrt{n}\)You have six or more repeats and want a defensible random uncertainty

Whichever you use, state it once in the write-up — "the uncertainty in each length is taken as half the smallest division of the metre rule, ±0.5 mm" — and then apply it consistently. And remember the resolution floor: no amount of repetition reduces an uncertainty below the instrument's resolution, because every reading carries it. Once the standard error of the mean drops below the resolution uncertainty, repeating is genuinely pointless.

Worked example 10 IA-style · 7 marks · Statistics from repeats

Question. Six measurements of the period of a pendulum, in seconds, are: 1.42, 1.39, 1.44, 1.40, 1.43, 1.41. (a) Determine the mean, the standard deviation and the standard error. (b) State how you would quote the result. (c) The stopwatch reads to 0.01 s and your reaction time is about 0.15 s. Comment on whether more repeats would help.

  1. (a) Mean: \(\bar{T} = (1.42+1.39+1.44+1.40+1.43+1.41)/6 = 8.49/6 = 1.415\ \text{s}\).
  2. Deviations: +0.005, −0.025, +0.025, −0.015, +0.015, −0.005. Squares: \(2.5\times10^{-5}\), \(6.25\times10^{-4}\), \(6.25\times10^{-4}\), \(2.25\times10^{-4}\), \(2.25\times10^{-4}\), \(2.5\times10^{-5}\). Sum \(= 1.75\times10^{-3}\).
  3. Divide by \(n-1 = 5\): \(3.5\times10^{-4}\). Square root: \(s = 0.0187\ \text{s}\).
  4. Standard error: \(0.0187/\sqrt{6} = 0.0076\ \text{s}\).
  5. (b) Quote: round the uncertainty to one significant figure, 0.008 s, and match the decimal place: \(T = 1.415 \pm 0.008\ \text{s}\). (Two significant figures in the uncertainty is also acceptable here, since the first digit is small.)
  6. (c) Comment. The scatter between repeats, \(s \approx 0.019\ \text{s}\), is far smaller than the 0.15 s reaction time you would expect for a single manual timing — so these are almost certainly timings over many oscillations divided down, not single swings. The standard error is already 0.008 s, well below the 0.01 s resolution of the stopwatch. Taking more repeats would therefore gain nothing: the measurement is limited by resolution and by the timing method, not by random scatter. The next improvement is a light gate, not more data.
Answer: (a) mean 1.415 s, \(s = 0.019\ \text{s}\), SE \(= 0.008\ \text{s}\); (b) \(1.415 \pm 0.008\ \text{s}\); (c) more repeats would not help — resolution now limits the result.

When to discard a data point

Almost never. A point that lies far from the trend is the most interesting thing in your data, and the criterion rewards an explanation far more than a deletion. The honest procedure is:

  1. Plot it, circle it, and keep it in the table.
  2. Ask whether it has an identifiable cause — a slip in reading, a disturbance, a change in conditions. If you can identify a cause, say so, and you may then justify excluding it while still showing it in the raw data.
  3. If you cannot identify a cause, keep it in the analysis and comment on its effect: does including it change the gradient by more than the uncertainty? If not, it does not matter and you can say so.
  4. Never delete a point simply because it spoils a straight line. That is not analysis; it is fabrication, and moderators are trained to spot a suspiciously clean dataset.
Vocabulary — statistics

mean — the arithmetic average. · range — largest minus smallest. · standard deviation — the spread of individual readings about the mean. · standard error of the mean — the uncertainty in the mean, \(s/\sqrt{n}\). · resolution — the smallest change an instrument can display. · repeat / replicate — one of several measurements of the same quantity. · anomaly — a reading inconsistent with the rest, which must be explained.

Extension — one complete analysis, start to finish

The best way to make all of this stick is to watch one small dataset go the whole way: raw readings, processed values, uncertainties, a graph, a gradient, a result, and a comparison. Here it is, in full.

Worked example 11 IA-style · 12 marks · Malus's law, from raw data to conclusion

Question. A student directs a beam of plane-polarised light through an analyser and records the transmitted intensity \(I\) for several angles \(\theta\) between the analyser axis and the plane of polarisation:

\(\theta\) / °020406080
\(I\) / W m⁻²1008959253

Test whether the data support \(I = I_0\cos^2\theta\), determine \(I_0\), and comment on the result.

  1. Linearise. The relationship is not linear in \(\theta\), so do not plot \(I\) against \(\theta\) and try to fit a straight line. \(I = I_0\cos^2\theta\) is linear in \(\cos^2\theta\), so plot \(I\) against \(\cos^2\theta\). Compute the second column: \(\cos^2 0^\circ = 1.000\), \(\cos^2 20^\circ = 0.883\), \(\cos^2 40^\circ = 0.587\), \(\cos^2 60^\circ = 0.250\), \(\cos^2 80^\circ = 0.030\).
  2. Table with uncertainties. The intensity meter reads to 1 W m⁻², so \(\Delta I = \pm 1\). The uncertainty in \(\cos^2\theta\) comes from the angle: the protractor scale is readable to ±1°, and near 80° the function changes fastest, so that point carries the largest horizontal error bar. Recording this shows you have thought about where the uncertainty actually is.
  3. Plot. \(I\) on the vertical axis, \(\cos^2\theta\) on the horizontal, error bars on the intensity. The points lie close to a straight line through the origin, which already supports the relationship.
  4. Gradient. Using the endpoints of the best-fit line: \((1.000 - 0.030)\) horizontally and \((99 - 4)\) vertically gives a gradient of \(95/0.970 = 98\ \text{W m}^{-2}\). Since the theory predicts the line passes through the origin and the intercept is consistent with zero (±2), forcing through the origin is legitimate here — and saying so explicitly is the mark.
  5. Uncertainty. Steepest and shallowest acceptable lines give gradients of about 101 and 95, so \(\Delta(\text{gradient}) \approx 3\ \text{W m}^{-2}\). Hence \(I_0 = 98 \pm 3\ \text{W m}^{-2}\).
  6. Comparison. The measured intensity at \(\theta = 0^\circ\) was 100 W m⁻², so the percentage difference is \(2/100 = 2\%\), comfortably inside the 3% uncertainty. The data are consistent with Malus's law.
  7. Physics commentary. Two points are worth making. First, the point at 80° is the least reliable: \(\cos^2\theta\) changes by about 0.017 per degree there, so a 1° error shifts it by 6% of its value — a good illustration of why error bars grow where a function is steep. Second, the intercept being consistent with zero tells you there was no significant stray light reaching the sensor; a positive intercept would have indicated exactly that, and would have been the interesting systematic effect to discuss.
Answer: \(I\) against \(\cos^2\theta\) is straight through the origin with gradient \(98 \pm 3\ \text{W m}^{-2}\), consistent with the directly measured 100 W m⁻² — Malus's law is supported within experimental uncertainty.
The transferable checklist this example demonstrates
  1. Linearise before you plot — choose axes that make the theory predict a straight line.
  2. Put uncertainties in the table, and say where each came from.
  3. Draw error bars, and note where they are largest and why.
  4. Fit one line, then steepest and shallowest, and quote the gradient with its uncertainty.
  5. Interpret the intercept — it is where systematic error hides.
  6. Compare with an independent value using percentage difference against percentage uncertainty.
  7. Finish with the physics: what the result means, and what would be worth doing next.

Extension — using software without losing the marks

Spreadsheets and fitting tools are allowed, and they are genuinely useful. But they change what you must show, because the criteria reward demonstrated understanding rather than a printed output.

What software is good at

Propagating the same formula down fifty rows without arithmetic slips; plotting with error bars; doing a least-squares fit and reporting the standard error of the gradient and intercept; and letting you try several linearisations quickly to see which gives the straightest line.

What you must still show

One hand-worked example of every calculation the software did — including the uncertainty propagation. A graph with no explanation of how the error bars were obtained scores in the middle band, however pretty it is.

What a least-squares fit does not give you

Judgement. Software will happily fit a straight line to data that are curved, and it will include an outlier without complaint. You still have to look at the residuals: if they form a curve rather than random scatter, your model is wrong and the line is meaningless.

What to write in the write-up

Name the tool, state what you asked it to do, and note that you checked the result against your own hand-worked gradient from a large triangle. That single sentence demonstrates understanding and protects you if the two disagree.

Trap

A software fit reports a gradient with many decimal places and a "standard error" that only accounts for the scatter of the points — it knows nothing about your instrument resolution or your systematic errors. Quote the larger of the software's uncertainty and your own estimate from the steepest/shallowest construction, and say which you chose and why. Copying six decimal places straight from the output is the giveaway that the uncertainty was not thought about.

Two habits that separate a good write-up from a great one

Keep the raw data visible. Examiners and moderators want to see what you actually wrote down in the laboratory, with the number of digits your instrument gave, before any processing. A table of "processed" values that appears from nowhere cannot be checked, and it hides the very thing the data-analysis criterion asks you to demonstrate. The standard layout is a raw-data table first, then a processed table in which every derived column carries its own uncertainty column, then one worked sample calculation.

Comment as you go, not only at the end. The strongest write-ups have a sentence of interpretation under each graph and each table: what the shape tells you, where the scatter is largest, what the intercept implies, whether the trend survives to the ends of the range. This does two things. It shows the analysis is yours rather than the software's, and it means the conclusion can build on observations you have already made instead of repeating them from scratch. A paragraph that begins "As noted when the graph was plotted, the residuals curve upwards above 6 N…" is doing exactly the work the top band asks for.

Exam technique

The same two habits transfer directly to Paper 1B and Paper 2 data-based questions, which are worth a substantial share of the marks and are answered under time pressure. In those questions you are usually given a table and asked to process it, plot it and comment. Doing the school-version habit — uncertainty column, one worked propagation, error bars, best-fit line, comment on the intercept — is worth several marks every time, and it is faster than improvising because it is the same routine you have already practised on your IA.

Check yourself

Practice

  1. [3 marks] A student writes \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E_k}{m}}\) and obtains a speed in m s⁻¹. Show by dimensional analysis that the expression is consistent.

    Show answer

    \(E_k\) has dimensions kg m² s⁻²; \(m\) has kg. So \(\dfrac{2E_k}{m}\) has dimensions \(\dfrac{\text{kg m}^2\text{s}^{-2}}{\text{kg}} = \text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2}\). Taking the square root gives m s⁻¹, matching \(v\). ✓ Consistent.

  2. [4 marks] A resistance is \(R = (220 \pm 2)\ \Omega\) and the current through it is \(I = (0.50 \pm 0.01)\) A. Determine the power dissipated and its absolute uncertainty.

    Show answer

    \(P = I^2R = (0.50)^2(220) = 55\ \text{W}\).

    Percentage uncertainty in \(I\): \(0.01/0.50\times100\% = 2\%\); since \(I\) is squared, 4%.

    Percentage uncertainty in \(R\): \(2/220\times100\% = 0.91\%\).

    Total 4.91%, so \(\Delta P = 0.0491\times55 = 2.7\ \text{W}\). Thus \(P = 55 \pm 3\ \text{W}\). The current dominates because it is squared — measure the current more carefully.

  3. [4 marks] State the difference between a random and a systematic error, and give one example of each from a pendulum experiment.

    Show answer

    Random errors cause scatter in repeated measurements and can be reduced by averaging: e.g. the variation in the measured time for 20 oscillations due to reaction time.

    Systematic errors shift all measurements in the same direction and cannot be reduced by repeating: e.g. measuring the length to the top of the bob instead of to its centre of mass, which makes every length too small by the bob's radius and biases the gradient.

  4. [5 marks] For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, \(pV = \text{constant}\). State what you would plot to obtain a straight line, and what the gradient would be.

    Show answer

    Rewrite as \(p = \dfrac{\text{constant}}{V} = (\text{constant})\dfrac{1}{V}\). So plot \(p\) on the vertical axis against \(1/V\) on the horizontal axis. The gradient is the constant \(pV = nRT\), with units of J (Pa m³ = J).

    Alternatively plot \(p\) against \(V\) and get a curve — which is why linearising is necessary if you want a gradient and an uncertainty in it.

  5. [5 marks] A student measures \(g = 10.4\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) with a percentage uncertainty of 3%. Determine the percentage difference from 9.81 m s⁻² and state what the comparison tells you.

    Show answer

    Percentage difference \(= \dfrac{|10.4 - 9.81|}{9.81}\times100\% = \dfrac{0.59}{9.81}\times100\% = 6.0\%\).

    Since 6.0% > 3.0%, the accepted value lies outside the uncertainty range, so the result does not agree with the accepted value and there is evidence of a significant systematic error that must be identified — e.g. measuring the pendulum length to the wrong point, or using too large an amplitude.

  6. [6 marks] A student's evaluation says: "The experiment was affected by human error and the equipment was not accurate. To improve, I would use better equipment and take more readings." Rewrite this to score full marks.

    Show answer

    "The largest source of uncertainty was the reaction time in starting and stopping the stopwatch. Over 20 oscillations the three repeats spanned up to 0.16 s, giving an uncertainty of ±0.08 s in the total time and therefore ±0.004 s in each period — a fractional uncertainty of 0.4%, which exceeds the 0.2% uncertainty in the length. Replacing the stopwatch with a light gate at the equilibrium position, where the bob's speed is greatest, would reduce this to below 0.05%, leaving the measurement of length as the limiting factor. In addition, there is a systematic error: the length was measured to the top of the bob rather than to its centre of mass, making every length 1.1 cm too small; this shifts the best-fit line horizontally and produces a positive intercept on the \(l\)-axis, which is consistent with the observed intercept of +1.2 cm."

    Every element is named, quantified, and given a concrete improvement with an expected magnitude.

  7. [6 marks] A student measures the count rate from a source and obtains 320, 315, 325 counts per minute. With the source removed, 28, 32, 30 counts per minute are recorded. Determine the corrected count rate with its uncertainty, and explain why the correction matters.

    Show answer

    Mean with source \(= 320\); spread is ±5, so the uncertainty in the mean is about ±3 (or use \(\pm\frac{\text{range}}{2}\) conservatively).

    Mean background \(= 30\), spread ±2, uncertainty about ±1.

    Corrected rate \(= 320 - 30 = 290\) counts per minute, with absolute uncertainty \(3 + 1 = 4\) (add absolute uncertainties for a subtraction).

    So \(290 \pm 4\) counts per minute (1.4%). The correction matters because the background is 9% of the raw count — leaving it in would make the source appear significantly more active than it is and would distort any half-life calculated later.

  8. [7 marks] Design an investigation to determine the resistivity of a metal wire. Your answer should include the research question, the variables with justifications, the linearised graph, and one quantified improvement.

    Show answer

    RQ: How does the resistance of a constantan wire depend on its length?

    Variables. Independent: length \(L\) between the crocodile clips, from 0.20 m to 1.00 m in 0.10 m steps. Dependent: resistance \(R = V/I\) measured with an ammeter and voltmeter. Controlled: the same wire throughout (constant cross-sectional area and material); current kept below 0.5 A so the wire does not heat and change its resistivity; the wire straightened and taped to a metre rule so the length between clips is well defined; readings taken promptly and the circuit opened between them.

    Graph. \(R = \dfrac{\rho}{A}L\), so plot \(R\) against \(L\). Gradient \(= \rho/A\), and since \(A = \pi d^2/4\) with \(d\) measured by micrometer at six points along the wire, \(\rho = \text{gradient}\times A\).

    Improvement. The diameter dominates: \(A\) depends on \(d^2\), so a 1% uncertainty in \(d\) becomes 2% in \(A\) and hence in \(\rho\). Measuring \(d\) with a micrometer at ten points (rather than six) and taking the mean reduces the random component by roughly \(\sqrt{10/6} \approx 1.3\), bringing the contribution down to about 1.5%. A further improvement would be to use a four-terminal (Kelvin) connection to eliminate contact resistance, which is the cause of any non-zero intercept.

  9. [4 marks] Evaluate \(v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}\) for \(r = 0.150\ \text{m}\) and \(T = 0.42\ \text{s}\), quoting your answer to an appropriate number of significant figures. Explain your choice.

    Show answer

    \(v = 2\pi(0.150)/0.42 = 0.9425/0.42 = 2.244\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

    \(r\) has 3 sf and \(T\) has 2 sf, so for a multiplication/division the answer is limited to the smaller: 2 sf, giving \(v = 2.2\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

    Note that \(2\pi\) is exact, so it never limits the significant figures — a common error is to count the digits of \(\pi\) as data and then "round down" to match a calculator display of 3.14159.

  10. [5 marks] A length is recorded as \(L = 12.4\ \text{cm}\) and an extension as \(\Delta L = 0.28\ \text{cm}\). A student computes the strained length as \(12.68\ \text{cm}\). Explain what is wrong and give the correct value.

    Show answer

    The rule for addition is decimal places, not significant figures. \(L\) is known to one decimal place (0.1 cm), so the sum must also be quoted to one decimal place: \(12.4 + 0.28 = 12.68 \rightarrow 12.7\ \text{cm}\).

    Quoting 12.68 cm claims knowledge of the hundredth of a centimetre, which the measurement of \(L\) does not support. This is the most common significant-figure error in practical work, because students apply the multiplication rule to an addition.

  11. [6 marks] Describe how you would determine the uncertainty in the gradient of a straight-line graph from plotted data, and state two mistakes that would invalidate your answer.

    Show answer

    Plot the points with error bars and draw the best-fit line. Then draw the steepest line and the shallowest line that are still consistent with the error bars. Calculate the gradient of each using a large triangle drawn on the line (at least half its length), and take half the difference between the two gradients as the uncertainty in the gradient.

    Mistake 1: computing the gradients from two adjacent data points instead of from the fitted line — the scatter then dominates and the uncertainty is wildly overestimated.

    Mistake 2: forcing the line through the origin when the relationship does not require it. This biases the gradient, and it hides exactly the information a non-zero intercept would have given you about systematic error.

  12. [5 marks] Six repeated measurements of a time give a mean of 2.35 s and a standard deviation of 0.12 s. State, with reasoning, what uncertainty you would quote on the mean, and explain how many repeats you would need to halve it.

    Show answer

    The uncertainty in the mean is the standard error, \(s/\sqrt{n} = 0.12/\sqrt{6} = 0.049\ \text{s}\), so quote \(2.35 \pm 0.05\ \text{s}\). Quoting the standard deviation, 0.12 s, would describe the spread of individual readings rather than the precision of the mean.

    The standard error falls as \(1/\sqrt{n}\), so to halve it you need four times as many repeats: about 24. That is usually a poor use of laboratory time — a better instrument or a better method almost always beats brute repetition.

  13. [6 marks] A student writes: "My value for the specific heat capacity of aluminium was 980 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, and the data-booklet value is 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. The experiment was accurate because my answer was close." The percentage uncertainty in the result is 5%. Rewrite the statement as it should appear.

    Show answer

    "My value is \(980\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}\) with a percentage uncertainty of 5%, i.e. \(980 \pm 49\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}\). The accepted value is 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, a percentage difference of \(|980-900|/900 \times 100\% = 8.9\%\).

    Since 8.9% exceeds the 5% uncertainty, the accepted value lies outside my uncertainty range, so the result does not agree with the accepted value within experimental uncertainty — "close" is not the same as "consistent". The discrepancy is positive, meaning my measured temperature rise was too small or the energy supplied was underestimated. The most likely cause is heat lost to the surroundings while the block warmed, which reduces \(\Delta T\); the appropriate fix is better insulation plus a cooling correction obtained by extrapolating the post-heating temperature curve back to the mid-point of heating."

  14. [6 marks] For a fixed mass of ideal gas at constant pressure, \(V \propto T\). (a) State what you would plot to test this, what the gradient represents, and what you would expect the intercept to be. (b) Explain what a non-zero intercept would tell you, and name a likely cause.

    Show answer

    (a) Plot \(V\) on the vertical axis against \(T\) on the horizontal axis, with \(T\) in kelvin. The gradient is \(nR/p\), and the intercept on the \(T\)-axis should be at \(T = 0\ \text{K}\), i.e. the line should pass through the origin — this is the experimental basis of absolute zero.

    (b) A non-zero intercept indicates a systematic error in one of the two variables. If the intercept on the temperature axis is negative, the recorded temperatures are too high — for example if the thermometer was not calibrated, or if the gas was not allowed to reach thermal equilibrium with the water bath before each reading. If the volume intercept is positive, there may be a "dead volume" in the connecting tubing whose gas is not at the bath temperature. Either way the intercept is more informative than the gradient, because it points directly at a mechanism.
