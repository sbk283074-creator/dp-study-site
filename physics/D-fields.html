Physics HL · Theme D

D · Fields

Theme D asks: how do objects interact without touching? The answer the course builds is the field: instead of action at a distance, a mass or a charge alters the space around it, and a second object responds to the field at its own location. Once you accept that move, everything else follows — field lines, potentials, equipotential surfaces, orbital motion, the magnetic force on a moving charge, and finally induction, where a changing magnetic field creates a voltage. Induction is the physics behind every power station on Earth.

The one idea that organises the whole theme

Gravity and electrostatics have identical mathematics. Newton: \(F = -\dfrac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}\). Coulomb: \(F = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r^2}\). Both are inverse-square, both have a field strength defined as force per unit "charge" (mass or charge), both have a potential defined as energy per unit "charge", and both have \(1/r\) potential and \(1/r^2\) field. Learn one and you get the other free. The differences are: gravity is always attractive, mass has only one sign, and gravity is far weaker.

D.1 Gravitational fields SL / HL

Newton's law of gravitation

\[F = \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}\]

\(G\)
gravitational constant, \(6.67\times10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2\text{kg}^{-2}\)
\(m_1, m_2\)
the two masses (kg), treated as point masses or spherically symmetric bodies
\(r\)
the separation of their centres (m)

The force is always attractive, acts along the line joining the centres, and obeys an inverse-square law: double the separation and the force drops by a factor of four.

Gravitational field strength

\[g = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{GM}{r^2}\]

Unit N kg⁻¹ (equivalently m s⁻²). It is a vector, pointing in the direction a test mass would accelerate. At Earth's surface \(g \approx 9.81\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\).

Common trap

\(r\) is measured from the centre of the planet, not from its surface. A satellite at height \(h\) above a planet of radius \(R\) has \(r = R + h\). Using \(r = h\) is the single most common error in D.1, and it produces wildly wrong answers. Write \(R\) and \(h\) on your diagram before you start.

Worked example 1 IB-style · 4 marks · field strength

Question. Determine the gravitational field strength at a height of 400 km above the Earth's surface. Earth: mass \(5.97\times10^{24}\) kg, radius \(6.37\times10^6\) m.

  1. \(r = 6.37\times10^6 + 4.00\times10^5 = 6.77\times10^6\ \text{m}\).
  2. \(g = \dfrac{GM}{r^2} = \dfrac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(5.97\times10^{24})}{(6.77\times10^6)^2} = \dfrac{3.98\times10^{14}}{4.58\times10^{13}} = 8.69\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\).
Answer: \(8.7\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\). Check: this is about 89% of the surface value, so gravity at the ISS orbit is still strong — astronauts are weightless because they are in free fall, not because gravity is absent. That distinction is a favourite exam point.

Orbital motion and Kepler's laws

Intuition. An orbit is perpetual free fall. The spacecraft is falling towards Earth, but it is also moving sideways fast enough that the surface curves away beneath it. The force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes direction without changing speed — circular motion (A.2) with gravity supplying the centripetal force.

Derivation Orbital speed and the derivation of Kepler's third law
  1. Gravity provides the centripetal force: \(\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\).
  2. Cancelling \(m\) and one \(r\): \(v^2 = \dfrac{GM}{r}\), so \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}\). Note the satellite's own mass does not matter.
  3. The period is \(T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2\pi r}{\sqrt{GM/r}} = \dfrac{2\pi r^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}}\).
  4. Squaring: \(T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3\).
  5. Since \(\dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}\) is constant for a given central body, \(T^2 \propto r^3\) — which is Kepler's third law.

What it means: all satellites orbiting the same planet obey the same \(T^2\)–\(r^3\) relationship regardless of their mass. Application: compare two satellites by ratio — \(\dfrac{T_1^2}{T_2^2} = \dfrac{r_1^3}{r_2^3}\) — which avoids using \(G\) at all.

Worked example 2 IB-style · 6 marks · geostationary orbit

Question. A geostationary satellite orbits the Earth with a period of 24 hours. Determine (a) its orbital radius and (b) its height above the Earth's surface. Earth mass \(5.97\times10^{24}\) kg, radius \(6.37\times10^6\) m.

  1. \(T = 24\times3600 = 86\,400\ \text{s}\).
  2. From \(T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2 r^3}{GM}\): \(r^3 = \dfrac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2} = \dfrac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(5.97\times10^{24})(86\,400)^2}{39.48}\).
  3. Numerator: \(3.98\times10^{14}\times7.46\times10^9 = 2.97\times10^{24}\). So \(r^3 = \dfrac{2.97\times10^{24}}{39.48} = 7.53\times10^{22}\ \text{m}^3\).
  4. \(r = (7.53\times10^{22})^{1/3} = 4.22\times10^7\ \text{m}\).
  5. (b) Height \(= 4.22\times10^7 - 6.37\times10^6 = 3.58\times10^7\ \text{m} = 35\,800\ \text{km}\).
Answer: \(r = 4.2\times10^7\ \text{m}\), height 35 800 km. The accepted value is about 35 786 km, so this is spot on. A geostationary satellite must orbit above the equator and in the same sense as Earth's rotation — those conditions are part of the definition, not consequences of the maths.

Gravitational potential and potential energy HL only

Intuition. Field strength tells you the force per kilogram; potential tells you the energy per kilogram. Potential is defined by the work done in bringing a unit mass from infinity — where the potential is defined to be zero — to that point.

\[V_g = -\frac{GM}{r}, \qquad E_p = mV_g = -\frac{GMm}{r}\]

\(V_g\)
gravitational potential (J kg⁻¹). Negative everywhere, zero at infinity.
\(E_p\)
gravitational potential energy (J), also negative, zero at infinity.
Why negative?
Gravity is attractive. You would have to add energy to pull the mass out to infinity, so a bound mass has less energy than a free one — i.e. negative.
Derivation \(V_g = -GM/r\) by integrating the field strength
  1. The work done by the field moving a unit mass from infinity to \(r\) is \(\displaystyle\int_\infty^r g\,dr\). Since \(g = GM/r^2\) points inwards while \(dr\) is measured outwards, the work done by the field on an inward displacement is \(\displaystyle\int_\infty^r \left(-\frac{GM}{r^2}\right)dr\).
  2. \(\displaystyle\int_\infty^r -\frac{GM}{r^2}\,dr = \left[\frac{GM}{r}\right]_\infty^r = \frac{GM}{r}\).
  3. The potential is the work done by an external agent per unit mass, which is the negative of this: \(V_g = -\dfrac{GM}{r}\).
  4. Equivalently: \(g = -\dfrac{dV_g}{dr}\), and differentiating \(-GM/r\) gives \(-(-GM/r^2)\cdot(-1)\)… check directly: \(-\dfrac{d}{dr}\left(-\dfrac{GM}{r}\right) = -\left(\dfrac{GM}{r^2}\right)\cdot(-1)\cdot(-1)\) — the cleanest statement is that field strength is the negative gradient of potential, and the pair \(g = -GM/r^2\) (inward), \(V_g = -GM/r\) is consistent.

What it means: potential is a scalar, so you can add contributions from several masses by simple addition — far easier than adding field vectors. When it is wrong: the \(-GMm/r\) formula is for point masses or spheres; the near-Earth approximation \(mgh\) is only valid for small height changes.

Common trap

Students write \(E_p = +GMm/r\) and then get escape speed wrong by a sign. Also: \(mgh\) and \(-GMm/r\) are the same thing for small heights — the difference in \(-GMm/r\) between \(R\) and \(R+h\) is approximately \(mgh\). If a question involves a large height change (satellites, escape), use \(-GMm/r\); if it involves a few metres near the surface, \(mgh\) is fine and faster.

Escape speed and orbital energy HL only

Derivation Escape speed \(v_e = \sqrt{2GM/r}\) from energy conservation
  1. "Escape" means reaching infinity with (just) zero speed, i.e. total energy = 0 there.
  2. Total energy at the surface: \(E = \tfrac12 mv_e^2 + \left(-\dfrac{GMm}{r}\right)\).
  3. By conservation of energy this must equal the total energy at infinity, which is 0.
  4. \(\tfrac12 mv_e^2 - \dfrac{GMm}{r} = 0 \Rightarrow v_e^2 = \dfrac{2GM}{r} \Rightarrow v_e = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM}{r}}\).
  5. Compare with orbital speed \(v_{\text{orb}} = \sqrt{GM/r}\): escape speed is \(\sqrt2\) times larger, independent of the mass of the escaping object.

What it means: escape speed depends only on the planet's mass and radius. When it is wrong: it ignores air resistance and the rotation of the planet (launching eastwards from the equator gives you a useful head start).

Total energy of a satellite in a circular orbit (HL): combining \(E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12 m\cdot\dfrac{GM}{r} = \dfrac{GMm}{2r}\) with \(E_p = -\dfrac{GMm}{r}\):

\[E_{\text{total}} = -\frac{GMm}{2r}\]

Note \(E_k = +\dfrac{GMm}{2r}\) is positive and exactly half the magnitude of the negative potential energy. To move a satellite from a low orbit to a higher one you must add energy — yet it ends up moving slower. Counter-intuitive, and frequently examined.

Worked example 3 HL · 7 marks · escape speed and orbit change

Question. A 500 kg satellite is in a circular orbit of radius \(7.0\times10^6\) m around the Earth (\(M = 5.97\times10^{24}\) kg). (a) Determine its speed, kinetic energy and total energy. (b) Determine the escape speed from that radius. (c) Hence determine the additional energy needed to escape.

  1. (a) \(v = \sqrt{GM/r} = \sqrt{\dfrac{3.98\times10^{14}}{7.0\times10^6}} = \sqrt{5.69\times10^7} = 7.54\times10^3\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
  2. \(E_k = \tfrac12(500)(7.54\times10^3)^2 = 1.42\times10^{10}\ \text{J}\).
  3. \(E_{\text{total}} = -\dfrac{GMm}{2r} = -\dfrac{(3.98\times10^{14})(500)}{2(7.0\times10^6)} = -1.42\times10^{10}\ \text{J}\). (Notice \(E_{\text{total}} = -E_k\), as the derivation predicted.)
  4. (b) \(v_e = \sqrt{2GM/r} = \sqrt2\times7.54\times10^3 = 1.07\times10^4\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
  5. (c) To escape, the total energy must become 0. Currently it is \(-1.42\times10^{10}\) J, so \(1.42\times10^{10}\ \text{J}\) must be supplied.
Answer: (a) 7.5 km s⁻¹, \(E_k = 1.4\times10^{10}\) J, \(E_{\text{total}} = -1.4\times10^{10}\) J; (b) 10.7 km s⁻¹; (c) \(1.4\times10^{10}\) J. The energy needed to escape is exactly the magnitude of the (negative) total energy — a neat check that examiners like.
Worked example 4 HL · 6 marks · raising an orbit

Question. A satellite moves from a circular orbit of radius \(r\) to one of radius \(2r\). Explain why energy must be supplied even though the satellite's speed decreases.

  1. Speed: \(v \propto 1/\sqrt r\), so doubling \(r\) reduces the speed by a factor of \(\sqrt2\). The kinetic energy therefore halves.
  2. Potential energy: \(E_p = -GMm/r\), so doubling \(r\) makes \(E_p\) less negative — it increases by \(\dfrac{GMm}{2r}\).
  3. Total energy: \(E = -\dfrac{GMm}{2r}\), so going from \(r\) to \(2r\) changes \(E\) from \(-\dfrac{GMm}{2r}\) to \(-\dfrac{GMm}{4r}\) — an increase of \(\dfrac{GMm}{4r}\).
  4. The gain in potential energy (\(\dfrac{GMm}{2r}\)) is twice the loss in kinetic energy (\(\dfrac{GMm}{4r}\)), so the net change is positive: energy must be supplied.
Answer: \(\Delta E = +\dfrac{GMm}{4r}\) must be supplied. This is the classic "counter-intuitive orbit" question: higher orbits are slower but have more total energy. If a question asks you to "explain, with reference to energy", this two-term argument is the whole answer.
Linking question

D.1 links to A.2 circular motion (gravity supplies the centripetal force) and A.3 energy (escape speed is pure energy conservation). It links to A.4 angular momentum: Kepler's second law is conservation of angular momentum, since gravity is a central force with zero torque. And it links to E.5 stars: the mass–luminosity relation and stellar evolution are governed by gravity.

D.2 Electric and magnetic fields SL / HL

Charge and Coulomb's law

\[F = \frac{k q_1 q_2}{r^2}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^9\ \text{N m}^2\text{C}^{-2}\]

Charge \(q\)
Unit C. Quantised: \(q = ne\) with \(e = 1.60\times10^{-19}\) C. Two kinds, positive and negative.
Attractive / repulsive
Like charges repel, unlike charges attract — unlike gravity, which is only attractive.
Permittivity of free space \(\varepsilon_0\)
\(8.85\times10^{-12}\ \text{F m}^{-1}\).

Electric field strength and field lines

\[E = \frac{F}{q}\]

Unit N C⁻¹ (equivalently V m⁻¹). It is a vector, defined as the force per unit positive charge, so it points in the direction a positive charge would accelerate.

For a point charge: \(E = \dfrac{kQ}{r^2}\). Between parallel plates: \(E = \dfrac{V}{d}\), uniform.

How to use it — reading and drawing field lines
  1. Field lines point from positive to negative; the arrow shows the force on a positive test charge.
  2. The density of lines indicates field strength: closer lines, stronger field.
  3. Field lines never cross (the field has one direction at each point) and never form closed loops (electrostatic fields are conservative).
  4. Field lines meet conducting surfaces at right angles, and the field inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is zero.
  5. Field lines cross equipotential surfaces at 90°.

Uniform electric field between parallel plates

Derivation \(E = V/d\) from work and potential difference
  1. Move a charge \(q\) from one plate to the other across a uniform field \(E\). The force on it is \(F = qE\) and it acts over distance \(d\).
  2. Work done \(= Fd = qEd\).
  3. By the definition of potential difference, the work done per unit charge is \(V\), so \(V = \dfrac{qEd}{q} = Ed\).
  4. Therefore \(E = \dfrac{V}{d}\), and \(E\) is uniform because \(V\) changes linearly with distance.

Units: V m⁻¹ is the same as N C⁻¹ — show this if asked: \(\dfrac{\text{V}}{\text{m}} = \dfrac{\text{J C}^{-1}}{\text{m}} = \dfrac{\text{N m C}^{-1}}{\text{m}} = \text{N C}^{-1}\). When it is wrong: near the edges of the plates (fringing), where the field is not uniform.

Magnetic fields

Intuition. A magnetic field is produced by moving charge — a current in a wire, or the orbital and spin motion of electrons in a permanent magnet. Crucially, the magnetic force acts only on charges that are already moving, and it acts perpendicular to their velocity. That is why it changes direction, not speed, and why it does no work.

Common trap

Electric field lines start and end on charges; magnetic field lines are closed loops (there are no magnetic monopoles in this syllabus). If you draw a magnetic field line starting at a north pole and stopping at a south pole, you have drawn it wrong — it continues through the magnet. Also: the force on a moving charge is \(F = Bqv\sin\theta\) — if the charge moves parallel to the field, \(\theta = 0\) and the force is zero.

Electric potential and potential energy HL only

\[V_e = \frac{kQ}{r}, \qquad E_p = qV_e = \frac{kQq}{r}\]

Electric potential \(V_e\)
Work done per unit positive charge in bringing a test charge from infinity to that point. Unit V (= J C⁻¹). It is a scalar — positive near a positive charge, negative near a negative one.
Electric potential energy
\(E_p = qV\). For like charges it is positive (they repel, so pushing them together stores energy); for unlike charges it is negative (they attract, so they are bound).
Equipotential surfaces
Surfaces of constant potential. No work is done moving a charge along one, so \(\Delta E_p = 0\). They are always perpendicular to field lines.
How to use it — the gravity/electrostatics translation table
  1. \(g = GM/r^2 \longleftrightarrow E = kQ/r^2\) — both inverse-square field strengths.
  2. \(F = GMm/r^2 \longleftrightarrow F = kQq/r^2\) — both inverse-square forces.
  3. \(V_g = -GM/r \longleftrightarrow V_e = +kQ/r\) — both \(1/r\) potentials. (Sign differs because gravity is always attractive.)
  4. \(W = q\Delta V\) is the electric version of \(W = mg\Delta h\). If a charge moves between two points, the work done on it is \(q(V_1 - V_2)\) — independent of the path taken.
  5. Energy gained by a charge accelerated through a pd \(V\): \(\Delta E_k = qV\). This is where the electron-volt comes from: 1 eV = \(1.60\times10^{-19}\) J.
Worked example 5 IB-style · 6 marks · parallel plates

Question. Two parallel plates are 4.0 cm apart with a pd of 2000 V across them. An electron is released from rest at the negative plate. Determine (a) the electric field strength, (b) the force on the electron and (c) its speed on reaching the positive plate.

  1. (a) \(E = V/d = 2000/0.040 = 5.0\times10^4\ \text{V m}^{-1}\).
  2. (b) \(F = qE = (1.60\times10^{-19})(5.0\times10^4) = 8.0\times10^{-15}\ \text{N}\), directed towards the positive plate because the electron is negative.
  3. (c) Energy: \(qV = \tfrac12 mv^2\), so \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2qV}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2(1.60\times10^{-19})(2000)}{9.11\times10^{-31}}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{6.4\times10^{-16}}{9.11\times10^{-31}}} = \sqrt{7.03\times10^{14}} = 2.65\times10^7\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
Answer: (a) \(5.0\times10^4\ \text{V m}^{-1}\), (b) \(8.0\times10^{-15}\ \text{N}\) towards the positive plate, (c) \(2.7\times10^7\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) (about 9% of \(c\), so non-relativistic treatment is acceptable). The energy gained is 2000 eV = 2 keV — this is exactly how an old cathode-ray tube accelerated its beam.
Worked example 6 HL · 7 marks · point-charge potential

Question. Two point charges, \(+4.0\ \mu\text{C}\) and \(-6.0\ \mu\text{C}\), are 0.30 m apart. Determine (a) the electric potential at the midpoint and (b) the work done bringing a \(+2.0\ \text{nC}\) charge from infinity to the midpoint.

  1. (a) Potentials add as scalars. Each charge is 0.15 m from the midpoint: \(V = \dfrac{k(4.0\times10^{-6})}{0.15} + \dfrac{k(-6.0\times10^{-6})}{0.15}\).
  2. \(V = \dfrac{8.99\times10^9}{0.15}(4.0\times10^{-6} - 6.0\times10^{-6}) = (5.99\times10^{10})(-2.0\times10^{-6}) = -1.20\times10^5\ \text{V}\).
  3. (b) \(W = qV = (2.0\times10^{-9})(-1.20\times10^5) = -2.4\times10^{-4}\ \text{J}\).
Answer: (a) \(-1.2\times10^5\ \text{V}\), (b) \(-2.4\times10^{-4}\ \text{J}\). The negative sign means the field does positive work — the negative charge dominates, so a positive test charge is attracted and the external agent must hold it back. Note the field strength at the midpoint is not zero (both fields point the same way), even though we used scalar addition for the potential. That contrast is a very common exam question.

D.3 Motion in electromagnetic fields SL / HL

Charged particle in a uniform electric field

Force \(F = qE\) is constant in magnitude and direction (along/against the field for a positive/negative charge). So the acceleration is constant: \(a = \dfrac{qE}{m}\). This is projectile motion (A.1) with \(g\) replaced by \(qE/m\) — the same two-component method, the same parabolic path. Note that unlike gravity, the direction depends on the sign of the charge.

Charged particle in a uniform magnetic field

\[F = Bqv\sin\theta\]

When \(\theta = 90^\circ\) the force has its maximum value \(Bqv\) and is perpendicular to the velocity, so it provides a centripetal force and the particle moves in a circle.

Derivation \(r = \dfrac{mv}{Bq}\) and the cyclotron frequency
  1. Magnetic force = centripetal force: \(Bqv = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\).
  2. Cancel one \(v\): \(Bq = \dfrac{mv}{r}\), so \(r = \dfrac{mv}{Bq}\).
  3. The radius is proportional to momentum \(p = mv\) — which is why particle physicists measure momentum from the curvature of tracks in a bubble chamber.
  4. The period: \(T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2\pi mv}{Bqv} = \dfrac{2\pi m}{Bq}\).
  5. So \(f = \dfrac{Bq}{2\pi m}\) — the cyclotron frequency. Remarkably, it is independent of the speed and radius: a faster particle goes in a bigger circle but takes the same time. That is what makes a cyclotron possible.

What it means: the magnetic force does no work (it is perpendicular to the motion), so the speed and kinetic energy are constant — only the direction changes. When it is wrong: at relativistic speeds, where \(m\) must be replaced by \(\gamma m\) (A.5), causing the cyclotron to fall out of sync — hence the synchrotron.

Common trap

Direction. The force on a positive charge is given by Fleming's left-hand rule (or \(\mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\)): First finger = field (N→S), second finger = current (direction of motion of positive charge), thumb = force. For an electron you must reverse the direction, because it is negatively charged. Nearly every "state the direction" mark lost in D.3 is this one.

Combined electric and magnetic fields

With both fields present and perpendicular, the two forces can balance:

\[qE = Bqv \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \frac{E}{B}\]

Only particles with this speed pass through undeflected — that is a velocity selector. It is the operating principle of mass spectrometers and of J.J. Thomson's measurement of \(e/m\).

Worked example 7 IB-style · 6 marks · electron in a magnetic field

Question. An electron is accelerated from rest through 500 V and then enters a uniform magnetic field of 8.0 mT perpendicular to its velocity. Determine (a) its speed and (b) the radius of its circular path.

  1. (a) \(qV = \tfrac12 mv^2\): \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2(1.60\times10^{-19})(500)}{9.11\times10^{-31}}} = \sqrt{1.756\times10^{14}} = 1.33\times10^7\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
  2. (b) \(r = \dfrac{mv}{Bq} = \dfrac{(9.11\times10^{-31})(1.33\times10^7)}{(8.0\times10^{-3})(1.60\times10^{-19})} = \dfrac{1.21\times10^{-23}}{1.28\times10^{-21}} = 9.5\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}\).
Answer: (a) \(1.3\times10^7\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), (b) 9.5 mm. A radius of about a centimetre in a modest field is typical for electrons — which is why electron-beam tubes need fairly strong fields to bend the beam visibly.
Worked example 8 IB-style · 7 marks · velocity selector

Question. In a velocity selector, a uniform electric field of \(4.0\times10^4\ \text{V m}^{-1}\) and a uniform magnetic field of 0.20 T are at right angles to each other and to the beam. (a) Determine the speed of the particles that pass through undeflected. (b) Explain what happens to faster particles.

  1. (a) \(qE = Bqv \Rightarrow v = E/B = 4.0\times10^4/0.20 = 2.0\times10^5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
  2. (b) The electric force \(qE\) is unchanged but the magnetic force \(Bqv\) increases with \(v\). For a faster particle \(Bqv \gt qE\), so the magnetic force dominates and the particle is deflected in the direction of the magnetic force. (For a slower particle, the electric force dominates and it deflects the other way.)
Answer: (a) \(2.0\times10^5\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), (b) faster particles are deflected by the magnetic force. Note the charge \(q\) cancels, so the selector works for any charge — but the direction of deflection depends on the sign.

Force on a current-carrying conductor

\[F = BIL\sin\theta\]

\(B\)
magnetic flux density (T, tesla)
\(I\)
current (A)
\(L\)
length of conductor in the field (m)
\(\theta\)
angle between the conductor (current direction) and the field lines
Derivation \(F = BIL\) from \(F = Bqv\) on the drifting electrons
  1. Consider a wire of length \(L\) and cross-sectional area \(A\), carrying current \(I\). Suppose it contains \(n\) charge carriers per unit volume, each of charge \(q\), drifting at speed \(v\).
  2. Current \(I = nAqv\) (a standard result: \(I = \dfrac{\Delta q}{\Delta t}\) with the charge in a length \(v\Delta t\) being \(nA\,v\Delta t\,q\)).
  3. The number of carriers in the wire is \(nAL\), and each experiences force \(Bqv\), so the total force is \(F = (nAL)(Bqv) = B(nAqv)L\).
  4. Substituting \(I = nAqv\): \(F = BIL\). With the angle included: \(F = BIL\sin\theta\).

What it means: the force on a wire is really the sum of the forces on its moving charges — the same physics as D.3's first section. When it is wrong: if the wire is parallel to the field, \(\theta = 0\) and there is no force. Also, \(L\) is only the length actually inside the field.

Force between parallel current-carrying wires

\[\frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi r}\]

Currents in the same direction attract; in opposite directions they repel. (Mnemonic: each wire sits in the other's field; apply \(F = BIL\) and the left-hand rule twice and you get attraction for parallel currents.)

Worked example 9 IB-style · 5 marks · parallel wires

Question. Two long parallel wires 0.10 m apart carry currents of 8.0 A and 12 A in opposite directions. Determine the force per unit length on each wire and state whether it is attractive or repulsive.

  1. \(\dfrac{F}{L} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1I_2}{2\pi r} = \dfrac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(8.0)(12)}{2\pi(0.10)}\).
  2. Numerator: \(4\pi\times10^{-7}\times96 = 1.206\times10^{-4}\). Denominator: 0.628.
  3. \(\dfrac{F}{L} = 1.92\times10^{-4}\ \text{N m}^{-1}\).
Answer: \(1.9\times10^{-4}\ \text{N m}^{-1}\), repulsive because the currents are in opposite directions. The forces on the two wires are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction — a Newton's third law pair (A.2), which is a nice link to state if asked.

D.4 Induction HL only

Magnetic flux and flux linkage

Intuition. Imagine the magnetic field lines as rain falling through a window frame. The flux is how much rain passes through the frame: more field, bigger frame, or a frame turned more face-on to the rain all increase it.

\[\Phi = BA\cos\theta \qquad N\Phi = BAN\cos\theta\]

\(\Phi\)
magnetic flux, unit weber (Wb). \(1\ \text{Wb} = 1\ \text{T m}^2\).
\(B\)
magnetic flux density (T)
\(A\)
area of the coil (m²)
\(\theta\)
the angle between the field and the normal to the plane of the coil
\(N\Phi\)
flux linkage (Wb-turns) — flux through one turn times the number of turns
Common trap

The angle in \(\Phi = BA\cos\theta\) is measured from the normal to the coil, so a coil "face-on" to the field (maximum flux) has \(\theta = 0\) and \(\cos\theta = 1\). A coil "edge-on" (plane parallel to field) has \(\theta = 90^\circ\) and zero flux. Many students use \(\sin\theta\) here and then get the generator graph a quarter-cycle out of phase. Rule of thumb: face-on ⇒ maximum flux; use \(\cos\) with the angle from the normal.

Faraday's law and Lenz's law

\[\varepsilon = -N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \varepsilon = -\frac{\Delta(N\Phi)}{\Delta t}\]

The emf is the rate of change of flux linkage. Not the flux — the rate of change of it. A huge constant flux produces nothing.

Why the minus sign? (Lenz's law)

The minus sign is Lenz's law in symbols: the induced emf drives a current whose magnetic effect opposes the change that caused it. Why must it? Suppose it didn't. If the induced current helped the change, a small change would produce a current that increases the change, which produces a bigger current — free energy, forever. That violates conservation of energy. So the opposition is not a rule of thumb about coils; it is energy conservation expressing itself. That argument is a 3-mark "explain" answer in full.

How to use it — the four-step Lenz's law procedure
  1. Identify the change: is the flux increasing or decreasing, and in which direction does the original field point?
  2. Determine what the induced field must do: oppose the change. If flux is increasing, the induced field points against the original field; if decreasing, it points with it.
  3. Use the right-hand grip rule to find the current direction that produces that induced field.
  4. State the direction of the current and mark it on the diagram. Practise with a bar magnet pushed into a coil, then pulled out, then with the other pole — the three cases examiners always use.

Motional emf

Derivation \(\varepsilon = BLv\) for a rod moving through a field
  1. A conducting rod of length \(L\) moves at speed \(v\) perpendicular to a uniform field \(B\).
  2. Each charge \(q\) in the rod moves at speed \(v\) through the field, so it experiences a magnetic force \(F = Bqv\) along the rod (Fleming's left-hand rule).
  3. Charges separate until the electric force \(qE\) balances the magnetic force \(Bqv\), so \(E = Bv\).
  4. The pd between the ends of the rod is \(\varepsilon = EL = BLv\).
  5. Alternatively from Faraday: in time \(\Delta t\) the rod sweeps out area \(Lv\Delta t\), so \(\Delta\Phi = BLv\Delta t\) and \(\varepsilon = \dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = BLv\). Same answer by two routes — which is a good check.

What it means: a conductor moving through a field becomes a battery. When it is wrong: if the rod moves along its own length, no emf (no charge separation across it); if it moves parallel to the field, no magnetic force at all.

The AC generator: a coil rotating in a field

Derivation \(\varepsilon = BAN\omega\sin\omega t\) for a rotating coil
  1. A coil of \(N\) turns and area \(A\) rotates at angular speed \(\omega\) in a uniform field \(B\). Let \(\theta\) be the angle between the field and the normal to the coil.
  2. If the coil starts face-on (\(\theta = 0\)) at \(t = 0\), then \(\theta = \omega t\) and the flux linkage is \(N\Phi = BAN\cos\omega t\).
  3. Faraday's law: \(\varepsilon = -\dfrac{d(N\Phi)}{dt} = -\dfrac{d}{dt}(BAN\cos\omega t) = BAN\omega\sin\omega t\).
  4. So the emf varies sinusoidally with peak value \(\varepsilon_0 = BAN\omega\) (also written \(NBA\omega\)).

What it means: the emf is maximum when the flux is changing fastest — which is when the flux itself is zero (coil edge-on). The emf is zero when the flux is maximum (coil face-on). That phase relationship is the single most tested idea in D.4.

Common trap

Three, all common. (1) Drawing the emf curve in phase with the flux — it is 90° out of phase. (2) Using peak values where rms is needed: for a sinusoid, \(V_{\text{rms}} = V_0/\sqrt2\) and \(I_{\text{rms}} = I_0/\sqrt2\). Mains "230 V" is an rms value; its peak is 325 V. (3) Saying "a bigger flux gives a bigger emf" — no: a bigger rate of change does. A coil sitting in a huge static field has zero induced emf.

Worked example 10 HL · 6 marks · motional emf

Question. An aircraft with a wingspan of 35 m flies horizontally at 250 m s⁻¹ through the Earth's magnetic field. The vertical component of the field is \(5.0\times10^{-5}\) T. Determine the emf induced between the wingtips.

  1. The wings move horizontally, cutting the vertical component of the field, so \(\varepsilon = BLv\) with \(B = 5.0\times10^{-5}\) T.
  2. \(\varepsilon = (5.0\times10^{-5})(35)(250) = 0.44\ \text{V}\).
Answer: 0.44 V. Small but real — and it is why aircraft are fitted with static discharge wicks. Notice the horizontal component of the field produces nothing here, because the wings move along it rather than cutting it.
Worked example 11 HL · 8 marks · AC generator

Question. A rectangular coil of 200 turns, each of area 0.020 m², rotates at 50 Hz in a uniform magnetic field of 0.15 T. (a) Determine the peak emf. (b) Determine the rms emf. (c) Determine the emf at the instant when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field. (d) Explain your answer to (c).

  1. \(\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi(50) = 314\ \text{rad s}^{-1}\).
  2. (a) \(\varepsilon_0 = BAN\omega = (0.15)(0.020)(200)(314) = 188\ \text{V}\).
  3. (b) \(\varepsilon_{\text{rms}} = 188/\sqrt2 = 133\ \text{V}\).
  4. (c) When the plane of the coil is parallel to the field, the normal is perpendicular to the field, so \(\theta = 90^\circ\) and the flux linkage is zero — but the emf is at its maximum, 188 V.
  5. (d) The emf depends on the rate of change of flux linkage, not on the flux itself. At this orientation the flux is zero but changing fastest, so \(|\varepsilon|\) is maximum.
Answer: (a) 190 V, (b) 130 V, (c) 190 V, (d) emf peaks when flux linkage is zero. This is the exam's favourite D.4 question; if you can explain (d) in that sentence, you have the topic.
Worked example 12 HL · 7 marks · Lenz's law with a falling magnet

Question. A bar magnet is dropped through a horizontal copper ring, north pole first. Describe and explain the force on the magnet as it approaches, as it passes through, and as it leaves.

  1. Approaching: the downward flux through the ring is increasing. By Lenz's law the induced current creates a field opposing that increase — i.e. an upward field. The top of the ring becomes a north pole, which repels the approaching north pole. The magnet experiences an upward force and accelerates at less than \(g\).
  2. Inside the ring (centre): at the instant the magnet is at the centre the flux is at a maximum, so its rate of change is momentarily zero, the induced emf is zero, and there is no magnetic force.
  3. Leaving: the downward flux is now decreasing. The induced current reverses, creating a downward field to oppose the decrease. The top of the ring becomes a south pole, attracting the receding north pole — again an upward force on the magnet.
Answer: upward force on the way in, none at the centre, upward force on the way out — the magnet is always retarded. The kinetic energy lost becomes thermal energy in the ring. This is exactly Lenz's law as energy conservation: the magnet can never be accelerated by the ring, or we would have a perpetual motion machine.
Linking question

D.4 links to B.5 circuits (the induced emf drives a current through the circuit; \(P = \varepsilon^2/R\)), to C.1 SHM (the output is sinusoidal with angular frequency \(\omega\)), and to A.3 energy (Lenz's law is energy conservation, and the mechanical work done turning a generator becomes electrical energy). It also links to B.4: every power station is a heat engine turning a generator, so the Carnot ceiling limits the electricity, not just the heat.

Check yourself

Practice

  1. [4 marks] Calculate the gravitational force between two 5.0 kg spheres whose centres are 0.20 m apart. Comment on the magnitude.

    Show answer

    \(F = \dfrac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2} = \dfrac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(25)}{0.04} = 4.2\times10^{-8}\ \text{N}\).

    Tiny — far below the weight of the spheres (~49 N) and below what you could detect without a Cavendish torsion balance. This is why we only notice gravity when at least one mass is planet-sized.

  2. [5 marks] A planet orbits a star with twice the period of Earth and the same star. Determine the ratio of its orbital radius to Earth's.

    Show answer

    \(T^2 \propto r^3\), so \(\left(\dfrac{r_p}{r_E}\right)^3 = \left(\dfrac{T_p}{T_E}\right)^2 = 2^2 = 4\).

    \(\dfrac{r_p}{r_E} = 4^{1/3} = 1.59\). The planet orbits at about 1.6 times Earth's radius — and since \(v \propto 1/\sqrt r\), it moves more slowly.

  3. [6 marks] Determine the escape speed from the surface of the Moon. Moon: mass \(7.35\times10^{22}\) kg, radius \(1.74\times10^6\) m. Hence comment on why the Moon has no atmosphere.

    Show answer

    \(v_e = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2(6.67\times10^{-11})(7.35\times10^{22})}{1.74\times10^6}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{9.80\times10^{12}}{1.74\times10^6}} = \sqrt{5.63\times10^6} = 2.37\times10^3\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

    At lunar surface temperatures the rms speed of light gas molecules (hydrogen, helium) is comparable to or greater than 2.4 km s⁻¹, so over geological time those molecules escape. Heavier molecules are too slow to escape, but without a magnetic field the solar wind also strips the atmosphere. Hence the Moon has essentially none.

  4. [5 marks] An oil drop carries a charge of \(3.2\times10^{-19}\) C in a uniform field of \(2.5\times10^4\ \text{V m}^{-1}\). Determine the electric force on it and compare it with its weight (mass \(1.0\times10^{-14}\) kg).

    Show answer

    \(F_E = qE = (3.2\times10^{-19})(2.5\times10^4) = 8.0\times10^{-15}\ \text{N}\).

    Weight \(= mg = 1.0\times10^{-14}\times9.81 = 9.81\times10^{-14}\ \text{N}\). The weight is about 12 times larger than the electric force, so the drop falls — you would need a field about 12 times stronger (≈ 3.1 × 10⁵ V m⁻¹) to hold it stationary. This is the principle of Millikan's oil-drop experiment.

  5. [6 marks] A proton moves at \(4.0\times10^6\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of 0.50 T. Determine the radius of its path and the time for one revolution.

    Show answer

    \(r = \dfrac{mv}{Bq} = \dfrac{(1.67\times10^{-27})(4.0\times10^6)}{(0.50)(1.60\times10^{-19})} = \dfrac{6.68\times10^{-21}}{8.0\times10^{-20}} = 0.0835\ \text{m}\).

    \(T = \dfrac{2\pi m}{Bq} = \dfrac{2\pi(1.67\times10^{-27})}{(0.50)(1.60\times10^{-19})} = \dfrac{1.049\times10^{-26}}{8.0\times10^{-20}} = 1.31\times10^{-7}\ \text{s}\).

    Note the period does not depend on the speed — double the speed and the radius doubles but the period is unchanged.

  6. [6 marks] A straight wire of length 0.25 m carries 6.0 A at right angles to a magnetic field of 0.40 T. Determine the force on the wire. State what happens if the wire is rotated to lie parallel to the field.

    Show answer

    \(F = BIL\sin 90^\circ = 0.40\times6.0\times0.25 = 0.60\ \text{N}\).

    Parallel: \(\sin 0^\circ = 0\), so the force is zero. The force is maximum when the wire is perpendicular to the field and zero when parallel — that \(\sin\theta\) is the whole story of the motor effect.

  7. [7 marks] A coil of 500 turns and area 0.010 m² is in a field of 0.080 T. It is rotated from face-on to edge-on in 0.20 s. Determine the average emf induced.

    Show answer

    Initial flux linkage \(= BAN\cos 0^\circ = 0.080\times0.010\times500 = 0.40\ \text{Wb-turns}\).

    Final flux linkage \(= BAN\cos 90^\circ = 0\).

    Average emf \(= \dfrac{|\Delta(N\Phi)|}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{0.40}{0.20} = 2.0\ \text{V}\).

    This is an average — the instantaneous emf varies during the rotation, starting at zero (flux maximum) and ending at its peak (flux changing fastest).

  8. [8 marks] Explain, with reference to Faraday's and Lenz's laws, why the induced current in a coil must oppose the change in flux linkage that produces it, and describe an experiment that demonstrates this.

    Show answer

    Faraday: the induced emf equals the rate of change of flux linkage, \(\varepsilon = -N\Delta\Phi/\Delta t\). The minus sign encodes Lenz's law.

    Why opposition is necessary: suppose the induced current produced a magnetic field that aided the change in flux. Then a small change would induce a current that increases the change, inducing a larger current, and so on — energy would be created from nothing, violating conservation of energy. Therefore the induced effect must oppose the cause.

    Demonstration: drop a strong magnet through a vertical copper tube and time it; then time a non-magnetic object of the same mass and shape. The magnet takes far longer. As it falls, the changing flux in the tube induces circulating (eddy) currents whose magnetic field opposes the magnet's motion, producing an upward force. The magnet's lost gravitational potential energy appears as thermal energy in the tube, which warms slightly. A plastic tube gives no such delay — the control.
