🔁 Week 5 Recall — 60 Terms by Function

Read each word and recall its precise definition before looking. They are ordered by job, not by day — this interleaving is what turns five lists into one toolkit.

① Claiming & Defending (Days 29 + 31 critical method · 12 terms)

The words that stake, attack, support, and qualify a position in argumentative writing.

assert
To state a claim firmly as true — the baseline argumentative move.
refute
To prove a claim or opposing view wrong through counter-evidence.
corroborate
To support a claim with additional evidence that confirms it.
validate
To establish a claim as legitimate or sound — not merely confirm.
hedge
A word / structure that softens commitment ("might", "seems") — modal caution.
stance
The writer's adopted position / attitude toward the subject.
assertive
A tone / manner that states positions firmly (opposite of tentative).
tentative
A manner that withholds full commitment — hedged, cautious.
caveat
A warning or limiting condition attached to a claim.
tenable
(Of a position) able to be defended without contradiction.
cogent
(Of an argument) clear, logical, and convincing.
critique
A detailed analysis that judges strengths and weaknesses.

② Evaluating & Judging (Days 31 + 33 inquiry · 12 terms)

The words that name how you assess quality, detect fine effects, and position a reading.

evaluate
To make a judgement of value or significance.
assess
To estimate or measure the weight / quality of something.
perceive
To become aware of / take in a feature, pattern, or effect.
discern
To detect or distinguish finely a subtle effect / difference.
judicious
Showing wise, careful judgement.
measured
Restrained, balanced in tone — not overstated.
salient
Most noticeable / important — the point that stands out.
criterion
A standard by which something is judged.
interrogate
To probe a text / claim closely with questions.
triangulate
To confirm a reading by cross-checking multiple sources / lenses.
heuristic
A provisional rule-of-thumb for inquiry, not a fixed law.
situate
To place a text / claim in its context (historical, cultural).

③ Comparing & Relating Texts (Days 29 + 30 · 12 terms)

The multi-text vocabulary for placing texts in dialogue and naming their relations.

juxtapose
To place elements side by side to invite comparison / contrast.
analogous
Similar in function / relation though different in detail.
reconcile
To show how apparently conflicting readings can coexist.
dialectic
A method of arriving at truth through opposing positions.
parallelism
Repeated grammatical structure to link or contrast ideas.
antithesis
Opposing ideas in balanced / parallel structure.
concession
A concessive clause yielding ground to the opposing view.
synthesis
Combining distinct elements into a connected whole.
counterpoint
A contrasting element that sets off the main idea.
intertextuality
A text's dialogue with other texts (allusion, echo, quotation).
correspondence
A matching / echoing relation between two texts.
foil
A character / detail that highlights another by contrast.

④ Contrastive Dynamics & Register Framing (Days 30 + 32 · 12 terms)

The words that name how elements move apart / together, and how argumentative prose is framed.

equivalence
A relation of equal weight / value between elements.
symmetry
Balanced, mirroring structure between parts.
divergence
Elements moving apart / differing from one another.
convergence
Elements coming together / agreeing.
modality
Grammatical encoding of certainty / commitment (will, may, must).
objectivity
Freedom from personal bias in treatment.
impersonality
Absence of personal voice / first-person in style.
discursive
Moving between topics in a reasoned, exploratory way.
exposition
Writing that explains / sets out information.
attribution
Crediting a claim / idea explicitly to its source.
evidential
Markers showing the source of knowledge ("according to").
formality
The level of formal register appropriate to the context.

⑤ Critical Synthesis & Extending Reasoning (Days 29 + 33 · 12 terms)

The words that manage readings across lenses, hold conclusions open, and extend reasoning.

negotiate
To work through the tensions between competing readings.
mediate
To stand between / connect two positions.
conflate
To wrongly merge distinct things — a trap to avoid.
provisional
A conclusion held as temporary / open to revision.
contingent
Dependent on particular circumstances (not necessary).
paradigm
A framework / model organising how we see things.
subsume
To place a case correctly under a broader category.
matrix
A grid / framework for mapping relations between ideas.
nuance
A subtle distinction or shade of meaning.
mitigate
To soften or reduce the force of a claim / problem.
problematic
(adj.) open to challenge / raising difficulties.
extrapolate
To extend a reading / reasoning beyond the given evidence.
Self-assessment: The five grids answer five different analytical questions. ① Claiming & defending = "what move am I making on this claim?" ② Evaluating & judging = "how good is this reasoning?" ③ Comparing & relating = "how do these texts speak to each other?" ④ Contrastive dynamics & register = "how do the parts move, and how does the prose sound?" ⑤ Critical synthesis = "how do I hold multiple readings without collapsing them?" A strong Paper 2 essay reaches for at least one term from each grid. Today's job is to make all sixty retrievable — not as five lists, but as one web. Notice the bridges: synthesis (③) is what reconcile (③) and negotiate (⑤) both aim toward; hedge (①) is the register-level face of provisional (⑤); attribution (④) is how you make a validated (①) claim survive marker scrutiny.

⚖️ The 15 Distinction Pairs (Your Week 5 Exam Armour)

IB markers reward precise discrimination. These are the pairs most often confused across the five Week 5 pillars — know the exact seam between each (three per pillar).

① assert vs. refute

assert — to state your own claim firmly as true (build).
refute — to tear down an opposing claim with counter-evidence (attack).
Both are claim-moves, but assert constructs your position while refute dismantles theirs. An essay needs both: assert your reading, then refute the obvious alternative.

② hedge vs. caveat

hedge — a grammatical / lexical softener of commitment ("might", "appears to") woven into the claim itself.
caveat — a separate limiting clause or warning attached after the claim ("with the caveat that…").
Both reduce overstatement, but hedge is internal modulation of force; caveat is an external qualification. Name which one you see.

③ assertive vs. tentative

assertive — a manner that commits fully and states positions without qualification.
tentative — a manner that withholds, hedges, and signals openness to revision.
These are opposite stances (①). Top-band writing modulates between them deliberately: assertive on the thesis, tentative when conceding limitation.

④ evaluate vs. assess

evaluate — to make a judgement of value / significance (good, effective, revealing).
assess — to estimate or measure the weight / scale of something (how much, how far).
Often near-synonyms, but evaluation carries the value-verdict; assessment carries the measurement. "Assess the effect, then evaluate its success" is the cleaner sequence.

⑤ perceive vs. discern

perceive — to become aware of / take in a feature or pattern broadly.
discern — to detect or distinguish something finely, often against noise.
Perceive is the wide take; discern is the sharp eye. You perceive a mood shift, then discern the exact device producing it.

⑥ judicious vs. measured

judicious — showing wise judgement in what is chosen or said.
measured — restrained and balanced in tone, not overstated.
Judicious is about the wisdom of the choice; measured is about the temperance of the delivery. A judicious point can still be delivered unmeasured (shouted); aim for both.

⑦ juxtapose vs. synthesis

juxtapose — to place elements side by side so the reader makes the comparison.
synthesis — to combine those elements into a single connected whole or argument.
Juxtaposition invites comparison; synthesis performs the merging. A comparative essay juxtaposes first, then synthesises — don't confuse the two moves.

⑧ parallelism vs. antithesis

parallelism — repeated grammatical structure linking ideas (similar or contrasting).
antithesis — opposing ideas held in that parallel structure.
Antithesis is a species of parallelism: all antithesis uses parallel structure, but not all parallelism is antithetical. "Speech is silver; silence is golden" = antithesis; a list of three parallel clauses = parallelism.

⑨ counterpoint vs. correspondence

counterpoint — a contrasting element that sets the main idea in relief.
correspondence — a matching / echoing relation between texts.
Opposite relational directions: counterpoint is difference that sharpens; correspondence is similarity that resonates. Name which relation you are arguing.

⑩ divergence vs. convergence

divergence — elements moving apart / differing from one another.
convergence — elements coming together / agreeing.
The opposed dynamics of multi-text comparison (④). Two texts converge on a theme but diverge in tone — state both to show the nuance.

⑪ objectivity vs. impersonality

objectivity — a quality of stance: freedom from personal bias in treatment.
impersonality — a feature of style: absence of first-person / personal voice.
You can write impersonally yet be biased, or write personally yet be objective. One is about the mind's position; the other about the page's voice.

⑫ modality vs. evidential

modality — grammatical encoding of certainty / commitment (will, may, must).
evidential — markers showing the source of knowledge ("according to", "evidently").
Both manage how a claim is pinned down, but modality governs how sure you sound; evidential governs where your evidence comes from. A hedged claim with clear evidentials is stronger than either alone.

⑬ negotiate vs. mediate

negotiate — to work through the tensions between competing readings.
mediate — to stand between / connect two positions without choosing.
Negotiate is active resolution-in-progress; mediate is holding the line between. Both belong to critical synthesis (⑤) — use negotiate when you resolve, mediate when you map.

⑭ provisional vs. contingent

provisional — a conclusion held as temporary / open to revision.
contingent — a claim dependent on circumstances (not universally necessary).
Both are epistemic caution (⑤), but provisional is about time (subject to change); contingent is about condition (true only if…). Don't collapse "I may be wrong later" with "this holds only here".

⑮ conflate vs. subsume

conflate — to wrongly merge distinct things, distorting both (the trap).
subsume — to place a case correctly under a broader category.
The difference is correctness. Conflate is the error markers punish; subsume is the disciplined move that builds a paradigm (⑤). Recognising the seam is itself a top-band skill.

🎯 Building Your Week 5 Toolkit

  1. Week 5 is the deployment week of the whole programme so far. Weeks 1–4 gave you the words for what texts do; Week 5 gives you the words for what you do with texts — claim, compare, judge, frame, synthesise. Together they are the vocabulary for a complete Paper 2 essay and a confident IOC.
  2. Regroup, don't re-list. Today you met the same sixty words ordered by function. That interleaving is the difference between memorising five lists and owning one toolkit. When you write a real comparative essay, reach for one term from each grid rather than twelve from one.
  3. The fifteen distinction pairs are where marks are won or lost. Saying "these texts juxtapose" gets you the identification; saying "they juxtapose rather than synthesise, because the opposition is left unresolved" gets you the top band. Precision of discrimination is the IB differentiator.
  4. The grids are bridged, not walled. Synthesis (③) is what reconcile (③) and negotiate (⑤) both aim toward; hedge (①) is the register-level face of provisional (⑤); attribution (④) is how a validated (①) claim survives marker scrutiny. A band-7 paragraph names the bridge: "the writer hedges the claim, keeping it provisional, then validates it through attribution."
  5. Register is the skin your argument wears. The same cogent claim reads entirely differently in an assertive versus a tentative register; objectivity and impersonality are how you signal that the judgement is the text's, not your mood's. Pair every claim-move (①) with a deliberate register choice (④).
  6. Conflate is the trap; subsume is the discipline. Week 5's hardest single skill is holding two readings without wrongly merging them. Practise on a real pair of set-texts this week: juxtapose their positions, then either reconcile / synthesise them correctly (subsume under a shared paradigm) or state the divergence — but never silently conflate. By Day 35's assessment you'll have a complete, connected Week 5 toolkit ready under exam conditions.
