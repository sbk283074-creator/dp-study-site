🔁 Day 17 Spaced Review — 12 Structure & Narrative Terms

A 15-day spaced pass on the structural toolkit from Day 17 — the machinery of how a story is built and told. Read each, recall its precise mechanism before looking, then check. You will be arguing about these very structures in today's new words — a confident academic register is how you make that argument land.

foreshadowing
Hints or clues planted early about events that will occur later — looks forward.
flashback
An interruption of chronology to show an earlier event (analepsis) — looks back.
flash-forward
A scene that jumps ahead of present time (prolepsis) — dramatic irony or suspense.
frame narrative
A story that encloses one or more inner stories — vertical nesting (story within story).
unreliable narrator
A narrator whose account the reader should doubt — bias, limited knowledge, deception, or instability.
stream of consciousness
Unedited, continuous flow of a character's thoughts — a mode, not necessarily false.
interior monologue
A character's inward thoughts voiced to themselves — more ordered and grammatical than SoC.
epiphany
A sudden moment of revelation or insight (Joyce's "showing-forth") — the internal "end".
denouement
The final resolution / unravelling of plot after the climax — the external "end".
non-linear narrative
Plot not in strict chronological order — the broad category containing flashback / flash-forward.
retrospective narration
A later-vantage voice recounting past events — a whole present tense situated in the future.
cyclic structure
Ending returns to / echoes the beginning — a horizontal loop signalling fate or stasis.
Self-assessment: Could you separate flashback from flash-forward (direction of the time-jump — Day 17 box), unreliable narrator from stream of consciousness (truth-value vs. mode — Day 17 box), and frame narrative from cyclic structure (nesting vs. looping — Day 17 box), without peeking? Those three pairs anchor Day 17. Today's new words are the register you drape over these structures: you don't just name the unreliable narrator, you write about it with objectivity and stance; you don't just spot the cyclic structure, you argue its effect in a discursive, assertive paragraph. Keep the structural layer live — the register is useless without something to say.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Discourse & Register Terms

NEW CATEGORY — "DISCOURSE & REGISTER FOR ARGUMENTATIVE WRITING"

From saying to sounding right. Days 29–31 gave you what to argue and how to judge it. Today gives you the register — the academic tone and discourse machinery that makes a comparative claim read as authoritative without reading as dogmatic. Three words handle caution (hedge, modality, tentative); three handle position (stance, objectivity, impersonality); three handle argument shape (discursive, exposition, formality); and three handle grounding (assertive, attribution, evidential). Together they are the difference between an essay that states and one that persuades — the final register piece before Week 5's critical-synthesis capstone.

hedge /hɛdʒ/ verb / noun

To use cautious, non-committal language that reduces the force of a claim — softening certainty without abandoning the point.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The essay hedges its claim by]…" · "[a hedge such as 'arguably' / 'to some extent']" · "[hedge the generalisation]"

"Rather than asserting that the poem is 'about grief', the paragraph hedges — 'the speaker appears, at least provisionally, to figure loss as enclosure' — and the caution is what makes the reading credible rather than over-claimed." — comparative paragraph

IB Tip The verb behind "hedging". Distinct from qualify (Day 22) — qualify = add a stated limitation or exception; hedge = soften via modality / understatement more broadly (cf. Day 31's measured). A hedge is the how; a qualification is one kind of hedge. In top-band writing, hedge selectively: hedge the bold generalisation, never the evidence. Over-hedging collapses into the tentative register (see box C) — the failure mode that caps essays at band 4–5. Use hedge to sound authoritative-yet-honest.

Common collocations: hedge the claim · a hedge against · hedging language · carefully hedged · hedge by saying

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: hedge vs. qualify

Both soften a claim, but they soften in different ways:

hedge — Broad caution. Reduce a claim's force via modality / understatement ("arguably", "to some extent", "it may be that").
→ Lowers certainty without stating a specific limit.

qualify — Specific limit. Add a clause or exception that bounds the claim (Day 22: "the reading holds, provided we weigh the second stanza").
→ States the precise boundary.

The test: am I lowering certainty generally (hedge) or stating a specific limit (qualify)? Every qualification is a kind of hedge, but not every hedge is a qualification. In exams, a measured paragraph does both — hedges the bold opening, then qualifies the synthesis (Day 30) with a stated caveat (Day 31). Confusing them (calling a vague "perhaps" a "qualification") leaves the claim undefined. This pair, with assertive / tentative, is exam armour for the register question.

modality /məʊˈdælɪti/ noun

The grammatical expression of the speaker's degree of certainty, possibility, or obligation — realised through modal verbs and adverbs.

Literary Usage Pattern "[the shift from 'must' to 'might'] [signals]…" · "[high modality] [asserts] / [low modality] [hedges]" · "[modal verbs encode stance]"

"The critic's move from 'the text must be read as' to 'it might be read as' is a shift in modality — the high-modality opening asserts authority, the low-modality close hedges, and the slide itself performs intellectual humility." — discourse-analysis note

IB Tip The technical term for the grammar of hedge (word 1). Distinct from tone — modality is the linguistic mechanism (will / would / may / must), tone is the attitude it conveys. Connects to Day 23's illocutionary act (modality shapes a sentence's pragmatic force). In Paper 1 commentary, name a modality shift and state what it does — "the modal slide performs doubt" is a 7-band observation. Avoid the vague "the writer uses cautious language"; say which modality and why.

Common collocations: high / low modality · shift in modality · epistemic modality · modality encodes · modal verbs

stance /stɑːns/ noun

The writer's attitudinal or intellectual position toward the subject, signalled through word-choice, tone, and evidence selection.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The essay adopts a stance of] [detached critique]" · "[the critic's stance toward the narrator]" · "[signal a clear stance]"

"The comparative essay takes a stance of measured feminist critique — not outrage but close attention to how the two texts figure agency differently — and the restraint is what makes the position persuasive rather than polemical." — Paper 2 framing

IB Tip The position you argue from. Distinct from thesis (Day 22) — thesis = the specific claim; stance = the broader intellectual posture behind it (feminist, sceptical, celebratory). Connects to Day 24's lenses (a Marxist stance deploys Marxist critique). In the IO and HLE rationale, name your stance explicitly; in essays, let it show through modality and evidence choice rather than declaring it. A clear stance marks a positioned, personal response — the difference between band 5 and band 7.

Common collocations: adopt a stance · critical stance · the writer's stance · a clear stance · stance toward

objectivity /ɒbdʒɛkˈtɪvɪti/ noun

The quality of being detached, factual, and evidence-based rather than personal, emotional, or impressionistic.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The analysis maintains objectivity by]…" · "[objectivity is achieved through evidence]" · "[apparent objectivity masks a stance]"

"The commentary's objectivity is not neutrality — it weighs both texts' treatments of enclosure against the same criterion (Day 30 / Day 31) before pronouncing, so the judgement reads as earned, not imposed." — comparative-method note

IB Tip The register goal for academic writing. Distinct from impersonality (word 5) — objectivity is the quality of detachment; impersonality is the grammatical means (agentless constructions) that helps achieve it. Connects to Day 31's measured and salient. In exams, objectivity is earned by evidence and stated criteria, not by stripping all voice — a fully voiceless essay is not objective, it is empty. Show the marker you hold a stance while weighing evidence fairly.

Common collocations: maintain objectivity · apparent objectivity · objective analysis · objectivity requires · false objectivity

impersonality /ɪmˌpɜːsəˈnælɪti/ noun

The use of agentless, non-personal constructions (passives, nominalisations, "it is argued that…") to signal academic distance.

Literary Usage Pattern "[impersonality is signalled by the passive]…" · "[the essay achieves impersonality through] [agentless claims]" · "[impersonal constructions distance the writer]"

"The paragraph's impersonality — 'it may be observed that', 'the effect is produced by' — removes the first-person writer and lets the textual evidence speak, which is exactly the register Day 23's nominalisation and passive build grammatically." — style note

IB Tip The grammatical how of objectivity (word 4). Distinct from outright omission of voice — impersonality is a register choice, not the absence of thought. Links to Day 23's nominalisation / passive (the syntactic engines of impersonality) and to Day 31's avoidance of the intentional fallacy ("the author wants us to feel"). Use impersonality to signal academic distance, but keep a stance (word 3) visible through evidence choice — pure impersonality with no position is the empty-essay trap.

Common collocations: achieve impersonality · impersonal constructions · impersonality signals · the impersonality of · strategic impersonality

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: objectivity vs. impersonality

Both describe academic distance, but one is the aim and the other is the method:

objectivity — The quality: detachment, fairness, evidence-based judgement (a stance of weighing both sides).
→ What you are trying to achieve.

impersonality — The means: agentless, non-personal grammar (passives, "it is argued that") that signals distance.
→ How you signal it grammatically.

The test: am I describing the fairness of the judgement (objectivity) or the grammar of distance (impersonality)? You can be objective without being impersonal (a clear first-person stance that weighs evidence) and impersonal without being objective (agentless prose that smuggles a bias). In exams, aim for objective judgement expressed through partly-impersonal grammar — never confuse the two, and never let impersonality hollow out your stance. This pair anchors the register toolkit.

discursive /dɪsˈkɜːsɪv/ adjective

Relating to reasoned argument and the orderly, connected development of ideas in prose (as opposed to lyric or purely descriptive writing).

Literary Usage Pattern "[a discursive essay]" · "[the discursive mode]" · "[discursive development of the claim]"

"The Paper 2 response is firmly discursive: each paragraph advances the argument one step — claim, evidence, effect, link — rather than merely describing a device, so the essay reads as reasoning, not catalogue." — essay-structure note

IB Tip The mode of the argumentative essay itself. Distinct from "descriptive" — a discursive essay develops a thesis (cf. Day 22's thesis / contention); a descriptive one just lists. Connects to Day 1's foreground (you foreground the argument, not the plot) and to Day 31's evaluate (discursive writing evaluates, it does not summarise). Use discursive to describe your own essay's shape, and to justify why a paragraph belongs — it moves the claim forward.

Common collocations: a discursive essay · discursive mode · discursive development · more discursive · discursive reasoning

exposition /ˌɛkspəˈzɪʃən/ noun

The clear, systematic explanation or laying-out of an argument, position, or set of ideas.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The opening paragraph offers a succinct exposition of]…" · "[exposition of the criterion]" · "[the exposition establishes the terms]"

"The essay's exposition states the comparative criterion (Day 31) up front — 'which text more fully dramatises enclosure' — and that one clear sentence frames everything that follows, so the comparison is principled from the first line." — comparative-method note

IB Tip The opening move of a discursive essay. Distinct from argument itself — exposition lays out the terms / thesis; argument then defends them. Links to Day 22's thesis / premise and to Day 31's stated criterion. In Paper 2, a tight exposition (state texts, state criterion, state contention) is the single highest-leverage paragraph — it pre-empts false analogy (Day 29) and establishes comparability (Day 30). Write exposition as the map; the rest of the essay is the journey.

Common collocations: a clear exposition · exposition of · the exposition establishes · succinct exposition · expository

tentative /ˈtɛntətɪv/ adjective

Hesitant or cautious in commitment — a register that under-claims and defers judgement.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a tentative claim]" · "[overly tentative] [perhaps might suggest]" · "[tentative opening that gains confidence]"

"The draft's tentative register — 'this perhaps might be suggesting something about freedom' — abandons the argument instead of advancing it; the revised sentence states the claim and hedges only the limit, and the essay immediately reads a band higher." — register-revision note

IB Tip The register failure mode to avoid. Distinct from measured (Day 31) — measured = confident but proportionate; tentative = unsure and evasive. Over-tentative prose ("this possibly could be seen as maybe suggesting") is the classic band 4–5 tell. The fix is not to over-claim (Day 29's warning) but to be assertive about what the evidence supports and hedge only where genuinely uncertain. See box C: tentative vs. assertive.

Common collocations: a tentative claim · overly tentative · tentative opening · sounds tentative · tentative conclusion

assertive /əˈsɜːtɪv/ adjective

Confident and direct in advancing a claim — the register to cultivate, holding a position without arrogance or over-claim.

Literary Usage Pattern "[an assertive claim]" · "[assertive without dogmatic]" · "[the essay is assertive about the effect]"

"The revised paragraph is assertive: 'The locked gate figures freedom as externally imposed' — stated cleanly, then hedged only at the boundary ('in both texts, though the subjects enclosed differ'). The claim is held; the limit is visible. That is the register of a 7." — register-revision note

IB Tip The register target. Distinct from over-claim (Day 29's warning) — assertive = confident about what the text supports; over-claim = saying more than the evidence allows. Links to Day 31's measured and tenable. The mature move is assertive about the defensible core, hedged at the edge — assertive, not dogmatic. See box C: tentative vs. assertive. An assertive essay is the visible product of all Week 5's moves (assert, qualify, evaluate) working together.

Common collocations: an assertive claim · assertive without dogmatic · assertive tone · become assertive · assertive analysis

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: tentative vs. assertive

Both are registers of commitment, but they sit at opposite ends of the confidence axis:

tentative — Under-claims. "This perhaps might be suggesting…" — defers judgement, abandons the argument.
→ The failure mode that caps essays at band 4–5.

assertive — Holds the claim. "The gate figures freedom as imposed" — confident about what the evidence supports, hedged only at the edge.
→ The register of a 7 (confident, not dogmatic).

The test: does my sentence advance the claim (assertive) or abandon it (tentative)? The middle ground is measured (Day 31) — assertive about the defensible core, hedged at the boundary. Tentative is not the same as careful: careful writing still states; tentative writing whispers. In exams, audit your openers — if three consecutive sentences begin "perhaps / maybe / it could be argued that", you are in the tentative trap. Flip them to assertive claims and watch the band rise. This pair, with hedge / qualify, is the spine of fluent argumentative register.

attribution /ˌætrɪˈbjuːʃən/ noun

The act of ascribing a view, claim, or wording to a source ("X argues that…", "as Y observes…").

Literary Usage Pattern "[The essay uses attribution to] [frame the critic's view]" · "[attribution of the claim to the text]" · "[explicit attribution]"

"The paragraph opens with attribution — 'As the Marxist lens would have it, the form naturalises privilege' — which lets the essay deploy Day 24's critique without asserting the position as its own unexamined axiom." — IO / extended-essay framing

IB Tip The politeness-and-rigour move of academic register. Distinct from quotation (the words) — attribution = the credit / framing of a view. Connects to Day 24's lenses (attribute the lens, don't own it) and to Day 31's critique. In the EE, IO rationale, and HLE, attribution is mandatory — you must show whose idea a claim is. In essays, attribution lets you stage a position before evaluating it (Day 31), so the reader sees you reasoning, not parroting.

Common collocations: use attribution · explicit attribution · attribution of · attribute the claim · attribution to

evidential /ˌɛvɪˈdɛnʃəl/ adjective

Relating to the evidence that supports a claim; signalling that a statement is grounded in textual proof.

Literary Usage Pattern "[an evidential sentence]" · "[evidential support for the claim]" · "[make the analysis evidential]"

"The strongest sentences are evidential — 'the repeated locked gate (ll. 4, 11, 29) figures enclosure as the poem's governing image' — because the line references turn a reading into proof the marker can verify." — close-reading note

IB Tip The quality that makes a claim tenable (Day 31). Distinct from assertive (confidence) — evidential = the claim is backed; assertive = the claim is stated. An evidential assertion is the goal: state boldly, ground in text. Connects to Day 30's "name relation AND effect" and to Day 1's evidence. Audit each claim for an evidential anchor (a line, a feature, a contrast) — a claim with no evidence is a floating assertion, however confident.

Common collocations: evidential support · make it evidential · evidential sentence · evidential basis · lacks evidential

formality /fɔːˈmælɪti/ noun

The degree of rigid, conventional structure in language — the axis along which register runs from intimate / casual to academic / ceremonial.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The essay maintains high formality]" · "[a shift in formality]" · "[formality signals the register]"

"The commentary holds high formality throughout — no contractions, no slang, no second-person address — which is what lets a personal reading of the poem read as literary analysis rather than a text message." — register note

IB Tip The axis, not the system (the system is register, taught Day 26 as a word-meaning term — here we use formality for the academic-essay end of that axis). Distinct from register — register is the whole context-appropriate variety; formality is its formality/informality dimension. In IB, the essay and commentary demand high formality: no contractions, no colloquialism, no "you". A sudden drop in formality (e.g. "the poet really wants us to feel…") signals the intentional fallacy (Day 31) and breaks the academic stance. Hold formality; vary tone (Day 30 / Day 31).

Common collocations: high formality · shift in formality · maintain formality · level of formality · formality signals

🎯 Study Tips — Sounding Authoritative, Not Dogmatic

  1. Register is the clothes your argument wears. Days 29–31 gave you the moves, the comparative grammar, and the judgement language. Today's register (formality, impersonality, stance) is what makes that content read as academic. Hold high formality in the essay and commentary — no contractions, no "you", no slang — but vary tone (measured, urgent, ironic). Formality is the axis; tone is the attitude.
  2. Hedge selectively, never globally. A hedge (word 1) lowers certainty; modality (word 2) is its grammar. Hedge the bold generalisation, never the evidence. A paragraph that hedges its opening but states its synthesis (Day 30) reads as confident-and-honest; a paragraph that hedges everything reads as tentative (word 8) — the band 4–5 tell. And remember: a qualification (Day 22) is a specific kind of hedge, not the same thing.
  3. Objectivity is earned, not declared. Objectivity (word 4) comes from weighing evidence against a stated criterion (Day 31) and a clear exposition (word 7) — not from stripping all voice. Impersonality (word 5) is the grammatical help (passives, "it is argued that", Day 23's nominalisation), but pure impersonality with no stance (word 3) is the empty-essay trap. Hold a position; let the evidence speak.
  4. Be assertive, not dogmatic, not tentative. The register target is assertive (word 9): state what the text supports, hedge only the limit. This is the measured middle between over-claim (Day 29) and the tentative whisper. Audit your openers — three "perhaps / maybe / it could be argued" in a row means you are in the tentative trap. Flip them to assertive claims and the band rises.
  5. Anchor every claim evidentially. Evidential (word 11) = back the claim with a line reference, a named feature, or a contrast (Day 30's "name relation AND effect"). An assertive claim with no evidence is a floating assertion; an evidential assertion is the goal — state boldly, ground in text. This is what makes a reading tenable (Day 31) under the marker's scrutiny.
  6. Attribute before you evaluate. Attribution (word 10) lets you stage a lens (Day 24) or a critic's view before weighing it (Day 31) — "as the Marxist lens would have it…" — so the reader sees you reasoning, not parroting. In the EE, IO rationale, and HLE, attribution is mandatory; in essays it is the politeness-and-rigour move that separates discursive (word 6) argument from assertion. Name whose idea a claim is.
