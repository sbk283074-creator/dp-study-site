🔁 Day 16 First Review — 12 More Device Terms

Your first spaced pass on Day 16's device set. Read each word, recall its precise definition before looking, then check. These twelve extend your Day 15 toolkit — keep them sharp.

paradox
A self-contradictory statement that reveals a deeper truth.
synecdoche
A part standing for the whole (or vice versa).
imagery
Figurative, sense-appealing language; a text's image-pattern.
allegory
A narrative operating on both literal and symbolic levels.
antithesis
Contrasting ideas in balanced, parallel wording.
epistrophe
Repetition at the END of successive clauses.
apostrophe
Addressing an absent/abstract figure as if present.
alliteration
Repetition of initial consonant sounds.
euphemism
A mild term substituted for a harsh one.
pun
A play on similar-sounding words with different senses.
satire
A mode using ridicule to expose vice or folly.
tricolon
A series of three parallel units — the rule of three.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Structural & Narrative Terms

CONTINUING — "STRUCTURAL & NARRATIVE TERMS"

From devices to architecture. You can now name what a text says (imagery, metaphor) and how it argues (antithesis, satire). Today's twelve name how the story is built and told — time (foreshadowing, flashback, flash-forward, non-linear), framing (frame narrative, cyclic structure), and voice (unreliable narrator, stream of consciousness, interior monologue, retrospective narration), plus the turning points (epiphany, denouement). These are the backbone of prose-fiction and IOC analysis: before you can interpret a novel, you must name its machinery. By Day 21 this structural vocabulary completes your Week 3 toolkit alongside the device vocabulary.

foreshadowing /ˈfɔːʃædəʊɪŋ/ noun

Hints or clues planted early about events that will occur later in the narrative.

Literary Usage Pattern "[the] [broken mirror] [that precedes] [the accident]" · "[a] [storm] [gathers] [before] [the fall]"

"In Romeo and Juliet, the prologue's declaration that the lovers are 'star-cross'd' foreshadows their doomed end from the very first line." — Shakespeare analysis

IB Tip Distinct from flashback (see box below): foreshadowing looks forward, planting anticipation; a flashback looks back. Connect to Day 1's foreground and Day 15's motif (a recurring motif often carries foreshadowing). In narrative analysis, name the clue and what it predicts — foreshadowing is how authors engineer a sense of inevitability.

Common collocations: subtle foreshadowing · heavy foreshadowing · foreshadow an event

flashback /ˈflæʃbæk/ noun

An interruption in the story's chronology to depict an earlier event (technical term: analepsis).

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [memory] [that interrupts] [the present scene]"

"In Morrison's Beloved, Sethe's trauma surfaces through flashbacks that rupture the present tense of the narrative." — Morrison analysis

IB Tip The backward mirror of flash-forward (see box). Flashbacks reveal cause — they explain why a character is as they are. Connect to Day 15's motif (recurring flashbacks = a trauma motif). Distinguish flashback (a past scene) from retrospective narration (a present voice looking back) — both are in today's set. Name the disruption and what it explains.

Common collocations: a flashback to · open with a flashback · recurrent flashback

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: flashback vs. flash-forward

Both are temporal jumps out of the story's present; the difference is direction:

flashback (analepsis) — Jumps backward to an earlier event. Reveals cause, origin, trauma.
→ "The novel cuts to his childhood to explain the fear."

flash-forward (prolepsis) — Jumps forward to a later event. Creates dramatic irony (we know the end) or suspense.
→ "Mid-story we glimpse the empty chair where she will die."

The test: does the jump move the clock earlier (flashback) or later (flash-forward)? Both contrast with foreshadowing — that is only a hint of the future, not a full scene. In exams, naming analepsis/prolepsis shows technical precision; just don't confuse the direction. This pair anchors Day 17's time-vocabulary.

flash-forward /ˈflæʃ fɔːwəd/ noun

A scene that temporarily jumps ahead of the narrative's current time (technical term: prolepsis).

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [glimpse] [of the ending] [mid-story]"

"In Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five, the jumps to Billy's death collapse the novel's chronology and normalise the unthinkable." — Vonnegut analysis

IB Tip The forward twin of flashback (see box). Flash-forwards create dramatic irony (the reader knows the end) — link Day 15's irony. Name whether a jump is a full scene (flash-forward) or a mere hint (foreshadowing) — they're often confused by students. Flash-forwards are a hallmark of postmodern, non-linear fiction.

Common collocations: a flash-forward to · open with a flash-forward

frame narrative /freɪm ˈnærətɪv/ noun

A story that encloses one or more secondary stories, providing an outer "frame" (a tale told by a character within the tale).

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [story] [that contains] [another story]"

"In Frankenstein, Walton's letters frame Victor's confession, which frames the creature's own account — three nested layers." — Shelley analysis

IB Tip Distinct from cyclic structure (see box below). Frame narrative = a story inside a story (vertical nesting); cyclic = the ending returns to the beginning (horizontal loop). Connect to Day 1's juxtapose (the frame juxtaposes teller and tale). In comparative work, frames often signal unreliability or bias in the inner story — ask who is framing whom, and why.

Common collocations: a frame narrative · the frame story · nested frame

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: frame narrative vs. cyclic structure

Both are structural shapes, but they organise the text differently:

frame narrative — Vertical nesting: an outer story encloses an inner one (story within story). The relationship is one of containment.
→ Frankenstein: letters → Victor's tale → the creature's tale.

cyclic structure — Horizontal loop: the ending returns to or echoes the beginning. The relationship is one of return.
→ A novel that closes on the same image it opened with.

The test: does the text contain a story in a story (frame) or return to where it started (cyclic)? A frame implies a teller distinct from the tale; a cycle implies stasis, fate, or the impossibility of escape (link Day 5's transgressive — the transgressor is often trapped in the loop). Name the shape and what it asserts about change.

unreliable narrator /ʌnrɪˈlaɪəbəl næˈreɪtə/ noun

A narrator whose account the reader has reason to doubt, due to bias, limited knowledge, deception, or psychological instability.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [narrator] [whose] [version] [we must distrust]"

"In The Catcher in the Rye, Holden's evasions and contradictions make him a classic unreliable narrator — his very sincerity is the question." — Salinger analysis

IB Tip Distinct from stream of consciousness (see box below). An unreliable narrator distorts truth (deliberately or through blindness); SoC is raw, unfiltered thought (not necessarily false). Connect to Day 5's insidious / ambivalent. To prove unreliability, cite specific contradictions — don't just assert it. This is essential for prose fiction and the IOC.

Common collocations: an unreliable narrator · narrator's unreliability · increasingly unreliable

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: unreliable narrator vs. stream of consciousness

Both concern a character's interior world, but they are not the same thing:

unreliable narrator — A question of truth-value: the narrator's account cannot be trusted. The distortion may be hidden from the narrator themselves.
→ Holden (Salinger) — we can't take his self-report at face value.

stream of consciousness — A question of mode: thought rendered as a raw, unedited flow. It may be perfectly "true" to the mind, just unstructured.
→ Septimus (Woolf) — we hear thought as it arrives, associative and unmediated.

The test: is the problem that the narrator lies or misperceives (unreliable) or that the style mimics unprocessed thought (stream of consciousness)? A text can use SoC through an unreliable narrator (then we get distorted thought) — but the two are analytically separate. Naming both precisely is exactly what distinguishes a top IOC commentary.

stream of consciousness /striːm əv ˈkɒnʃəsnəs/ noun

A narrative mode presenting a character's thoughts and feelings as a continuous, often unpunctuated, unedited flow.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [running] [interior] [flow] [without logical order]"

"In Woolf's Mrs Dalloway, Septimus's sections dissolve into stream of consciousness where clocks, faces, and fears collide without logical order." — Woolf analysis

IB Tip Distinct from unreliable narrator (see box) and from interior monologue (next card). SoC mimics the mind's raw stream (associative, non-logical); interior monologue is a single voiced thought addressing itself. Connect to Day 12's texture words (fragmented, discordant) — SoC feels exactly that. Name it when syntax itself mimics thought.

Common collocations: stream-of-consciousness style · dissolve into SoC · SoC technique

interior monologue /ɪnˈtɪəriə məˈnɒlɒɡ/ noun

A character's inward thoughts rendered as if spoken to themselves, usually more ordered and grammatical than stream of consciousness.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [character] [thinking aloud] [to themselves]"

"In Joyce's 'The Dead,' Gabriel's closing reflections form an interior monologue that turns outward into snow, 'general, all over Ireland.'" — Joyce analysis

IB Tip Close cousin of stream of consciousness but more coherent and grammatical. Interior monologue = a mind addressing itself; SoC = a mind spilling. Both contrast with apostrophe (Day 16) — that addresses the absent other, not the self. Useful precision for modernist texts; name which mode a passage uses.

Common collocations: an interior monologue · sustained monologue

epiphany /ɪˈpɪfəni/ noun

A sudden moment of revelation or profound insight experienced by a character (Joyce's term for the "showing-forth").

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [sudden] [realisation] [that changes everything]"

"In Joyce's Araby, the boy's epiphany in the darkening bazaar — 'I saw myself as a creature driven and derided by vanity' — closes the story with hard self-knowledge." — Joyce analysis

IB Tip The emotional climax of a short story or coming-of-age narrative. Distinct from denouement (that is plot resolution, not insight). Connect to Day 5's ambivalent — epiphanies often resolve ambivalence into clarity. In commentary, locate the exact sentence where perception shifts; that sentence is usually the story's true centre.

Common collocations: a moment of epiphany · arrive at an epiphany

denouement /deɪˈnuːmɒ̃/ noun

The final resolution or unravelling of the plot after the climax, where loose ends are tied and the new state is revealed.

Literary Usage Pattern "[the] [falling action] [that resolves] [the conflict]"

"In Macbeth, the denouement sees Malcolm restored, the witches' prophecy fulfilled, and order re-established after the carnage." — Shakespeare analysis

IB Tip The structural close — distinct from epiphany (insight vs. plot resolution). Connect to Day 1's resolve and Day 15's motif (motifs often converge at the denouement). Name it when analysing how a text chooses to end and what that ending asserts about the world it built.

Common collocations: the denouement of · a tidy denouement

non-linear narrative /nɒn ˈlɪniə ˈnærətɪv/ noun

A storyline that does not proceed in strict chronological order, using jumps, loops, or multiple timelines.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [plot] [that] [scrambles] [time]"

"In Pulp Fiction, Tarantino's non-linear narrative reorders scenes so cause and effect must be reconstructed by the viewer." — Film narrative analysis

IB Tip The broad category that contains flashback and flash-forward (those are the techniques; non-linear is the structure). Distinct from frame narrative (see box) — non-linear scrambles the inside, frame nests a story within a story. Connect to Day 1's destabilise — non-linear form destabilises the reader's grasp of truth. Name the structure before interpreting its effect.

Common collocations: a non-linear narrative · deliberately non-linear

retrospective narration /rɛtrəˈspɛktɪv næˈreɪʃən/ noun

A narrative told by a narrator looking back on past events from a later vantage point.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [now-adult voice] [recounting] [childhood]"

"In Great Expectations, the adult Pip's retrospective narration filters his childhood through regret and grown understanding." — Dickens analysis

IB Tip Distinct from flashback (a scene that returns to the past) — retrospective narration is a whole present-tense voice situated in the future, telling of the past. Connect to unreliable narrator (a retrospective voice may be biased by hindsight). Essential precision for the first-person bildungsroman; name the gap between then-experienced and now-told.

Common collocations: retrospective narration · a retrospective narrator

cyclic structure /ˈsaɪklɪk ˈstrʌktʃə/ noun

A narrative form in which the ending returns to or echoes the beginning, creating a loop.

Literary Usage Pattern "[the] [ending] [that] [mirrors] [the opening]"

"In Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale, the closing 'Historical Notes' loop back to the opening, reframing Offred's story as recovered archive." — Atwood analysis

IB Tip Distinct from frame narrative (see box). Cyclic = horizontal return (end meets beginning); frame = vertical nesting (story in story). Cyclic structures often signal fate, stasis, or the impossibility of escape. Connect to Day 5's transgressive — a loop can trap the transgressive subject. Name the loop and what it implies about change (or its absence).

Common collocations: a cyclic structure · circular/cyclic form

🎯 Building Your Device Toolkit

  1. Day 17 completes the "how a story is told" vocabulary. Time (foreshadowing, flashback, flash-forward, non-linear), framing (frame narrative, cyclic structure), and voice (unreliable narrator, stream of consciousness, interior monologue, retrospective narration) plus turning points (epiphany, denouement). Together they let you analyse any prose text or film at the level the IOC and Paper 2 demand.
  2. Three distinction pairs anchor the day. flashback/flash-forward (direction of time-jump), unreliable narrator/stream of consciousness (truth-value vs. mode), frame narrative/cyclic structure (nesting vs. looping). These six terms form a tight, nameable cluster — master the pairings and your narrative analysis is structurally precise.
  3. Link every structural term to a Day 1-16 word. foreshadowing → foreground; non-linear → destabilise; frame → juxtapose; cyclic → transgressive; epiphany → ambivalent; unreliable → insidious. Your device and structural vocabularies should reinforce each other, not sit in separate boxes.
  4. Voice terms are where interpretation lives. unreliable narrator, stream of consciousness, and retrospective narration are not just labels — they tell you how much to trust what you're reading. In the IOC, naming the narration type is step one; explaining its effect is the mark.
  5. Epiphany and denouement are the two "ends." One is an insight (internal), the other a resolution (external). Don't confuse them: a story can have a denouement without an epiphany, and a lyric short story can have an epiphany with no tidy denouement. Name which "end" you mean.
  6. Deploy these on your next set text. When you next write on a novel or short story, force yourself to name at least four Day 17 terms precisely and trace one structural pattern (e.g., a recurring flashback, or a frame) across the whole text. Structural precision is what lifts a commentary from "what happens" to "how it's made."
