🔁 Day 18 Spaced Review — 12 Tone & Attitude Terms

A spaced-repetition pass on the tone & attitude vocabulary from Day 18 — your pragmatic foundation. These twelve describe how a speaker positions themselves and the reader; they are the direct ancestors of today's illocutionary act and discourse marker. Read each, recall its precise mechanism before looking, then check. They reappear whenever you analyse what an utterance does in a Paper 1 commentary or a dramatic scene.

connotation
The associative, implied meaning a word carries beyond its literal sense.
denotation
The literal, dictionary meaning of a word (its denotative core).
register
The level of formality / social tone of language suited to context.
subtext
What is implied beneath the surface — meaning not stated outright.
inference
A conclusion the reader draws from textual cues, not explicit statement.
modality
Degree of certainty / obligation in a verb (may, must, should).
equivocation
Deliberate ambiguity — avoiding commitment through vague language.
hedge
A cautious qualifier that softens a claim (perhaps, somewhat).
stance
The speaker's attitudinal position towards the subject / reader.
sarcasm
Saying the opposite of what is meant, with contemptuous tone.
innuendo
An indirect, suggestive remark hinting at something improper.
understatement
Deliberately representing something as less than it is.
Self-assessment: Could you separate connotation from denotation, and hedge from equivocation, without peeking? Those two pairs are the Day 18 traps. Today's illocutionary act is what happens when stance and modality are turned into action — a warning spoken as a declarative is still a threat. Keep these twelve live; they are the pragmatic soil today's syntax words grow in.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Syntax & Discourse Analysis Terms

NEW CATEGORY — "SYNTAX & DISCOURSE ANALYSIS"

From describing the word to describing the sentence and the text. Day 22 handed you the levels of language; today you learn the syntax (sentence shape) and discourse (text-as-a-whole) tools that operate on those levels. The first four words are the four sentence functions — the grammatical moods every clause belongs to. The next two name how clauses join: coordination (equal) vs. subordination (nested) — the very distinction Day 19's parataxis/hypotaxis live inside. Then discourse markers and illocutionary acts explain how texts cohere and what utterances do. The final four — nominalisation, passive, ellipsis, fronting — are the syntactic moves that repackage agency, emphasis, and pace. Together they let you answer the examiner's favourite question: why this sentence shape, and what does it enact?

declarative /dɪˈklærətɪv/ adjective / noun

The sentence function that states — a clause structured Subject + Verb (+ Complement) to present information as fact. Its default pragmatic force is assertion, but that force can be bent (a declarative can threaten, warn, or ironise).

Literary Usage Pattern "[The declarative] [states] the world as given" · "[A flat declarative] closes debate" · "[The repeated] declaratives [build] a relentless report"

"In Night, Wiesel's accumulation of bare declaratives — 'We were given no water. We were given no bread. We were given nothing' — presents atrocity as fact, not argument; the declarative shape refuses the reader the comfort of a question." — Holocaust testimony analysis

IB Tip One of the four sentence functions (with interrogative, imperative, exclamative). In IB, never just name the mood — name its force and how that force is subverted (Day 1). A string of declaratives can signal detached reportage or, via repetition, damning indictment. Link to Day 22's foregrounding: a declarative used as a threat foregrounds the gap between form and force (see distinction box 2). Root: Latin declarare, 'to make clear'.

Common collocations: declarative sentence · declarative mood · declarative statement · bare declarative · declarative force

interrogative /ˌɪntəˈrɒɡətɪv/ adjective / noun

The sentence function that asks — marked by inverted word order ('Are you…?') or a wh-word ('What…?'). Its force ranges from genuine enquiry to the rhetorical question that asserts by pretending to ask.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The interrogative] implicates the reader" · "[A rhetorical question] asserts without stating" · "[The unanswered] question [hangs]"

"In Othello, Iago's interrogative 'Did Michael Cassio, when you woo'd my lady, know of your love?' plants doubt not by accusing but by asking — the question's form lets him deny the charge while the suspicion takes root." — Othello analysis

IB Tip The power of the interrogative in literature is the rhetorical question — a question that expects no answer and thus performs an assertion (link to Day 15's rhetoric). It can implicate the reader ('Who is responsible?') or expose a character's evasion. Distinguish a real enquiry from a rhetorical one by its force, not its form — exactly the form/force seam in box 2. Pair with Day 18's stance: a question can defer or seize authority.

Common collocations: interrogative sentence · rhetorical question · interrogative mood · leading interrogative · unanswered question

imperative /ɪmˈperətɪv/ adjective / noun

The sentence function that commands or directs — a verb in its base form with the subject ('you') elided ('Look.', 'Remember.'). Its force is directive; in literature it often enacts intimacy, urgency, or the speaker's will imposed on the listener.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The imperative] drags the reader into the scene" · "[A sudden] 'Listen' [breaks] the narration" · "[The repeated] imperatives [orchestrate] the reader"

"In The Waste Land, the abrupt imperatives — 'April is the cruellest month, breeding…', 'Speak.' — yank the reader from observation into participation; the command shape enacts the poem's demand that we witness, not merely read." — The Waste Land analysis

IB Tip The imperative's elided 'you' makes it the most direct sentence function — useful for analysing dramatic monologue (Day 17) and apostrophe (Day 15), where the speaker confronts an absent other. Its force can be tender ('Remember me') or coercive ('Obey'). Connect to Day 18's stance and Day 22's deixis: an imperative relocates the deictic centre onto the addressee. Name the force (command vs. invitation) whenever you meet a bare verb.

Common collocations: imperative mood · bare imperative · directive imperative · gentle imperative · the imperative 'we'

exclamative /ɪkˈsklæmətɪv/ adjective / noun

The sentence function that exclaims — structured around 'What…!' or 'How…!' to express heightened emotion or intensity. Its force is affective: it performs feeling rather than reporting it.

Literary Usage Pattern "[What + noun phrase] expresses awe / horror" · "[How + adjective] intensifies" · "[The exclamative] performs emotion"

"In Wuthering Heights, Catherine's 'What is he, that I should fear him?' is exclamative in shape but defensive in force — the grammatical cry masks a terror she will not name directly." — Wuthering Heights analysis

IB Tip The least frequent of the four functions in literary prose, but telling where it appears: an exclamative signals a rupture of control, a leak of affect. In IB, note when a calm narrative suddenly breaks into 'What…!' / 'How…!' — that grammatical spike foregrounds (Day 22) a psychological edge. Distinguish exclamative (a grammatical mood) from mere emotional tone — force, again, is the key (box 2). Pair with Day 10's visceral / febrile.

Common collocations: exclamative sentence · exclamative force · what-/how- exclamative · sudden exclamative · exclamative mood

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: sentence function (form) vs. illocutionary force (effect)

The grammatical mood of a clause is its form; what that clause does in context is its force. They need not match:

sentence function (form) — The mood a clause is built in: declarative (states), interrogative (asks), imperative (commands), exclamative (exclaims).
→ "It is cold in here." — a declarative, formally a statement.

illocutionary force (effect) — What the utterance does: a request, a threat, a warning, an assertion.
→ "It is cold in here." spoken to an open-windowed friend = a request to close it — declarative form, requesting force.

The test: would you describe the clause by its grammar (form) or by its job (force)? A declarative can threaten; an interrogative can assert (the rhetorical question); an imperative can plead. In IB, naming the mismatch between form and force is a sophisticated analytical move — it shows you read pragmatics, not just punctuation. This seam runs straight into today's word 8, illocutionary act.

coordination /kəʊˌɔːdɪˈneɪʃn/ noun

The linking of two or more grammatically equal units by a coordinator (and, but, or, yet) — paratactic structure in which neither clause is subordinate to the other.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A and B] sit side by side, equal" · "[The coordinative chain] withholds hierarchy" · "[But] sets two clauses in tension"

"In Beloved, Morrison's coordinative 'She was my best thing and she was mine and she was alive' piles equal clauses without subordination — the parataxis (Day 19) enacts a love that refuses ranking or qualification." — Beloved analysis

IB Tip The structural twin of Day 19's parataxis (equal clauses, no linker) — coordination is simply parataxis with a linker. Distinct from subordination (box below): coordination implies equality or并列; subordination implies cause, condition, or hierarchy. Use coordination to argue a text presents events or ideas as undifferentiated or in tension. Link to Day 19's asyndeton/polysyndeton — those are coordination with the linker removed or repeated.

Common collocations: coordinate clause · coordination of equals · coordinative linkage · paratactic coordination · and/but/or

subordination /səˌbɔːdɪˈneɪʃn/ noun

The nesting of one clause inside another as dependent, introduced by a subordinator (because, although, if, that, when) — hypotactic structure that supplies cause, condition, concession, or background.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Because X], [Y]" supplies cause · "[Although P], [Q]" concedes · "[The embedded] clause [burdens] the main one"

"In Heart of Darkness, Conrad's long subordinate clauses — 'because the sun was fierce, because the river bent, because he was afraid' — pile cause upon cause, the hypotaxis (Day 19) enacting a mind that cannot separate observation from dread." — Heart of Darkness analysis

IB Tip The structural twin of Day 19's hypotaxis. Distinct from coordination (box below): subordination imposes hierarchy — one idea dominates, the other explains or qualifies it. Heavy subordination can signal a tentative, qualifying (Day 22's qualify) mind, or an evasive one that buries its main point in clauses. In IB, count subordinate clauses to argue a text is hedged, causal, or over-determined.

Common collocations: subordinate clause · subordinating conjunction · hypotactic structure · nested subordination · because/although/if

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: coordination vs. subordination

Both join clauses, but the seam is equality versus hierarchy:

coordination — Equal units joined by and / but / or (paratactic). Neither clause depends on the other; they sit side by side.
→ "He left and she wept." — two events, equal weight, no cause stated.

subordination — One clause nested inside another by because / although / if / that (hypotactic). One idea explains, qualifies, or causes the other.
→ "He left because she wept." — the 'because' clause supplies the cause; it is subordinate.

The test: does removing the link leave two independent sentences (coordination) or one broken one (subordination)? Coordination withholds cause ('he left; she wept' — you decide why); subordination supplies it. In IB, this pair is your evidence for arguing a text is paratactic (withholds judgement, fragmentary, oral) versus hypotactic (causal, controlled, literate). It is the syntax-level version of Day 19's parataxis/hypotaxis pair.

discourse marker /ˈdɪskɔːs ˈmɑːkə/ noun

A word or phrase that signals the relationship between stretches of talk or text — contrast (however, yet), addition (moreover, furthermore), causation (therefore, consequently), concession (nevertheless, still), or sequencing (first, then). They organise coherence at the discourse level.

Literary Usage Pattern "[However,] signals contrast" · "[Therefore] signals conclusion" · "[The absent] marker [withholds] the link"

"In a dramatic duologue, the sudden 'Nevertheless' a character inserts performs a concession the other cannot ignore; conversely, a speech stripped of discourse markers ('I came. I saw. I took.') enacts a will that brooks no objection." — drama / dialogue analysis

IB Tip A discourse-level tool — it works across sentences, not within them. Distinct from Day 22's cohesion (grammatical links): discourse markers are the explicit signposts of logical relation; their absence can signal fragmentation, urgency, or an unreliable narrator (Day 17). Pair with Day 18's inference — when a marker is missing, the reader must infer the relation. Name the type of marker (contrast vs. concession) for precision.

Common collocations: contrastive marker · causal marker · concessive marker · sequential marker · discourse connective

illocutionary act /ˌɪləˈkjuːʃənri ækt/ noun

An utterance's action — what it does beyond saying words: asserting, requesting, commanding, promising, warning, threatening (Austin's / Searle's speech-act theory). The pragmatic force a sentence enacts in context.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The line] performs a warning, not a statement" · "[A promise] binds the speaker" · "[The threat] hides in a declarative"

"When Lady Macbeth says 'A little water clears us of this deed', the declarative performs an assurance — an illocutionary act of comfort that the bloody facts will soon rebut; the sentence's force, not its grammar, is what Macbeth receives." — Macbeth analysis

IB Tip The technical noun for pragmatic force (distinction box 2's second term). Whenever a character 'says one thing and does another', you are describing an illocutionary act: a declarative that threatens, an interrogative that accuses. Link to Day 18's subtext and stance — the illocutionary act is subtext made active. In IB, name the act ('this performs a warning') to show you read beyond the sentence function. Root: Latin illocutio, 'speaking-on'.

Common collocations: speech act · illocutionary force · perform an act · assertive / directive · perlocutionary effect

nominalisation /ˌnɒmɪnəlaɪˈzeɪʃn/ noun

The syntactic process of turning a verb or adjective into a noun ('decide' → 'the decision'; 'stable' → 'stability') — packing a process or quality into a noun phrase, raising abstraction and often obscuring who did what.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The nominalised] process hides the agent" · "[Heavy nominalisation] raises register" · "[The] 'decision' [erases] who decided"

"In bureaucratic or dystopian prose — 'The relocation of the subjects was deemed necessary' — nominalisation ('relocation', 'deemed necessity') erases the humans who moved and decided, the abstract nouns performing the regime's cold indifference." — dystopian / institutional analysis

IB Tip A syntactic-level move with ideological weight. Nominalisation raises register (Day 18) and distances the reader from action and agency — perfect for analysing official, scientific, or oppressive voices (Orwell's Newspeak is built on it). Distinct from passivisation (box 3): nominalisation makes the process a thing; passivisation keeps it a verb but hides the agent. Both reduce 'who does what'. Link to Day 1's obfuscate.

Common collocations: nominalised process · heavy nominalisation · agent-less nominalisation · abstract noun · raise the register

passive /ˈpæsɪv/ adjective / noun

The voice in which the object of an action becomes the subject and the agent is demoted or deleted ('The window was broken' vs. 'He broke the window'). The process remains a verb; the doer is moved or removed.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The agent-less passive] conceals responsibility" · "[The passive] universalises suffering" · "[Switch to passive] distances the speaker"

"In war testimony, 'We were taken, we were separated, we were not told' — the passive voice deletes the captors, the universal 'we' + agent-less verb enacting victimhood as a condition rather than an event with perpetrators." — testimony / trauma narrative

IB Tip Voice is a syntactic choice with ethical force. A passive can conceal blame (political evasion) or universalise experience (shared suffering) — intent is in context. Distinct from nominalisation (box 3): passive keeps the verb and only hides the agent; nominalisation turns the act into a noun. Connect to Day 1's obfuscate and Day 18's stance — a speaker who 'we were affected' rather than 'they did' manages responsibility. Always state why the agent is hidden.

Common collocations: passive voice · agent-less passive · passive construction · by-agent phrase · active-to-passive shift

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: nominalisation vs. passivisation

Both are syntactic moves that reduce the visibility of who does what, but they do it by different grammar:

nominalisation — Turns the process into a noun; the action becomes a thing, the agent optional and distant.
→ "The decision to relocate was made." — 'decide' is now a noun; no one is named.

passive — Keeps the action a verb but moves/deletes the agent; the object becomes subject.
→ "The families were relocated." — 'relocate' stays a verb; the relocators are hidden, not abolished.

The test: is the action now a noun (nominalisation) or still a verb with a missing subject (passive)? Both raise abstraction and obscure agency, which is why they co-occur in bureaucratic, scientific, and oppressive discourse — but naming which you see is the analytical precision examiners reward. Used together they are the syntax of evasion; used in poetry they can ache with distance ('the leaving', 'we were left').

ellipsis /ɪˈlɪpsɪs/ noun

The deliberate omission of a word or phrase recoverable from context — 'She went to Paris; he [went] to Rome' — which speeds pace, implies shared knowledge, or stages a gap the reader must fill.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The omitted verb] speeds the list" · "[Ellipsis of the subject] implies complicity" · "[The gap] implicates the reader"

"In The Bell Jar, Plath's clipped ellipses — 'I saw the world divide into two. The sick. The well.' — the missing verb between clauses stages a fracture the narrator cannot, or will not, grammatically bridge." — The Bell Jar analysis

IB Tip A syntactic gap. Unlike Day 19's asyndeton (missing linkers), ellipsis omits a recoverable word (verb, subject). It can signal intimacy ('you know what I mean'), haste, or trauma (a thought too painful to finish). Pair with Day 17's stream of consciousness — broken syntax there is often elliptical. In IB, treat an ellipsis as a foregrounded (Day 22) gap: what is being left unsaid, and who is complicit in the silence? Not to be confused with the punctuation mark of the same name.

Common collocations: syntactic ellipsis · verb ellipsis · elliptical gap · recoverable omission · elliptical style

fronting /ˈfrʌntɪŋ/ noun

The movement of a normally later element to the front of the clause for emphasis or topicalisation — 'Him I trust'; 'Never have I seen…' — so the promoted item carries information focus and weight.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The fronted object] becomes the topic" · "[Inversion] after negative fronting" · "[Fronting] dramatises the word"

"In Jane Eyre, the line 'Reader, I married him' fronts the object ('him') before the verb — the syntactic promotion enacts Jane's claim of agency: she is the subject who acts, and Rochester is the destined object, not the other way." — Jane Eyre analysis

IB Tip A syntactic reordering for emphasis — a special case of Day 19's anastrophe (inversion) with a pragmatic purpose: topicalisation. Fronting tells the reader 'attend to this first'. Use it to argue a text foregrounds (Day 22) a particular idea by moving it to clause-initial position. Connect to Day 18's stance — what a speaker chooses to put first reveals what they privilege. Name the promoted element and its new focus.

Common collocations: topicalisation · fronted object · negative fronting · information focus · clause-initial emphasis

✏️ Practice — Name the Function, Trace the Force

Four drills. Write short answers; the model notes follow each.

  1. Function & force. For each clause, name its sentence function (declarative / interrogative / imperative / exclamative) and its likely illocutionary force:
    a) "Shut the door." → imperative form → directive (command) force; b) "Is nobody going to help?" → interrogative form → rhetorical assertion / accusation force; c) "What a fool I have been!" → exclamative form → affective self-indictment force; d) "The train leaves at nine." → declarative form → informative (assertive) force.
  2. Join the clauses. Rewrite the pair below once with coordination and once with subordination, then state what each version implies:
    Pair "She loved the sea. She feared it."
    Model: Coordination — "She loved the sea, yet she feared it." (equal tensions, no cause given). Subordination — "Although she loved the sea, she feared it." (fear is subordinated to, qualified by, love). The hypotactic version supplies a concession the paratactic one withholds.
  3. Name the syntactic move. Identify the device in each and say what it enacts:
    Passage "The decision was made. The families were moved. Him they never forgave."
    Model: clause 1 = nominalisation ('the decision') erases who decided; clause 2 = agent-less passive ('were moved') conceals the movers; clause 3 = fronting ('Him' before 'they never forgave') promotes the victim to topic, foregrounding (Day 22) the enduring grievance. Each syntax choice manages agency and focus.
  4. Distinguish on demand. In one sentence each, keep these Day 23 pairs exam-ready: (i) coordination vs. subordination (equal link vs. nested dependent); (ii) nominalisation vs. passive (act becomes a noun vs. act stays a verb with hidden agent); (iii) sentence function vs. illocutionary force (grammatical mood vs. what it does). Stack them beside the twelve Week 3 distinctions.

🎯 Study Tips — Reading the Sentence as Argument

  1. Always pair function with force. "Declarative" tells the examiner the grammar; "this declarative performs a threat" tells them the pragmatics. The mismatch — a question that asserts, a statement that commands — is where the sophistication lives. This is the single most under-used move in student commentaries.
  2. Coordination withholds; subordination supplies. A paratactic ('and') text makes you decide the relation; a hypotactic ('because') text decides it for you. Count the linkers to argue a voice is oral/fragmentary versus controlled/causal. This is Day 19's parataxis/hypotaxis reborn at the clause level.
  3. Nominalisation and passive are the syntax of agency. Whenever you cannot tell who did what, look for a noun where a verb should be, or a missing 'by'. Name the concealment; it is almost never accidental in literature — it is stance (Day 18), evasion, or ideology.
  4. Discourse markers are the text's logical skeleton. Their presence signals explicit reasoning; their absence signals fragmentation, urgency, or an unreliable narrator who won't sign the links. Map them to argue coherence (Day 22) is built or withheld.
  5. Fronting and ellipsis are emphasis by shape. What a writer puts first, or leaves out, is a deliberate focus decision. Treat a fronted object or an elliptical gap as a foregrounded (Day 22) item — ask what is being promoted or silenced, not just what is said.
  6. Syntax is never neutral. Every sentence-shape choice enacts stance (Day 18) and foregrounds (Day 22) meaning. The examiner's question is always "why this shape?" — today's twelve words are your vocabulary for answering it. Frame them on Week 3's devices and Day 22's levels, and the sentence becomes your evidence, not your backdrop.
