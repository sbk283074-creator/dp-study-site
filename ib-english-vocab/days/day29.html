🔁 Day 22 Spaced Review — 12 Stylistic & Linguistic / Argumentative Terms

A 7-day spaced-repetition pass on the metalanguage and argumentative frame from Day 22 — the exact toolkit today's synthesis words will operate on. Read each, recall its precise mechanism before looking, then check. These are the words that hold your Week 3 devices together; they must be instantaneous before Week 5's application work begins.

cohesion
The grammatical/lexical links (reference, conjunction, repetition) binding a text on the page.
coherence
The semantic sense a reader constructs, even without those links.
deixis
Context-dependent shifters (I / here / now) anchoring language to the speaker's situation.
lexical field
A network of semantically related words clustered around a domain, accumulating meaning.
foregrounding
The effect when language deviates from norm, making an item salient — why a device matters.
intonation
The pitch contour carrying attitude and force beyond the literal words.
thesis
The specific, provable claim an essay argues.
contention
The broader debatable stance a writer enters.
premise
The proposition an argument logically rests on.
qualify
To restrict a claim's scope so it survives counter-evidence.
concede
To grant a point to the opposing view before resuming one's own.
rebut
To counter an opposing claim with evidence.
Self-assessment: Could you separate cohesion from coherence (page-level link vs. reader-built sense), and thesis from contention (specific provable claim vs. broad debatable stance), without peeking? Those two pairs anchor Day 22. Today's words — assert, refute, corroborate, validate — sit directly on top of this frame: you assert a thesis, you refute a contention, you corroborate a premise. Keep the frame live; the synthesis words are useless without it.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Application & Synthesis Terms

NEW CATEGORY — "APPLICATION & SYNTHESIS"

From naming the text to steering the argument. Day 22's twelve words gave you the frame (thesis, premise, qualify, concede, rebut). Today's twelve give you the moves that frame performs in motion — the active verbs and nouns of argumentation and synthesis. Four build and defend a claim (assert, refute, corroborate, validate); four organise comparison and resolution (juxtapose, analogous, reconcile, dialectic); four refine judgement and reasoning (nuance, mitigate, problematic, extrapolate). Together they are the difference between a commentary that lists devices and one that argues with them — the signature of the top band in both Paper 1 and Paper 2.

assert /əˈsɜːt/ verb

To state a position or claim firmly and explicitly as one's own — the active act of putting forward a proposition to be argued.

Literary Usage Pattern "[This essay asserts] that [X]" · "[The speaker asserts] agency [through…]" · "[I assert] the reading that…"

"Where a weak essay merely 'suggests', a 7-band commentary asserts: 'The text asserts the irreducibility of grief to language' — the verb stakes a claim the evidence must then earn." — essay / commentary framing

IB Tip Distinct from refute (word 2): you assert your own claim; you refute an opponent's. Assert is the thesis-planting move — pair it with qualify (Day 22) so the assertion is scoped, not bald. Connects to Day 1's contend / argue. In drama, a character who asserts controls the conversation — name it when analysing dialogue power. Root: Latin asserere, 'to claim'.

Common collocations: assert a claim · strongly assert · assert one's position · assert agency · the text asserts

refute /rɪˈfjuːt/ verb

To prove a claim false or unsustainable by counter-evidence or logic — the active dismissal of an opposing position.

Literary Usage Pattern "[This view is refuted by] [evidence Y]" · "[The passage refutes] the reading that…" · "[X refutes] Y"

"A weak essay merely disagrees; a strong one refutes: 'The bland "happy ending" reading is refuted by the final image of the unopened letter, which withholds the reconciliation the plot seemed to promise.'" — counter-argument framing

IB Tip Distinct from concede / rebut (Day 22): concede yields a point, rebut counters and resumes, refute demolishes. Refutation must meet the claim on its own ground with evidence, not assertion. Connects to Day 1's subvert / undermine. In Paper 2, refuting a critical position shows command of the global argument. Pair assert → refute as your claim-and-defend spine.

Common collocations: refute a claim · refute the argument · directly refute · refute the reading · refute a misconception

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: assert vs. refute

Both are argumentative moves, but they point in opposite directions:

assert — Creation. You put your own claim forward to be argued.
→ "This essay asserts that grief is figured as chosen, not inflicted."

refute — Demolition. You knock an opposing claim down with evidence.
→ "The 'passive victim' reading is refuted by the syntax of self-blame."

The test: are you building your case (assert) or tearing down another's (refute)? They are not opposites in the sense of cancelling each other — you can assert without refuting, and refute without stating your own alternative. In IB, a mature paragraph often does both: assert a thesis early, then refute the predictable counter-reading before resuming. Used together they are the architecture of a 7.

corroborate /kəˈrɒbəreɪt/ verb

To confirm or lend support to a claim with additional, independent evidence — strengthening a position by the convergence of sources or details.

Literary Usage Pattern "[This detail corroborates] the reading that…" · "[Two passages corroborate] each other" · "[The imagery corroborates] the thesis"

"In a Paper 2, the recurring lexical field of 'light' in Text A corroborates the 'blindness' motif in Text B — the two texts independently point to the same conclusion about perception, and the correspondence is itself evidence." — comparative essay

IB Tip The collaborative cousin of refute. Where refute knocks down, corroborate builds up by agreement across evidence. Use it to show your reading is not isolated: when two details — or two texts — agree, name the corroboration. Links to Day 26's semantic field / collocation: shared fields corroborate. In comparative essays, 'corroborate' is the hinge word that joins the two texts. Distinct from validate (box below). Root: Latin corroborare, 'to strengthen'.

Common collocations: corroborate a claim · mutually corroborate · corroborating evidence · corroborate the thesis · evidence corroborates

validate /ˈvælɪdeɪt/ verb

To demonstrate that a claim, feeling, or reading is legitimate or well-founded — to show a position holds up under scrutiny.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The structure validates] the interpretation that…" · "[This reading is validated by] [the form]" · "[X validates] Y"

"The sonnet's volta does not merely restate the premise; it validates it — the turn from doubt to conviction enacts, in form, the very argument the poem has been building." — poetic structure analysis

IB Tip Distinct from corroborate (box below): corroborate = adds supporting evidence from elsewhere; validate = shows the claim is sound on its own terms, often via the text's own form or logic. Both build; validate is internal, corroborate external. Use validate when you show a reading 'works' because the text's structure demands it. Pair with Day 22's premise — a valid argument follows from a sound premise.

Common collocations: validate a claim · validate the reading · validate an interpretation · validate one's experience · the form validates

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: corroborate vs. validate

Both strengthen a claim, but the source of the strength differs:

corroborate — External. Adds independent evidence from elsewhere (another detail, another text) that agrees.
→ "Text B's 'blindness' field corroborates Text A's thesis."

validate — Internal. Shows the claim is sound on the text's own terms (its form, its logic).
→ "The volta validates the reading — the form enacts the argument."

The test: is the support outside the claim (corroborate) or inside it (validate)? In a comparative essay you corroborate across texts; in a single-text commentary you validate through structure. Using both precisely tells the examiner you know where your evidence lives — a subtle but real top-band signal.

juxtapose /ˌdʒʌkstəˈpəʊz/ verb

To place two or more items side by side so that their contrast or comparison produces meaning — the compositional act of putting X against Y.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The poet juxtaposes] [image A] with [image B]" · "[Juxtaposed], the scenes [mean]" · "[The extract juxtaposes] [past and present]"

"In Carol Ann Duffy's monologues, the mundane 'cooking, cleaning' is juxtaposed with the mythic — the gap between them is the poem's whole point about ordinary women erased from history." — The World's Wife analysis

IB Tip The core comparative verb. In Paper 2 you do not 'compare' vaguely; you juxtapose specific features ('Text A's claustrophobic deixis juxtaposed with Text B's open spatial field'). Distinct from analogous (box below): juxtapose stages contrast; analogous marks similarity. Links to Day 15's contrast and the critical lenses. The juxtaposition is only half the work — you must then state its effect for it to count as analysis.

Common collocations: juxtapose A with B · sharp juxtaposition · juxtapose past and present · visual juxtaposition · the juxtaposition of

analogous /əˈnæləɡəs/ adjective

Similar in a relevant structural or functional respect, such that a mapping from one case to another clarifies both.

Literary Usage Pattern "[X is analogous to] [Y in that…]" · "[An analogous movement occurs] in…" · "[The structure is analogous]"

"The entrapment of the narrator in Text A is analogous to the speaker's paralysis in Text B — not identical, but the same underlying logic of confinement, which lets a reading of one illuminate the other." — comparative analysis

IB Tip Distinct from juxtapose (box below): analogous claims similarity; juxtapose stages contrast. In comparative work, 'analogous' is your word for 'these two are usefully alike' without collapsing their differences. Guard against false analogy — the similarity must be structural, not superficial. Connects to Day 26's semantic field (shared fields often signal analogy). Used well, it is the bridge between two set-texts in Paper 2.

Common collocations: analogous to · strikingly analogous · an analogous structure · analogous movement · broadly analogous

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: juxtapose vs. analogous

Both organise comparison, but they organise it around opposite relations:

juxtapose — Places items together to highlight difference / contrast.
→ "The mythic is juxtaposed with the domestic — the gap is the point."

analogous — Marks items as similar in a relevant respect.
→ "The two speakers' paralysis is analogous — same logic of confinement."

The test: am I stressing the gap (juxtapose) or the resonance (analogous)? A comparative paragraph usually needs both — juxtapose to expose difference, mark analogy to build a shared reading. Confusing them is a common slip: do not call a contrast 'analogous', nor a similarity a 'juxtaposition'. This pair is exam armour for Paper 2.

reconcile /ˈrekənsaɪl/ verb

To show how apparently contradictory readings, details, or positions can coexist or be resolved — to hold tension without flattening it.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The essay reconciles] [reading A] with [reading B]" · "[These need not be reconciled] if…" · "[X reconciles] the two impulses"

"A sophisticated commentary reconciles the text's bleak ending with its moments of tenderness: the tenderness is not a denial of bleakness but its human cost, and holding both is the point." — interpretive synthesis

IB Tip The move that separates a 7 from a 5. Where amateur analysis picks one reading and dismisses the other, you reconcile — show how both are true at different levels. Links to Day 1's nuance / problematize and Day 22's qualify. In multi-lens work, reconcile a feminist and a Marxist reading rather than choosing. Avoid forced reconciliation that erases real contradiction — sometimes texts refuse to be reconciled, and naming that is itself insight.

Common collocations: reconcile two readings · difficult to reconcile · reconcile A with B · reconcile the contradiction · cannot be reconciled

dialectic /ˌdaɪəˈlektɪk/ noun

A method or dynamic in which opposing positions engage and are resolved or advanced into a synthesis — the productive tension of thesis meeting antithesis.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A dialectic between] [X and Y] drives…" · "[The text stages] a dialectic of…" · "[The essay deploys] a dialectic"

"The novel's structure enacts a dialectic between imprisonment and escape: each escape attempt generates a tighter confinement, so the two terms define each other rather than cancel." — narrative structure analysis

IB Tip Naming 'dialectic' lets you describe a text as thinking through conflict, not merely containing it. Distinct from 'debate' (which stays unresolved) — a dialectic moves toward synthesis (see word 7). Connects to Hegelian critical theory (Day 24) and to Day 22's concede–rebut architecture. In Paper 2, framing your comparison as a dialectic between the two texts is a top-band organisational move.

Common collocations: dialectic between · a dialectic of · stages a dialectic · Hegelian dialectic · resolve the dialectic

nuance /ˈnjuːɑːns/ noun / verb

A subtle distinction or gradation of meaning, tone, or attitude; as a verb, to refine or qualify a claim with such distinctions.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The nuance lies in] [the shift from… to…]" · "[To nuance the claim]: [X, though not Y]" · "[A crucial nuance]"

"The difference between the two speakers is a nuance of register, not of vocabulary: both use 'love', but one's ironic intonation (Day 22) hollows it out while the other's asserts it — the word is identical, the meaning opposite." — tone & register analysis

IB Tip The word that names precision itself. In IB, 'nuanced' is the highest-praise adjective; deploying 'nuance' (and its verb form) shows you can hold fine discriminations — exactly Day 1's nuanced trait made operational. Pair with qualify (Day 22): to nuance is to qualify with detail. Guard against using it as a vague compliment; always specify which nuance. Links to Day 18's connotation / register.

Common collocations: a crucial nuance · nuance of tone · nuanced reading · nuance the claim · subtle nuance

mitigate /ˈmɪtɪɡeɪt/ verb

To reduce the force, severity, or persuasiveness of something — typically used to lessen the impact of an opposing point or a damaging reading.

Literary Usage Pattern "[This detail mitigates] the charge that…" · "[The ending mitigates] the bleakness" · "[X mitigates] Y"

"One might read the protagonist's silence as complicity; yet the framing of that silence — low-angle, held — mitigates the charge, relocating agency from absence to withheld speech." — counter-reading

IB Tip The soft-counterpart of refute (word 2). You do not always demolish the counter-claim; sometimes you mitigate it — show it is weaker than it first appears. Distinct from concede (Day 22): concede yields the point; mitigate shrinks it. A concede–mitigate–rebut sequence is a complete counter-argument architecture. Connects to Day 1's qualified / measured. Use when the opposition has real force you cannot simply dismiss.

Common collocations: mitigate the charge · mitigate the effect · partly mitigate · mitigate a reading · serve to mitigate

problematic /ˌprɒbləˈmætɪk/ adjective

Open to objection or critique; conceptually or ideologically contestable — not 'wrong' but requiring interrogation.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The representation of X is] problematic because…" · "[A problematic assumption] underlies…" · "[This is problematic] in its…"

"The text's framing of the servant's dialect (Day 26) is problematic: it marks class through language in a way that risks naturalising the very hierarchy the novel elsewhere critiques — a contradiction worth interrogating, not overlooking." — critical-theory reading

IB Tip Your critical-theory verb (Day 24 lenses deploy through it). 'Problematic' signals you are applying a lens — feminist, Marxist, postcolonial, psychoanalytic — to expose ideology, not merely noting a flaw. Distinct from 'wrong': problematic invites analysis of why and how the text does the thing. Links to Day 1's interrogate / problematize. In IB, calling something problematic and then showing the mechanism is the move that earns critical-perceptive marks.

Common collocations: problematic representation · a problematic assumption · ideologically problematic · problematic because · deeply problematic

extrapolate /ɪkˈstræpəleɪt/ verb

To infer or project a general principle or conclusion from specific textual evidence — to reason outward from the instance to the claim.

Literary Usage Pattern "[From this detail we can extrapolate] that…" · "[The pattern extrapolates to] the whole text" · "[Extrapolating]: [X implies Y]"

"From the single repeated image of the locked door, the essay extrapolates a governing metaphor of psychological enclosure that reorganises the entire narrative — one detail, properly read, opens the whole." — close-reading to thesis

IB Tip The reasoning verb that connects close reading to thesis. You 'extrapolate' when a local feature licenses a global claim ('this comma implies this worldview'). Distinct from 'generalise' (which risks losing specificity) — extrapolation keeps the evidence visible as its ground. Links to Day 1's infer / deduce and Day 22's premise. In Paper 1, extrapolation is the bridge from 'what the text does' to 'what it means'. Pair with qualify so the extrapolation is scoped, not overblown.

Common collocations: extrapolate from · extrapolate a principle · extrapolate to · extrapolate the pattern · can be extrapolated

✏️ Practice — Deploy, Don't Just Name

Four drills. Write short answers; the model notes follow each.

  1. Word-bank insertion. Choose the correct Day 29 word for each gap (each used once):
    Word bank: assert · refute · corroborate · validate · juxtapose · analogous · reconcile · dialectic · nuance · mitigate · problematic · extrapolate
    a) The two texts' shared 'light' imagery ____________ each other's reading of perception. → corroborate
    b) The sonnet's volta ____________ the interpretation that doubt gives way to conviction. → validate
    c) A Marxist reading and a feminist reading can be ____________ rather than set against one another. → reconciled
    d) From the locked-door motif we can ____________ a metaphor of enclosure for the whole text. → extrapolate
    e) The servant's dialect is ____________ because it risks naturalising the class hierarchy. → problematic
    f) The mundane is ____________ with the mythic to expose the gap. → juxtaposed
  2. Mini synthesis. On the passage below, deploy at least three Day 29 words and state each one's effect:
    Passage "I am not your mirror. I am the woman who cooked, who waited, who watched you become a myth. You wrote me small; I write myself large."
    Model: the speaker asserts agency by rejecting the 'mirror' role (the verb stakes a claim the rest earns); the domestic 'cooked / waited / watched' is juxtaposed with 'myth', exposing the gap between erasure and self-making; the two clauses stage a dialectic of silence and voice that the final sentence resolves into assertion. Each move is tied to an effect, not just named.
  3. Frame the claim. Turn this blunt thesis into a 7-band version by (a) asserting it, (b) qualifying it (Day 22), and (c) adding a concede–mitigate–rebut beat:
    Blunt "The poem is about women being silenced."
    Model: Asserted + qualified — "This essay asserts that the poem figures female silence as chosen rather than inflicted, though only insofar as that choice is forged under constraint." Concede–mitigate–rebut — "Admittedly the speaker is literally unheard; yet the form mitigates that charge by giving her the final word, which refutes any reading of pure victimhood." The claim is now arguable, scoped, and defended.
  4. Distinguish on demand. In one sentence each, separate these two Day 29 pairs you will be tested on: (i) assert vs. refute (create your claim vs. demolish an opposing one); (ii) juxtapose vs. analogous (staged contrast vs. marked similarity). Keep both pairs in your exam armour alongside the Day 22 distinctions.

🎯 Study Tips — Making the Moves Automatic

  1. Assert your claim; don't merely suggest. "This suggests that…" is a 5; "This essay asserts that…" is a 7. The verb signals ownership of the argument — and it sets up the concede–rebut architecture that follows.
  2. Refute on the opponent's ground, with evidence. A rebuttal that only disagrees is weak; one that meets the counter-claim where it stands and answers it with a textual detail is what examiners reward. Refute is demolition done precisely.
  3. Corroborate across texts; validate on internal form. In Paper 2, use corroborate to join your two set-texts; in a single-text commentary, use validate to show the reading follows from the text's own structure. Knowing which verb fits tells the examiner where your evidence lives.
  4. Juxtapose for contrast, mark analogy for similarity. A comparative paragraph usually needs both — expose the gap, then build the resonance. Confusing them (calling a contrast 'analogous') is a slip that costs the top band. These two are your Paper 2 hinge words.
  5. Reconcile contradictions; don't flatten them. Holding two readings at once — and showing how both are true at different levels — is the single highest-leverage move in interpretive work. Forced reconciliation that erases real tension is the failure mode to avoid.
  6. These twelve words make your argument move; pair every one with a device or detail. 'Juxtapose' without the images, 'extrapolate' without the detail, 'validate' without the form — these are empty. Every synthesis verb should hang a Week 3 device or a textual fact on it. The move without the evidence is scaffolding with no building.
