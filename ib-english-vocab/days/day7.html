🔥 Day 5 Active Recall — 2nd Pass

These are last Wednesday's evaluative adjectives. Day 5's first exposure was 5 days ago; this is the second retrieval — the forgetting-curve's hardest gate. For each, recall the full definition before clicking to reveal, then answer the deeper question. Anything you can't answer cold re-enters tomorrow's warm-up.

polemical "Her polemical essay attacks the policy head-on…"
Expressing strong, controversial opinions intended to provoke debate or attack opposing views; combative in tone.
How does "polemical" differ from "tendentious"? (Hint: polemical wears its bias openly; tendentious hides bias behind a mask of neutrality.)
subversive "The subversive framing erodes the reader's faith…"
Intending to undermine or overthrow established authority/norms, often by indirect means.
subversive vs. satirical? (subversion = the aim of toppling power; satire = the method of mockery. A text can be one without the other.)
satirical "A sustained satirical allegory exposing the pigs' corruption…"
Using humour, irony, exaggeration, or ridicule to expose and criticise folly or vice.
Is every satirical text subversive? (No — establishment-approved satire mocks safely; subversion seeks to topple.)
allegorical "The allegorical knights embody virtues in conflict…"
Operating as an allegory — a narrative carrying a second, symbolic level of meaning.
What is the danger of over-reading a text as allegorical? (It can become "reductive" — collapsing every detail into a single symbol.)
tendentious "The tendentious editing makes the case seem inevitable…"
Biased or partisan while appearing objective; the bias is disguised.
Where do you most often catch tendentious framing? (Unreliable narrators, documentaries, propaganda — anywhere neutrality is performed.)
reductive "Reading it merely as anti-war is reductive…"
Oversimplifying; flattening complex reality into an overly simple account that loses nuance.
What is the direct opposite of reductive? (nuanced — the IB gold-standard adjective.)
insidious "The insidious appeal of the Party lies in its banality…"
Proceeding harmfully in a subtle, stealthy, gradual way; treacherous because unnoticed.
insidious vs. subversive? (subversion = the force undermining; insidious = the quality of that creep — stealthy and gradual.)
transgressive "The transgressive desire of Gothic heroines…"
Violating or crossing accepted boundaries, norms, or moral codes.
transgressive vs. subversive? (transgression = crossing a taboo; subversion = overthrowing a system. A desire can be transgressive without toppling anything.)
provocative "Kafka's provocative opening implicates the reader…"
Deliberately stimulating strong reaction, thought, or controversy.
provocative vs. polemical? (provocative jolts without committing to a stance; polemical argues a position aggressively.)
ambivalent "The play is deeply ambivalent about empire…"
Holding mixed, contradictory feelings or attitudes at once; attracted and repelled simultaneously.
Why is spotting ambivalence a sign of sophisticated reading? (It shows you've moved past "the text says X" to "the text holds contradiction.")
didactic "Unapologetically didactic, yet emotionally forceful…"
Intended to teach or instruct, especially morally; can be neutral or slightly critical (preachy).
When does didactic intent succeed vs. fail as literature? (Succeeds when the lesson is earned through feeling; fails when teaching overwhelms art.)
nuanced "A nuanced portrayal refuses easy judgement…"
Characterised by subtle distinction and sensitivity to complexity; holding contradiction rather than resolving it.
Why is "nuanced" the single most valuable adjective in your IB toolkit? (Examiners reward it; it is the opposite of the reductive readings they penalise.)
Self-assessment: How many of the 12 could you define and answer the deeper question for, cold? If 3+ were shaky, those words re-enter your Day 8 warm-up. The second pass is where recognition becomes ownership — push through it before the essay.

WEEK 1 ASSESSMENT — PURE APPLICATION

THE TASK

Write one sustained analytical paragraph of 250–350 words on a literary text you know well (or the unseen extract below). Your goal is to deploy as many Week 1 words as possible — correctly and naturally. The paragraph must:

  • use at least 4 analysis verbs (Days 1–2) to say what the text does;
  • use at least 4 craft verbs (Days 3–4) to say how it is built;
  • use at least 4 evaluative adjectives (Day 5) to judge the effect;
  • and respect the 9 distinction clusters — never swap near-synonyms that the plan told you apart.

Option B — Unseen Extract (IO-style stretch)

"The lighthouse had always kept its own hours. Maren returned each summer to find the paint peeled a little further, the gulls bolder, the silence thicker. Her grandfather's maps still hung crooked on the wall, plotting channels that no longer existed. She told herself the place was unchanged; the tide, indifferent, disagreed. At night the lamp stuttered, and for a moment the whole coastline seemed to hold its breath — as if the island were waiting to be remembered, or forgiven."

If you choose this extract, write your paragraph on how the writer crafts atmosphere and foregrounds memory — without ever naming the feeling directly.

A Model Paragraph (how the words should sit inside real analysis)

Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale is a fundamentally subversive text that works to undermine the reader's complacency about how swiftly rights erode. By foregrounding Offred's mundane domestic voice, the novel deploys a deliberately subdued register that gradually suffuses the scene with quiet dread; an insidious atmosphere of surveillance permeates every chapter. Atwood crafts the narrative so that seemingly minor images — the red of the dresses, the wall of the dismissed — are woven into a larger allegorical critique of patriarchal control, while the blood motif is threaded throughout to underscore the body's political claims. The text provocatively juxtaposes Offred's inward meditation with the stark rituals of Gilead, and this contrast elucidates the gap between official ideology and lived experience. Rather than offering a reductive portrait of victimhood, Atwood renders Offred as ambivalent: complicit yet resistant, so the reader must hold contradiction rather than resolve it. The novel's power is nuanced — it neither didactically lectures nor polemically rants, but calibrates its revelations so that horror accumulates through layering rather than shock. In this way the text destabilises any stable moral position, inviting us to interrogate our own comfort with the ordinary face of oppression.

— 26 Week 1 words deployed in ~230 words. Note how each verb carries its specific metaphor and each adjective earns its judgement.

Self-Marking Rubric (Criterion A/D style)

  1. Accuracy (owning the word): Is each word used with its precise meaning and metaphor? (e.g. "suffuse" needs an atmosphere as subject; "infuse" needs an author as agent.) Deduct for any word used vaguely or wrongly.
  2. Range across registers: Did you use analysis verbs + craft verbs + evaluative adjectives, not just one family? A paragraph of only craft verbs is one-dimensional.
  3. Distinction discipline: Did you respect the 9 clusters? Penalise yourself if you swapped weave/thread, infuse/suffuse, permeate/saturate, calibrate/modulate, construct/arrange, hone/sharpen, polemical/tendentious, satirical/subversive, or reductive/nuanced.
  4. Naturalness: Do the words sit inside the argument, or do they stick out like ornaments? Forced deployment reads as padding — rewrite those sentences.
  5. Judgement earned: Are your evaluative adjectives backed by evidence from the text, not asserted bare? "Nuanced" must be shown, not just claimed.

Score yourself: 5/5 = the words are now yours · 3–4/5 = solid, a few to fix · below 3 = redo the paragraph tomorrow before Day 8.

Distinction Quick-Reference (the 9 Week 1 clusters)

⚠️ Don't let assessment day be where the near-synonyms collapse

Day 3: weave = many strands into cloth · thread = one strand through a text · infuse = author puts a quality in · suffuse = atmosphere spreads on its own.
Day 4: permeate = spread through · saturate = fill to capacity · calibrate = precise tuning · modulate = variation/shift · construct = build the whole · arrange = order the parts · hone = perfect over time · sharpen = intensify at a moment.
Day 5: polemical = open attack · tendentious = disguised bias · satirical = mocks (method) · subversive = topples (aim) · reductive = flattens · nuanced = expands.

🎯 What Today Proves

  1. Recognition ≠ ownership. You could probably define all 60 words by now. Today tested whether you can produce them under pressure — the IO and Paper 2 skill. If the paragraph felt stiff, that's normal: active deployment is a separate muscle from passive recognition, and it strengthens with each assessment.
  2. The three registers must interlock. Strong analysis sentences pair an analysis verb (what the text does), a craft verb (how it's built), and an evaluative adjective (whether it works). The model paragraph does exactly this in every sentence. Train that triad.
  3. Distinction discipline is the difference between 5 and 7. Examiners notice when a student uses "weave" where "thread" is precise, or calls a text "nuanced" when the evidence shows it's "reductive." The 9 clusters are your precision edge — guard them even under timed pressure.
  4. Assessment days are not a break — they're the test of the system. Weeks 2–16 will each close with a Day-7-style assessment. Treat today as the template: recall → deploy → self-mark → fix. That loop is what makes the vocabulary stick for the final exams.
  5. Carry the 60 into your real schoolwork this week. In your next IO practice or essay, consciously deploy 5–10 of these words. Transfer from the plan to actual graded work is the whole point — otherwise the list stays decorative.
