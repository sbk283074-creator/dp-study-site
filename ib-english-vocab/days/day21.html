✍️ Guided Mini-Analysis — Model & Rubric

The capstone skill is deploying these terms on a real passage. Below: a model paragraph that names terms from all four pillars. Highlighted = Week 3 vocabulary.

A novel that opens in medias res through a frame narrative quickly establishes an unreliable narrator whose retrospective narration colours every event with irony. The author's use of foreshadowing — a recurring motif of broken clocks — builds symbolic weight long before the epiphany that resolves the denouement. Sentences alternate parataxis ("he left. she waited.") with hypotaxis that supplies cause, while anaphora ("we remember, we remember, we remember") mounts urgency and polysyndeton slows the grief. The tone is suffused with understatement and a strategic hedge that lets the subtext — the unspoken guilt — carry more than any denotation. The effect is a text where structure, trope, tone, and scheme interlock so tightly that no single device can be read in isolation.

That paragraph names 17 distinct Week 3 terms across all four pillars — structure (frame narrative, unreliable narrator, retrospective narration), trope (irony, foreshadowing, motif, symbolism, epiphany, denouement), scheme (parataxis, hypotaxis, anaphora, polysyndeton), tone (understatement, hedge, subtext, denotation). It moves macro → micro and ties each device to an effect, not just a label.

⚖️ Distinction Quick-Ref (Your Exam Armour)

The twelve pairs most often confused. In an essay, naming the right term from a pair is what separates 5 from 7.

① metaphor vs. simile

metaphor asserts identity ("the city is a jungle"); simile asserts resemblance with "like/as". Test: fact or signpost?

② irony vs. satire

irony is a device (a gap between saying and meaning); satire is a mode that deploys irony to expose folly. One technique vs. a whole posture.

③ symbolism vs. allegory

symbolism = one object carries meaning within a realist text; allegory = the entire narrative is a second-level system. Scale is the seam.

④ anaphora vs. epistrophe

anaphora repeats at the START of units; epistrophe at the END. Same device, opposite position.

⑤ anaphora vs. anadiplosis

anaphora = parallel opens, independent; anadiplosis = the last word of one clause becomes the first of the next (a chain).

⑥ synecdoche vs. metonymy

synecdoche = part/whole ("all hands on deck"); metonymy = broader association ("the crown declared war"). Synecdoche is a subset.

⑦ asyndeton vs. polysyndeton

asyndeton omits conjunctions → speed, staccato; polysyndeton piles them → slowness, accumulation. Opposite rhythms.

⑧ chiasmus vs. antimetabole

chiasmus mirrors concepts (A–B–B–A, synonyms OK); antimetabole repeats the SAME words reversed. Antimetabole is stricter.

⑨ parataxis vs. hypotaxis

parataxis = equal clauses, no linker ("he left. she stayed") → withholds cause; hypotaxis = nested by subordinators → supplies cause. Equal vs. nested.

⑩ connotation vs. denotation

denotation = literal meaning; connotation = felt associations. Word-choice analysis lives in connotation.

⑪ subtext vs. inference

subtext resides IN the text (author's implied layer); inference is what the READER draws. You infer the subtext.

⑫ hedge vs. equivocation

hedge = honest softener ("perhaps"); equivocation = evasive dodge. Both reduce force, opposite intent.

🎯 Assessment Tips

  1. Recognition is not ownership. You "know" a Week 3 term only when you can spell it, define it, and deploy it on a unseen passage in under a minute. The Master Sheet tests the first two; the Your Task tests the third. All three must pass.
  2. Reach for one term from each pillar, every time. A commentary that names twelve tropes but no scheme or tone term is unbalanced and caps below the top band. The four-pillar balance row in the rubric is the single biggest lever on your mark.
  3. The pair is the differentiator. "This is a metaphor" gets identification; "this is a metaphor, not a simile, because it asserts identity and so risks more" gets the top band. The twelve distinctions are your edge — keep them alive into Phase 4.
  4. Devices must drive meaning, not decorate. Top commentaries use each term to explain an effect ("the polysyndeton slows the grief, forcing the reader to dwell"). If you can delete the device name and the sentence loses nothing, you've listed, not analysed.
  5. Macro before micro. Orient with structure (frame narrative, unreliable narrator, foreshadowing) before zooming into a scheme. The model paragraph moves whole-text → sentence on purpose. Examiners reward that architecture of thought.
  6. Week 3 is the metalanguage spine of the whole programme. Everything after — stylistic and linguistic analysis, critical theory, argumentative-essay vocabulary — hangs off these sixty terms. Lock them in now and Phase 4 becomes application, not relearning.
