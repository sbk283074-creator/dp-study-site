🔁 Day 17 Spaced Review — 12 Structure & Narrative Terms

A spaced-repetition pass on the structure & narrative vocabulary from Day 17 — at a 7-day interval, the sweet spot where memory consolidates. These twelve describe how a story is told: time (foreshadowing, flashback, flash-forward, non-linear, cyclic), framing (frame narrative, retrospective narration), and voice (unreliable narrator, stream of consciousness, interior monologue, epiphany, denouement). They are the direct ancestors of today's critical vocabulary — every lens you learn today asks whose perspective the narration represents. Read each, recall its precise mechanism before looking, then check.

foreshadowing
Hinting at future events early, so they land as fate or inevitability.
flashback
A return to an earlier time within the narrative present.
flash-forward
A jump ahead to future events before the chronology resumes.
frame narrative
A story that encloses one or more inner stories (a tale within a tale).
unreliable narrator
A narrator whose account the reader cannot fully trust.
stream of consciousness
The unfiltered, continuous flow of a character's thoughts.
interior monologue
A character's thoughts presented directly, as if spoken inwardly.
epiphany
A sudden moment of insight or revelation (Joycean peak).
denouement
The resolution — the unravelling of the plot's complications.
non-linear narrative
A plot that refuses chronological order, scrambling time.
retrospective narration
Narration spoken from a later vantage, looking back on events.
cyclic structure
A structure that returns to its beginning, implying repetition.
Self-assessment: Could you separate flashback from flash-forward, and frame narrative from retrospective narration, without peeking? Those two pairs are the Day 17 traps. Today's lenses depend on them: focalisation (whose eye) is the narrative fact that patriarchy and othering then interpret — a text can only "silence the subaltern" (today) if you first know who is allowed to narrate (Day 17). Keep these twelve live; they are the structural floor today's theory stands on.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Critical Theory Vocabulary Terms

NEW CATEGORY — "CRITICAL THEORY VOCABULARY"

From describing the text to reading it from a position. The metalanguage of Days 22–23 is descriptive — it names what language does. Critical theory is interpretive — it supplies the lenses that make a description an argument. We group the twelve by the four canonical lenses IB rewards: feminist (patriarchy, intersectionality, the male gaze), Marxist (ideology, hegemony, alienation), postcolonial (othering, the subaltern, hybridity), and psychoanalytic (the unconscious, repression, the uncanny). You do not need to 'apply all four' to every text — you need to know which lens explains the pattern you spotted, then name it precisely. Each term is a thesis-engine: pair it with Day 22's thesis and Day 23's syntax, and your commentary gains a position, not just an observation.

patriarchy /ˈpeɪtriɑːki/ noun

A social system in which men hold primary power and women are largely excluded from it — not merely in laws, but in the deep structures of family, language, and literature that naturalise male authority as 'normal' or 'natural'.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The text] naturalises male authority" · "[The heroine] resists patriarchal scripts" · "[Patriarchal] norms [constrain] the female voice"

"In Charlotte Perkins Gilman's The Yellow Wallpaper, the narrator's confinement by her physician husband enacts patriarchy's medicalised control of women's minds; her final crawling along the wall is a grotesque parody of the domestic sphere that imprisoned her." — The Yellow Wallpaper analysis

IB Tip Feminist lens. Distinct from the male gaze (box 3): patriarchy is the system of male dominance; the male gaze is one mechanism within it (the grammar of looking). In IB, name patriarchy when a text's structure — who speaks, who is silenced, whose story counts — reflects male power. Link to Day 17's unreliable narrator: a female narrator's 'madness' may be the only permitted form of resistance under patriarchy. Avoid claiming 'the author is sexist'; argue the text represents or destabilises patriarchy. Root: Greek patriarkhēs, 'father-ruler'.

Common collocations: patriarchal society · patriarchal norms · challenge patriarchy · patriarchal structure · undermine the patriarchy

intersectionality /ˌɪntəsekʃəˈnæləti/ noun

The recognition (coined by Kimberlé Crenshaw) that axes of identity — gender, race, class, sexuality — do not add up but intersect, so that oppression is compounded and cannot be analysed on a single axis alone.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Race and gender] intersect to compound marginalisation" · "[An intersectional reading] refuses single-axis analysis" · "[The character] is positioned at a crossroads"

"A reading of Toni Morrison's Beloved that treats Sethe only as 'a mother' or only as 'a former slave' misses how intersectionality makes her suffering irreducible — her subjection is simultaneously racial, gendered, and historical, and the novel refuses to let one explain the other away." — Beloved analysis

IB Tip Feminist / critical-race lens; the single best guard against reductionist analysis. Always ask whether your chosen lens is flattening a character into one identity. Connects to today's othering and the subaltern — those mark the margins; intersectionality explains why the margin is not one place. In IB, use it to complicate a thesis (Day 22) rather than abandon it: 'Sethe's silence is racial and gendered and historical.' That nuance is what earns the top band.

Common collocations: intersectional analysis · intersectional feminism · axes of identity · compound oppression · single-axis critique

the male gaze /ðə meɪl ɡeɪz/ noun

(Laura Mulvey) The way visual media and narrative are structured around a presumed male viewer, so that women are framed as objects to be looked at and possessed, while the act of looking itself is coded as male power.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The camera / narration] objectifies the female body" · "[The male gaze] reduces her to spectacle" · "[She] returns the gaze, subverting it"

"In many film adaptations of The Great Gatsby, Daisy is lit and shot as a glittering object of Gatsby's longing; a feminist analysis notes the male gaze even in prose — Nick's description lingers on her voice and surface, never her interiority, reproducing the structure Mulvey names." — The Great Gatsby analysis

IB Tip Feminist lens; the mechanism by which patriarchy (box 3) operates visually. Distinct from patriarchy (the system) — the male gaze is one channel of it. Use it for any text where looking = power: painting, film, but also the descriptive passages of novels. Link to Day 17's focalisation — whose eye we see through is the gaze made narrative. Name when a female character is 'shown' rather than 'known'; name too when she 'returns the gaze' and subverts it.

Common collocations: the male gaze · subject / object split · to be looked at · return the gaze · visual pleasure

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: patriarchy vs. the male gaze

Both belong to feminist criticism, but the seam is system versus mechanism:

patriarchy — The broad structure of male dominance: who holds power, whose story is centred, which norms count as 'natural'.
→ "The novel's plot only lets women marry or die." — a structural fact about power.

the male gaze — One specific mechanism within that structure: the framing of women as objects to be looked at, the coding of looking as male.
→ "The narration describes her body, never her mind." — a mechanism of how the dominance is enacted visually.

The test: are you describing the whole system of power (patriarchy) or the particular grammar of looking (male gaze)? A text can be patriarchal without a male gaze (a woman silenced by law, never by description), and can deploy a male gaze in order to subvert it (a heroine who 'returns the gaze'). In IB, name both when present — 'the text is patriarchal in structure and reproduces the male gaze in its descriptions, until chapter X, where she looks back' — that is a precise, band-7 move.

ideology /ˌaɪdiˈɒlədʒi/ noun

(Marx / Althusser) The system of ideas, values, and beliefs that a society treats as 'common sense' and that serves the interests of the powerful by presenting a partial order as natural and inevitable.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The text] naturalises bourgeois ideology" · "[Ideology] masks material inequality" · "[A rupture] exposes the ideology"

"In George Orwell's Animal Farm, the pigs' gradual adoption of human habits is ideology in motion — 'All animals are equal' is rewritten until the contradiction is invisible, showing how ruling ideas come to seem inevitable." — Animal Farm analysis

IB Tip Marxist lens. Distinct from hegemony (box 1): ideology is the content of the beliefs; hegemony is how dominance is won and held by consent. In IB, never just say 'it's ideological' — name which ideology (class, gender, colonial) and what it hides. Connect to Day 1's obfuscate and Day 23's nominalisation — ideology often travels in abstract, agent-less nouns ('progress', 'order'). Root: Greek idea + -logy.

Common collocations: dominant ideology · bourgeois ideology · ideological critique · naturalise an ideology · false consciousness

hegemony /hɪˈɡeməni/ noun

(Gramsci) The dominance of one class or group achieved not by force but by consent — by shaping what counts as normal, desirable, and true, so that the ruled partly endorse their own subordination.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Cultural] hegemony secures consent" · "[The text] naturalises the dominant order" · "[A counter-hegemonic] voice resists"

"In Pride and Prejudice, the marriage plot can be read as hegemonic — it makes a woman's survival appear to depend on securing a husband, so readers consent to a structure that confines Elizabeth even as she 'chooses' within it." — Pride and Prejudice analysis

IB Tip Marxist / cultural lens. Distinct from ideology (box 1): hegemony is the process / relation of winning consent; ideology is the content believed. The key IB move is to spot where coercion is replaced by consent — where a character 'freely' accepts what harms them. Link to Day 22's thesis: a hegemonic reading argues the text reproduces or quietly subverts the ruling order. Root: Greek hēgemonia, 'leadership'.

Common collocations: cultural hegemony · hegemonic discourse · win consent · counter-hegemonic · hegemonic normality

alienation /ˌeɪliəˈneɪʃn/ noun

(Marx) The estrangement of individuals from the products of their labour, from themselves, and from others under capitalism — a sense of disconnectedness, of being a stranger in one's own life and world.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The worker] is alienated from their labour" · "[The city] alienates the individual" · "[A ruptured] community enacts alienation"

"In Franz Kafka's The Metamorphosis, Gregor's transformation literalises alienation — he is estranged from his body, his family, and his labour all at once, and his death is met with relief rather than grief, the ultimate measure of a bond already broken." — The Metamorphosis analysis

IB Tip Marxist lens; the experience of an exploitative order (ideology and hegemony are its operation). In IB, use alienation for texts of isolation, bureaucracy, and modernity — but specify the kind (labour, social, self). Connect to Day 17's unreliable narrator and stream of consciousness — a fractured, alienated self often speaks in fractured form. Avoid using it vaguely for any sadness; name the structures that produce the estrangement. Root: Latin alienus, 'belonging to another'.

Common collocations: alienate the worker · feel alienated · alienation from labour · social alienation · alienated labour

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: ideology vs. hegemony

Both belong to Marxist criticism, but the seam is content versus relation:

ideology — The content of belief: the set of ideas a society treats as common sense (e.g. 'hard work is rewarded', 'marriage completes a woman').
→ "The slogan 'all animals are equal' is the ideology made verbal."

hegemony — The relation of dominance: how the powerful stay on top not by force but by winning the consent of the ruled.
→ "Readers cheer Elizabeth's marriage, thereby consenting to the very order that limits her." — that consent is hegemony.

The test: are you naming the beliefs (ideology) or the process of securing agreement (hegemony)? They travel together — ideology is what hegemony makes feel natural. In IB, the sophisticated pairing is 'the text naturalises bourgeois ideology (content), and does so through hegemony (consent), because the heroine appears to choose freely.' That sentence uses both correctly and shows you understand the machinery, not just the vocabulary.

othering /ˈʌðərɪŋ/ noun

(Said, postcolonial theory) The process by which a group defines itself as superior by marking another group as fundamentally different, strange, or inferior — the 'other' exists to secure the 'self'.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The colonial text] others the native" · "[Stereotype] is a tool of othering" · "[The other] is constructed, not found"

"In Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness, the African characters are repeatedly reduced to sounds and darkness — a classic othering that lets the European narrators define their own 'civilisation' against a silenced, exoticised contrast." — Heart of Darkness analysis

IB Tip Postcolonial lens. Distinct from the subaltern (box 2): othering is the act of marking difference; the subaltern is the marked, silenced subject produced by it. In IB, name othering whenever a text builds identity by contrast ('us' vs. 'them'). Link to Day 17's focalisation — who is seen as 'other' depends on whose perspective frames the text. Pair with intersectionality: the 'other' is never just one thing. Root: from 'other' + -ing (the process of making-other).

Common collocations: the other / Other · othering discourse · construct the other · colonial othering · racialised other

the subaltern /ðə səˈbɔːltən/ noun

(Spivak) The subordinate, marginalised subject — especially the colonised, the poor, the gendered — who is excluded from positions of power and voice, and who cannot easily 'speak' within the dominant system.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The subaltern] cannot speak" · "[The text] silences the subaltern" · "[Recovering] the subaltern voice"

"Spivak's question 'Can the subaltern speak?' frames a reading of Things Fall Apart — the Igbo world is rendered largely through a colonial anthropological gaze, so the novel's achievement is partly its attempt to let the subaltern narrate himself against that frame." — Things Fall Apart analysis

IB Tip Postcolonial / Marxist lens. Distinct from othering (box 2): the subaltern is the result of othering — the silenced subject. The IB move is to ask who is denied a voice and whether the text recovers or reproduces that silence. Connect to Day 17's retrospective narration and unreliable narrator — whose history gets told, and by whom, is the subaltern's question. Root: Latin subalternus, 'subordinate'.

Common collocations: the subaltern · subaltern voice · subaltern studies · can the subaltern speak · silence the subaltern

hybridity /haɪˈbrɪdəti/ noun

(Bhabha) The cultural blending produced in colonial contact zones, where coloniser and colonised mix — not a comfortable merger but a 'Third Space' that unsettles both, a site of both resistance and ambivalence.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The colonised] produces hybrid forms" · "[Hybridity] subverts the pure / authentic binary" · "[A third space] of meaning"

"In Midnight's Children, Rushdie's mingling of English and Urdu, of magical and historical, is hybridity as method — the postcolonial subject is neither simply colonised nor simply free, and the novel's form performs that in-between." — Midnight's Children analysis

IB Tip Postcolonial lens; the productive counterpart to othering: where othering fixes difference, hybridity shows difference is already mixed. Use it against the binary opposition of a 'pure self' vs. an 'impure other'. In IB, argue a text's form (code-switching, genre-mixing) as much as its content is hybrid. Link to Day 22's lexical field — a hybrid text has more than one. Root: Latin hybrida, 'mixed offspring'.

Common collocations: cultural hybridity · hybrid identity · the Third Space · hybrid forms · postcolonial hybridity

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: othering vs. the subaltern

Both belong to postcolonial criticism, but the seam is act versus subject:

othering — The act / process of marking a group as different and inferior, so the dominant group can define itself as superior.
→ "The narration describes the natives as 'savage' to secure European 'civilisation'." — the doing of difference.

the subaltern — The subject produced by that process: the marginalised, silenced figure who cannot easily speak within the dominant system.
→ "The Igbo elder is never given his own chapter; he is the subaltern the novel must rescue." — the result of othering.

The test: are you describing the process of marking (othering) or the silenced person it produces (subaltern)? Othering is the verb; the subaltern is its object. In IB, the strong sentence is 'the text others the colonised subject and thereby produces a subaltern whose voice the narrative must then recover' — you show the machinery and its human cost. Pair with intersectionality to note the subaltern is never a single, unified figure.

the unconscious /ðə ʌnˈkɒnʃəs/ noun

(Freud) The reservoir of desires, fears, and memories repressed below conscious awareness that nevertheless drives behaviour and surfaces in slips, dreams, and symbols — the hidden author of much literature.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The dream / image] betrays the unconscious" · "[A slip] reveals the repressed" · "[Symbols] surface the unconscious"

"In D. H. Lawrence's Sons and Lovers, Paul's inability to love without guilt reads as the unconscious at work — the mother-fixation he cannot name returns in every failed relationship, the buried motive governing the visible plot." — Sons and Lovers analysis

IB Tip Psychoanalytic lens. Distinct from repression (next word): the unconscious is the store; repression is the mechanism that keeps it down. In IB, use the unconscious for symbols, dreams, doubles, and repeated compulsions — but always tie the symbol to a reading, not just 'it's Freudian'. Link to Day 17's epiphany and stream of consciousness — where the conscious mind breaks, the unconscious leaks. Avoid over-claiming the author's real psychology; analyse the text's unconscious.

Common collocations: the unconscious · unconscious desire · surface the unconscious · freudian unconscious · the return of the repressed

repression /rɪˈpreʃn/ noun

(Freud) The mind's defence of pushing unacceptable impulses, memories, or desires out of conscious awareness — they do not vanish but return in disguised form (symptoms, slips, art).

Literary Usage Pattern "[The character] represses the trauma" · "[Repression] returns as symptom" · "[The text] enacts return of the repressed"

"In Tennessee Williams's The Glass Menagerie, Amanda's relentless nostalgia is repression made theatrical — she cannot bear the present loss, so the forbidden past is compulsively replayed, the returned repressed wearing the mask of anecdote." — The Glass Menagerie analysis

IB Tip Psychoanalytic lens. Distinct from the unconscious (it is the mechanism; the unconscious is the content kept down). The IB payoff is 'the return of the repressed' — what a text or character forbids returns in symbol or slip. Connect to Day 17's retrospective narration — looking back is often an attempt to master what was repressed. Name what is being repressed and what form its return takes, or the term is empty.

Common collocations: repress a memory · return of the repressed · repressed desire · psychological repression · repression and symptom

the uncanny /ðə ʌnˈkeɪni/ noun

(Freud, das Unheimliche) The strangely familiar — something that should be known but feels eerie, often because it reveals a repressed truth or a double of the self; the homely made unsettling.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The double] is uncanny" · "[The familiar] turns strange" · "[The uncanny] reveals the repressed"

"In Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre, the laughter from the attic — Bertha — is uncanny: it is Jane's own repressed fury given a monstrous, estranged voice, the familiar self returned as a threatening double." — Jane Eyre analysis

IB Tip Psychoanalytic lens; the aesthetic of the returned repressed. In IB, the uncanny names the moment a text turns the known strange — doubles, automatons, the returned dead, the house that is also a mind. Link to Day 15's doppelgänger / grotesque and Day 17's cyclic structure — repetition with a difference is uncanny rhythm. Root: German unheimlich, 'un-homely'. Pair with the unconscious / repression pair: the uncanny is where they surface.

Common collocations: the uncanny · uncanny double · the unhomely · freudian uncanny · uncanny effect

✏️ Practice — Choose the Lens, Build the Argument

Four drills. Write short answers; the model notes follow each.

  1. Name the lens. For each observation, name the critical-theory term (and its lens) that turns it into an argument:
    a) "The novel only lets women marry or die." → patriarchy (feminist); b) "The natives are described as 'savage' so Europe looks 'civilised'." → othering (postcolonial); c) "The heroine is filmed as a beautiful object, never shown thinking." → the male gaze (feminist); d) "The text presents working hard as the only path to worth." → ideology (Marxist).
  2. Pair a structure word with a theory word. Take Day 17's unreliable narrator and explain how two different lenses would read the same unreliable narrator:
    Model A female narrator whose 'madness' is never explained by the male doctors around her can be read by a feminist lens as patriarchy silencing her (her unreliability is what the powerful call her resistance); the same narrator can be read by a psychoanalytic lens as repression — she cannot speak the trauma, so it returns in fractured form. Same fact, two theses (Day 22).
  3. Build a thesis sentence. Using at least two today's terms, write one sentence arguing a reading of a text you know (e.g. Jane Eyre, Things Fall Apart, The Great Gatsby):
    Model "In Jane Eyre, Brontë reproduces the male gaze in Rochester's descriptions of Jane as surface, yet the uncanny figure of Bertha is the returned repressed of patriarchy itself — Jane's own fury estranged and monstered." (three terms, one argument)
  4. Distinguish on demand. In one sentence each, keep these Day 24 pairs exam-ready: (i) ideology vs. hegemony (believed content vs. consent won); (ii) othering vs. the subaltern (act of marking vs. silenced subject); (iii) patriarchy vs. the male gaze (system of power vs. grammar of looking); (iv) the unconscious vs. repression (the store vs. the mechanism). Stack them beside the fifteen Week 3 distinctions and the three Day 23 syntax pairs.

🎯 Study Tips — From Description to Argument

  1. A lens is a thesis-engine, not a label. "This is sexist" is a judgement; "the text's structure is patriarchal and its descriptions reproduce the male gaze" is analysis. Name the lens, then show the evidence (Day 23's syntax, Day 22's devices) that the lens explains. The examiner rewards the move from observation to positioned argument.
  2. Distinguish system from mechanism inside each lens. Patriarchy vs. male gaze; ideology vs. hegemony; othering vs. subaltern; unconscious vs. repression. The four distinction boxes today are exactly these seams. Naming the right level — structure or mechanism, content or relation, act or subject — is what separates band 5 from band 7.
  3. Let structure words carry the theory. Today's lenses only bite when anchored to Day 17's toolkit: who narrates (unreliable narrator, focalisation) decides whether the subaltern can speak; how time is ordered (retrospective narration, cyclic structure) decides how repression returns. Theory without textual structure is assertion; structure without theory is description.
  4. Use intersectionality to refuse flattening. The moment you catch yourself reducing a character to one axis ('she is oppressed as a woman'), add the others ('and as colonised, and as poor'). Intersectionality is the discipline that keeps your reading honest — and it is the move examiners explicitly look for in top-band comparative work.
  5. Psychoanalysis needs a reading, not a diagnosis. 'It's Freudian' says nothing. Name the symbol, name what is repressed, name the form of its return (the uncanny double, the slip, the symptom). Tie the unconscious to the text's patterns, not the author's biography — the latter is a trap in IB, where the work, not the writer's life, is the object.
  6. One lens per passage, applied precisely. You do not need all four lenses on every text. Pick the lens that explains the pattern you already spotted with the metalanguage, then deploy its terms with the distinctions exam-ready. A sharp feminist reading beats a shallow four-lens tour. Frame it on Days 22–23, and critical theory becomes your position — not a costume.
