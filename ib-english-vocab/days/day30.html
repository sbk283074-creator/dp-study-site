🔁 Day 15 Spaced Review — 12 Foundational Tropes

A 15-day spaced-repetition pass on the core tropes from Day 15 — the figures every comparative and stylistic claim ultimately rests on. Read each, recall its precise mechanism before looking, then check. These devices are what today's comparative structures will operate on: you cannot stage a parallelism of two texts if you cannot name the metaphor each text builds.

metaphor
A figure of substitution — "X is Y" — that carries meaning by equating two unlike things without "like" or "as".
simile
A stated comparison using "like" or "as" — "X is like Y". Weaker claim than metaphor; the gap stays visible.
personification
Attributing human qualities, motives, or actions to non-human things, animals, or abstractions.
symbolism
A concrete item standing for an abstract idea beyond itself (the door = opportunity / enclosure).
irony
A gap between what is said and what is meant, or what happens and what is expected — meaning lives in the gap.
motif
A recurring element (image, idea, sound, object) that accumulates significance across a text.
juxtaposition
Placing items side by side so that their contrast or comparison produces meaning (the device-level twin of Day 29's verb).
hyperbole
Deliberate, obvious exaggeration for emphasis or effect — not a factual claim.
allusion
A brief, often un-signalled reference to another text, person, event, or work — meaning borrowed from outside.
metonymy
A thing substituted for something closely associated with it (the crown = monarchy; the pen = writing).
oxymoron
A self-contradictory pairing of adjacent words (deafening silence) that compresses a tension into a phrase.
anaphora
Repetition of a word or phrase at the start of successive clauses / lines — emphasis through rhythm (a scheme that also stages accumulation).
Self-assessment: Could you separate metaphor from simile (equivalence without "like" vs. stated comparison with "like"), and juxtaposition from irony (placement-for-contrast vs. saying/meaning gap), without peeking? Those two pairs anchor Day 15. Today's words — parallelism, antithesis, counterpoint — are the structures you build on top of these tropes: a metaphor in Text A set in parallelism with a metaphor in Text B is a comparative claim. Keep the device layer live; the comparative grammar is useless without it.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Comparative & Multi-text Terms

NEW CATEGORY — "COMPARATIVE & MULTI-TEXT"

From the move to the relation. Day 29 gave you the moves (you juxtapose, you reconcile, you synthesise). Today gives you the grammar and logic of the relation itself — the words that say precisely how two texts sit together. Three are the contrastive sentence structures that stage comparison (parallelism, antithesis, concession); four are the multi-text relations that hold two texts in one reading (synthesis, counterpoint, intertextuality, correspondence); five are the comparative dynamics that map similarity and difference exactly (equivalence, symmetry, divergence, convergence, foil). Together they are the difference between a Paper 2 that merely mentions both texts and one that genuinely compares them — the signature of the top band.

parallelism /ˈpærəlɛlɪzəm/ noun

The repetition of similar syntactic structure across clauses or sentences, setting comparable items side by side so the reader reads them as one system of meaning.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Text A and Text B both] open with [X], a parallelism that…" · "The parallelism between [clause A] and [clause B]…" · "[The writer deploys parallelism] to stage…"

"Both set-texts open on a closed door — 'the gate was locked' / 'the door would not give' — and the parallelism forces the reader to read the two enclosures as a single structure of meaning rather than two isolated images." — comparative opening paragraph

IB Tip The grammatical engine of comparison. Distinct from mere repetition (which emphasises within one text) — parallelism across two texts is the comparative structure that makes them weighable together. It is the sentence-level form of Day 29's juxtapose: you juxtapose by building parallel lines. Pair with antithesis (word 2) — parallel structure carries either similarity or opposition. In Paper 2, open with a parallelism of the two texts' openings to signal immediate comparison. Links to Day 19's isocolon (balanced clauses) at the device level.

Common collocations: syntactic parallelism · parallelism between · deploy parallelism · marked parallelism · parallelism of structure

antithesis /ænˈtɪθɪsɪs/ noun

The juxtaposition of sharply contrasting ideas in balanced, often parallel, grammatical structure — opposition made visible and measurable.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] and [not-A] stand in antithesis" · "[The antithesis between] [X and Y]" · "[The sentence stages] an antithesis"

"In the soliloquy, 'to be or not to be' is the archetypal antithesis: two impossible positions held in one balanced line, and the balance itself is the meaning — the speaker cannot occupy either, only the tension between." — dramatic structure analysis

IB Tip A specific kind of balanced contrast. Distinct from paradox (which is self-contradictory within one idea) — antithesis sets two opposed ideas against each other. In multi-text work, an antithesis between the two texts' worldviews is a top-band comparative claim: Text A affirms X, Text B affirms not-X, and the essay reads the gap. Connects to Day 15's juxtaposition (the placement) and Day 19's antimetabole / chiasmus (the structural figures). Use antithesis when the opposition is the point; use parallelism when the alignment is.

Common collocations: stark antithesis · antithesis between · an antithesis of · stage an antithesis · rhetorical antithesis

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: parallelism vs. antithesis

Both are balanced structures, but they balance opposite relations:

parallelism — Alignment. Repeats similar structure to set items side by side as comparable.
→ "Both texts open on a locked door — the parallelism reads them as one system."

antithesis — Opposition. Balances contrasted ideas so the gap is the point.
→ "Freedom / confinement stand in antithesis — the balance enacts the conflict."

The test: am I aligning (parallelism) or opposing (antithesis)? They often appear in the same sentence — parallel structure carrying an antithetical relation ("Text A affirms…; Text B denies…"). Naming which you intend tells the examiner you control the structure. In Paper 2, parallelism opens the comparison; antithesis drives the argument.

concession /kənˈsɛʃən/ noun (concessive clause)

The grammatical structure (typically "although / while / even though") that yields a point before resuming one's own — the sentence-level form of Day 22's concede.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Although X], [Y]" · "[While Text A does…], [Text B…]" · "[The concessive clause] concedes…"

"A strong comparative opening runs: 'While both poets figure the sea as freedom, they diverge sharply in the cost they attach to it' — the concessive clause concedes the similarity before the antithesis earns the argument." — Paper 2 topic sentence

IB Tip The structure that performs Day 22's concede. Distinct from rebut (which answers) — concession yields, then you resume your own claim. The concede–mitigate–rebut sequence (Day 29) almost always opens with a concessive clause. In Paper 2, leading with a concession shows command of the global argument: you grant the obvious similarity, then earn the difference. Links to Day 23's subordination / clause linkage (the concessive clause is a subordinate clause). Never let a concession hang — always resume.

Common collocations: concessive clause · a concession to · open with a concession · concession of · grammatical concession

synthesis /ˈsɪnθɪsɪs/ noun

The fused reading that emerges from holding two texts together — a single argument neither text alone supports.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The essay's synthesis] is that…" · "[X and Y synthesise into] [a reading]" · "[A dialectic resolves into] synthesis"

"The two texts never meet in the world, but the essay's synthesis — that both figure freedom as a discipline imposed from without — joins them into one claim that neither carries alone." — Paper 2 conclusion

IB Tip The goal of Paper 2. Distinct from summary (which lists both texts) — synthesis fuses them into a new claim. Connects to Day 29's dialectic (which resolves into synthesis) and reconcile. The marker of the top band: your conclusion is a synthesis, not a recap of Text A then Text B. Guard against a synthesis that flattens difference (cf. reconcile's failure mode — forced unity erases real tension). Name the synthesis, then show it follows from both texts' evidence.

Common collocations: a synthesis of · reach a synthesis · synthesise X and Y · textual synthesis · the synthesis is

counterpoint /ˈkaʊntəpɔɪnt/ noun

A contrasting element set against another so that each is clarified by their opposition — comparison achieved through deliberate contrast.

Literary Usage Pattern "[X serves as] counterpoint to [Y]" · "[The secondary character is] a counterpoint" · "[A counterpoint emerges]"

"The boisterous chorus is counterpoint to the solitary protagonist; read together, the noise defines the silence, and the silence the noise — each only legible against the other." — character / texture analysis

IB Tip Contrast-as-clarification. Distinct from foil (word 12, a specific figure thrown into relief) — counterpoint is the broader structural relation. In multi-text work, set Text B as counterpoint to Text A to expose what A suppresses. Links to Day 15's juxtaposition and to Day 29's analogous (counterpoint is the contrastive twin of analogy — one marks difference, the other similarity). Use counterpoint when the opposition is what makes both texts intelligible.

Common collocations: a counterpoint to · serve as counterpoint · striking counterpoint · counterpoint between · set in counterpoint

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: synthesis vs. counterpoint

Both hold two texts in one reading, but they hold them differently:

synthesis — Fusion. The two texts merge into one claim neither carries alone.
→ "The essay's synthesis: both figure freedom as imposed discipline."

counterpoint — Contrast. The two texts are set against each other so each clarifies the other.
→ "Text B is counterpoint to Text A, exposing what A withholds."

The test: am I merging (synthesis) or opposing (counterpoint)? A mature Paper 2 does both — uses counterpoint to expose difference, then synthesis to fuse the difference into a claim. Confusing them is a common slip: do not call a fused reading 'counterpoint', nor a contrastive relation a 'synthesis'. This pair is exam armour for the comparison and the conclusion.

intertextuality /ˌɪntətɛkstʃuˈælɪti/ noun

The network of relations a text has to other texts; meaning made across texts rather than within one.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The poem's intertextuality] with [Text B]" · "[An intertextual echo] of…" · "[Intertextuality] frames…"

"The protagonist's 'nevermore' is an intertextual echo of the canonical elegy; the essay reads the later text against the earlier one, and the echo becomes an argument about literary inheritance." — intertextual reading

IB Tip The theoretical frame for all comparative work (cf. Day 24's lenses — intertextuality is the structural counterpart to influence / echo / rewriting). Names the fact that no IB set-text is read in isolation. In Paper 2, naming intertextuality lets you discuss allusion, pastiche, or re-vision as argument, not ornament. Distinct from allusion (Day 15, a specific device) — intertextuality is the whole relation the allusion participates in. Use it to justify why comparing these two texts is itself meaningful.

Common collocations: intertextual echo · intertextuality with · an intertextual reading · marked intertextuality · intertextual relation

correspondence /ˌkɒrɪˈspɒndəns/ noun

A recognisable match between features of two texts that licenses a comparative claim.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A clear correspondence] between [X in A] and [Y in B]" · "[The correspondence suggests]…" · "[Note the correspondence]"

"There is a close correspondence between Text A's repeated 'window' and Text B's 'frame': both are thresholds the speaker cannot cross, and the match grounds the essay's reading of enclosure." — evidence-level comparison

IB Tip The evidence-level word for "these two things line up". Distinct from analogy (Day 29, a claimed structural similarity) — correspondence is the observable match you then interpret. In Paper 2, spot correspondences first, then decide whether they are analogous (similar) or counterpoint (contrast). Links to Day 26's semantic field / collocation (shared fields are correspondences). Name the correspondence before you argue from it — evidence before claim.

Common collocations: a correspondence between · close correspondence · the correspondence suggests · note the correspondence · structural correspondence

equivalence /ɪˈkwɪvələns/ noun

The structural or semantic equality that lets two items be compared on the same footing.

Literary Usage Pattern "[X is in equivalence with] [Y]" · "[The grammar establishes] equivalence" · "[An equivalence of] scale"

"Both monologues run to the same fourteen lines; the formal equivalence signals that the two speakers are to be weighed, not ranked — the essay reads them as equal witnesses rather than one authoritative, one minor." — formal comparison

IB Tip The condition that makes comparison fair. Without equivalence (different scale, mode, era, or genre), comparison risks false analogy (Day 29). Distinct from symmetry (word 9, a mirrored shape) — equivalence is about comparability, symmetry about mirrored form. In Paper 2, establish equivalence before comparing, or name why the difference is itself productive (then you are doing divergence, word 10). Equivalence is the gatekeeper of a valid comparison.

Common collocations: structural equivalence · equivalence of · in equivalence with · establish equivalence · formal equivalence

symmetry /ˈsɪmɪtri/ noun

Balanced correspondence between elements across two texts or within one, so that parts mirror or answer one another.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A symmetry between] [A's opening] and [B's close]" · "[The symmetry answers]…" · "[Mirror symmetry]"

"Text A opens on birth, Text B closes on death; the symmetry between the two frames turns the pair into a single arc about continuity, and the essay builds its thesis on that mirror." — macro-structural comparison

IB Tip The visible shape of a relation. Distinct from equivalence (comparability) — symmetry is the mirrored form you can point to. In multi-text analysis, symmetry is often the detail that triggers a synthesis. Links to Day 19's isocolon (balanced clauses) at the sentence level and to Day 15's motif (recurrence that can mirror across texts). Use symmetry to show design, not accident — "the texts happen to both begin with X" is weaker than "the symmetry is deliberate and means Y".

Common collocations: a symmetry between · mirror symmetry · formal symmetry · the symmetry answers · broken symmetry

divergence /daɪˈvɜːdʒəns/ noun

The point at which two texts or strands move apart; difference made productive rather than merely noted.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A sharp divergence] between [A and B]" · "[The texts diverge] on [X]" · "[The divergence is productive]"

"Both poets mourn a parent, yet they diverge on form: one fragments the line, the other holds it whole — and the divergence is the essay's evidence that grief has no single shape." — comparative difference

IB Tip The difference-half of comparison made active. Distinct from counterpoint (which sets contrast for clarification) — divergence is the measured point of parting you then explain. In Paper 2, name the divergence and its cause / effect; a bare "they differ" is not analysis. Pair with convergence (word 11) — the two moves that keep a comparison honest. If you cannot explain the divergence, it is coincidence, not comparison.

Common collocations: a divergence between · sharp divergence · the texts diverge · productive divergence · point of divergence

convergence /kənˈvɜːdʒəns/ noun

The point at which two texts or strands meet; agreement made productive.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A convergence] between [A and B]" · "[The texts converge] on [X]" · "[The convergence grounds]"

"Though separated by a century, the two novels converge on the same image of the unopened letter — and that convergence, not their many differences, is what lets the essay synthesise them." — comparative agreement

IB Tip The agreement-half of comparison. Distinct from correspondence (observable match) — convergence is the meeting-point you argue from. In Paper 2, convergence often seeds the synthesis; divergence tests it. Together with divergence they are the two moves that keep a comparison honest: converge to build the shared claim, diverge to show where it breaks and why that matters. Links loosely to Day 25's rhyme / caesura (sound-level meeting) only as a metaphor.

Common collocations: a convergence between · the texts converge · point of convergence · convergence on · striking convergence

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: divergence vs. convergence

Both describe how two texts relate, but they point in opposite directions:

divergence — Parting. The texts move apart; difference made productive.
→ "They diverge on form — one fragments the line, one holds it whole."

convergence — Meeting. The texts come together; agreement made productive.
→ "They converge on the unopened letter — the seed of the synthesis."

The test: are the texts parting (divergence) or meeting (convergence)? A mature Paper 2 uses both — converge to build the shared claim, diverge to show where it breaks and why that matters. Confusing them (calling a meeting-point a 'divergence') inverts your argument. This pair, with correspondence / equivalence, is the evidence logic of every top-band comparison.

foil /fɔɪl/ noun

A character, detail, or text that, by contrast, throws another into relief — the comparative device at the level of figures.

Literary Usage Pattern "[X is] a foil to [Y]" · "[The secondary text foils] the primary" · "[A foil reveals]"

"Read alone, the hero seems rash; set beside the cautious foil-character, his rashness becomes a principle, not an accident — the foil resolves an interpretive question the single text left open." — character comparison

IB Tip The character / element-level cousin of counterpoint and juxtaposition. In multi-text work, set Text B as foil to Text A to expose what A withholds. Distinct from counterpoint (broader structural relation) — foil is specifically the figure thrown into relief. Classic IB move: compare two characters as foils, or two texts as foils. Links to Day 15's juxtaposition and Day 29's analogous (a foil is the contrastive version of an analogy). Use foil when one text exists partly to define the other.

Common collocations: a foil to · serve as a foil · foiled by · the foil reveals · character foil

✏️ Practice — Write the Comparative Sentence

Four drills. Write short answers; the model notes follow each.

  1. Word-bank insertion. Choose the correct Day 30 word for each gap (each used once):
    Word bank: parallelism · antithesis · concession · synthesis · counterpoint · intertextuality · correspondence · equivalence · symmetry · divergence · convergence · foil
    a) "While both texts mourn loss, they ____________ sharply on the form it takes." → diverge
    b) "The boisterous chorus is ____________ to the silent protagonist, defining each by the other." → counterpoint
    c) "A ____________ clause opens the paragraph: 'Although both poets…', before the argument resumes." → concession
    d) "The two novels ____________ on the image of the unopened letter, seeding the essay's claim." → converge
    e) "The essay's ____________ — that both figure freedom as imposed — joins the texts into one claim." → synthesis
    f) "The ____________ between Text A's 'window' and Text B's 'frame' grounds the reading of enclosure." → correspondence
  2. Mini comparative paragraph. On the two lines below, deploy at least three Day 30 words and state each one's effect:
    Texts Text A: "I am the door they locked."  ·  Text B: "She was the window they framed."
    Model: the parallelism of the two openings ('door' / 'window', both governed by 'they') forces the reader to read the figures as one system of confinement (the structure aligns them); the correspondence between 'locked' and 'framed' is then read as a divergence — A's enclosure is active denial, B's is display — and naming that divergence is what turns a similarity into an argument about two kinds of control.
  3. Write the top-band comparative sentence. Combine a concessive clause (concession) + a parallelism of the two texts + a named relation (divergence / convergence / antithesis) + its effect, in one sentence:
    Starter "Although both poets figure the sea as freedom…"
    Model: "Although both poets figure the sea as freedom, the parallelism of their closing lines — A's 'I swim' beside B's 'I sink' — exposes a sharp divergence in the cost each attaches to that freedom, and the divergence is what lets the essay argue that liberation is text-specific, not shared." One sentence: relation named and effect stated — the move Day 29 previewed.
  4. Distinguish on demand. In one sentence each, separate these Day 30 pairs you will be tested on: (i) parallelism vs. antithesis (alignment vs. opposition, both balanced); (ii) divergence vs. convergence (texts parting vs. meeting). Keep both pairs in your exam armour alongside the Day 29 and Day 15 distinctions.

🎯 Study Tips — Making Comparison Automatic

  1. Lead comparative paragraphs with a concessive clause. "Although both texts…, they diverge on…" yields the similarity, then earns the difference. The concessive structure (concession) is the single most reliable way to open a 7-band Paper 2 paragraph — it shows you see both texts before you judge them.
  2. Parallelism is your comparison engine. Repeat the same syntactic shape across the two texts ("Text A opens on X; Text B closes on Y") so the reader reads them as one system. Without parallel structure, a comparison reads as two separate observations; with it, as one argument.
  3. Name the relation AND its effect in a single sentence. The top-band comparative move — previewed in Day 29 — is one clause that says "they diverge (relation) because each attaches a different cost to freedom (effect)". A comparison that names the relation but not the effect is description; both together is analysis.
  4. Synthesis, not summary. Your Paper 2 conclusion should fuse the two texts into one claim neither carries alone (synthesis), not recap Text A then Text B. If your final paragraph could be split back into two texts, it is summary — rewrite it as fusion.
  5. Use correspondence and equivalence to prove the comparison is fair. Before arguing, show the two texts actually match (correspondence) and are comparable on the same footing (equivalence). Skipping this step is how comparisons collapse into false analogy — the failure mode Day 29 warned about.
  6. Divergence and convergence keep a comparison honest. Converge to build the shared claim; diverge to show where it breaks and why that matters. A comparison that only converges is naively unified; one that only diverges is two texts that happen to sit near each other. Use both, and tie each to cause and effect.
