🔄 Day 1 Quick Recall — 12 Words

For each word below, try to recall the meaning before reading the definition. Then ask yourself: Can I use this word in an analytical sentence about a text I'm studying? If not, flag it for extra review tonight.

subvert

To undermine or overturn established conventions/authority.
"The author subverts the genre by…"

juxtapose

To place side by side to highlight contrast.
"Fitzgerald juxtaposes X with Y to…"

foreground

To bring to prominence; make central.
"The narrative foregrounds the theme of…"

undermine

To gradually weaken or erode.
"The irony undermines the reader's confidence in…"

evoke

To bring a feeling/atmosphere to mind.
"The imagery evokes a sense of…"

elucidate

To make clear; shed light on.
"The metaphor elucidates the relationship between…"

problematize

To complicate/question what was taken for granted.
"The novel problematizes the notion of…"

conflate

To merge two distinct things into one.
"Orwell conflates X and Y, suggesting…"

obfuscate

To deliberately make unclear or obscure.
"The fragmented structure obfuscates the truth…"

interrogate

To subject to rigorous critical questioning.
"The text interrogates assumptions about…"

nuanced

Characterised by subtle distinctions; not simplistic.
"A nuanced portrayal that avoids…"

didactic

Intended to teach/instruct, often with a moral lesson.
"The tone is didactic, suggesting…"

Self-check: Cover the definitions above. Can you recall each word's meaning and its "literary usage pattern" template? Which 2–3 words feel least secure? Star them — they'll appear again in Day 3's review.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Terms

Category: More Analysis Verbs (7) + Effect & Causation Verbs (5)
Yesterday you learned verbs for what authors do to texts and conventions. Today's verbs go further — they describe what texts do to readers (provoke, elicit, engender), how texts take ideas apart (dismantle, deconstruct, destabilise), and how texts prove or sustain arguments (exemplify, instantiate, perpetuate, vindicate). Together with Day 1, these 24 verbs form the core analytical toolkit you'll use in every IB essay.

underscore /ˌʌndəˈskɔː/ verb

To emphasise or give additional weight to something; to reinforce the significance of an idea through structural or stylistic means.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [technique/motif] underscores [theme/idea] by [specific method]…"

"The recurring motif of decay — from the Valley of Ashes to Gatsby's unmaintained mansion — underscores the novel's argument that the American Dream is structurally self-destroying." — The Great Gatsby analysis

IB Tip More formal and precise than "emphasise." Use it when a literary element reinforces a theme structurally — through repetition, patterning, or motif — rather than through direct statement. "The author emphasises" is about intention; "the text underscores" is about effect.

Common collocations: underscore a theme · underscore the significance · underscore the contrast · underscore the tension · underscore the point

illuminate /ɪˈluːmɪneɪt/ verb

To clarify or make intelligible; to shed light on a complex idea, hidden motivation, or layered relationship within a text.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [scene/metaphor/dialogue] illuminates [aspect of character/theme/motivation]…"

"Blanche's desperate monologue about the 'paper lantern' illuminates the fragility of the persona she has constructed — beauty that cannot survive exposure to harsh light." — A Streetcar Named Desire analysis

IB Tip Use when a moment or device reveals something previously hidden or only half-understood. Unlike "elucidate" (which means to explain), "illuminate" suggests a flash of insight — a sudden clarity. Perfect for moments of dramatic revelation or symbolic density.

Common collocations: illuminate a character's motivation · illuminate the theme · illuminate the human condition · illuminating (adj.) · illumination (n.)

dismantle /dɪsˈmænt(ə)l/ verb

To take apart systematically; to deconstruct an argument, ideology, or social structure piece by piece until it can no longer function.

Literary Usage Pattern "The text dismantles [ideology/stereotype/hierarchy] by [specific technique]…"

"Austen dismantles the social hierarchy of Regency England not through direct political critique but through the weaponised irony of free indirect discourse, exposing Lady Catherine's authority as hollow performance." — Pride and Prejudice analysis

IB Tip Implies a methodical, deliberate taking-apart — not a single blow but a systematic disassembly. Use when a text shows how a belief system falls apart, piece by piece. Compare with "subvert" (overturning) — "dismantle" is more analytical and structural.

Common collocations: dismantle an argument · dismantle stereotypes · dismantle the ideology · dismantle the hierarchy · dismantlement (n.)

deconstruct /ˌdiːkənˈstrʌkt/ verb

To break down a text or concept to reveal its internal contradictions and hidden assumptions; to show that categories we treat as natural are actually constructed and unstable.

Literary Usage Pattern "By [technique], the text deconstructs the [binary/assumption/concept], revealing it to be [unstable/contradictory]…"

"Winterson deconstructs the binary of male and female by presenting gender as a fluid narrative construction, showing that the body itself is a text that can be rewritten." — Written on the Body analysis

IB Tip A term from poststructuralist theory (Derrida). In IB, use it when a text reveals that categories we take for granted — gender, truth, sanity, civilisation — are unstable constructions, not natural facts. Don't use it as a fancy synonym for "analyse" — it specifically means to expose contradictions within a system of meaning. Pairs powerfully with "binary" and "construct."

Common collocations: deconstruct a binary · deconstruct the narrative · deconstruct assumptions · deconstruct the category · deconstruction (n.)

destabilise /diːˈsteɪbɪlaɪz/ verb

To make unstable or uncertain; to shake the foundations of a belief, identity, narrative, or social order.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [technique] destabilises [the reader's certainty / the character's identity / the social order]…"

"The shifting narrative perspective — sometimes first person, sometimes third, sometimes uncertain which — destabilises the reader's confidence in any single version of events, mirroring the epistemological uncertainty at the heart of the novel." — Generic postmodern analysis

IB Tip Use when a text deliberately unsettles something that seemed stable. The instability is meaningful — it creates space for new meaning to emerge. Distinguish from "undermine" (which weakens): "destabilise" is about making something wobbly, not necessarily destroying it.

Common collocations: destabilise identity · destabilise the narrative · destabilise assumptions · destabilise the social order · destabilisation (n.)

engender /ɪnˈdʒendə/ verb

To cause or produce; to give rise to a feeling, attitude, or social condition within the text or in the reader.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [setting/imagery/situation] engenders [emotion/attitude/condition] in [the reader/the character]…"

"The claustrophobic domestic setting — the barred windows, the nailed-down bed, the wallpaper pressing inward — engenders a pervasive sense of entrapment that mirrors and amplifies the narrator's psychological deterioration." — The Yellow Wallpaper analysis

IB Tip More formal than "cause" or "create." Reserve it for the production of abstract things — feelings, attitudes, social conditions. "The imagery engenders a sense of dread" is notably more sophisticated than "The imagery makes you feel scared." It implies a generative relationship between the literary device and the effect.

Common collocations: engender sympathy · engender a sense of · engender distrust · engender hope · engender complacency

provoke /prəˈvəʊk/ verb

To deliberately stimulate or incite a response — emotional, intellectual, or physical — often challenging comfort or complacency.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [scene/technique] provokes [reaction] by [method], forcing the reader to [confront/reconsider]…"

"The abrupt shift from lyrical nature description to the visceral brutality of the hunt provokes the reader into confronting the violence that the preceding lyricism has been aesthetically concealing." — Generic prose analysis

IB Tip Implies intentionality — the author is doing this deliberately. "Provoke" is stronger and more confrontational than "cause"; it suggests a challenge to the reader's comfort, assumptions, or moral position. Perfect for describing shock tactics, tonal shifts, or graphic imagery that serves a purpose.

Common collocations: provoke a response · provoke unease · provoke the reader · provoke controversy · provocation (n.) · provocative (adj.)

elicit /ɪˈlɪsɪt/ verb

To draw out or bring forth a response from the reader or audience, often through careful prompting, technique, or dramatic design.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [technique] elicits [sympathy/discomfort/reflection] from the [reader/audience]…"

"The dramatic irony of the banquet scene — where only the audience sees Banquo's ghost — elicits a mixture of pity and revulsion, complicating any straightforward response to Macbeth's downfall." — Macbeth analysis

IB Tip "Elicit" focuses on the response drawn out from the reader or audience. It's more precise than "make the reader feel" because it implies a technique that pulls a reaction. Distinguish carefully from "evoke" (Day 1): evoke = bringing an atmosphere/feeling to mind; elicit = drawing a specific reaction from someone. See the distinction box below.

Common collocations: elicit sympathy · elicit a response · elicit discomfort · elicit reflection · elicit empathy · elicitation (n.)

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: evoke vs. elicit

These two verbs are frequently confused, and using the wrong one is a red flag for examiners.

evoke (Day 1) — The subject is usually a textual element (imagery, setting, sound). The object is an atmosphere or feeling. The feeling arises in the reader's mind without being directly solicited.
→ "The imagery evokes a sense of melancholy." (The imagery brings melancholy to mind.)

elicit (Day 2) — The subject is a technique or design choice. The object is a specific, deliberate reaction from the reader/audience. There's an implied causal mechanism.
→ "The dramatic irony elicits sympathy from the audience." (The technique pulls sympathy out of the audience.)

Memory trick: evoke → emotion/atmosphere · elicit → extract a reaction from someone

exemplify /ɪɡˈzemplɪfaɪ/ verb

To serve as a typical example of; to illustrate a broader pattern, concept, or theme through a specific instance.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [character/scene] exemplifies [theme/concept/archetype] through [specific quality/behaviour]…"

"Tom Buchanan exemplifies the moral bankruptcy of the old-money elite — his casual cruelty is not an aberration but the logical expression of unchecked inherited privilege, presented without self-awareness." — The Great Gatsby analysis

IB Tip Use when a specific element perfectly illustrates a broader theme. More precise than "shows" or "is an example of" — it means this element is a representative instance of a larger pattern. Examiners like it because it demonstrates that you can move between the specific (one character) and the general (a theme).

Common collocations: exemplify a theme · exemplify the archetype · exemplify the conflict · exemplify a trope · exemplification (n.)

instantiate /ɪnˈstænʃieɪt/ verb

To give concrete form to an abstract concept; to embody or represent a general idea in a specific, tangible instance.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [character/scene] instantiates [abstract concept] as [concrete manifestation]…"

"Winston Smith instantiates the conflict between individual memory and collective history — his job of falsifying records makes him both the agent and the victim of institutional forgetting." — 1984 analysis

IB Tip A high-register alternative to "is an example of" or "represents." Use it when a character or scene doesn't merely illustrate a concept (cf. "exemplify") but embodies it — the abstract idea is made flesh. Examiners at HL will recognise this as a term from philosophy and literary theory. Use sparingly — once or twice per essay for maximum impact.

Common collocations: instantiate a concept · instantiate the archetype · instantiate the theme · instantiate an ideal · instantiation (n.)

perpetuate /pəˈpetʃueɪt/ verb

To cause something to continue indefinitely; to sustain, reinforce, or keep in circulation a system, belief, or cycle — usually with negative connotations.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [narrative structure/social dynamic] perpetuates [inequality/ideology/cycle of violence] by [mechanism]…"

"The cyclical structure of the novel — ending where it began, with a new generation inheriting the same conflicts — perpetuates the sense that the cycle of colonial violence is structurally unbreakable, offering no easy redemption." — Generic postcolonial analysis

IB Tip Usually negative — perpetuating stereotypes, inequality, cycles of violence. Use it to show that a text depicts (or itself participates in) the reinforcement of a harmful pattern. A sharp analytical question: Does the text critique the cycle, or does it perpetuate it? This tension is often where the most sophisticated IB arguments live.

Common collocations: perpetuate stereotypes · perpetuate inequality · perpetuate the cycle · perpetuate the myth · perpetuate the status quo · perpetuation (n.)

vindicate /ˈvɪndɪkeɪt/ verb

To justify or prove right; to show that someone or something was correct, often retrospectively, after a period of doubt or opposition.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [ending/revelation/structure] vindicates [character's actions / earlier suspicion / thematic argument]…"

"The novel's final revelation — that the narrator's seemingly paranoid suspicions were justified all along — vindicates her perspective retrospectively, transforming the reader's previous doubt into guilt." — Generic unreliable-narrator analysis

IB Tip Use when a later development in the text confirms an earlier position. It's about retrospective justification — the text proves that a character or perspective was right all along. Extremely powerful in essays about unreliable narrators, delayed revelation, or texts that reframe their own earlier events.

⚠️ Don't confuse with vindictive (adj. — seeking revenge). Same root, very different meaning.

Common collocations: vindicate a character · vindicate a claim · vindicate the protagonist · vindicate a position · vindication (n.)

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: exemplify vs. instantiate

Both describe the relationship between a specific element and a general concept, but with different shades of meaning:

exemplify — The element is a typical example of a broader pattern. It illustrates the concept clearly. Neutral in register.
→ "Tom Buchanan exemplifies the carelessness of the wealthy." (He's a clear example of this type.)

instantiate — The element doesn't just illustrate the concept — it embodies it, gives it concrete form. Higher register; implies a tighter, more philosophical connection between the abstract and the concrete.
→ "Winston instantiates the conflict between memory and power." (He IS the conflict, made flesh.)

Exercise 1: Fill in the Blank (Day 2 Words)

Complete each sentence with a word from today's list. Use context clues to choose the right word.

Word Bank: underscore · illuminate · dismantle · deconstruct · destabilise · engender · provoke · elicit · exemplify · instantiate · perpetuate · vindicate
  1. The recurring image of broken mirrors ________ the theme of fractured identity that runs throughout the novel.
  2. Rather than simply condemning colonialism, the author ________ its ideological foundations, showing how "civilising" language masks economic exploitation.
  3. The unreliable narration ________ the reader's trust in the narrator's account, making every "fact" suspect.
  4. The oppressive summer heat ________ a sense of mounting tension that erupts in the novel's climactic confrontation.
  5. The playwright's use of direct address ________ the audience into uncomfortable complicity with the protagonist's decisions.
  6. The final scene, in which the protagonist's warnings prove accurate, ________ her earlier suspicions that everyone had dismissed as paranoia.
  7. The cyclical narrative structure ________ the cycle of generational trauma, offering no exit from the pattern of violence.
  8. The protagonist ________ the existential conflict between individual freedom and social conformity — he is not merely caught between them but is that conflict in human form.

Exercise 2: Cross-Day Sentences (Day 1 + Day 2)

Write one analytical sentence for each pair, using both words in a single sentence about a text you are studying. This trains you to combine analytical verbs naturally.

  1. juxtapose (Day 1) + underscore (Day 2) — Write about how contrasting elements reinforce a theme.
  2. evoke (Day 1) + engender (Day 2) — Write about how atmosphere creates a specific emotional response.
  3. problematize (Day 1) + destabilise (Day 2) — Write about how a text complicates and unsettles a concept.
  4. interrogate (Day 1) + dismantle (Day 2) — Write about how a text questions and takes apart a social structure.

Exercise 3: Distinguish & Defend

Explain the difference between each pair in 1–2 sentences. Why would you choose one over the other in an essay? Use a literary example to illustrate.

  1. provoke vs. elicit — both are about reader response, but what's the difference in intensity and intentionality?
  2. dismantle vs. deconstruct — both involve taking apart, but what's the difference in what is being taken apart and how?
  3. exemplify vs. instantiate — see the distinction box above. Give your own literary example for each.
  4. perpetuate vs. vindicate — how do these represent opposite textual functions?

🎯 Building Your Analytical Verb Toolkit

  1. You now have 24 analytical verbs. That's enough to never use "shows" or "highlights" in an essay again. Print or bookmark this list and keep it beside you when writing. The goal is not to use all 24 in every essay — it's to choose the right one for each analytical move.
  2. Match the verb to the analytical function. Ask yourself: Is the author taking something apart (dismantle, deconstruct, destabilise)? Reinforcing something (underscore, perpetuate)? Producing a response (engender, provoke, elicit)? Proving something right (vindicate)? Each function has its own verbs — using the right one demonstrates precision.
  3. Stack verbs for complex analysis. A single sentence can use two analytical verbs to show layered effects: "By juxtaposing the two settings, the author underscores the contrast between… and engenders in the reader a sense of…" This layered structure is what separates 6/7-level analysis from 4/5-level.
  4. Watch register: don't overuse the highest-level verbs. "Instantiate" and "deconstruct" are powerful precisely because they're uncommon. Use them once or twice per essay. "Underscore," "provoke," and "elicit" are workhorses — use them freely. Calibrate your register like a musician calibrates dynamics.
  5. Start using these verbs in your reading notes, not just essays. When you annotate a set text, write "underscores" or "dismantles" in the margin instead of "shows." This builds instinct — by exam time, the right verb will come automatically.
  6. The distinction boxes matter. Examiners can tell when a student uses "evoke" and "elicit" interchangeably. The precision of choosing the right word is worth marks in Criterion D (Language). Review the evoke/elicit and exemplify/instantiate distinctions until they're instinctive.
