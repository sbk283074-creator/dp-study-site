🔥 Day 1 Active Recall — 2nd Pass

For each word below, you see the word and its usage pattern. Try to recall the full definition before clicking to reveal. Then read the deeper question — it tests whether you can use the word, not just define it. If you can't answer the deeper question, flag the word for tonight's re-study.

subvert "The author subverts the genre by…"
To undermine or overturn established conventions/authority. Subversion works from within — using the genre's own rules against it.
What's the difference between subverting a genre and simply not following it? (Hint: subversion requires engagement with the convention, not just ignoring it.)
juxtapose "Fitzgerald juxtaposes X with Y to…"
To place side by side to highlight contrast or unexpected connection. The proximity itself creates meaning.
What does juxtaposition create that simple comparison doesn't? (Hint: it's about the spatial relationship generating insight, not just logical comparison.)
foreground "The narrative foregrounds the theme of…"
To bring to prominence; make central and visible. From visual art — what's in front vs. what recedes.
What's the difference between foregrounding a theme and simply mentioning it? (Hint: foregrounding is structural, not just thematic.)
undermine "The irony undermines the reader's confidence in…"
To gradually weaken or erode from beneath. Implies a process, not a single blow.
How does undermining differ from destroying? (Hint: the thing being undermined still stands — but shakily.)
evoke "The imagery evokes a sense of…"
To bring a feeling/atmosphere to mind. The subject is a textual element; the object is an atmosphere or emotion.
Remember the Day 2 distinction: evoke vs. elicit? Which one takes a reader's response as its object? (Answer: elicit. Evoke takes an atmosphere.)
elucidate "The metaphor elucidates the relationship between…"
To make clear; shed light on something complex. More formal and intellectual than "explain."
When would you choose "elucidate" over "explain"? (Hint: elucidate implies the thing being clarified is complex, not just unknown.)
problematize "The novel problematizes the notion of…"
To complicate or question what was taken for granted. Not the same as "criticise" — it makes something that seemed simple become complex.
What does problematizing do that simple questioning doesn't? (Hint: problematizing reveals hidden complexity in something that seemed settled — it doesn't just ask "is this right?")
conflate "Orwell conflates X and Y, suggesting…"
To merge two distinct things into one, often erroneously or strategically. The conflation itself is the analytical point.
Why might an author deliberately conflate two things? (Hint: the conflation can reveal ideological assumptions — e.g., conflating "civilised" with "Western.")
obfuscate "The fragmented structure obfuscates the truth…"
To deliberately make unclear or obscure. Key word: deliberately. Not the same as being accidentally confusing.
How is obfuscating different from simply being unclear? (Hint: intentionality — the author is using complexity as a technique.)
interrogate "The text interrogates assumptions about…"
To subject to rigorous critical questioning. From legal/military interrogation — sustained, systematic pressure.
What makes "interrogate" different from "question"? (Hint: interrogation is sustained and systematic — it doesn't stop at the first answer.)
nuanced "A nuanced portrayal that avoids…"
Characterised by subtle distinctions; not simplistic. Signals to the examiner that you see the shades of grey.
What does calling an interpretation "nuanced" signal to an IB examiner? (Hint: it says you've moved beyond binary thinking — not just "good/bad" but aware of complications.)
didactic "The tone is didactic, suggesting…"
Intended to teach or instruct, often with a moral lesson. In literary criticism, often used negatively — but not always.
Is "didactic" always negative? Can you think of a text where didacticism is a deliberate artistic choice? (Hint: fables, allegories, political literature — didacticism can be a genre feature, not a flaw.)

🔄 Day 2 First Review — 12 Words

These are yesterday's words — 24 hours have passed. Try to recall each meaning before reading the definition. Ask yourself: Can I use this word in a sentence about a text I'm studying? If not, flag it — it will reappear in Day 4's review.

underscore

To emphasise or reinforce significance structurally.
"The motif underscores the theme by…"

illuminate

To shed light on; reveal what was hidden.
"The scene illuminates the character's motivation…"

dismantle

To take apart systematically, piece by piece.
"The text dismantles the ideology by…"

deconstruct

To reveal internal contradictions in a concept/binary.
"The text deconstructs the binary of…"

destabilise

To make unstable or uncertain; to shake foundations.
"The technique destabilises the reader's certainty…"

engender

To cause or produce (feelings, attitudes, conditions).
"The setting engenders a sense of entrapment…"

provoke

To deliberately incite a response; to challenge comfort.
"The scene provokes the reader into confronting…"

elicit

To draw out a response from the reader/audience.
"The dramatic irony elicits sympathy from the audience…"

exemplify

To serve as a typical example of a broader pattern.
"The character exemplifies the archetype through…"

instantiate

To embody an abstract concept; give it concrete form.
"The character instantiates the conflict as…"

perpetuate

To cause to continue indefinitely (usually negative).
"The structure perpetuates the cycle of…"

vindicate

To prove right retrospectively; to justify.
"The revelation vindicates the protagonist's earlier suspicion…"

Key distinctions from Day 2 — can you still articulate them?
• evoke (atmosphere) vs. elicit (reader response)
• exemplify (typical example) vs. instantiate (embodiment of an abstract)
• dismantle (systematic taking apart) vs. deconstruct (revealing internal contradictions)
If any of these still feel fuzzy, revisit the Day 2 distinction boxes tonight.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Writing-Method Verbs

NEW CATEGORY

A conceptual shift: Days 1 and 2 gave you verbs for what texts do — they subvert, undermine, destabilise, engender. Today's verbs describe how texts are built — how authors craft, weave, orchestrate, and sculpt their work. This is the vocabulary of authorial craft, and it's essential for IB Paper 1 (where you must discuss how a text is constructed, not just what it means) and Paper 2 (where you compare authors' methods). Each verb carries a specific metaphor — textile, musical, sculptural, geological — and choosing the right metaphor demonstrates precision.

craft /krɑːft/ verb

To construct with deliberate skill, care, and artistry; to shape a text with conscious attention to form and effect — every choice serving a purpose.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author crafts [element] with [quality], ensuring [effect]…"

"Ishiguro crafts the narrative voice of Kathy H. with a devastating flatness — the restrained, matter-of-fact tone makes the horror of the disclosure all the more unbearable, as if the narrator herself has been trained not to feel what she describes." — Never Let Me Go analysis

IB Tip Unlike "write" or "create," "craft" implies intentional artistry — every detail is a deliberate choice. Use it when you want to foreground the author's conscious construction. Also works as a noun: "the craft of the prose." In IB, "craft" connects directly to the assessment criterion that rewards discussion of writer's choices.

Common collocations: craft a narrative · craft a voice · craft the structure · craft the opening · craftsmanship (n.)

deploy /dɪˈplɔɪ/ verb

To position and use a technique, device, or resource strategically; to put into effective action within a text — borrowed from military tactics.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author deploys [technique/device] [adverbially], [effect]…"

"Atwood deploys biblical language and naming conventions with surgical precision — the 'Of' prefixed to the Handmaids' names reduces them to possessions, while simultaneously invoking the language of divine authority to sanctify their subjugation." — The Handmaid's Tale analysis

IB Tip The military origin matters — "deploy" implies tactical intent. Use it when an author uses a technique not just once but as part of a broader strategy. More dynamic than "uses" or "employs" — it suggests positioning for maximum effect. "Atwood deploys irony" is sharper than "Atwood uses irony" because it implies strategic placement.

Common collocations: deploy a technique · deploy irony · deploy a motif · deploy free indirect discourse · deployment (n.)

weave /wiːv/ verb

To interlace multiple strands — narrative threads, images, themes — into a unified whole; to integrate disparate elements so seamlessly they become inseparable.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author weaves [element A] and [element B] into [a unified whole]…"

"García Márquez weaves myth and history so tightly that the impossible ascension of Remedios the Beauty and the historical massacre of banana workers exist in the same narrative register, forcing the reader to question which version of events is more 'real' — the magical or the documentary." — One Hundred Years of Solitude analysis

IB Tip The textile metaphor is key — multiple threads becoming cloth. Use when multiple narrative elements are integrated so seamlessly that separating them would damage the whole. The past tense is wove, the past participle is woven: "a woven narrative." Compare with thread (Word 10) — weaving is the overall process of interlacing; threading is a single element running through.

Common collocations: weave together · weave narrative strands · weave myth and history · weave a tapestry · interweave (v.)

render /ˈrendə/ verb

To represent, depict, or translate into a particular form; to cause to become or be in a specified condition. Two key uses: (1) "render X as Y" = represent/translate; (2) "render X [adjective]" = cause to become.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author renders [abstract quality] as [concrete form]…" / "The technique renders [element] [adjective]…"

"Chopin renders Edna's awakening not as a triumphant epiphany but as a slow, tactile process — the sensory world of the sea, the sound of music, the taste of freedom becomes indistinguishable from her psychological state, so that desire and environment are the same thing." — The Awakening analysis

IB Tip A versatile, high-register verb. "Render X as Y" = represent/translate (rendering the abstract concrete). "Render X [adjective]" = cause to become (rendered speechless, rendered invisible, rendered powerless). Both are sophisticated analytical moves. Not the same as "depict" — "render" implies a transformative act of translation, not just representation. Use when form and meaning are inseparable.

Common collocations: render visible · render abstract as concrete · render powerless · render vivid · rendering (n.)

orchestrate /ˈɔːkɪstreɪt/ verb

To arrange and coordinate multiple elements — voices, perspectives, time frames — with deliberate precision, as a conductor coordinates instruments in an orchestra.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author orchestrates [multiple elements] so that [specific effect]…"

"Miller orchestrates the transitions between Willy's past and present with devastating precision — the flute melody, the wall boundary, the lighting shift all signal movement between time frames, yet the transitions grow more jarring as Willy's grip on reality disintegrates, the structure itself enacting the collapse." — Death of a Salesman analysis

IB Tip The musical metaphor is essential — multiple elements working together in a coordinated whole. Use when a text has complex structural coordination: multiple narrators, time shifts, interwoven plotlines. Implies control and intentionality. Perfect for discussing narrative structure, dramatic construction, or the arrangement of scenes. "The author orchestrates the multiple perspectives" is far more analytical than "the author has many narrators."

Common collocations: orchestrate the transitions · orchestrate multiple perspectives · orchestrate the climax · orchestrate the structure · orchestration (n.)

sculpt /skʌlpt/ verb

To shape and refine with careful, deliberate craftsmanship; to carve away the inessential until only the precisely intended form remains.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author sculpts [element] into [form], achieving [quality]…"

"Morrison sculpts Sethe's fragmented memories into a nonlinear architecture where past and present bleed into each other — the prose itself enacts the traumatic memory it describes, refusing the comfort of chronological order, forcing the reader to experience disorientation as understanding." — Beloved analysis

IB Tip The metaphor is from sculpture — carving, shaping, removing material to reveal form. Use when an author shapes something with evident care and precision, especially by cutting away what's unnecessary. More tactile and physical than "craft" — implies the pressure of hands on material. Perfect for discussing prose style, sentence structure, or character construction. "The author sculpts the prose" suggests every word is essential; nothing is wasted.

Common collocations: sculpt a character · sculpt the prose · sculpt the narrative · sculpt the opening · sculptural (adj.)

layer /ˈleɪə/ verb

To build up meaning through superimposed levels; to construct in strata so that multiple meanings coexist simultaneously, each adding depth to the whole.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author layers [element] upon [element], creating [effect of depth/complexity]…"

"Fitzgerald layers the green light with so many resonances — hope, money, the past, the American Dream itself — that by the final page it has become a symbol whose meaning exceeds any single reading, a site where personal longing and national mythology converge and become inseparable." — The Great Gatsby analysis

IB Tip The geological metaphor — strata building on strata, each layer adding depth. Use when meaning accumulates through repetition and variation. Different from "repeat" (which is flat) — layering implies depth, each new instance adds a new dimension. Perfect for discussing symbolism, motif development, or thematic complexity. "Fitzgerald layers the symbol" means it gets richer with each appearance, not just repeated.

Common collocations: layer meaning · layer symbolism · layer the imagery · layer upon layer · multi-layered (adj.)

embed /ɪmˈbed/ verb

To fix firmly within a surrounding context; to insert an element deeply into a narrative or structure so it becomes integral rather than decorative.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author embeds [element] within [context], so that it [function]…"

"Orwell embeds the political apparatus of Oceania within the most mundane domestic routines — the morning exercises, the rationing of chocolate, the routine of the Two Minutes Hate — making totalitarianism feel like normality, which is precisely the ideological point: the system works best when it feels invisible." — 1984 analysis

IB Tip Implies deep insertion — the element is not on the surface but integrated into the fabric of the text. Use when a technique, critique, or theme is woven into the narrative structurally rather than stated directly. "The critique is embedded in the narrative structure" is far more analytical than "the author criticises." Shows you understand that meaning can be structural, not just thematic — a key IB distinction.

Common collocations: embed a critique · embed a motif · embed meaning · embed within the structure · embedded (adj.)

frame /freɪm/ verb

To present within a particular context, perspective, or boundary; to enclose and shape meaning by what is included, excluded, or foregrounded. Also a noun: the structure that encloses and shapes interpretation.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author frames [the narrative/subject] through [perspective/device], [effect]…"

"Brontë frames the central story of Catherine and Heathcliff through the double narration of Lockwood and Nelly Dean — the outsider and the servant — ensuring that the passionate central narrative is always mediated by perspectives that cannot fully comprehend it, creating a gap between event and understanding that the reader must navigate." — Wuthering Heights analysis

IB Tip Crucial concept for IB. "Framing" is both a technique (frame narrative, framing device) and a critical concept (how a text frames its subject). The frame is never neutral — it changes the meaning of what's inside it. Use when perspective, narration, or structure shapes how the reader encounters the content. "The frame narrative filters the story through Nelly's class bias" is a sophisticated analytical move.

Common collocations: frame the narrative · frame through a perspective · frame the reader's response · framing device (n.) · reframing (n.)

thread /θred/ verb

To pass a single element — a motif, image, or theme — subtly and continuously through a text; to run one strand through the fabric of a narrative so it's always present but not always visible.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author threads [motif/element] throughout the [text], [effect]…"

"Shakespeare threads the motif of blood through Macbeth with increasing density — from the soldiers' blood on the battlefield, to the indelible stain on Lady Macbeth's hands, to the final image of Macbeth as a 'butcher' — the motif accumulates meaning with each appearance, transforming from courage to guilt to damnation without ever being stated directly." — Macbeth analysis

IB Tip The key is singularity and continuity — one element running through the whole. Compare with weave (Word 3): weaving interlaces multiple strands; threading runs one strand through. Use when tracking a single motif's journey through a text. The metaphor suggests subtlety — the thread is not always visible on the surface but is always there, holding things together. "Shakespeare threads the blood motif" is a precise observation about continuity.

Common collocations: thread a motif · thread throughout · thread the imagery · thread the theme · a thread of (n.)

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: weave vs. thread

Both are textile metaphors, but they operate at different scales:

weave — Interlaces multiple strands into a whole. The emphasis is on the process of integration — many elements becoming one fabric.
→ "García Márquez weaves myth, history, and magical realism into a single narrative fabric."

thread — Runs one element continuously through a text. The emphasis is on continuity — a single strand, always present.
→ "Shakespeare threads the motif of blood throughout Macbeth."

Memory trick: weave = many threads → cloth · thread = one strand → through the cloth. You can do both in the same sentence: "The author weaves three narrative strands while threading a single motif throughout."

infuse /ɪnˈfjuːz/ verb

To instil or fill with a particular quality; to introduce a quality throughout so it becomes an essential characteristic of the whole — like tea infusing water.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author infuses [the text/the scene] with [quality], creating [effect]…"

"Achebe infuses the narrative with Igbo proverbs, oral rhythms, and communal rituals so that the language itself resists colonial erasure — the prose enacts the cultural integrity the novel thematically defends, making form and content inseparable." — Things Fall Apart analysis

IB Tip Implies the introduction of a quality from within — the text is filled with something, like flavour infusing water. Use when a quality or tradition permeates the texture of the prose itself, not just the plot. "Infused with" is the most common construction. Distinguish carefully from suffuse (Word 12) — see the distinction box below.

Common collocations: infuse with meaning · infuse with culture · infuse the prose with · infuse energy into · infusion (n.)

suffuse /səˈfjuːz/ verb

To spread through or over completely; to bathe a scene, setting, or passage entirely in a particular quality, colour, or atmosphere — like light or colour spreading through water.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Quality/atmosphere] suffuses [the scene/the passage], [effect]…"

"The pattern of the yellow wallpaper suffuses the narrator's perception so completely that the boundary between observer and observed dissolves — she does not merely see the pattern, she becomes part of it, and the reader is pulled into the same claustrophobic identification, unable to separate her perception from the narrator's." — The Yellow Wallpaper analysis

IB Tip More total and enveloping than "infuse." Note the grammatical difference: with infuse, the subject is usually the author ("Achebe infuses the prose with oral tradition" — he puts something in). With suffuse, the subject is usually the quality or atmosphere ("an atmosphere of dread suffuses the scene" — it spreads through everything). Use when an atmosphere fills every corner of a scene. Perfect for discussing mood, setting, or atmospheric description.

Common collocations: suffuse the scene · suffuse with light · suffuse with dread · a suffusion of (n.) · suffused with (adj.)

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: infuse vs. suffuse

Both describe a quality spreading through a text, but the directionality and subject differ:

infuse — The subject is the author or text. Something is put in. The quality is introduced deliberately, like steeping tea.
→ "Achebe infuses the prose with Igbo oral tradition." (He puts the tradition into the prose.)

suffuse — The subject is the quality or atmosphere. Something spreads through. The quality fills everything, like colour through water.
→ "An atmosphere of inevitability suffuses the final chapters." (The atmosphere spreads through the text.)

Memory trick: infuse = inject a quality → active, deliberate · suffuse = suffocate with a quality → total, enveloping. You can use both: "Achebe infuses the prose with oral rhythms, while an atmosphere of tragic inevitability suffuses the final chapters."

🎯 The Shift: From "What" to "How"

  1. You now have two categories of analytical verb. Days 1–2 gave you verbs for what texts do — they subvert, destabilise, engender, vindicate. Day 3 gives you verbs for how texts are built — authors craft, weave, orchestrate, sculpt. In IB essays, you need both: the analysis verb explains the effect; the writing-method verb explains the method. "By threading the blood motif throughout the play, Shakespeare underscores the theme of inescapable guilt" — method + effect in one sentence.
  2. Each writing-method verb carries a specific metaphor — choose with care. Textile metaphors (weave, thread) suggest integration and continuity. Musical metaphors (orchestrate) suggest coordination of multiple parts. Sculptural metaphors (sculpt, craft) suggest deliberate shaping. Geological metaphors (layer, embed) suggest depth and accumulation. Choosing the right metaphor demonstrates that you understand the nature of the author's construction, not just that construction happened.
  3. These verbs are your Paper 1 secret weapon. Paper 1 requires you to analyse how a text creates meaning — not just what it means. Writing-method verbs let you discuss craft with precision. Instead of "the author uses a lot of imagery," write "the author layers imagery upon imagery, embedding symbolic resonance within each descriptive detail." The shift from "uses" to "layers/embeds" is the shift from a 4 to a 6.
  4. For Paper 2, these verbs let you compare methods, not just themes. "While both Austen and Brontë explore female autonomy, Austen embeds her critique within the irony of free indirect discourse, whereas Brontë frames hers through the passionate first-person voice of a narrator who refuses to be mediated." This is comparative analysis at its best — different methods, same theme.
  5. Don't confuse "weave" and "thread" — or "infuse" and "suffuse." These pairs are the Day 3 distinction traps. Examiners can tell when a student uses "weave" where "thread" is meant (or vice versa). Review the distinction boxes until the difference is instinctive. The grammatical test: if the subject is the author, you can use either infuse or weave; if the subject is the quality/atmosphere, use suffuse; if you're describing one element running through, use thread.
  6. Start using these verbs in your reading annotations. When you annotate a set text, write "crafts" or "orchestrates" or "threads" in the margin instead of "shows" or "uses." This builds instinct — by exam time, you won't have to think about which verb to use; you'll feel the right one. The metaphor will match the technique.
