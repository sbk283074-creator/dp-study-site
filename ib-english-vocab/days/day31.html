🔁 Day 16 Spaced Review — 12 More Literary Devices & Rhetoric

A 15-day spaced pass on the second device set from Day 16 — the adjacent figures (contrast, part & sense, whole-text modes, sound & structure) that round out your technical vocabulary. Read each, recall its precise mechanism before looking, then check. You will be evaluating these very devices in today's new words — solid recall here makes the judgement language land.

paradox
A statement that seems self-contradictory yet may reveal a deeper truth (spans a sentence or whole text).
synecdoche
A part made to represent the whole (or whole for part) — "all hands on deck" (hands = sailors).
imagery
Figurative / sensory language appealing to the senses; the pattern of images in a text.
allegory
A narrative whose characters / events stand for abstract ideas — sustained symbolism across a whole text.
antithesis
Contrasting ideas in balanced, parallel structure ("best of times, worst of times").
epistrophe
Repetition of a word / phrase at the END of successive clauses — the twin of anaphora.
apostrophe
Addressing a dead / absent / imaginary figure or abstraction as if present and able to reply.
alliteration
Repetition of initial consonant sounds for rhythm, mood, or emphasis (ask what the sound does).
euphemism
A mild word substituted for a harsh / blunt one — a power device that obscures.
pun
A play on words exploiting similar sounds or double meanings (compressed, constructed ambiguity).
satire
A mode using humour / irony / ridicule to expose and criticise vice or folly — the stance behind "satirical".
tricolon
A series of three parallel units — the "rule of three" for balance, rhythm, rhetorical force.
Self-assessment: Could you separate synecdoche from metonymy (part/whole vs. association — the Day 16 box), and antithesis from juxtaposition (parallel wording vs. side-by-side placement — the Day 16 box), without peeking? Those two pairs anchor Day 16. Today's new words are the judgement layer you drape over these devices: you don't just name the paradox, you evaluate what it reveals; you don't just spot the tricolon, you judge whether its rhythm is cogent. Keep the device layer live — the evaluative grammar is useless without it.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Evaluative & Judgement Terms

NEW CATEGORY — "EVALUATIVE & JUDGEMENT LANGUAGE"

From naming to judging. Day 29 gave you the moves (assert, refute, nuance, mitigate) and Day 30 the comparative grammar. Today gives you the register of judgement — the words that say precisely how you weigh a text. Four are the verbs of evaluation (evaluate, assess, perceive, discern); five are the adjectives of judgement that mark a 7-band writer (judicious, measured, salient, cogent, tenable); three are the nouns of critical method (caveat, critique, criterion). Together they are the difference between an essay that describes a device and one that evaluates it — the signature of perceptive, personal response in both Paper 1 and Paper 2, and the spine of your IO and HLE rationale.

evaluate /ɪˈvæljueɪt/ verb

To judge or determine the significance, worth, or quality of something, especially by close examination — not merely to describe it.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The essay must evaluate] [the extent to which]…" · "[We can evaluate] [the effect] [by asking]…" · "[evaluate] [what the device gains the text]"

"A top-band commentary does not merely describe the shift to free verse; it evaluates the shift — arguing that the loosened form is what lets the poet register fragmentation as form, not just as content." — commentary judgement

IB Tip The verb behind the Paper 1 / Paper 2 command term "evaluate". Distinct from describe or summarise — evaluate = pronounce a judgement of value or significance. In both papers, an "evaluate" question demands you weigh (cf. Day 22's qualify / concede). Pair with salient (word 7): first decide what is salient, then evaluate it. Connects to Day 29's nuance — evaluation that holds fine distinctions is the goal. Never end a paragraph on description; end it on an evaluated claim.

Common collocations: evaluate the effect · evaluate the significance · evaluate to what extent · critically evaluate · evaluate the device

assess /əˈsɛs/ verb

To estimate the nature, quality, or extent of something; to take stock of strengths and limits, often two-sided.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Assess the effectiveness of]…" · "[The essay assesses] [both the gain and the cost]" · "[assess] [whether the premise holds]"

"Rather than asserting the metaphor works, the paragraph assesses it — naming what the 'door' gains the poem (closure as threat) and what it costs (the speaker's agency), so the reader sees the figure weighed, not celebrated." — comparative paragraph

IB Tip Distinct from evaluate (which judges value) — assess = take stock / measure extent, often inventorying both sides. In Paper 2, assess both texts' treatments before you synthesise (cf. Day 30's convergence / divergence). Links to Day 22's premise (you assess whether the premise holds). Use assess when you are cataloguing strengths AND limits; use evaluate when you are pronouncing significance. The two verbs are the engine of a balanced paragraph.

Common collocations: assess the effectiveness · assess the impact · assess both · assess the cost · assess whether

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: evaluate vs. assess

Both are judgement verbs, but they land on different endpoints:

evaluate — Value. Judge the significance / worth of something — "this device evaluates to a critique of power."
→ Ends on a pronouncement of meaning.

assess — Extent. Measure the nature / quality / limits — "the paragraph assesses both the gain and the cost."
→ Ends on a taken-stock balance.

The test: am I pronouncing significance (evaluate) or measuring extent (assess)? A mature paragraph does both — assesses the figure's moving parts, then evaluates its overall effect. Confusing them (calling a stock-take an 'evaluation') leaves the paragraph without a judgement; the examiner reads description, not analysis. This pair is exam armour for any "evaluate" command term.

perceive /pəˈsiːv/ verb

To become aware of or interpret meaning, intent, or effect — to read a text's signal rather than merely see its surface.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The reader is positioned to perceive]…" · "[We perceive] [not X but Y]" · "[perceive the subtext]"

"The deadpan opening positions the reader to perceive the narrator not as unreliable but as meticulously withholding — the perception is built into the gap between what is said and what is withheld." — narrative-position analysis

IB Tip Distinct from literal "see" — perceive = interpretive awareness. Connects to Day 18's subtext / inference (you perceive the subtext) and to Day 24's lenses (you perceive through a lens). Vital reader-response language: "the reader perceives X" names the text's work on its audience. Avoid the intentional fallacy "the author wants us to feel" — say "the text positions the reader to perceive". Perceive is the verb that keeps your analysis on the page, not in the author's head.

Common collocations: the reader perceives · positioned to perceive · perceive as · perceive the gap · how we perceive

discern /dɪˈsɜːn/ verb

To distinguish or recognise fine differences; to separate signal from noise.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Discern the difference between]…" · "[The critic must discern] [what is structural vs. incidental]" · "[discern X from Y]"

"A perceptive reading discerns between the repeated 'window' that is a motif (structural) and the isolated 'window' that is mere setting (incidental) — only the first earns analytical weight." — close-reading move

IB Tip Distinct from perceive (awareness) — discern = fine discrimination, the separation of like things. This is the verb behind Day 29's nuance. In exams, discern is your guard against conflate (Day 1) — name the difference you discern, then argue its effect. High-value phrasing: "discern X from Y" shows the marker you control similarity AND difference. A reading that cannot discern structural from incidental detail is a reading that analyses the wrong things.

Common collocations: discern between · discern X from Y · difficult to discern · discern the pattern · discern what is

judicious /dʒuˈdɪʃəs/ adjective

Showing sound judgement, wisdom, and discretion — especially in selection or decision.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a judicious choice of] [evidence]" · "[judicious restraint]" · "[a judicious analyst]"

"The essay's judicious selection of just two stanzas — rather than paraphrasing the whole poem — lets the analysis go deep instead of wide, and the restraint itself reads as confidence." — essay-strategy note

IB Tip Describes the writer's judgement, not the text. Distinct from "clever" or "skilful" — judicious specifically marks wisdom in choice / restraint. Pair with measured (word 6): a judicious analyst writes in measured prose. In self-reflection (Learner Portfolio / IO rationale) you can call your own selection judicious — but in textual analysis apply it to the author's craft choices, not to the text's content. Avoid overusing it on the text itself ("a judicious metaphor" is off); reserve it for decisions and selection.

Common collocations: judicious choice · judicious use · judicious selection · judicious restraint · a judicious reader

measured /ˈmɛʒəd/ adjective

Careful, restrained, proportionate in expression — controlled rather than exaggerated.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a measured claim]" · "[measured tone]" · "[a measured response]"

"The conclusion is measured: it does not overstate the synthesis ('the two texts concur on enclosure') but qualifies it ('on enclosure, though not on who is enclosed'), and the qualification is what makes the claim credible." — argument-quality note

IB Tip The register of a 7-band essay. Distinct from tentative (uncertain) — measured = confident but proportionate. Links to Day 22's qualify / concede and to Day 29's mitigate (measured writing mitigates its own claims). Use measured to describe your own evaluative sentences: a measured judgement names the limit without collapsing the point. This is the antidote to both overclaim and hedge — the two failure modes that cap essays at band 4–5.

Common collocations: a measured claim · measured tone · measured response · measured language · carefully measured

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: judicious vs. measured

Both are quality-of-judgement adjectives, but they praise different things:

judicious — Wisdom in choice: sound discretion in what you select or decide (a judicious choice of evidence).
→ About the decision.

measured — Restraint in expression: proportionate, controlled, not overstated (a measured claim).
→ About the wording.

The test: am I praising the selection (judicious) or the tone (measured)? They often co-occur — a judicious analyst writes in measured prose — but they are not interchangeable. Calling a well-chosen quotation "measured" (or a well-balanced sentence "judicious") mismatches the praise. In exams, deploy judicious on your method, measured on your claims; both signal a writer in control.

salient /ˈseɪliənt/ adjective

Most noticeable, prominent, or important — the feature that stands out and demands analytical attention.

Literary Usage Pattern "[the salient feature is]…" · "[the salient contrast between]…" · "[what is most salient]"

"Across both set-texts the salient feature is not the grief itself but its grammar — one text fragments the line, the other holds it — and naming that formal salience is what lifts the comparison above plot-summary." — comparative framing

IB Tip Your filter for what to analyse. Distinct from "interesting" (subjective) — salient = prominently significant. Connects to Day 1's foreground (you foreground the salient) and to Day 30's correspondence (the salient match). In Paper 2, state what is salient before you evaluate it (word 1) — evidence-selection logic. Avoid analysing the merely obvious; analyse the salient. A comparison built on the salient feature is a comparison that earns its conclusion.

Common collocations: the salient feature · most salient · salient point · salient contrast · salient detail

cogent /ˈkəʊdʒənt/ adjective

Clear, logical, and convincing — an argument that compels assent by the quality of its reasoning.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a cogent argument]" · "[cogent because]…" · "[less cogent when]…"

"The paragraph's claim that enclosure is gendered is cogent: it chains three pieces of evidence (the locked gate, the framed window, the withheld key) into one inference the reader cannot dodge." — argument-quality note

IB Tip Describes reasoning quality, not the writer. Distinct from tenable (word 9, merely defensible) — cogent = actively convincing. In exam feedback, "cogent" is the praise you want; earn it by linking claim → evidence → effect in one move (cf. Day 30's "name relation AND effect"). Use cogent of arguments, tenable of positions — see the box below. A cogent paragraph is the visible product of all Week 5's moves working together.

Common collocations: a cogent argument · cogent analysis · less cogent · cogent because · cogent case

tenable /ˈtɛnəbl/ adjective

Able to be defended or maintained against challenge — a position that survives scrutiny.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a tenable position]" · "[no longer tenable]" · "[a reading remains tenable]"

"Reading the 'door' as pure optimism is no longer tenable once the second stanza locks it from outside; the essay keeps its reading only by acknowledging the turn." — claim-defensibility note

IB Tip The floor of a good argument. Distinct from cogent (convincing) — tenable = merely survivable under challenge. In exams, before calling a reading "correct", ask if it is tenable: can you defend it with text? A tenable-but-unexciting claim beats a bold-but-indefensible one. Links to Day 22's premise (a tenable premise supports the claim) and to Day 29's corroborate (corroboration makes a reading tenable). Tenable is the gate; cogent is the goal.

Common collocations: a tenable position · no longer tenable · remain tenable · intellectually tenable · barely tenable

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: tenable vs. cogent

Both judge argument quality, but at different thresholds:

tenable — Defensible. The position survives challenge — "the reading is still tenable if you weigh the second stanza."
→ The floor: it holds up.

cogent — Convincing. The reasoning compels assent — "the argument is cogent because it chains three proofs."
→ The ceiling: it persuades.

The test: does the claim merely hold (tenable) or actively persuade (cogent)? Every cogent argument is tenable, but not every tenable one is cogent. In exams, aim for cogent; if a counter-reading is tenable but not cogent, you can concede it (Day 22) without letting it collapse your thesis. Confusing them — calling a merely-defensible claim "cogent" — overstates your case and invites the marker to push back. This pair, with evaluate / assess, is the spine of defensible judgement.

caveat /ˈkævɪæt/ noun

A warning or qualification that limits a claim; a stated exception.

Literary Usage Pattern "[with the caveat that]…" · "[a necessary caveat]" · "[the caveat is that]…"

"The essay's synthesis holds — with the caveat that both poets figure freedom differently, so the shared 'enclosure' is structural, not emotional, and the reader is warned before over-reading the parallel." — qualifying move

IB Tip The noun behind Day 22's qualify / concede. Distinct from refutation — a caveat limits, it does not destroy. In top-band writing, plant a caveat proactively (cf. Day 29's mitigate) to show you see the limit; it strengthens, not weakens, the claim. Use caveat when guarding a generalisation: "X is true, with the caveat that…". This is the visible structure of perceptive judgement — the marker sees you holding the claim and its boundary at once.

Common collocations: with the caveat that · a necessary caveat · the caveat is · one caveat · important caveat

critique /krɪˈtiːk/ noun / verb

A detailed, reasoned evaluation of a text's merits, methods, or assumptions — not mere fault-finding.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a critique of] [the narrator's reliability]" · "[the essay critiques] [the ideology]" · "[offer a critique]"

"The IO rationale offers a critique of the text's class politics — not 'the book is bad' but a reasoned account of how its form naturalises privilege, which is exactly the move Day 24's Marxist lens names." — IO / extended-essay framing

IB Tip Distinct from "criticise" (negative) — critique = reasoned evaluation, which can be affirmative. Connects to Day 24's lenses (a feminist / Marxist critique) and to Day 22's thesis / contention. In the IO and HLE, "critique" is the expected verb; use it precisely. Avoid the colloquial "critique = pan" — an IB critique weighs, it does not merely fault. A critique that only attacks is not a critique; it is a dismissal.

Common collocations: a critique of · offer a critique · feminist critique · Marxist critique · critical critique

criterion /kraɪˈtɪəriən/ noun (pl. criteria)

A standard or principle by which something is judged or evaluated.

Literary Usage Pattern "[by what criterion]…" · "[the criterion of] [coherence]" · "[apply a single criterion]"

"Before comparing the two texts, the essay states its criterion — not 'which is better' but 'which more fully dramatises enclosure' — and holding both texts to one explicit standard is what makes the comparison fair (cf. Day 30's equivalence)." — comparative-method note

IB Tip The backbone of any evaluation. Distinct from evidence (the data) — criterion = the standard you judge by. In Paper 2, name your criterion before comparing (it pre-empts false analogy, Day 29). Links to Day 30's equivalence (the criterion establishes comparability). Plural is criteria (not "criterions"). Use criterion to show the examiner your judgement is principled, not impressionistic — a positioned, defensible reading starts from a stated criterion.

Common collocations: by what criterion · the criterion of · single criterion · stated criterion · apply the criterion

✏️ Practice — Write the Evaluative Sentence

Four drills. Write short answers; the model notes follow each.

  1. Word-bank insertion. Choose the correct Day 31 word for each gap (each used once):
    Word bank: evaluate · assess · perceive · discern · judicious · measured · salient · cogent · tenable · caveat · critique · criterion
    a) "Before comparing the two texts, state your ____________ — 'which more fully dramatises enclosure', not 'which is better'." → criterion
    b) "A perceptive reading must ____________ the repeated 'window' (a motif) from the isolated one (mere setting)." → discern
    c) "The conclusion is ____________: it qualifies the synthesis instead of overstating it." → measured
    d) "The essay's ____________ selection of two stanzas lets the analysis go deep, not wide." → judicious
    e) "Reading the 'door' as pure optimism is no longer ____________ once the second stanza locks it." → tenable
    f) "The IO rationale offers a Marxist ____________ of how the form naturalises privilege." → critique
  2. Mini evaluative paragraph. On the two lines below, deploy at least three Day 31 words and state each one's effect:
    Texts Text A: "the gate was locked"  ·  Text B: "the door would not give"
    Model: the salient feature of both openings is enclosure (the effect: the comparison is lifted above plot to form); the essay assesses what the 'lock' gains each poem and what it costs the speaker (the effect: the figure is weighed, not celebrated); the resulting claim that both texts figure freedom as externally imposed is cogent because it chains the two images into one inference the reader cannot dodge.
  3. Write the top-band evaluative sentence. Combine a concessive clause (Day 30 / Day 22 concede) + a caveat (word 10) + an evaluate (word 1) of the effect, in one sentence:
    Starter "Although both texts figure the door as enclosure…"
    Model: "Although both texts figure the door as enclosure, the parallelism is — with the caveat that they enclose different subjects — best evaluated as a shared critique of externally imposed freedom, since each text's lock denies the speaker agency rather than merely withholding space." One sentence: relation conceded, limit stated, significance pronounced. The move Day 29 previewed, now fluent.
  4. Distinguish on demand. In one sentence each, separate these Day 31 pairs you will be tested on: (i) evaluate vs. assess (value pronounced vs. extent measured); (ii) tenable vs. cogent (defensible vs. convincing); (iii) judicious vs. measured (wise choice vs. restrained expression). Keep all three pairs in your exam armour alongside the Day 29 and Day 30 distinctions.

🎯 Study Tips — Making Judgement Visible

  1. Evaluate, don't just describe. The verb in the command term "evaluate" demands a judgement of significance, not a paraphrase. End every paragraph on an evaluated claim — "this device evaluates to a critique of…" — never on a description of the device. Description is band 3; evaluation is band 7.
  2. Assess both sides before you synthesise. In Paper 2, assess what each text gains and costs before fusing them (cf. Day 30's convergence / divergence). A synthesis built on an un-assessed figure collapses the moment a counter-reading appears. Assess is the inventory; evaluate is the verdict.
  3. Perceive through a lens, not an intention. "The text positions the reader to perceive X" (word 3) keeps your analysis on the page; "the author wants us to feel X" commits the intentional fallacy. Perception language (perceive / discern) is how you discuss reader-response without speculating about the writer's mind — and discern is your guard against conflating like details.
  4. Measured beats both overclaim and hedge. A measured claim names its limit without collapsing its point (word 6). Overclaim ("this proves…") invites the marker to push back; hedge ("this perhaps might suggest…") abandons the argument. Measured — confident but proportionate — is the register of a 7. Plant a caveat (word 10) proactively to make the limit visible.
  5. State your criterion before you compare. In Paper 2, name the standard you judge by (word 12) up front — "which more fully dramatises enclosure", not "which is better". A stated criterion pre-empts false analogy (Day 29) and establishes the comparability Day 30's equivalence requires. Principled judgement starts from a principle.
  6. Tenable is the floor, cogent the goal. Before calling a reading "correct", check it is tenable (defensible with text); then push it to cogent (convincing by reasoning). A tenable-but-unexciting claim beats a bold-but-indefensible one — and a cogent paragraph is the visible product of all Week 5's moves (assert, qualify, parallel, evaluate) working together. Hold the claim and its boundary at once.
