📖 Today's New Words — 12 Technical & Rhetorical Terms

NEW CATEGORY — "LITERARY DEVICES & RHETORIC"

From describing to naming. Weeks 1-2 gave you verbs for what texts do (subvert, juxtapose, foreground) and adjectives for what they depict (oppressive, mercurial, evocative). Today you learn the technical terms for the devices themselves — metaphor, irony, symbolism, anaphora. This is the vocabulary of IB Paper 1 commentary and Paper 2 analysis: the words that let you say precisely how a text produces meaning. Notice the architecture we're building: a device spectrum spanning figurative language (metaphor, simile, personification) → interpretive concepts (symbolism, motif, irony) → structural / rhetorical schemes (juxtaposition, hyperbole, allusion, metonymy, oxymoron, anaphora). Picking the exact device is what separates a 5 from a 7 — and, like Day 1's "juxtapose," several of today's nouns are the noun forms of verbs you already know.

metaphor /ˈmetəfə(r)/ noun

A figure of speech that identifies one thing as another, asserting a likeness without "like" or "as"; an implicit comparison that transfers qualities from one domain to another.

Literary Usage Pattern "X [is / becomes] Y" — "the [city] is a [jungle]" · "his [words] [are] weapons"

"In The Great Gatsby, the green light is a metaphor for the unreachable American Dream — desire itself made visible across the dark water, always approached and never reached." — The Great Gatsby analysis

IB Tip The cornerstone device of literary analysis. Distinct from simile (see box below): metaphor asserts identity; simile only likens. In IB, never stop at naming it — analyse what is transferred and why. A metaphor is an argument: equating two domains makes a claim about both. Connect to Day 1's foreground and subvert — a metaphor can foreground an idea or subvert a reader's expectation in a single move. Watch for the extended metaphor (sustained across a passage) and the mixed metaphor (a flaw when two comparisons collide).

Common collocations: extended metaphor · mixed metaphor · dead metaphor · sustained metaphor · central metaphor

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: metaphor vs. simile

Both are figurative comparisons, but they commit differently:

metaphor — Asserts identity: "X is Y" (the city is a jungle). Total, compressed, no qualifier.
→ "All the world's a stage."

simile — Compares with "like / as": "X is like Y" (the city is like a jungle). Explicit, hedged.
→ "My love is like a red, red rose."

The test: does the text equate the two domains (metaphor) or merely liken them (simile)? Memory trick: simile = "similar" + "like / as"; metaphor carries the weight alone. A metaphor fuses; a simile holds the two apart so the reader can watch the comparison happen. In IB, name which the author chose and why that degree of commitment matters. This pair anchors your Day 15 device toolkit.

simile /ˈsɪməli/ noun

A figure of speech that compares two unlike things using "like" or "as", making the resemblance explicit rather than asserted.

Literary Usage Pattern "X [is] [like / as] Y" — "he [fled] [like] a shadow" · "cold [as] stone"

"In Frankenstein, Shelley writes that the creature's 'yellow eye' fixes on Victor 'like' a 'malignant thing' — the simile externalises Victor's terror as a predator he himself has made." — Frankenstein analysis

IB Tip Distinct from metaphor (see box above): a simile hedges with "like / as"; a metaphor commits. A simile can signal a speaker's caution or a character's limited grasp; a metaphor signals total identification. Note when an author shifts simile → metaphor — that escalation often marks a turning point. In Paper 1 commentary, the choice between them is itself worth a sentence.

Common collocations: simile and metaphor · apt simile · stock simile · Homeric simile · sustained simile

personification /pəˌsɒnɪfɪˈkeɪʃn/ noun

A figure of speech that attributes human qualities, actions, or emotions to non-human things, abstractions, or animals.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The wind] [whispers / remembers / refuses]" · "[Death] [comes knocking]"

"In Emily Dickinson's poetry, 'Death' is personified as a courteous gentleman who 'kindly stopped for me' — mortality rendered as a social call, at once defusing and unsettling." — Dickinson analysis

IB Tip Go beyond "gives human traits." Ask which human trait, and to what effect? Personification often reveals a text's attitude to nature, time, or fate. It overlaps with pathetic fallacy (nature reflecting a character's emotion) — a useful sub-category to name. Connect to Day 1's evoke: personification is a prime way a text evokes feeling. Avoid the weak reading "the writer is being descriptive" — push to theme.

Common collocations: personification of · anthropomorphic personification · pathetic fallacy · use of personification

symbolism /ˈsɪmbəlɪzəm/ noun

The use of symbols — objects, characters, colours, or actions that stand for something beyond their literal sense — to convey deeper, often abstract, meaning.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The bird] [symbolises] freedom" · "[The colour red] [recurs as] a symbol of…"

"In A Streetcar Named Desire, the paper lantern Blanche drapes over the bare bulb symbolises her fragile attempt to soften a harsh reality she cannot bear to see." — A Streetcar Named Desire analysis

IB Tip Distinguish a symbol from a motif (see Day 15, word 6): a symbol is a single resonant signifier; a motif is the pattern of its recurrence. Avoid static "X = Y" decoding — IB rewards showing how a symbol's meaning shifts across the text. Connect to Day 1's foreground: what the text foregrounds as symbolic is never accidental. Root: Greek symbolon, a token of recognition shared between two parties.

Common collocations: symbol of · richly symbolic · symbolic resonance · use of symbolism · Christian symbolism

irony /ˈaɪrəni/ noun

A contrast between appearance and reality, expectation and result, or what is said and what is meant. Three principal kinds: verbal (saying the opposite of intent), situational (outcome contrary to expectation), and dramatic (the audience knows what a character does not).

Literary Usage Pattern "[The fire station] [burns down] — the [irony] is…" · "verbal [irony] in her 'thanks'"

"In Othello, the audience's knowledge that Iago deceives makes Othello's trust devastatingly ironic — dramatic irony that tightens the tragedy with every kiss of betrayal." — Othello analysis

IB Tip Always name the type. Verbal irony ≠ sarcasm (sarcasm is aggressive; verbal irony need not be). Irony is the engine of IB Paper 1 tone analysis and links directly to Day 5's satirical and subversive. Distinct from mere coincidence (see box below: verbal vs. situational). A text's irony often marks where it wants you to read against the surface — exactly where marks are won.

Common collocations: dramatic irony · situational irony · verbal irony · heavy irony · bitter irony

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: verbal irony vs. situational irony

Both are "irony," but the gap sits in a different place:

verbal irony — A gap between what is said and what is meant (a language / tone effect). "Thanks a lot," said to the person who ruined it.
→ "What a perfect day," spoken in a storm.

situational irony — A gap between what happens and what was expected (a plot / event effect). The fire station burns down.
→ The pacifist wins the war by becoming a tyrant.

The test: is the contrast in language (verbal) or in outcome (situational)? A third kind — dramatic irony — sits in knowledge: the audience knows more than a character (Othello trusts Iago). Name the exact type in essays; lumping them as "ironic" loses the precision IB rewards. This pair is a cornerstone of your Day 15 rhetoric cluster.

motif /məʊˈtiːf/ noun

A recurring element — image, idea, sound, colour, or action — that gains significance through repetition and helps develop a theme.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The bird] [recurs as] a motif of…" · "[Light and dark] [form] a [motif]"

"In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov's recurring encounters with the colour yellow form a motif of moral sickness that builds toward his psychological collapse." — Dostoevsky analysis

IB Tip Distinct from symbol and theme: a motif is the pattern of recurrence; a symbol is a single resonant object; a theme is the idea the motif helps articulate. IB rewards tracing a motif across the whole text, not spotting it once. Map its trajectory (where it appears, how it changes) and you have the spine of a strong commentary or Paper 2 paragraph.

Common collocations: recurring motif · central motif · dominant motif · musical motif · visual motif

juxtaposition /ˌdʒʌkstəpəˈzɪʃn/ noun

The placement of two or more things side by side for the purpose of comparison or contrast; the resulting device when elements are set against each other.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The] [juxtaposition] of [wealth and poverty]" · "by [juxtaposing] X and Y"

"In A Tale of Two Cities, Dickens opens with the juxtaposition 'It was the best of times, it was the worst of times' — opposing states yoked to frame a world on the brink." — Dickens analysis

IB Tip The noun form of Day 1's verb juxtapose. You already know how to juxtapose; now name the resulting device. Juxtaposition is structural — it works through arrangement, not wording. Ask what the contrast reveals about class, power, or theme. Connect to Day 1's foreground and contrast: placing two things together foregrounds the difference. One of the highest-yield devices for Paper 2 comparative essays.

Common collocations: stark juxtaposition · deliberate juxtaposition · juxtaposition of · striking juxtaposition

hyperbole /haɪˈpɜːbəli/ noun

Deliberate exaggeration for emphasis, effect, or humour; an overstatement not meant to be taken literally.

Literary Usage Pattern "[I've told you] [a million times]" · "[the] [world's] [end]"

"In Macbeth, Lady Macbeth's 'all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand' is hyperbole that measures a guilt too vast for any cleansing — the exaggeration is the point." — Macbeth analysis

IB Tip Exaggeration with purpose. Don't dismiss it as "just exaggeration" — analyse what the overstatement reveals (guilt, scale, absurdity). It often pairs with understatement (litotes) for ironic effect. Connect to Day 5's polemical: hyperbole amplifies a stance. In satire, hyperbole is a weapon; in tragedy, a measure of feeling too large for words.

Common collocations: mere hyperbole · rhetorical hyperbole · hyperbole and understatement

allusion /əˈluːʒn/ noun

An indirect reference to a person, event, text, myth, or work of art, presumed familiar to the reader; a second text summoned inside the first.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] [clear] allusion to [the Bible]" · "[the] [myth of]…"

"In Lord of the Flies, the boys' fall from order alludes to the Garden of Eden and the Fall — Golding borrows a myth to frame civilisation as fragile and original sin as innate." — Golding analysis

IB Tip An allusion is a second text invoked inside the first. Name the source and the effect: what does the echo add? Allusions build intertextuality — a key IB concept (connect to Day 2's interrogate and contextualise). A biblical or classical allusion can charge a modern scene with inherited meaning in a single phrase. Trace the allusion to its source and your analysis deepens instantly.

Common collocations: biblical allusion · classical allusion · passing allusion · direct allusion · mythological allusion

metonymy /mɪˈtɒnəmi/ noun

A figure of speech in which one word or concept is substituted for another with which it is closely associated — part for whole, container for contents, or institution for the people who run it.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The crown] [declared war]" (crown = monarchy) · "[the pen] [is mightier]"

"In Orwell's 1984, 'the Party' stands as metonymy for total power — the organisation substituted for the system that governs every thought." — Orwell analysis

IB Tip Distinct from metaphor (see box below): metonymy substitutes by association / contiguity (the crown = the monarchy — things that sit together in the real world); metaphor transfers by resemblance across distinct domains (time is a thief). Both condense meaning; metonymy is about relation, metaphor about likeness. Naming metonymy shows you see how a text wields power through substitution.

Common collocations: metonymy for · synecdoche and metonymy · use of metonymy

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: metonymy vs. metaphor

Both substitute one term for another, but on different grounds:

metonymy — Substitution by association / contiguity: the crown = the monarchy; the pen = writing; the White House = the administration. The two things are adjacent in the real world.
→ "The crown declared war."

metaphor — Transfer by resemblance across distinct domains: time is a thief; the city is a jungle. The two things are not otherwise related — only a shared quality links them.
→ "Time is a thief."

The test: are the two terms next to each other in reality (metonymy) or linked only by a shared quality (metaphor)? "The White House spoke" = metonymy (place for power); "the White House is a fortress" = metaphor (likeness). Confusing them is a classic IB slip — name the right one and your device analysis is airtight. This pair rounds out your Day 15 toolkit.

oxymoron /ˌɒksɪˈmɔːrɒn/ noun

A figure of speech that pairs two contradictory or opposite terms (e.g., "deafening silence," "bitter-sweet"), yoking opposition within a single phrase.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [civil war]" · "[the] [cruel kindness]"

"In Romeo and Juliet, 'parting is such sweet sorrow' yokes oxymoron to capture love and loss at once — the contradiction is the feeling." — Shakespeare analysis

IB Tip Two contradictory words in one phrase. Distinct from paradox (a contradictory statement or idea, larger than a phrase): oxymoron is local, paradox is structural. Both signal a truth that logic alone can't hold. Connect to Day 5's ambivalent: an oxymoron often names an ambivalent response in two words. Watch for clusters of oxymora building a theme of irresolution.

Common collocations: apparent oxymoron · rhetorical oxymoron · string of oxymora

anaphora /əˈnæfərə/ noun

The repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses or sentences, used to build rhythm, emphasis, or accumulating force.

Literary Usage Pattern "[We shall fight]… [we shall fight]… [we shall fight]"

"In Martin Luther King's 'I Have a Dream,' the anaphora 'I have a dream' returns like a refrain, building momentum from vision to vision until the speech becomes a chant." — MLK analysis

IB Tip A structural / rhetorical device of repetition. Name it in speeches, sermons, manifestos, and polemical prose (connect to Day 5's polemical). Ask what the repeated unit accumulates toward. Distinct from epistrophe (repetition at the end of clauses). Anaphora is especially valuable for non-literary texts (Paper 1, Section 2) — it signals insistence, unity, or rising urgency. A must for your rhetoric vocabulary.

Common collocations: use of anaphora · rhetorical anaphora · anaphoric repetition

🎯 Building Your Device Toolkit

  1. You now have the vocabulary to NAME the devices, not just feel them. Phase 1 gave you verbs for what texts do; Phase 2 gave you adjectives for what they depict; today you gain the technical terms for how they mean. In IB, naming the device precisely is the difference between "the writer uses a comparison" (vague) and "the extended metaphor equates desire with distance, foregrounding unattainability" (analytical).
  2. Name the device as a verb, not a label. Never end at "this is a metaphor." Push to effect: what does the metaphor do to meaning, tone, or theme? The device is evidence for an argument, exactly as Day 1 taught with foreground / subvert / problematize — now you can cite the precise mechanism.
  3. The three distinction pairs are exam armour. metaphor/simile (commitment), verbal/situational irony (where the gap sits), metonymy/metaphor (association vs. resemblance). These confusions cost marks; master them and your device analysis is airtight. They'll consolidate in your Week 3 review (Day 21).
  4. juxtaposition is the bridge to comparative work. As the noun of Day 1's "juxtapose," it is your sharpest tool for Paper 2 — set two texts side by side and the contrast reveals each. Practise naming juxtapositions in the texts you're comparing this week.
  5. Rhetoric belongs to non-literary texts too. anaphora, hyperbole, and irony are everywhere in speeches, adverts, and op-eds (Paper 1, Section 2). Today's toolkit works double duty: literary and non-literary. Spot them in the news this week and you'll be ready for any text type.
  6. Deploy these in your next commentary. When you next write on a set text, force yourself to name at least four Day 15 devices precisely and trace one motif across the passage. By Paper 2, the right term should arrive without thinking — that's technical precision.
