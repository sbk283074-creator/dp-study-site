✍️ Guided Mini-Analysis — Model & Rubric

The capstone skill is deploying these terms on a real passage. Below: a model paragraph that names terms from all five grids. Highlighted = Week 4 vocabulary.

A thesis that the text foregrounds female entrapment is advanced through cohesion built on a lexical field of confinement, while the register shifts from formal to colloquial as the narrator's stance frays. Nominalisation ("the erasure of her name") and the passive voice background the agent, and an imperative ("leave") jolts the declarative calm — a coordination of equal clauses that withholds cause where subordination would supply it. Alliteration ("silent, shuttered") and sibilance ("she slipped, she sighed") slow the line toward a caesura, and enjambment spills the confession across the metre. Through a patriarchy lens, the male gaze frames her as object, and the uncanny recurrence of her mother's voice reads as repression surfacing from the unconscious. The effect is a text where stylistic choice, sentence move, and critical lens interlock so tightly that meaning cannot be read at a single level.

That paragraph names 24 distinct Week 4 terms across all five grids — metalanguage (thesis, foregrounding, cohesion, lexical field, register, stance), syntax (nominalisation, passive, imperative, declarative, coordination, subordination), word-meaning (formal, colloquial), sound (alliteration, sibilance, caesura, enjambment, metre), lens (patriarchy, male gaze, uncanny, repression, unconscious). It moves macro → micro and ties each device to an effect, not just a label.

⚖️ Distinction Quick-Ref (Your Exam Armour)

The fifteen pairs most often confused across the five grids. In an essay, naming the right term from a pair is what separates 5 from 7.

① cohesion vs. coherence

Cohesion is the visible linking (pronouns, conjunctions, lexical ties); coherence is the invisible logical sense. A text can be cohesive but incoherent.

② thesis vs. contention

Thesis is the overall central claim; contention is the specific asserted position argued within it. Umbrella vs. the point being fought for.

③ qualify vs. concede

Qualify limits your own claim ("this is largely true"); concede grants a point to the opponent before rebutting. Both hedge force, opposite direction.

④ coordination vs. subordination

Coordination links equal clauses (and/but); subordination nests unequal clauses (because/although). Equal vs. nested.

⑤ sentence function vs. illocutionary act

Sentence function is grammatical (declarative/interrogative); illocutionary act is pragmatic — what the utterance DOES (request, warn). Form vs. force.

⑥ nominalisation vs. passive

Nominalisation turns a process into a noun; passive shifts the patient to subject. Both background the agent, different mechanism.

⑦ denotation vs. connotation

Denotation = literal meaning; connotation = felt associations. Word-choice analysis lives in connotation.

⑧ semantic field vs. lexical set

Semantic field = related meanings around a concept (loose web); lexical set = words of the same topic/class (tighter). Field is meaning-based, set is class-based.

⑨ polysemy vs. ambiguity

Polysemy = one word, multiple related senses ("bank"); ambiguity = a structure/word with two distinct readings. Polysemy is inherent, ambiguity is undecidable.

⑩ alliteration vs. assonance

Alliteration repeats initial consonants; assonance repeats vowel sounds within words. Edge vs. middle of the word.

⑪ iamb vs. trochee

Iamb = unstressed-stressed (da-DUM); trochee = stressed-unstressed (DA-dum). Opposite foot.

⑫ caesura vs. enjambment

Caesura = a pause within a line; enjambment = a line running on without pause. Stop vs. flow.

⑬ ideology vs. hegemony

Ideology is a belief system; hegemony is that ideology's dominance felt as "common sense". Belief vs. its grip.

⑭ othering vs. the subaltern

Othering is the act of marking a group as alien; the subaltern is the marginalised group itself (no voice in power). Process vs. subject.

⑮ patriarchy vs. the male gaze

Patriarchy is the systemic structure of male authority; the male gaze is one mechanism — framing women as objects for male looking. System vs. lens.

🎯 Assessment Tips

  1. Recognition is not ownership. You "know" a Week 4 term only when you can spell it, define it, and deploy it on an unseen passage in under a minute. The Master Sheet tests the first two; the Your Task tests the third. All three must pass.
  2. Reach for one term from each grid, every time. A commentary that names twelve lens terms but no syntax move or sound device is unbalanced and caps below the top band. The five-grid balance row in the rubric is the single biggest lever on your mark.
  3. The pair is the differentiator. "This is coordination" gets identification; "this is coordination, not subordination, because the clauses are equal and withhold cause" gets the top band. The fifteen distinctions are your edge — keep them alive into the application phase.
  4. Devices must drive meaning, not decorate. Top commentaries use each term to explain an effect ("the enjambment spills the confession, refusing the closure a full stop would grant"). If you can delete the device name and the sentence loses nothing, you've listed, not analysed.
  5. Macro before micro. Orient with a critical lens or overall stylistic shape (patriarchy, foregrounding) before zooming into a single sound or syntactic move. The model paragraph moves whole-text → sentence on purpose. Examiners reward that architecture of thought.
  6. Week 4 is the application engine of the whole programme. These sixty terms turn your Phase 1–3 device toolkit into argument: the metalanguage frames the claim, the syntax moves show how the text persuades, and the lenses reveal what it hides. Lock them in now and the remaining weeks become synthesis, not relearning.
