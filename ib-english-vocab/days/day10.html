📖 Today's New Words — 12 Mood & Atmosphere Adjectives

NEW CATEGORY — "DESCRIBING MOOD"

From person to atmosphere. You can now render a setting (Day 8) and a character (Day 9). Today you learn to name the emotional weather — the felt quality of a passage. These adjectives answer "how does this text feel to read?" and they are the bridge between objective description and critical judgement. Notice they operate on a spectrum from dark/heavy (sombre, melancholic, glacial) through otherworldly (spectral, ethereal) to agitated/raw (febrile, visceral) and lingering (haunting, elegiac, wistful). In IB commentaries, mood words are your evidence for tone and effect — never assert "the mood is sad" when you can prove it is elegiac or sombre.

sombre/ˈsɒmbə(r)/adjective

Dark, gloomy, and serious in tone; heavy with gravity or sadness.

Literary Usage Pattern"a [sombre] palette / The [sombre] tone [refuses / weighs]…"

"The sombre palette of The Road — ashen greys and dying light — refuses the consolation of colour, mirroring a world drained of futurity."— The Road analysis

IB Tip Use for darkness that is serious and weighty, not merely scary. Distinct from "melancholic" (see box): sombre describes the external tone/atmosphere; melancholic the inward, pensive sadness. Pairs with Day 8's "bleak" — sombre is the tonal cousin of bleak. Vital for post-apocalyptic, elegiac, and tragic texts.

Common collocations: sombre palette · sombre tone · sombre mood · sombre atmosphere · sombre music

spectral/ˈspɛktrəl/adjective

Ghostly, phantom-like; seeming to belong to another, uncanny world.

Literary Usage Pattern"a [spectral] figure / The [spectral] presence [haunts]…"

"The spectral figures of the dead in Hamlet's Denmark — Old Hamlet's ghost, the procession of kings — unsettle the living's sense of an ordered world."— Hamlet analysis

IB Tip Use for the ghostly and the uncanny. Distinct from "ethereal" (see box): spectral = ghostly and unsettling (hints death/otherworld danger); ethereal = delicate and enchanting otherworldliness. Both are otherworldly but spectral disturbs, ethereal delights. Strong for gothic, revenge tragedy, and texts about memory's return.

Common collocations: spectral figure · spectral light · spectral presence · spectral image · spectral white

haunting/ˈhɔːntɪŋ/adjective

Lingering in the mind in a disturbingly memorable way; not easily shaken off.

Literary Usage Pattern"a [haunting] image / The [haunting] close [outlives]…"

"The haunting final image of the kite in The Kite Runner — 'there is a way to be good again' — outlives the novel's violence, returning whenever we think we understand guilt."— The Kite Runner analysis

IB Tip Use for that which refuses to leave the reader. Distinct from "foreboding" (see box): haunting looks back (a disturbance that lingers after); foreboding looks forward (dread of what is coming). A haunting image is the after-echo; a foreboding one is the warning. Excellent for endings, trauma, and unresolved memory.

Common collocations: haunting image · haunting memory · haunting beauty · haunting silence · haunting melody

elegiac/ˌɛlɪˈdʒaɪək/adjective

Mournful and lamenting; expressing sorrow for what is lost or passing.

Literary Usage Pattern"an [elegiac] tone / The [elegiac] passage [mourns]…"

"The elegiac cadence of Never Let Me Go mourns a childhood the clones were never permitted to keep, even as it pretends to recollect it calmly."— Never Let Me Go analysis

IB Tip Use for sorrowful remembrance — the tone of loss acknowledged. Root: elegy, a poem of mourning. Distinct from "wistful" (gentle regret) and "melancholic" (pensive sadness): elegiac is specifically lament for the lost. Pinnacle word for texts about memory, mortality, and empire's end. Deploy it when a passage weeps without tears.

Common collocations: elegiac tone · elegiac mood · elegiac poem · elegiac passage · elegiac note

foreboding/fɔːˈbɔːdɪŋ/adjective

Ominous; suggesting that something bad is about to happen.

Literary Usage Pattern"a [foreboding] opening / The [foreboding] detail [anticipates]…"

"The fog that opens Bleak House is a foreboding of the entangling 'fog' of Chancery that will blight every life the novel touches."— Bleak House analysis

IB Tip Use for anticipatory dread. Distinct from "haunting" (see box): foreboding points forward to doom; haunting points back to what won't leave. Often sits at a text's threshold (opening image, first scene). Pairs with Day 8's "forbidding" — both warn the reader before events. Classic for gothic and realist openings that foreshadow fate.

Common collocations: foreboding opening · foreboding atmosphere · foreboding silence · foreboding sense · foreboding detail

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: sombre vs. melancholic

Both name darkness, but at different levels:

sombre — Dark, serious, weighty in tone (external, atmospheric).
→ "The sombre palette of the film refuses any relief of colour."

melancholic — Deeply pensive, inwardly wistful-sad (a quality of feeling).
→ "The melancholic brooding of the narrator turns every memory into loss."

The test: sombre describes the atmosphere's weight; melancholic describes the mood's sadness. A sombre scene can be melancholic, but a melancholic passage need not be dark — it can be lit by gentle, regretful light. Memory trick: sombre = the room is dark; melancholic = the heart is sad.

melancholic/ˌmɛlənˈkɒlɪk/adjective

Deeply pensive and wistfully sad; given to thoughtful sorrow.

Literary Usage Pattern"a [melancholic] evocation / The [melancholic] strain [broods]…"

"Conrad's melancholic evocation of empire's end in Heart of Darkness broods over a civilisation aware, dimly, of its own decay."— Heart of Darkness analysis

IB Tip Use for thoughtful, reflective sadness. Distinct from "sombre" (see box): melancholic = inward pensive sorrow; sombre = external dark weight. Also distinct from "elegiac" (laments a specific loss) — melancholic need not name what is lost. Root: the medieval "humours" (black bile). Excellent for narrators who reflect rather than act.

Common collocations: melancholic mood · melancholic tone · melancholic strain · melancholic brooding · melancholic evocation

wistful/ˈwɪs(t)fʊl/adjective

Sadly longing; tinged with gentle regret for something absent or past.

Literary Usage Pattern"a [wistful] close / The [wistful] glance [aches]…"

"The wistful closing of The Great Gatsby — 'so we beat on, boats against the current' — aches for a past that cannot be recovered, yet without bitterness."— The Great Gatsby analysis

IB Tip Use for gentle, regretful longing (softer than melancholic). Distinct from "elegiac" (open lament) and "melancholic" (heavy brooding): wistful is the lightest of the sorrow family — a sigh, not a sob. Perfect for endings that yearn. Watch the softness: wistful should never be used where the feeling is crushing.

Common collocations: wistful tone · wistful glance · wistful mood · wistful remembrance · wistful smile

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: spectral vs. ethereal

Both are otherworldly, but with opposite affect:

spectral — Ghostly, unsettling; hints death or uncanny danger.
→ "The spectral lighting made the corridor feel haunted."

ethereal — Delicate, light, enchanting otherworldliness.
→ "Her ethereal presence seemed hardly of this earth."

The test: spectral disturbs (ghosts, threat); ethereal delights (angels, beauty). Both remove a figure from the mundane, but spectral is the gothic cousin, ethereal the romantic one. Memory trick: spectral = spooky spectre; ethereal = airy, like ether. In a commentary, choosing wrong flips your whole reading of a scene.

ethereal/ɪˈθɪəriəl/adjective

Delicate, light, and airy; seemingly too perfect or refined for this world.

Literary Usage Pattern"an [ethereal] quality / The [ethereal] figure [transcends]…"

"The ethereal quality of Laura's presence in The Glass Menagerie renders her almost a memory already, not quite of this world."— The Glass Menagerie analysis

IB Tip Use for delicate, otherworldly beauty. Distinct from "spectral" (see box): ethereal enchants; spectral unsettles. Often marks a character who is idealized, fragile, or already slipping into memory. Pairs with Day 8's "verdant" (both can signal an Edenic, not-quite-real quality). Watch: ethereal is praise-adjacent; don't use it for menace.

Common collocations: ethereal quality · ethereal beauty · ethereal light · ethereal voice · ethereal presence

languid/ˈlaŋɡwɪd/adjective

Weak, sluggish, and lacking energy; tiredly slow or indolent.

Literary Usage Pattern"a [languid] afternoon / The [languid] pace [drags]…"

"The languid Southern afternoons of The Sound and the Fury mirror the Compson family's exhausted, drifting decline."— The Sound and the Fury analysis

IB Tip Use for exhausted, energy-drained slowness. Distinct from "lethargic" (clinical) — languid can be luxurious or decadent, not just tired. Pairs as a contrast to "febrile" (see below): languid = low energy; febrile = high, agitated energy. Both describe a charged atmosphere, opposite poles. Good for decay, heat, and post-imperial exhaustion.

Common collocations: languid pace · languid afternoon · languid movement · languid heat · languid grace

glacial/ˈɡleɪʃl/adjective

Cold, hostile, and emotionally frozen; lacking warmth or human feeling.

Literary Usage Pattern"a [glacial] manner / The [glacial] silence [freezes]…"

"The glacial manner of the commanding officers in The Handmaid's Tale conveys a power that freezes the self into obedient stillness."— The Handmaid's Tale analysis

IB Tip Use for cold hostility. Distinct from Day 8's "forbidding" (menacing, keeps you out) — glacial is about frozen feeling, emotional coldness. Pairs with "sombre" (both heavy/dark) but glacial adds the chill of power. Excellent for dystopian authority, emotionally absent parents, and calcified institutions.

Common collocations: glacial manner · glacial silence · glacial stare · glacial indifference · glacial cold

febrile/ˈfiːbraɪl/adjective

Feverish, agitated, and restless; charged with nervous, barely-contained energy.

Literary Usage Pattern"a [febrile] atmosphere / The [febrile] crowd [teeters]…"

"The febrile energy of the Parisian streets in Les Misérables mirrors a society on the edge of revolutionary combustion."— Les Misérables analysis

IB Tip Use for agitated, overheated intensity. Distinct from "languid" (its opposite — sluggish calm); febrile = high, restless energy. Root: febris = fever. Perfect for pre-revolution, mania, and contagion-of-feeling scenes. Watch: febrile implies instability about to tip — pair it with foreboding for maximum tension.

Common collocations: febrile atmosphere · febrile energy · febrile excitement · febrile mood · febrile imagination

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: foreboding vs. haunting

Both deal with dread, but in opposite time directions:

foreboding — Anticipatory dread; senses doom before/as it arrives.
→ "The foreboding opening fog warns of entanglement to come."

haunting — Lingering disturbance; what won't leave after the fact.
→ "The haunting final image returns long after the book is closed."

The test: foreboding looks forward (warning); haunting looks back (after-echo). A text can open with foreboding and close with haunting — dread announced, then dread that remains. Memory trick: foreboding = before; haunting = haunts after. Both are cornerstone mood words for your Day 13 review.

visceral/ˈvɪsərəl/adjective

Felt in the gut; raw, instinctive, and bodily rather than intellectual.

Literary Usage Pattern"a [visceral] response / The [visceral] violence [recoils]…"

"The visceral violence of Disgrace forces a bodily recoil that no abstract argument about post-apartheid South Africa could achieve."— Disgrace analysis

IB Tip Use for raw, bodily impact. Distinct from "emotional" (broad) — visceral is specifically gut-level, physical feeling. Root: viscera = internal organs. Vital when you want to show a text acts on the body, not just the mind — trauma, violence, desire. A precision upgrade over "powerful" or "moving." Pairs with "febrile" (both high-intensity affect).

Common collocations: visceral response · visceral reaction · visceral violence · visceral fear · visceral intensity

🎯 Building Your Descriptive Toolkit

  1. You can now name the emotional weather. Setting (Day 8) + character (Day 9) + mood (today) = a fully rendered scene with feeling. These twelve adjectives answer "how does this feel to read?" — the question every IB commentary must answer.
  2. Build a mood spectrum, not a list. dark/heavy (sombre, melancholic, glacial) → otherworldly (spectral, ethereal) → agitated/raw (febrile, visceral) → lingering (haunting, elegiac, wistful). Match the exact shade to the passage; "sad" becomes "elegiac" or "wistful" depending on the loss.
  3. Mood words are evidence for tone and effect. Never assert a mood without the precise word. "The tone is sombre" is a claim; "the sombre palette refuses consolation" is analysis. The adjective does the argumentative work.
  4. The three Day 10 distinction pairs are exam armour. sombre/melancholic (atmospheric vs. inward), spectral/ethereal (unsettling vs. enchanting), foreboding/haunting (forward vs. back). They consolidate in your Day 13 weekly review alongside the Day 8 and Day 9 pairs.
  5. Pair mood with setting and character. A glacial manner (character) in a forbidding setting (place) under a sombre tone (mood) is a complete atmospheric analysis. The verb constructs, the adjective qualifies — now across three dimensions.
  6. Deploy these in your next commentary. When you next write on a set text, force three Day 10 adjectives into your effect paragraph. By Paper 2, "foreboding" vs. "haunting" should arrive without thinking.
