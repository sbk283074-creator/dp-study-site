🔁 Day 22 Spaced Review — 12 Stylistic & Linguistic Analysis Terms

A spaced-repetition pass on the stylistic & linguistic toolkit from Day 22 — at the 4-day interval, the point where a word either consolidates or begins to fade. These twelve are your argumentative and analytic scaffold: the six linguistic-metalanguage terms (cohesion, coherence, deixis, lexical field, foregrounding, intonation) and the six essay-architecture terms (thesis, contention, premise, qualify, concede, rebut). Read each, recall its precise sense before looking, then check. Notice how today's lexical field (Day 22) is the direct parent of today's semantic field and lexical set — the review and the new work are one continuous thread.

cohesion
The grammatical & lexical links (connectives, repetition, reference) that bind a text surface-level — how it sticks together.
coherence
The logical sense and flow of meaning — whether a text holds together as a whole in the reader's mind.
deixis
Context-dependent 'pointing' words (I, you, here, now, this) that anchor a text to its speech situation.
lexical field
A cluster of related words on a shared topic that recurs through a text, signalling its preoccupations.
foregrounding
When language deviates from the norm or repeats so as to draw the ear and the attention to itself.
intonation
The rise and fall of pitch in speech that conveys attitude, contrast, or emotion beyond the words.
thesis
The central claim or position an argument advances — the point the whole essay is built to prove.
contention
The assertive statement of the argument's main point, often in the opening topic sentence.
premise
A supporting proposition on which a conclusion rests; the 'given' a writer argues from.
qualify
To limit or add nuance to a claim so as to avoid overstatement and pre-empt objection.
concede
To acknowledge the strength of an opposing point before going on to rebut it.
rebut
To refute or counter an opposing argument, showing why it fails or is outweighed.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Lexical & Semantic Analysis Terms

NEW CATEGORY — "LEXICAL & SEMANTIC ANALYSIS"

From the sound of the word to the sense of the word. You can now hear a text (Day 25) and read it from a position (Day 24). Today you learn to name what words mean and how they mean it — the lexical and semantic metalanguage that turns "I like this word" into "the connotative field of 'dagger' enacts regicide before the plot names it." We split the twelve into four families: meaning-core (denotation, connotation — the literal vs. the summoned), meaning-networks (semantic field, lexical set, collocation — how words cluster), variation & register (register, dialect, jargon — language shaped by context and community), and figuration & multiplicity (euphemism, idiom, ambiguity, polysemy — when meaning bends, hides, or branches). In IB, semantic analysis is the bedrock: every commentary paragraph rests on a precise claim about which word was chosen and why.

denotation /ˌdiːnəʊˈteɪʃn/ noun

The literal, dictionary meaning of a word — its objective referent, independent of the feelings, associations, or ideology it carries.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The] denotation [of X is] Y" · "[Stripped to] bare denotation" · "[The] literal denotation [states]"

"In Orwell's 1984, the Party's newspeak works by shrinking denotation — 'doubleplusgood' collapses a range of meanings into a single approved referent, so thought itself loses the words to hold more than one idea." — Orwell, Nineteen Eighty-Four

IB Tip Denotation is the meaning a word states (contrast connotation, next box — the meaning it summons). In IB, the analytic move is to show the gap between the two: state the denotation precisely, then explore its connotative charge. It is the close cousin of Day 22's lexical field — a field is built from words that share denotation-space. Root: Latin de- + notare, 'to mark down'. The examiner rewards the candidate who can pin the literal sense before soaring into interpretation.

Common collocations: literal denotation · bare denotation · primary denotation · denotation and connotation · dictionary denotation

connotation /ˌkɒnəˈteɪʃn/ noun

The secondary, associative meaning a word carries beyond its literal denotation — the cultural, emotional, or ideological charge that rides on top of the referent.

Literary Usage Pattern "[X carries] connotative [weight]" · "[A] loaded connotation" · "[The] connotation [of power / violence]"

"In Shakespeare's Macbeth, 'dagger' denotes a weapon but connotes betrayal and regicide; the word's connotative field does the political work the plot only names." — Shakespeare, Macbeth

IB Tip Connotation = the meaning a word summons (contrast denotation = the meaning it states). This is the single most-used distinction in IB commentary. Link to Day 10's mood family (connotation is how a word produces feeling) and Day 24's lenses (connotation is where ideology hides — note which associations a text activates). Root: Latin con- + notare, 'to mark together with'. Name the connotation as precisely as the denotation.

Common collocations: loaded connotation · cultural connotation · negative connotation · connotative field · connotation and denotation

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: denotation vs. connotation

Both describe what a word means, but the seam is literal vs. associative:

denotation — The objective, dictionary meaning: what the word refers to.
→ "home" denotes a dwelling-place.

connotation — The subjective, associated meaning: what the word summons (warmth, safety, confinement, loss…).
→ "home" connotes belonging, or — for the exile — an aching absence.

The test: are you stating the referent (denotation) or the charge (connotation)? In IB, every strong commentary sentence lives in the gap between them — "the word denotes X, yet connotes Y, and that tension is the point." Keep this pair beside Day 22's foregrounding: connotation is meaning made felt. The examiner expects you to move from denotation to connotation as naturally as you move from quote to effect.

semantic field /səˈmæntɪk fiːld/ noun

A cluster of words in a text that share a related concept or area of meaning, accumulating into a network that signals the text's preoccupations — the poet's recurring light/dark or water/boundary vocabulary, for instance.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] semantic field [of X builds]" · "[The] field [of war / death] [recurs]" · "[Mapping] the field"

"In Wilfred Owen's war poetry, the semantic field of the body in pain — 'blood', 'gut', 'face', 'eyes' — accumulates until the human cost, not the glory, becomes the poem's true subject." — Wilfred Owen, war poems

IB Tip A semantic field is meaning-level cohesion (Day 22): words bind a text by concept, not just by sound. It is the broader sibling of Day 22's lexical field (a field named by a shared topic) — think of the semantic field as the conceptual map and the lexical field as the local cluster. In IB, mapping a field and naming its effect on tone is a band-7 move. Link to Day 24's ideology: which field a text builds is which world it imagines.

Common collocations: semantic field of · recurring semantic field · map the field · lexical and semantic field · the field accumulates

lexical set /ˈleksɪkl set/ noun

A group of words that are related in meaning and often share form or sound (a set of colour words, or the -ing verbs a speaker favours) — a tighter, more formal unit than a semantic field.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] lexical set [of verbs / nouns]" · "[The] set [signals] the speaker" · "[Group] the words into a set"

"In J.D. Salinger's The Catcher in the Rye, Holden's lexical set of negation — 'phony', 'crap', 'kill', 'lousy' — is his whole affective vocabulary, a set that marks him before he speaks a full sentence." — Salinger, The Catcher in the Rye

IB Tip A lexical set is a sub-group within a semantic field — tighter, often formal or phonological. It is gold for register and characterisation (Day 22's register builds from sets). In IB, naming a character's lexical set lets you say 'this is who they are in language' — and link to Day 24's lenses (a gendered or classed set = an ideological position). Distinguish from semantic field: the set is the inventory; the field is the conceptual territory it maps.

Common collocations: lexical set of · a tight lexical set · character's lexical set · group into a set · formal lexical set

collocation /ˌkɒləˈkeɪʃn/ noun

The habitual pairing of words that co-occur in a language (e.g. 'heavy rain', not 'strong rain') — the unspoken rules of which words keep company, revealing a text's register and naturalness.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] striking collocation [of X and Y]" · "[Unnatural] collocation [defamiliarises]" · "[Register-marked] collocation"

"In Emily Dickinson's poetry, the collocation of the homely and the cosmic — 'Because I could not stop for Death — / He kindly stopped for me' — yokes 'kindly' to 'Death', and the uneasy pairing is the poem." — Emily Dickinson

IB Tip Collocation = words that travel together. In IB, a broken or striking collocation is foregrounding (Day 22) by lexical means: when a writer pairs words that don't usually meet, the strangeness is the point. Link to Day 25's sound patterns — collocation is meaning-level, alliteration is sound-level; both are 'which words sit together'. Note: this is distinct from idiom (box 7), which is fixed and figurative rather than flexible and associative.

Common collocations: striking collocation · unnatural collocation · register collocation · collocation and idiom · strong collocation

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: semantic field vs. lexical set

Both are clusters of related words, but the seam is scope and basis:

semantic field — The broad conceptual territory: all the words in a text that orbit one idea (the field of war, of light, of memory).
→ Owen's field of the wounded body — 'blood', 'gut', 'face', 'eyes' — maps a whole worldview.

lexical set — The tighter, often formal or phonological inventory: a specific group of related words a speaker or text favours.
→ Holden's set of negations — 'phony', 'crap', 'lousy' — is an inventory you can list and count.

The test: are you mapping the conceptual territory (semantic field) or inventorying a specific group (lexical set)? The field is the map; the set is the labelled pins. In IB, the sophisticated move uses both: "the semantic field of war is realised through a lexical set of body-words, which in turn builds a register of intimacy with death." Keep this pair beside Day 22's lexical field — all three are meaning-clusters at different scales.

register /ˈredʒɪstə(r)/ noun

The level of formality and contextual appropriateness of language — shaped by field (subject), tenor (relationship), and mode (channel) — ranging from frozen/archaic to intimate/casual.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] formal / informal register" · "[A] register shift [marks] the turn" · "[The] register [drops]"

"In Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart, the switches between the court's proverb-rich register and the colonial administrator's clipped English encode the collision of two worlds — register is the politics." — Achebe, Things Fall Apart

IB Tip Register = language matched to context (who speaks to whom, about what, how). In IB, a register shift signals a changed relationship or a withheld self — track it across a text. Link to Day 22's deixis (who is 'I / you / here') and Day 24's lenses (register marks class, education, power). The three variables — field / tenor / mode — are your analytical grid. A dropped register can be intimacy or contempt; name which.

Common collocations: formal register · register shift · drop the register · situational register · register and dialect

dialect /ˈdaɪəlekt/ noun

A regional or social variety of a language with its own vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation — a marker of identity, place, and community that carries ideological weight in a text.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] regional dialect [marks] the speaker" · "[Code-switching] between dialect and standard" · "[The] dialect [asserts] belonging"

"In Alice Walker's The Color Purple, the letters' Black Southern dialect is not 'error' but identity — the orthography performs a voice the standard tongue would otherwise erase." — Alice Walker, The Color Purple

IB Tip Dialect = language as belonging (contrast register = language as appropriateness). In IB, a text's choice to render dialect orthographically is a political act — link Day 24's othering and the subaltern: whose voice is allowed to sound 'wrong'? Never call dialect 'bad grammar'; name it as a system with its own logic. This is a high-value lens for postcolonial and identity texts, and a reliable band-7 differentiator.

Common collocations: regional dialect · social dialect · dialect and standard · render the dialect · dialect marks identity

jargon /ˈdʒɑːɡɒn/ noun

The specialized vocabulary of a profession, discipline, or group — a register marker that includes insiders and excludes outsiders, often carrying institutional power.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The] legal / medical jargon [excludes]" · "[Jargon as] gatekeeping" · "[The] jargon [of the discipline]"

"In Franz Kafka's The Trial, the court's impenetrable jargon — 'the Law', 'the proceedings' — is never explained to K., and the reader shares his exclusion; jargon is the architecture of power." — Kafka, The Trial

IB Tip Jargon = specialized register (box 6) that does ideological work — link Day 24's hegemony and ideology: who controls the vocabulary controls the conversation. In IB, when a text deploys jargon, ask who is included and who is silenced. Contrast with dialect (identity) — jargon is about expertise/power, dialect about place/community. Orwell's newspeak is jargon weaponised.

Common collocations: technical jargon · professional jargon · legal jargon · jargon as gatekeeping · impenetrable jargon

euphemism /ˈjuːfəmɪzəm/ noun

A mild or indirect word substituted for one that is harsh, blunt, or taboo — a polite veil that can soften, obscure, or conceal the truth it names.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] euphemism [for death / war]" · "[The] bureaucratic euphemism [hides]" · "[Strip] the euphemism"

"In Orwell's 1984, the Ministry of 'Peace' wages war and the Ministry of 'Love' tortures — every euphemism reverses its truth, and the gap between the soft word and the hard deed is the satire." — Orwell, Nineteen Eighty-Four

IB Tip Euphemism = a soft word for a hard thing. In IB, the analytic gold is to strip the euphemism and expose what it hides — link Day 24's ideology and hegemony (euphemism is how power launders its acts). Bureaucratic euphemism ('collateral damage') is a favourite IB example. Contrast with idiom (box 7): euphemism conceals; idiom merely fixes a figurative phrase.

Common collocations: bureaucratic euphemism · a soft euphemism · strip the euphemism · euphemism for death · euphemism and truth

idiom /ˈɪdiəm/ noun

A fixed expression whose meaning cannot be deduced from its individual words (e.g. 'kick the bucket', 'spill the beans') — a community's shared figurative shorthand, often culture-specific.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] culture-bound idiom" · "[The] idiom [resists] literal translation" · "[Deploy] idiom for voice"

"In Zadie Smith's White Teeth, the characters' idioms — Jamaican, Bengali, London — are a multilingual idiom-field that performs the novel's hybrid, overlapping identities." — Zadie Smith, White Teeth

IB Tip Idiom = a fixed figurative phrase (contrast collocation, box 3 — flexible co-occurrence; and euphemism, box 4 — which conceals). In IB, idiom marks community and culture; a text's idiom-mix is its demographic map (link Day 24's hybridity). For translated or non-Anglophone texts, note which idioms survive and which are domesticated. Never analyse an idiom literally — its meaning is the whole phrase.

Common collocations: culture-bound idiom · fixed idiom · idiom and collocation · deploy idiom · idiom-field

ambiguity /ˌæmbɪˈɡjuːəti/ noun

The presence of two or more possible meanings in a word, phrase, or text — a productive uncertainty the writer may exploit deliberately to invite multiple readings.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Deliberate] ambiguity [opens] the text" · "[Structural] ambiguity [refuses] closure" · "[The] ambiguity [of the pronoun]"

"In Robert Frost's 'The Road Not Taken', the ambiguity of 'I took the one less travelled by' — was it less travelled, or merely the speaker's later fiction? — is the poem's entire emotional engine." — Robert Frost, The Road Not Taken

IB Tip Ambiguity = multiple possible readings (contrast polysemy, next box — one word, multiple related senses; ambiguity can span a whole line or structure). In IB, deliberate ambiguity is a strength to name, not a flaw to correct — link Day 23's ellipsis and fronting (what is left unsaid is where ambiguity lives). Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity is the classic reference; the examiner loves a candidate who can hold two readings at once.

Common collocations: deliberate ambiguity · structural ambiguity · productive ambiguity · ambiguity of meaning · embrace the ambiguity

polysemy /pəˈlɪsəmi/ noun

The capacity of a single word to carry multiple, related senses (e.g. 'head' = body-part, leader, top-of-list) — meaning branching from one root rather than from separate words.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The] polysemous [word X]" · "[A] pun on polysemy" · "[The] word's [senses] branch"

"In Shakespeare's Richard II, 'depose' is polysemous — a king 'deposed' from the throne and 'deposed' as treasure — the single word's two senses collide to dramatise the loss of power." — Shakespeare, Richard II

IB Tip Polysemy = one word, related senses (contrast ambiguity, box 3 — unrelated or conflicting readings). In IB, a writer's play on polysemy is a precision instrument: the pun is the simplest case. Link to Day 25's sound (a pun often rides on a homophone) and Day 22's lexical field (polysemy expands a field from within). Name the two senses, then state the effect of their collision.

Common collocations: polysemous word · rich polysemy · pun on polysemy · polysemy and ambiguity · the word branches

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: polysemy vs. ambiguity

Both involve multiple meanings, but the seam is related vs. conflicting:

polysemy — One word with several related senses branching from a single root ("head" = body-part, leader, top-of-list).
→ Shakespeare's "depose" = removed from throne and stored as treasure — the senses are cousins.

ambiguity — A word, line, or text with two conflicting or unresolved readings (often deliberate).
→ Frost's "less travelled" = factually fewer feet or the speaker's self-serving myth — the readings fight.

The test: do the meanings cohere as branches of one root (polysemy) or compete as alternative readings (ambiguity)? In IB, polysemy is a precision tool (the pun, the double sense); ambiguity is an opening (the text that refuses to close). Both reward the candidate who can hold complexity — but name which one you mean, because they do different work. Keep this pair beside Day 23's ellipsis (what is left unsaid is where both live).

✏️ Practice — Name the Meaning

Four drills. Write short answers; the model notes follow each.

  1. State denotation, then connotation. For each word, give its literal denotation and one loaded connotation an IB text might exploit:
    a) "home" → denotation: a dwelling; connotation: belonging / safe harbour (or, for the exile, aching absence). b) "crown" → denotation: a monarch's headwear; connotation: sovereign power, divine right, or — in a fallen-king play — lost authority. c) "green" → denotation: a colour; connotation: vitality, envy, or ecological innocence.
  2. Map the network. Take one IB extract and name its semantic field, then list the lexical set that realises it:
    Model In Owen's "Dulce et Decorum Est", the semantic field of the body in collapse — 'blood', 'gargling', 'white eyes', 'writhing' — is realised through a lexical set of physiological verbs; the field builds an anti-glory register that overturns the poem's Latin epigraph. Field = the map; set = the labelled pins.
  3. Spot register, dialect, or jargon. For each line, name which variation-term applies and what it reveals about power or identity:
    Model "The court finds the respondent in contempt" = jargon (legal register that excludes the layperson and enacts institutional power). "Ah ain't gonna tell you no lie" rendered in orthographic dialect = dialect (asserts identity, resists standardisation — link Day 24's the subaltern). A sudden shift from "My dear sir" to "Get out" = register shift marking a ruptured relationship.
  4. Distinguish on demand. In one sentence each, keep these Day 26 pairs exam-ready: (i) denotation vs. connotation (literal referent vs. associative charge); (ii) semantic field vs. lexical set (conceptual map vs. specific inventory); (iii) polysemy vs. ambiguity (related senses vs. competing readings). Stack them beside the Week 3 distinctions, the Day 23 syntax pairs, and the Day 24 theory seams.

🎯 Study Tips — Meaning Is a Choice

  1. The denotation / connotation gap is your default sentence. Every strong commentary line lives in the space between what a word states and what it summons. State the denotation precisely, then name the connotation — and the tension between them is usually the point the examiner wants. This one habit lifts more band-5 paragraphs into band-7 than any other.
  2. Map fields and sets at two scales. The semantic field is the conceptual territory a text keeps returning to; the lexical set is the specific inventory that realises it. Using both — "the field of war is built from a set of body-words" — shows the examiner you can move from macro to micro, exactly the skill Paper 1 and the IOC reward.
  3. Collocation is meaning-level foregrounding. When a writer pairs words that don't usually meet ('kindly' + 'Death'), the strangeness is deliberate. Treat a striking collocation like Day 25's sound-pattern: name the two words, then name the effect. It is the lexical cousin of alliteration — both answer "which words sit together, and why?"
  4. Register, dialect, and jargon are politics, not decoration. Register = appropriateness; dialect = belonging; jargon = power/gatekeeping. In IB, any shift among them is a signal — of changed relationship, asserted identity, or enforced exclusion. Always link to Day 24's lenses: ask who is allowed to sound 'standard' and who is not.
  5. Euphemism conceals; idiom fixes; both are culture. Strip a euphemism to expose what it hides (ideology at work); read an idiom as a community's fingerprint (hybridity at work). Never take either at face value, and never analyse an idiom literally — its sense is the whole phrase, not the sum of its parts.
  6. Polysemy and ambiguity are openings, not errors. Polysemy (related senses) is a precision tool — the pun, the double edge. Ambiguity (competing readings) is a deliberate refusal of closure. In IB, name the multiplicity and hold it; the candidate who can sustain two readings at once is doing real literary criticism. Both live where Day 23's ellipsis leaves things unsaid.
