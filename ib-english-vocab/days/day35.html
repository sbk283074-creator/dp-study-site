✍️ Guided Mini-Analysis — Model & Rubric

The capstone skill is deploying these terms on a real passage. Below: a model paragraph that names terms from all five grids. Highlighted = Week 5 vocabulary.

Texts A and B juxtapose a rural and an urban grief; A converges with the pastoral tradition while B diverges into satire. I assert the reading that reconciles both as critiques of progress, and corroborate it by triangulating the pair with a third lens. The writers hold a judicious, measured stance, hedging every claim so it stays provisional, their prose objective and impersonal. A cogent paragraph must evaluate the effect, discern the exact tonal turn, and situate the texts historically. The opening parallelism yields to a foil that sharpens the counterpoint. To avoid conflating the narrators we subsume each under its paradigm; the synthesis is contingent on the reader's readiness to negotiate the ambiguity, and we extrapolate from one stanza to the whole.

That paragraph names 28 distinct Week 5 terms across all five grids — claim (assert, reconcile, corroborate, stance, hedge, provisional, cogent), judge (judicious, measured, evaluate, discern, situate), relate (juxtapose, converge, diverge, parallelism, foil, counterpoint), dynamic (objectivity, impersonality), synthesis (conflate, subsume, paradigm, synthesis, contingent, negotiate, extrapolate). It moves macro → micro and ties each move to an effect, not just a label.

⚖️ Distinction Quick-Ref (Your Exam Armour)

The fifteen pairs most often confused across the five Week 5 grids. In an essay, naming the right term from a pair is what separates 5 from 7.

① assert vs. refute

assert — to state your own claim firmly as true (build); refute — to tear down an opposing claim with counter-evidence (attack). An essay needs both: assert your reading, then refute the obvious alternative.

② hedge vs. caveat

hedge — a grammatical / lexical softener woven into the claim itself ("might"); caveat — a separate limiting clause attached after the claim. Both reduce overstatement, but hedge is internal force-modulation; caveat is external qualification.

③ assertive vs. tentative

assertive — a manner that commits fully; tentative — a manner that withholds and signals openness to revision. Top-band writing modulates between them deliberately.

④ evaluate vs. assess

evaluate — a judgement of value / significance (good, effective); assess — an estimate of weight / scale (how much, how far). "Assess the effect, then evaluate its success" is the cleaner sequence.

⑤ perceive vs. discern

perceive — to take in a feature broadly; discern — to detect it finely, often against noise. You perceive a mood shift, then discern the exact device producing it.

⑥ judicious vs. measured

judicious — wise choice of what is said; measured — restrained tone of how it is delivered. A judicious point can still be delivered unmeasured; aim for both.

⑦ juxtapose vs. synthesis

juxtapose — place side by side so the reader compares; synthesis — combine into one connected whole. A comparative essay juxtaposes first, then synthesises.

⑧ parallelism vs. antithesis

parallelism — repeated structure linking ideas (similar or opposing); antithesis — opposing ideas held in that parallel structure. All antithesis uses parallel structure; not all parallelism is antithetical.

⑨ counterpoint vs. correspondence

counterpoint — a contrasting element that sets the main idea in relief; correspondence — a matching / echoing relation. Opposite relational directions: difference that sharpens vs. similarity that resonates.

⑩ divergence vs. convergence

divergence — elements moving apart; convergence — elements coming together. Two texts converge on a theme but diverge in tone — state both to show nuance.

⑪ objectivity vs. impersonality

objectivity — a quality of stance: freedom from bias; impersonality — a feature of style: absence of first-person voice. You can write impersonally yet be biased.

⑫ modality vs. evidential

modality — grammatical encoding of certainty (will, may, must); evidential — markers showing the source of knowledge ("according to"). A hedged claim with clear evidentials is stronger than either alone.

⑬ negotiate vs. mediate

negotiate — to work through the tensions between readings (active resolution); mediate — to stand between two positions without choosing (mapping). Resolve with negotiate, map with mediate.

⑭ provisional vs. contingent

provisional — held as temporary / open to revision (about time); contingent — true only under certain conditions (about circumstance). Don't collapse "I may be wrong later" with "this holds only here".

⑮ conflate vs. subsume

conflate — to wrongly merge distinct things (the trap markers punish); subsume — to place a case correctly under a broader category (the disciplined move). Recognising the seam is itself a top-band skill.

🎯 Assessment Tips

  1. Recognition is not ownership. You "know" a Week 5 term only when you can spell it, define it, and deploy it on an unseen passage in under a minute. The Master Sheet tests the first two; Your Task tests the third. All three must pass.
  2. Reach for one term from each grid, every time. A Paper 2 paragraph that names twelve claim-verbs but no synthesis or dynamic term is unbalanced and caps below the top band. The Five-grid balance row in the rubric is the single biggest lever on your mark.
  3. The pair is the differentiator. "These texts juxtapose" gets identification; "they juxtapose rather than synthesise, because the opposition is left unresolved" gets the top band. The fifteen distinctions are your edge — keep them alive into Phase 4.
  4. Terms must drive meaning, not decorate. Top essays use each term to explain an effect ("the convergence of the two openings forces the reader to read them as one argument"). If you can delete the term and the sentence loses nothing, you've listed, not analysed.
  5. Macro before micro. Orient with how the texts relate (juxtapose, converge, diverge) before zooming into a single move (parallelism, foil). The model paragraph moves whole-pair → single line on purpose. Examiners reward that architecture of thought.
  6. Week 5 is the application spine of the whole programme. Everything before — device metalanguage (Week 3) and stylistic / linguistic analysis (Week 4) — is the evidence; Week 5 is what you DO with it. Lock these sixty in now and the remaining phases become genuine mastery, not relearning.
