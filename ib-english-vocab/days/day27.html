① Stylistic & Argument Metalanguage (Days 22 + 23 pragmatic · 13 terms)

The words that describe how a text coheres, positions its speaker, and builds an argument.

cohesion
The grammatical & lexical links (connectives, repetition, reference) that bind a text surface-level.
coherence
The logical sense and flow of meaning — whether a text holds together as a whole.
deixis
Context-dependent 'pointing' words (I, you, here, now) anchoring a text to its speech situation.
foregrounding
When language deviates from the norm or repeats to draw the ear / attention to itself.
intonation
The rise and fall of pitch in speech conveying attitude beyond the words.
thesis
The central claim an argument advances — the point the essay is built to prove.
contention
The assertive statement of the argument's main point, often in the opening.
premise
A supporting proposition on which a conclusion rests.
qualify
To limit or add nuance to a claim to avoid overstatement.
concede
To acknowledge the strength of an opposing point before rebutting it.
rebut
To refute or counter an opposing argument.
discourse marker
A word / phrase signalling relationship between stretches of text ("however", "meanwhile").
illocutionary act
What a speaker DOES in saying something (promising, warning) — the pragmatic force.

② Syntax & Sentence Moves (Day 23 · 10 terms)

The words that name how sentences are shaped, linked, and shifted for effect.

declarative
A sentence that states a fact or opinion — the default 'statement' function.
interrogative
A sentence that asks a question (seeks information or challenges).
imperative
A sentence that gives a command or instruction.
exclamative
A sentence that expresses strong feeling ("what a…!" / "how…!").
coordination
Linking clauses of equal status with and / but / or (paratactic).
subordination
Nesting one clause inside another with a subordinator (because, while).
nominalisation
Turning a verb / adjective into a noun to tighten and formalise prose.
passive
A clause where the patient, not the agent, is the grammatical subject.
ellipsis
The deliberate omission of a recoverable word / phrase — what is left unsaid.
fronting
Moving a normally-late element to the front for emphasis or cohesion.

③ Word-Choice & Meaning (Day 26 + lexical field · 13 terms)

The lexical & semantic metalanguage for naming what words mean and how they mean it. (lexical field relocated here from Day 22.)

denotation
The literal, dictionary meaning of a word.
connotation
The associative, culturally-charged meaning beyond the denotation.
semantic field
A cluster of related words on a shared concept recurring through a text.
lexical set
A tighter group of related words a speaker / text favours.
lexical field
The shared-topic word cluster a text recurs to (Day 22 ancestor of semantic field).
collocation
The habitual pairing of words that co-occur in a language.
register
The level of formality / contextual appropriateness of language.
dialect
A regional / social variety with its own vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation.
jargon
The specialised vocabulary of a group — marks expertise & excludes outsiders.
euphemism
A mild term substituted for a harsh or taboo one.
idiom
A fixed expression whose sense can't be deduced from its parts.
ambiguity
Two or more possible readings (often deliberate) in a word / line / text.
polysemy
One word carrying multiple, related senses branching from a root.

④ Sound & Metre (Day 25 · 12 terms)

The phonological & prosodic vocabulary for analysing how a text is audible.

alliteration
Repetition of initial consonant sounds.
assonance
Repetition of vowel sounds within words.
consonance
Repetition of consonant sounds at / near word ends.
onomatopoeia
A word whose sound echoes its sense ("hiss", "crash").
sibilance
Repetition of sibilant /s/ sounds — a hissing effect.
rhyme
Corresponding sounds at line-ends (or internal).
metre
The rhythmic pattern of stresses in a line.
iamb
A foot of unstressed–stressed (da-DUM).
trochee
A foot of stressed–unstressed (DUM-da).
caesura
A pause within a line (often marked by punctuation).
enjambment
A line running on without pause into the next.
euphony
Pleasing, smooth sound — the musical whole.

⑤ Critical Lenses (Day 24 · 12 terms)

The theoretical frameworks that tell you FROM WHICH POSITION to read — feminist, Marxist, postcolonial, psychoanalytic.

patriarchy
A social system where men hold primary power; critique of that structure.
intersectionality
Overlapping identities (race / class / gender) creating compounded experience.
the male gaze
The framing of women as objects for male spectatorship.
ideology
The system of beliefs / values that naturalises a group's worldview.
hegemony
The dominance of one group secured by consent, not just force.
alienation
Marxist term: the worker's separation from the product / self under capital.
othering
Defining a group as different / excluded to secure an in-group identity.
the subaltern
The subordinated, historically voiceless group (postcolonial).
hybridity
The mixing of cultures producing new, in-between identities.
the unconscious
The repressed store of drives / desires beneath conscious thought.
repression
The psychic mechanism that pushes an unacceptable impulse from the conscious mind.
the uncanny
The strangely familiar — the homely made unsettling (Freud).

⚖️ The 15 Distinction Pairs (Your Week 4 Exam Armour)

IB markers reward precise discrimination. These are the pairs most often confused across the five Week 4 pillars — know the exact seam between each (three per pillar).

① cohesion vs. coherence

cohesion — the surface glue: connectives, pronouns, repetition that bind sentences grammatically.
coherence — the deep sense: whether the whole text holds together logically in the reader's mind.
A text can be cohesive (full of "however / therefore") yet incoherent (the ideas don't connect). Surface vs. sense is the seam.

② thesis vs. contention

thesis — the central claim an argument is built to prove (the position itself).
contention — the assertive statement of that claim, often in the opening topic sentence.
Thesis = the idea; contention = the act of advancing it. You state your thesis by making a contention. Don't collapse the two.

③ qualify vs. concede

qualify — to limit your OWN claim with nuance ("this is largely true, yet…").
concede — to acknowledge the strength of the OPPOSING point before rebutting it.
Both soften force, but qualify modifies your side; concede grants ground to theirs. In an essay, concede then rebut is the stronger move.

④ coordination vs. subordination

coordination — linking equal clauses with and / but / or ("he left and she stayed") → paratactic,并列.
subordination — nesting one clause inside another ("she stayed because he left") → hypotactic, hierarchical.
Clause relation is the seam: equal vs. nested. Coordination withholds cause; subordination supplies it. This is the syntax-level cousin of Week 3's parataxis / hypotaxis.

⑤ sentence function vs. illocutionary act (form vs. force)

sentence function — the grammatical form: declarative / interrogative / imperative / exclamative (what the sentence IS).
illocutionary act — the pragmatic force: what the speaker DOES in saying it (a question that orders, a statement that warns).
"Can you close the door?" is interrogative in form but a command in force. Form is grammar; force is pragmatics. Name both.

⑥ nominalisation vs. passive

nominalisation — turning a verb / adjective into a noun ("they decided" → "the decision") to tighten prose.
passive — making the patient the grammatical subject ("the law was broken") to shift or hide the agent.
Both are 'written-ness' moves, but nominalisation compresses; passive redistributes agency. A text can nominalise without passivising, and vice versa — state which one.

⑦ denotation vs. connotation

denotation — the literal dictionary meaning (what a word refers to).
connotation — the feelings / associations a word carries beyond that.
"Home" and "house" share a denotation; their connotations diverge entirely. Word-choice analysis lives in connotation. This is the single most-used IB distinction.

⑧ semantic field vs. lexical set

semantic field — the broad conceptual territory: all the words in a text orbiting one idea (the field of war).
lexical set — the tighter inventory: a specific group of related words a speaker favours.
The field is the map; the set is the labelled pins. Use both: "the field of war is realised through a lexical set of body-words."

⑨ polysemy vs. ambiguity

polysemy — one word with several related senses branching from a root ("head" = part, leader, top).
ambiguity — a word / line / text with two competing or unresolved readings (often deliberate).
Polysemy coheres as branches; ambiguity competes as alternatives. One is a precision tool (the pun); the other is an opening (the text that won't close).

⑩ alliteration vs. assonance

alliteration — repetition of initial consonant sounds ("dark door").
assonance — repetition of internal vowel sounds ("slow boat").
Consonant-onset vs. vowel-core is the seam. Both are sound-patterning; pair each with the mood it builds (link Day 10's mood family).

⑪ iamb vs. trochee

iamb — unstressed–stressed (da-DUM): the natural English beat ("be-LONG").
trochee — stressed–unstressed (DUM-da): a falling, often urgent beat ("TA-ble").
Direction of stress is the seam. A line's dominant foot shapes its tempo; trochaic opening can signal agitation or incantation.

⑫ caesura vs. enjambment

caesura — a pause within a line (often punctuated) → breath, halt, emphasis.
enjambment — a line running on without pause into the next → flow, speed, suspense.
Opposite manipulations of line-boundary. Caesura stops the eye; enjambment refuses to. Name which one to explain the poem's tempo.

⑬ ideology vs. hegemony

ideology — the system of beliefs / values that naturalises a group's worldview.
hegemony — the dominance of that group secured by consent, not just force.
Ideology is the content of belief; hegemony is how that belief rules. Hegemony explains why an ideology feels 'normal' rather than imposed.

⑭ othering vs. the subaltern

othering — the process of defining a group as different / excluded to secure an in-group.
the subaltern — the position of the subordinated, historically voiceless group produced by that process.
Othering is the act; subaltern is the result. You can name the mechanism (othering) and the subject it silences (the subaltern) in one sentence.

⑮ patriarchy vs. the male gaze

patriarchy — the systemic structure of male dominance (the cause, broad).
the male gaze — the specific cinematic / literary framing of women as objects for male spectatorship (one effect).
Structure vs. symptom is the seam. Patriarchy is the machine; the male gaze is one of its lenses. Name the level you mean.

🎯 Building Your Week 4 Toolkit

  1. Week 4 is the metalanguage spine of the whole programme. Day 22 tells you how a text coheres and argues; Day 23 tells you how its sentences are shaped; Day 24 tells you from which position to read; Day 25 tells you how it sounds; Day 26 tells you what its words mean. Together they are the vocabulary for a complete Paper 1 commentary and a precise Paper 2 essay.
  2. Regroup, don't re-list. Today you met the same sixty words ordered by function. That interleaving is the difference between memorising five lists and owning one toolkit. When you analyse a real passage, reach for one term from each grid rather than twelve from one.
  3. The fifteen distinction pairs are where marks are won or lost. Saying "this is alliteration" gets you the identification; saying "this is alliteration, not assonance, because it repeats initial consonants and so tightens the line's onset" gets you the top band. Precision of discrimination is the IB differentiator.
  4. The grids are bridged, not walled. Foregrounding (①) is what alliteration (④) and a striking collocation (③) both are; subordination (②) is the grammar of the hedge you met in Week 3; deixis (①) anchors the register shift (③). A band-7 paragraph names the bridge: "the repeated initial consonants foreground the violence, exactly as Day 22's foregrounding predicts."
  5. Lenses (⑤) frame the other four grids. Before you name a device, decide your position: a feminist reading hears the male gaze in a description; a Marxist reading hears ideology in a register shift; a postcolonial reading hears othering in a dialect rendered as 'error'. The lenses tell you why a device matters, not just what it is.
  6. Deploy these on a real text this week. Take any set-text paragraph and force yourself to name: one stylistic/argument term, one syntax move, one word-choice term, one sound device, and one lens — then show how they reinforce each other. By Day 28's assessment you'll have a complete, connected Week 4 toolkit ready to deploy under exam conditions.
