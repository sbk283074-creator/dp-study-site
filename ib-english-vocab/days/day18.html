📖 Today's New Words — 12 Tone & Attitude Terms

CONTINUING — "TONE & ATTITUDE TERMS"

From what is said to how it feels. You can now name a text's devices (Days 15-16) and its structure (Day 17). Today's twelve name its tone — the subtle machinery by which language conveys feeling, attitude, and stance. connotation/denotation are the meaning pair; register/subtext/inference are the layers; modality/hedge/equivocation are the grammar of certainty; sarcasm/innuendo/understatement are the tonal moves. This is the vocabulary of close reading and of Paper 1 (both literary and non-literary) — tone is where marks are won or lost.

connotation /ˌkɒnəˈteɪʃn/ noun

The associations, emotions, or ideas a word carries beyond its literal meaning.

Literary Usage Pattern "[home] [connotes] [warmth, belonging, safety]"

"In war poetry, 'dulce' (sweet) connotes glory even as Owen's poem systematically strips that glory away." — Owen analysis

IB Tip Distinct from denotation (see box below): connotation is the aura; denotation is the dictionary. Connect to Day 11's tone and Day 5's nuanced. In analysis, name which connotations a word summons and why the writer chose it over a neutral synonym — this is where tone lives. A commentary that only paraphrases denotation misses the music.

Common collocations: positive/negative connotation · strong connotations · the connotations of

denotation /ˌdɛnəʊˈteɪʃn/ noun

The literal, dictionary definition of a word, free of association or emotion.

Literary Usage Pattern "[the] [literal meaning] [of the term]"

"The denotation of 'thin' is merely 'of small width' — its connotations, unlike those of 'slender,' are rarely complimentary." — Lexical analysis

IB Tip The literal anchor against connotation (see box). IB commentary must move from denotation (what is said) to connotation (what is evoked). Connect to Day 15's imagery — imagery works through connotation. A precise denotation check prevents misreading tone; never skip the literal before the associative.

Common collocations: the denotation of · bare denotation

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: connotation vs. denotation

The fundamental meaning pair — every word has both:

denotation — The literal, dictionary meaning. What the word refers to.
→ "slender" denotes "gracefully thin."

connotation — The associative, emotional meaning. What the word evokes.
→ "slender" connotes elegance; "thin" connotes frailty; "skinny" connotes awkwardness.

The test: are you describing the dictionary sense (denotation) or the felt associations (connotation)? Strong IB analysis always moves denotation → connotation. This pair is the gateway to tone: tone is connotation deployed deliberately. Master it and Day 11's "tone" becomes a precise, nameable thing.

register /ˈrɛdʒɪstə/ noun

The level of formality or social variety of language appropriate to a context (formal, informal, colloquial, technical, intimate…).

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [formal] [register]" · "[a] [shift in] [register]"

"In a political speech, a sudden drop to colloquial register ('folks like you and me') narrows the distance between orator and crowd." — Rhetoric analysis

IB Tip Revisited from Day 11 (colloquial/formal were adjectives). Register is the noun for the whole formality spectrum. Connect to Day 11's colloquial/formal and Day 16's euphemism (a register choice). A register shift signals a change in relationship or intent — name it and its effect. Essential for both literary dialogue and non-literary texts.

Common collocations: a formal register · shift in register · register of

subtext /ˈsʌbtɛkst/ noun

The unspoken or implicit meaning beneath the surface of a text.

Literary Usage Pattern "[what is] [left] [unsaid]" · "[the] [subtext] [of the exchange]"

"In Pinter's The Birthday Party, the menacing small talk carries a subtext of threat the characters never name." — Pinter analysis

IB Tip Distinct from inference (see box): subtext is what the text implies; inference is what the reader deduces from it. Connect to Day 5's insidious and Day 1's obfuscate. Pinter's "comedy of menace" is pure subtext — name what the silence says. Subtext is the unsaid that the reader must supply.

Common collocations: the subtext of · heavy subtext · sexual/political subtext

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: subtext vs. inference

Both concern meaning that isn't stated — but from opposite sides:

subtext — What the text implies or withholds. It lives in the work.
→ "The unspoken threat beneath the polite dialogue."

inference — What the reader deduces from evidence. It lives in the act of reading.
→ "From the cold tea, we infer he fled in haste."

The test: is the implicit meaning authored by the text (subtext) or constructed by the reader (inference)? In Paper 1, the mark-winning move is to separate them: "the text implies X (subtext); therefore I infer Y." Confusing the two collapses the gap between author and reader that IB wants you to hold open. This pair is the spine of close reading.

inference /ˈɪnfərəns/ noun

A conclusion the reader draws from evidence in the text that is not explicitly stated.

Literary Usage Pattern "[the reader] [infers] [that…]"

"From the unmade bed and cold tea, we infer the character fled in haste — the text never states it." — Close-reading practice

IB Tip The reader's act; distinct from subtext (see box), which is the text's implicit offering. Connect to Day 1's interrogate and Day 15's allusion (inferences often rest on allusions). In Paper 1, separating textual evidence from inference is the core skill — always show both: the clue, then the inference drawn.

Common collocations: draw an inference · infer from · reasonable inference

modality /məʊˈdæləti/ noun

The degree of certainty, necessity, or possibility expressed in language (via modal verbs, adverbs, and tone).

Literary Usage Pattern "[must] [vs] [might]" · "[high/low] [modality]"

"A government leaflet that writes 'you must comply' deploys high modality to assert authority; 'you might consider' lowers it to suggestion." — Non-literary analysis

IB Tip The grammar of conviction. Connect to Day 11's dispassionate and Day 18's hedge (low modality) / boosters (high). Essential for non-literary analysis (Paper 1, Section 2): modality reveals how firmly a text wants you to believe. Name the modal and the power it performs — this is where persuasion is engineered.

Common collocations: high/low modality · modality of certainty

equivocation /ɪˌkwɪvəˈkeɪʃn/ noun

Deliberate use of ambiguous language to avoid committing to a clear position.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [non-answer] [that] [sounds] [like an answer]"

"In political interviews, equivocation — 'we remain committed to exploring options' — answers nothing while appearing to." — Rhetoric / media analysis

IB Tip Distinct from hedge (see box): equivocation is strategic ambiguity to dodge; a hedge is tentative softening. Connect to Day 5's obfuscate and Day 16's euphemism. In rhetoric analysis, equivocation is a power move — name who benefits from the fog. Spot it in any political or corporate text.

Common collocations: deliberate equivocation · a master of equivocation

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: hedge vs. equivocation

Both soften language, but with different intent:

hedge — Honest tentativeness: a word that weakens a claim's force ('perhaps,' 'arguably'). The writer signals awareness of counter-argument.
→ "It could be argued that…"

equivocation — Strategic evasion: ambiguity used to avoid committing at all. The writer hides behind vagueness.
→ "We are exploring a range of possibilities going forward."

The test: is the softening intellectually honest (hedge — the writer knows the limits of the claim) or evasive (equivocation — the writer refuses to take a position)? Both lower modality (see modality), but only equivocation is a dodge. In non-literary analysis, naming which one exposes whether a text is being careful or being slippery.

hedge /hɛdʒ/ noun

A word or phrase that weakens a claim's force, expressing caution or tentativeness (e.g., "perhaps," "to some extent," "it could be argued").

Literary Usage Pattern "[perhaps] [·] [might] [·] [it could be argued]"

"An opinion column that hedges with 'arguably' and 'in many cases' signals a writer aware of counter-argument." — Non-literary analysis

IB Tip The softener; low modality (see modality). Distinct from equivocation (see box) — a hedge is honest tentativeness, not evasion. Connect to Day 11's dispassionate. In non-literary texts, hedges build credibility; their absence can signal dogma. Name the hedge and what it protects.

Common collocations: hedge one's claim · a careful hedge

stance /stɑːns/ noun

The writer's intellectual or ethical position toward the subject and audience.

Literary Usage Pattern "[the author's] [stance] [on]…"

"Orwell's stance in 'Politics and the English Language' is didactic and urgent — he wants you to write more honestly." — Orwell essay analysis

IB Tip The writer's location in the argument. Connect to Day 5's didactic and Day 1's foreground. Distinct from tone (the emotional colour) — stance is the position, tone is the weather. In Paper 1/2, naming the stance is step one of any evaluation. Note: didactic appeared Day 1; here it's the noun-concept family completing the picture.

Common collocations: the writer's stance · adopt a stance

sarcasm /ˈsɑːkæzəm/ noun

A sharp, often mocking use of words to convey the opposite of their literal meaning, usually to wound or ridicule.

Literary Usage Pattern "[oh,] [brilliant] [—said of a disaster]"

"In Animal Farm, the sheep's 'Four legs good, two legs better' becomes sarcasm once the pigs walk upright — the slogan ironises itself." — Orwell analysis

IB Tip A biting subtype of irony (Day 15). Connect to Day 5's satirical / caustic (Day 11) and Day 16's satire. Sarcasm aims to hurt or expose; distinguish it from playful irony. Name the target of the sarcasm — sarcasm is always aimed. It's a tonal move, not just a device.

Common collocations: a note of sarcasm · dripping with sarcasm

innuendo /ˌɪnjuˈɛndəʊ/ noun

An indirect, usually suggestive remark that hints at something disparaging without stating it outright.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [veiled] [hint] [at impropriety]"

"In restoration comedy, innuendo threads through apparently polite dialogue, implying affairs and debts no one names." — Comedy of manners analysis

IB Tip Lives in subtext (see subtext). Distinct from euphemism (Day 16) — euphemism softens a harsh truth; innuendo implies a scandalous one. Connect to Day 5's subversive. In drama, innuendo is comic and subversive at once — name what is being dared without saying. It's a tone of implication.

Common collocations: a sly innuendo · thick with innuendo

understatement /ˈʌndəsteɪtmənt/ noun

A figure of speech that deliberately represents something as less significant than it is, for effect.

Literary Usage Pattern "[it was] [a bit] [chilly] [—at −20°C]"

"In war reporting, 'there were some casualties' is an understatement that withholds the full horror." — Journalism analysis

IB Tip The mirror of overstatement / hyperbole (Day 15) and kin to litotes (Day 19, coming). Understatement can be euphemistic (Day 16) or coolly ironic. Connect to Day 5's restrained and Day 11's dispassionate. Name it when restraint itself is the message — the gap between said and meant is the point. A sophisticated tonal move worth naming.

Common collocations: a master of understatement · deliberate understatement

🎯 Building Your Device Toolkit

  1. Day 18 completes the "tone" vocabulary. The meaning pair (connotation/denotation), the layers (register, subtext, inference), the grammar of certainty (modality, hedge, equivocation), and the tonal moves (stance, sarcasm, innuendo, understatement). Together they let you analyse how a text feels — the skill at the heart of close reading and Paper 1.
  2. Three distinction pairs anchor the day. connotation/denotation (aura vs. dictionary), subtext/inference (text implies vs. reader deduces), hedge/equivocation (honest tentativeness vs. strategic evasion). These six terms let you separate what a text says from what it does — the move IB rewards most.
  3. Tone is connotation deployed deliberately. Once you have Day 18's terms, "tone" (Day 11's adjective) becomes nameable: name the register, the modality, the connotations, and the subtext — and you've explained the tone instead of merely asserting it. That shift is worth a grade boundary.
  4. Modality and hedge/equivocation are non-literary gold. These three terms unlock any advert, op-ed, or political text in Paper 1, Section 2. A text's power often lives in how firmly it wants you to believe — name the modality and you've found the persuasion.
  5. Revisits tie the weeks together. register (Day 11), irony/satire (Day 15/16), euphemism (Day 16), didactic (Day 1/5) all reappear as nouns here. Naming the noun lets you discuss the whole concept, not just its flavour — your vocabulary is now vertically integrated across all three weeks.
  6. Deploy these on a real text this week. Take any poem or opinion piece and force yourself to name: 2 connotations, the register, one subtext/inference pair, and the modality. By Day 21's review you'll have a complete Week 3 toolkit — devices (15-16) + structure (17) + tone (18).
