🔥 Day 3 Active Recall — 2nd Pass

These are Saturday's writing-method verbs. For each, you see the word and its usage pattern. Recall the full definition before clicking to reveal. Then read the deeper question — it tests whether you can use the word, not just define it. Flag any word you can't answer for tonight's re-study.

craft "The author crafts the voice with flat restraint…"
To construct with deliberate skill and artistry. The author is a maker, not just a recorder.
How does "craft" differ from merely "write"? (Hint: craft implies conscious, skilful making — every choice is deliberate, the text is built like an object.)
deploy "Atwood deploys biblical naming with precision…"
To position and use a technique strategically.
Why "deploy" rather than "use"? (Hint: deploy implies tactical placement — the technique is positioned for maximum effect, like a piece moved on a board.)
weave "He weaves myth and history into one fabric…"
To interlace multiple strands into a unified whole.
weave vs. thread? (One builds a whole cloth from many strands; thread runs one strand continuously through an existing text.)
render "Chopin renders awakening as a tactile process…"
To represent or translate into a form; cause to become.
How is "render" different from "show"? (Hint: render implies translation — taking an abstract experience and making it perceivable in a specific medium.)
orchestrate "Miller orchestrates the time transitions…"
To coordinate multiple elements with deliberate precision.
Why "orchestrate" rather than "organise"? (Hint: orchestrate implies a conductor coordinating many independent parts into one unified effect — the musical metaphor signals control.)
sculpt "Morrison sculpts memory into nonlinear form…"
To shape by careful, deliberate craftsmanship.
sculpt vs. craft? (craft = overall construction of the whole; sculpt = shaping by refining/removing, like a sculptor working a block.)
layer "Fitzgerald layers the green light with resonance…"
To build up meaning through superimposed levels.
What does layering do that a single image doesn't? (Hint: layers accumulate meaning — each pass adds new resonance without erasing the old.)
embed "Orwell embeds politics in domestic routine…"
To fix firmly within a surrounding context.
embed vs. inscribe (Day 4)? (embed = fix within a context; inscribe = carve indelibly/permanently — embed is about placement, inscribe about permanent marking.)
frame "Brontë frames the story through double narration…"
To present within a perspective/boundary that shapes meaning.
Why does framing matter for interpretation? (Hint: the frame dictates how we read — the same story told through different narration means something different.)
thread "Shakespeare threads the blood motif throughout…"
To run one element continuously through a text.
thread vs. weave? (thread = one continuous strand through a text; weave = many strands interlaced into cloth.)
infuse "Achebe infuses the prose with oral tradition…"
To instil a quality into the text (author is the active subject).
infuse vs. suffuse? (infuse = author puts a quality in; suffuse = atmosphere spreads through on its own, atmosphere is the subject.)
suffuse "An atmosphere of dread suffuses the scene…"
To spread through completely (atmosphere is the subject).
Why is suffuse's subject different from infuse's? (Hint: in suffuse the atmosphere is the active spreader; in infuse the author is the active agent.)

🔄 Day 4 First Review — 12 Words

Yesterday's writing-method verbs II — the 24-hour recall window. Try to recall each meaning before reading the definition. Ask: Can I use this verb to describe a technique in a text I'm reading? If not, flag it — it reappears in Day 6's weekly review.

permeate

To spread throughout every part of something.
"The theme of surveillance permeates every level of 1984…"

saturate

To fill completely, to capacity.
"Colour imagery saturates the opening chapter…"

interlace

To bind distinct elements so tightly they're mutually dependent.
"McEwan interlaces writing with atonement…"

inscribe

To fix permanently into a text (from monumental marking).
"Morrison inscribes slavery into the novel's body…"

construct

To build methodically; assemble structure with design.
"Austen constructs the plot around dramatic irony…"

arrange

To order/sequence elements deliberately for effect.
"Bishop arranges the losses in ascending order…"

calibrate

To adjust precisely toward a fixed target.
"Ishiguro calibrates the revelation with precision…"

modulate

To vary or shift tone/pace/register.
"Woolf modulates between thought and description…"

attenuate

To weaken or diminish intensity deliberately.
"McCarthy's prose attenuates all comfort…"

distil

To extract and concentrate the essential essence.
"Plath distils anguish into the bell jar image…"

hone

To refine/perfect through repeated effort over time.
"Hemingway hones his economy across stories…"

sharpen

To make more acute or intense at a moment.
"The tense shift sharpens the trauma's immediacy…"

Key distinctions from Day 4 — can you still articulate them?
• permeate (spread through) vs. saturate (fill to capacity)
• calibrate (precise tuning) vs. modulate (variation/shift)
• construct (build the whole) vs. arrange (order the parts)
• hone (perfect over time) vs. sharpen (intensify at a moment)
If any still feel fuzzy, revisit the Day 4 distinction boxes tonight.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Evaluative Adjectives

NEW CATEGORY — "JUDGING TEXTS"

The shift from description to evaluation. Days 1–2 gave you verbs for what texts do (analysis). Days 3–4 gave you verbs for how texts are built (craft). Today's adjectives give you the vocabulary for judging — for saying whether a text's argument is sound, biased, subtle, or simplistic. This is the language of critical appraisal: the difference between "the novel shows X" (description) and "the novel's argument is nuanced rather than reductive" (evaluation). IB Paper 2 and your IOC commentary live or die on these words. Note: didactic and nuanced appeared on Day 1 — today we revisit them at a higher, evaluative level.

polemical /pəˈlemɪkl/ adjective

Expressing strong, often controversial opinions intended to provoke debate or attack opposing views; argumentative and combative in tone.

Literary Usage Pattern "The essay's [polemical] tone / The text's [polemical] stance [against X]…"

"Swift's A Modest Proposal is savagely polemical — its outrageous proposal is engineered to jolt the reader out of complacent indifference and force a reckoning with Ireland's exploitation." — A Modest Proposal analysis

IB Tip Use for texts or arguments that argue aggressively and take a confrontational stance. Distinct from the neutral "argumentative" — polemical carries the sense of attack and provocation. A polemical text wears its bias openly (contrast with "tendentious," below). Ideal for evaluating satire, manifestos, and essays.

Common collocations: polemical essay · polemical tone · polemical stance · polemical attack · polemical intent

subversive /səbˈvɜːsɪv/ adjective

Intending to undermine or overthrow established authority, norms, or power structures — often through indirect or covert means rather than open rebellion.

Literary Usage Pattern "A [subversive] text / The [subversive] potential of [element] challenges [authority/ideology]…"

"The Handmaid's Tale is fundamentally subversive: by normalising a theocratic dystopia through the mundane voice of Offred, Atwood dismantles our complacency about how quickly rights can erode." — The Handmaid's Tale analysis

IB Tip Use when a text quietly (or openly) works against the status quo. Subversive texts don't just criticise — they aim to topple. Note the link to "subvert" (Day 1 verb) — this is its adjective form. Distinct from "satirical" (see distinction box): satire mocks; subversion undermines. Strong for political and feminist readings.

Common collocations: subversive message · subversive text · subversive potential · subversive reading · subversive intent

satirical /səˈtɪrɪkl/ adjective

Using humour, irony, exaggeration, or ridicule to expose and criticise folly, vice, or stupidity — especially in politics or society. Satire is a mode, not merely a tone.

Literary Usage Pattern "A [satirical] portrayal / The [satirical] exposure of [target]…"

"Orwell's Animal Farm is a sustained satirical allegory — the farm's pigs becoming indistinguishable from the humans they overthrew exposes how revolution can betray its own ideals." — Animal Farm analysis

IB Tip Satire is a mode: a satirical text uses wit as a weapon. Distinguish from "subversive" (see distinction box) — satire mocks its target through humour; subversion works to erode power, with or without humour. A text can be satirical without being subversive (safe, establishment-approved satire), and subversive without being satirical (a quiet, lyrical dismantling).

Common collocations: satirical novel · satirical tone · satirical critique · satirical edge · satirical target

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: satirical vs. subversive

Both describe texts that challenge the status quo, but they differ in method vs. aim:

satirical — Uses wit, irony, exaggeration, and ridicule as the method. The target is mocked into exposure.
→ "The satirical portrait of the mayor exposes his vanity through comic exaggeration."

subversive — Works to undermine or overthrow power/authority as the aim. The method can be quiet, covert, or overt.
→ "The subversive framing of the dystopia erodes the reader's faith in the present order."

Overlap is real (satire is often subversive), but they're not identical: satire = how it attacks (with humour); subversion = what it seeks (to topple). Memory trick: satire holds up a fun-house mirror; subversion quietly loosens the foundations.

allegorical /ˌæləˈɡɒrɪkl/ adjective

Operating as an allegory — a narrative carrying a second, symbolic level of meaning beneath the literal story, where characters and events stand for abstract ideas or historical realities.

Literary Usage Pattern "An [allegorical] narrative / The [allegorical] representation of [abstract concept]…"

"In The Faerie Queene, Spenser's allegorical knights embody virtues in conflict, so the romance plot simultaneously functions as a moral and political map of Elizabethan England." — The Faerie Queene analysis

IB Tip Use when a text has a clear symbolic substructure. Allegorical readings require you to identify the "other" level — but beware over-allegorising (that's when a reading turns "reductive," below). The opposite trap: forcing every detail to "mean" something. A strong allegorical reading balances the literal and the symbolic.

Common collocations: allegorical reading · allegorical level · allegorical meaning · allegorical structure · allegorical dimension

tendentious /tenˈdenʃəs/ adjective

Biased or partisan; presenting an argument slanted toward a particular conclusion, often while appearing objective or neutral. The bias is there, but disguised.

Literary Usage Pattern "A [tendentious] account / The [tendentious] framing of [evidence] leans toward [conclusion]…"

"The prosecutor's tendentious framing of the evidence in the trial scene manipulates the jury by arranging facts to foreclose any interpretation but his own." — Trial-scene analysis

IB Tip A tendentious text pushes an agenda while pretending neutrality. Distinct from "polemical" (openly argumentative) — tendentious is biased but disguised. Critical test: does the text hide its bias behind a facade of objectivity? If yes, "tendentious" is your word. Excellent for exposing unreliable narrators and propaganda.

Common collocations: tendentious argument · tendentious history · tendentious bias · tendentious framing · tendentious account

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: polemical vs. tendentious

Both describe biased or argument-driven texts, but they differ in how the bias is presented:

polemical — Wears its bias openly. Combative, aggressive, picks a fight in plain sight.
→ "Her polemical essay attacks the policy head-on with furious rhetoric."

tendentious — Hides its bias behind a mask of neutrality. Slants the argument while appearing fair.
→ "The documentary's tendentious editing makes its case seem inevitable rather than chosen."

Memory trick: polemical = picks a fight in the open → honest about its stance · tendentious = rigs the game while pretending fairness → deceptive about its stance. Both are critical caution words, but tendentious carries the extra charge of disguised bias.

reductive /rɪˈdʌktɪv/ adjective

Oversimplifying; reducing complex reality to an overly simple, flattened account that loses essential nuance and contradiction.

Literary Usage Pattern "A [reductive] reading / The [reductive] portrayal [flattens the complexity of]…"

"Reading 1984 merely as anti-communist propaganda is reductive — it collapses Orwell's broader warning about totalitarianism in any form into a single, parochial political slogan." — 1984 analysis

IB Tip A key critical caution word. Examiners reward students who avoid reductive readings — and who can name when an interpretation (including their own first draft) has flattened a text. The direct opposite of "nuanced" (revisited below). Use it to diagnose weak readings: "This interpretation is too reductive because it ignores the narrator's ambivalence."

Common collocations: reductive reading · reductive analysis · reductive account · reductive interpretation · reductive framework

insidious /ɪnˈsɪdiəs/ adjective

Proceeding harmfully in a subtle, stealthy, or gradual way; treacherous precisely because it is unnoticed until the damage is done.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [insidious] influence / The [insidious] normalisation of [harmful thing]…"

"The insidious appeal of the Party in 1984 lies precisely in its banality — Newspeak and the Two Minutes Hate erode freedom so gradually that Winston barely notices the cage closing." — 1984 analysis

IB Tip Powerful for describing how oppressive systems or ideologies creep in unnoticed. Often paired with "normalise" — the danger is that the harmful thing becomes attractive or invisible. Distinct from "subversive": subversion is the force undermining from within; insidious is the quality of that creep (stealthy, gradual). Great for dystopia and gothic studies.

Common collocations: insidious influence · insidious power · insidious effect · insidious normalisation · insidious appeal

transgressive /trænzˈɡreʃəs/ adjective

Violating or crossing accepted boundaries, norms, or moral codes; challenging what is permitted or sanctioned.

Literary Usage Pattern "A [transgressive] act / The [transgressive] desire / A [transgressive] text [crosses the boundary of]…"

"The transgressive desire of Gothic heroines — their hunger, their curiosity — is precisely what makes them threatening to the patriarchal order that seeks to contain them." — Gothic fiction analysis

IB Tip Closely related to "subversive" but more about boundary-crossing than system-overthrowing. Useful for characters, desires, or forms that break taboos. A transgressive text may shock without necessarily aiming to topple a regime. Strong for gender, body, and censorship discussions.

Common collocations: transgressive desire · transgressive act · transgressive text · transgressive impulse · transgressive body

provocative /prəˈvɒkətɪv/ adjective

Deliberately stimulating strong reaction, thought, or controversy; challenging the reader's comfort or assumptions.

Literary Usage Pattern "A [provocative] gesture / The [provocative] claim / A [provocative] image [forces a response]…"

"Kafka's provocative opening — 'Someone must have been telling lies about Josef K.' — implicates the reader in guilt before any crime is named, unsettling our assumptions about innocence." — The Trial analysis

IB Tip From the verb "provoke" (Day 2). Use for moments that deliberately jolt the reader. Distinct from "polemical" — provocative need not be argumentative, just jolting or unsettling. "A provocative opening" names the author's intent to disturb without committing to a position.

Common collocations: provocative image · provocative claim · provocative gesture · provocative opening · provocative premise

ambivalent /æmˈbɪvələnt/ adjective

Having mixed, contradictory feelings or attitudes toward something; simultaneously attracted and repelled, or holding two incompatible positions at once.

Literary Usage Pattern "The text is [ambivalent] about [subject], [both X and Y]…"

"The play is deeply ambivalent about empire — celebrating its adventure while mourning its destruction, so the audience is never allowed a stable moral position." — Postcolonial drama analysis

IB Tip Crucial for sophisticated analysis — texts (and characters) are rarely one thing. Spotting ambivalence shows you're reading beyond the surface. Opposite of a "clear-cut" stance. Pairs naturally with "nuanced": an ambivalent text usually demands a nuanced reading. Avoid claiming a text "says X" when it's actually ambivalent about X.

Common collocations: ambivalent attitude · ambivalent portrayal · ambivalent tone · ambivalent about · ambivalent response

didactic /dɪˈdæktɪk/ adjective

Intended to teach or instruct, especially morally; a text whose primary purpose is to convey a lesson. Can be neutral (teaching) or slightly critical (preachy).

Literary Usage Pattern "The [didactic] purpose / A [didactic] text [instructs the reader to…]"

"While A Christmas Carol is unapologetically didactic — Scrooge's transformation is a moral lesson — its emotional force prevents it from feeling like mere preaching." — A Christmas Carol analysis

IB Tip Revisited from Day 1, now at the evaluative level. The key move: weigh didactic intent against artistic effect. A text can be didactic and still succeed (if the lesson is earned through feeling); it fails as a novel when the teaching overwhelms the art. Use "didactic" to name a text's purpose, then judge whether that purpose is achieved.

Common collocations: didactic purpose · didactic intent · didactic literature · didactic message · overtly didactic

nuanced /ˈnjuːɑːnst/ adjective

Characterised by subtle, fine-grained distinctions and sensitivity to complexity; avoiding oversimplification and holding contradiction rather than resolving it prematurely.

Literary Usage Pattern "A [nuanced] reading / The [nuanced] portrayal [captures the complexity of]…"

"Morrison's nuanced portrayal of Sethe refuses easy judgement — her love and her violence are shown as the same overwhelming maternal force, demanding that the reader hold contradiction rather than resolve it." — Beloved analysis

IB Tip Revisited from Day 1 — and it is the gold-standard adjective for IB. "Nuanced" is what you strive for in every essay, and what "reductive" (above) destroys. Use it to describe the best readings; use "reductive" to diagnose the weakest. They are direct opposites — see the final distinction box. If you can call a text or a reading "nuanced" with evidence, you're operating at a 6–7 level.

Common collocations: nuanced reading · nuanced portrayal · nuanced analysis · nuanced understanding · nuanced approach

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: reductive vs. nuanced

These are direct opposites — the single most important evaluative pair in your toolkit:

reductive — Flattens complexity; loses essential nuance; collapses contradiction into a single claim.
→ "Reading the novel as simply 'anti-war' is reductive — it ignores the text's ambivalence about sacrifice."

nuanced — Captures subtle distinction and complexity; holds contradiction.
→ "Her nuanced reading attends to both the novel's critique of war and its mournful respect for the fallen."

Memory trick: reductive = the text/reading shrinks → loses detail · nuanced = the text/reading expands → gains detail. Use "reductive" to critique weak interpretations (including your own drafts); use "nuanced" to describe strong ones. They will feature in your Day 6 weekly review cluster.

🎯 Building Your Critical Vocabulary

  1. You now have the vocabulary for judgement, not just description. Days 1–4 gave you verbs for what texts do (analysis) and how they're built (craft). Day 5 gives you adjectives for evaluating — whether a text's argument is sound, biased, subtle, or simplistic. This is the language of critical appraisal that IB Paper 2 and your IOC demand.
  2. The caution words (reductive, tendentious, insidious) are your critical armour. Use "reductive" to diagnose weak readings — including your own first drafts — and "tendentious" to expose hidden bias in narrators and propaganda. Naming the flaw precisely is a Criterion C/D move.
  3. The aspiration words (nuanced, ambivalent, allegorical) describe complex, rewarding texts. "Nuanced" is the IB gold standard — aim for it in every essay. "Ambivalent" lets you capture texts that refuse a stable stance, and "allegorical" names symbolic depth without over-reading.
  4. The stance words (polemical, satirical, subversive, transgressive, provocative) describe a text's mode of critique. Match the word to the precise kind of challenge the text mounts: satire mocks, subversion undermines, transgression crosses boundaries, polemic attacks openly, provocation jolts. The distinction boxes train you to pick the exact one.
  5. Watch the distinction traps for Day 5. The pairs most often confused: polemical/tendentious (open vs. disguised bias), satirical/subversive (method vs. aim), reductive/nuanced (opposites). These are exactly the near-synonym clusters your Day 6 weekly review will consolidate — review all three boxes until the differences are instinctive.
  6. Deploy evaluative adjectives in your essay conclusions and evaluations now. When you judge a text, name the quality: "The novel's argument is ultimately more nuanced than reductive" — that's evaluative precision. By Paper 2, the right adjective should arrive without thinking.
