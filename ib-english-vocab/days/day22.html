🔁 Day 19 Spaced Review — 12 Rhetorical Schemes (Arrangement)

A spaced-repetition pass on the figures of arrangement from Day 19 — the schemes that shape word order, not meaning. Read each, recall its precise mechanism before looking, then check. These twelve are the syntax-level cousins of Week 3's tropes; they reappear whenever you analyse sentence shape in a Paper 1 commentary.

chiasmus
Ideas repeated in reverse order (A–B–B–A), using different words.
zeugma
One word governing two others, fitting only one strictly.
asyndeton
The omission of conjunctions between units → speed, staccato.
polysyndeton
The repetition of conjunctions between units → slowness, accumulation.
litotes
Understatement via a double negative ("not unlike").
antimetabole
The SAME words repeated in reversed order (stricter than chiasmus).
isocolon
Parallel clauses of equal length and weight.
anadiplosis
A word ending one clause starts the next (a chain).
anastrophe
Inversion of the normal word order ("object before verb").
climax
A series rising in importance or force.
parataxis
Equal clauses joined by no linker → withholds cause.
hypotaxis
Clauses nested by subordinators → supplies cause.
Self-assessment: Could you distinguish asyndeton from polysyndeton, and chiasmus from antimetabole, without peeking? Those two pairs are the Day 19 traps — revisit the Day 19 document's boxes if either blurred. These schemes are exactly what today's syntax words (Day 23) will let you describe at the level of clause structure, so keep them live.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Stylistic & Linguistic Analysis Terms

NEW CATEGORY — "STYLISTIC & LINGUISTIC ANALYSIS"

From naming devices to describing language. Week 3 gave you the device vocabulary (metaphor, anaphora, parataxis). Today you learn the metalanguage that explains how language is built and how an argument is framed — the words linguists and IB examiners use for the levels of language: phonological (sound), syntactic (sentence shape), semantic (meaning), pragmatic (use-in-context), and discourse (text-as-a-whole). The first six words analyse the text; the last six frame the argument you build on it. Together they are the skeleton of every Paper 1 commentary and Paper 2 essay — the frame that holds your Week 3 evidence.

cohesion /kəʊˈhiːʒn/ noun

The grammatical and lexical devices that bind a text into a connected unity — reference (pronouns linking back), substitution, ellipsis, conjunction, and lexical cohesion (repetition, synonymy, collocation).

Literary Usage Pattern "[The pronoun] [refers back to] the noun" · "[And / but / so] [links] the clauses" · "[The chain of] [synonyms] [holds] the stanza"

"In Beloved, Morrison's chains of pronoun reference — 'she… her… she' — create a cohesion that stitches Sethe's fragmented memories into one consciousness; the reader tracks who 'she' is only through the grammatical glue." — Beloved analysis

IB Tip A textual (not semantic) property — it lives on the page, in the grammar. Distinguish it sharply from coherence (see box below). In IB, cohesion is your evidence when you argue a text 'holds together', or, conversely, withholds connection: broken reference can signal alienation or trauma. Link to Day 17's stream of consciousness — disrupted cohesion there is a feature, not a flaw. Root: Latin cohaerere, 'to stick together'.

Common collocations: textual cohesion · cohesive device · reference cohesion · lexical cohesion · break in cohesion

coherence /kəʊˈhɪərəns/ noun

The semantic and logical sense a text makes as a whole — the reader's inference that its parts connect in meaning, even when the grammar alone would not guarantee it.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The argument] [coheres] because…" · "[Despite the jumps], the [sense] holds" · "[The reader supplies] the missing links"

"A non-linear film may lack grammatical cohesion yet feel deeply coherent: the viewer supplies the missing links, reading the flashbacks as one grieving mind rather than a jumble of scenes." — narrative analysis

IB Tip Distinct from cohesion (box below): coherence is in the reader's mind; cohesion is on the page. A text can be cohesive but incoherent (empty connecting words) or incohesive but coherent (poetic fragmentation that still means). Use both terms precisely in Paper 1. Connects to Day 17's non-linear narrative and Day 18's inference — coherence is what inference builds.

Common collocations: semantic coherence · logical coherence · lack of coherence · achieve coherence · narrative coherence

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: cohesion vs. coherence

Both describe a text 'hanging together', but the seam is where the unity lives:

cohesion — On the page. The grammatical/lexical links (pronouns, conjunctions, repetition) that tie sentences together.
→ "The he… his… him chain shows tight cohesion."

coherence — In the mind. The semantic sense the reader constructs, even without those links.
→ "The fragmented poem is incohesive yet fully coherent — we supply the logic."

The test: can you point to the word that links (cohesion), or only feel the sense (coherence)? A text strong on one may be weak on the other. In IB, naming which you mean is the difference between describing the surface and describing the reading experience. This pair anchors your linguistic-analysis toolkit.

deixis /ˈdaɪksɪs/ noun

Context-dependent 'shifters' — words whose reference changes with the speech situation: person (I/you), place (here/there), time (now/then), and social (my/your). They anchor language to who speaks, where, and when.

Literary Usage Pattern "[I] [here] [now] shift meaning with context" · "[The deictic centre] moves to the child" · "[We] excludes the reader"

"In Room, the deictic centre is five-year-old Jack: 'this is Room, that is Outside' — the reader is trapped inside his here/now, so the world beyond the shed stays unimaginable until the centre shifts." — Room analysis

IB Tip A pragmatic concept (meaning-in-context). Crucial for dramatic monologue and first-person narration: whose 'here/now' are we in? Deixis reveals perspective and power — who controls the 'I' controls the truth. Links to Day 17's interior monologue and Day 18's stance. Name it whenever a text manipulates point of view. Root: Greek deixis, 'pointing'.

Common collocations: deictic centre · deictic shift · person deixis · spatial deixis · temporal deixis

lexical field /ˈleksɪkl fiːld/ noun

A network of semantically related words clustered around a concept or domain (e.g. a 'field' of war, of nature, of religion) that recurs through a text and accumulates meaning.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A cluster of] [blood / wound / scar] [words] forms a field" · "[The maritime] lexical field [recurs]"

"In Jane Eyre, a persistent lexical field of fire and ice — 'burning', 'frozen', 'kindle', 'ice' — maps the novel's emotional geography: passion versus restraint, long before any character names the feeling." — Jane Eyre analysis

IB Tip A semantic-level tool. Distinct from a motif (Day 15): a lexical field is the linguistic evidence; a motif is the recurring idea it serves. Use lexical fields to prove a text's preoccupations quantitatively ('the war field appears 40+ times'). Connects to Day 18's connotation — fields carry connotative weight. Powerful for both Paper 1 and Paper 2.

Common collocations: semantic field · lexical set · recurring field · maritime field · religious lexical field

foregrounding /ˈfɔːɡraʊndɪŋ/ noun

The stylistic effect produced when language deviates from, or over-observes, its norms, making an item salient against the background — the mechanism by which a text signals 'attend to this' (Leech & Short).

Literary Usage Pattern "[The unexpected] [inversion / repetition] foregrounds [the idea]" · "[Deviation] makes [X] stand out"

"In The Waste Land, Eliot's sudden shift into another language foregrounds the fragment's dislocation — the norm-breaking itself becomes the meaning the reader must confront." — The Waste Land analysis

IB Tip The technical noun behind Day 1's verb foreground: you already use it instinctively ('this stands out'); now you can name the mechanism. Two kinds — deviation (breaking a rule) and over-regularity (excess pattern, e.g. sustained parallelism). Foregrounding is why a device matters; pair it with any Week 3 device ('the metaphor foregrounds…'). Core to every stylistic claim.

Common collocations: stylistic foregrounding · deviation · over-regularity · foregrounded item · foregrounding effect

intonation /ˌɪntəˈneɪʃn/ noun

The pitch contour of an utterance — rise, fall, fall-rise — that carries attitude, stance, and illocutionary force beyond the literal words (the phonological level of analysis).

Literary Usage Pattern "[A falling tone] [closes] the matter" · "[A rising tone] [queries / undermines]" · "[The fall-rise] turns a statement into a challenge"

"In dramatic dialogue, a stage direction's 'softly' can only hint at intonation; the same line spoken with a fall-rise contour turns a calm statement into a challenge the printed page cannot show." — drama / dialogue analysis

IB Tip A phonological tool, essential for performance texts (drama, poetry read aloud). Intonation reveals stance and sarcasm (Day 18) that punctuation hides. In IB, name intonation when analysing drama or spoken register — it explains how tone is enacted, not just described. Pair with Day 18's register. Root: Latin intonare, 'to thunder in'.

Common collocations: falling intonation · rising intonation · fall-rise · intonational contour · attitudinal intonation

thesis /ˈθiːsɪs/ noun

The central proposition or claim an essay argues — the single debatable position the whole paper exists to prove.

Literary Usage Pattern "[This essay argues] that [X]" · "[The thesis] is that [Y differs from Z]" · "[My] thesis: [isolation is chosen]"

"A strong Paper 2 opener states its thesis explicitly: 'While both texts explore alienation, it is only in Text A that isolation is figured as chosen — and that difference reshapes the texts' political charge.'" — Paper 2 essay framing

IB Tip The spine of argumentative writing. Distinct from contention (box below): the thesis is your specific claim; the contention is the broader debatable position it occupies. A thesis must be arguable (not factual) and scope-limited. Link to Day 1's problematize — a good thesis problematizes, it does not assert the obvious. Always qualify it (see word 10).

Common collocations: central thesis · argue a thesis · defend a thesis · clear thesis · thesis statement

contention /kənˈtenʃn/ noun

A debatable position or claim put forward for argument — the larger assertion a writer enters into dispute, often broader than a single thesis.

Literary Usage Pattern "[My] contention [is] that [X matters]" · "[The] contention [rests on] the premise" · "[This essay contends] that…"

"The critic's contention — that the novel is fundamentally about complicity, not innocence — frames every close reading that follows; the thesis is the specific version of that contention the essay will prove." — critical essay framing

IB Tip Distinct from thesis (box below): contention = the arguable stance you take; thesis = the precise claim you prove within it. In IB, signal your contention early ('this essay contends that…') then narrow to a thesis. Connects to Day 1's interrogate and problematize. Root: Latin contendere, 'to stretch with'.

Common collocations: central contention · main contention · advance a contention · the essay's contention · rebut a contention

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: thesis vs. contention

Both name 'what you argue', but they sit at different altitudes:

thesis — The specific, provable claim your essay defends (narrow, testable).
→ "The ending is hopeful only insofar as hope is reframed as survival."

contention — The broader debatable stance that stance occupies (wide, the position you enter).
→ "This essay contends that the text is ultimately about survival, not redemption."

The test: could a whole essay prove it (thesis), or is it the camp the thesis fights from (contention)? A contention is too big to 'prove' in one paragraph; a thesis is small enough to win. In IB, state the contention, then the thesis — examiners read the gap between them as your sophistication.

premise /ˈpremɪs/ noun

A proposition on which an argument logically rests — the assumed ground from which a conclusion is drawn.

Literary Usage Pattern "[From the premise] that [X], [it follows] that [Y]" · "[The] premise [is contested]"

"An essay's premise might be that 'language shapes identity'; the thesis then argues how Text A enacts that premise — if the premise is weak, the whole argument wobbles." — argument structure

IB Tip The foundation of logical argument. In IB, expose an author's hidden premise ('the text assumes that power corrupts') — that is interrogating (Day 1) at the structural level. A premise can be challenged to subvert (Day 1) a position. Name premises when you map an argument's architecture; examiners reward showing you see the scaffolding, not just the claim.

Common collocations: underlying premise · false premise · stated premise · the premise that · build on a premise

qualify /ˈkwɒlɪfaɪ/ verb

To restrict or limit the scope or generality of a claim — 'to some extent', 'in part', 'though not wholly' — so the argument survives counter-evidence.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The claim holds], [but only] in [certain] contexts" · "[To qualify]: [X is true insofar as]"

"Rather than asserting 'the ending is hopeful', a nuanced essay qualifies: 'the ending is hopeful to the extent that hope is reframed as survival, not resolution' — the claim is stronger for the limit." — essay modulation

IB Tip The mark of a 7. Qualifying shows you have interrogated (Day 1) your own claim; it is not weakness but precision. Pair with hedge (Day 18) at the linguistic level and with concede (word 11) at the argument level. Avoid over-qualifying into mush — qualify the scope, not the conviction. This is exactly the 'nuanced' quality Day 1 prized.

Common collocations: qualify a claim · qualify the statement · heavily qualified · qualify one's position · qualifying clause

concede /kənˈsiːd/ verb

To grant a point to the opposing view before resuming one's own argument — a strategic admission that strengthens credibility.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Admittedly / One might concede] that [X]" · "[I concede] the force of [the counter-view]"

"'One might concede that the speaker is genuinely grieving; yet the very excess of that grief is what indicts the silence around it' — the concession sharpens the rebuttal that follows." — counter-argument framing

IB Tip Distinct from qualify (box below): qualify limits your own claim's scope; concede grants the opponent a point. Both are higher-order moves. Use concede to pre-empt objection ('admittedly…'), then rebut (word 12). Connects to Day 5's balanced / measured. A concede–rebut pair is the signature of top-band argumentative control.

Common collocations: concede a point · readily concede · one must concede · concede the force · concede that

rebut /rɪˈbʌt/ verb

To counter or refute an opposing claim with evidence — the active dismissal that follows a concession.

Literary Usage Pattern "[This view is mistaken because] [evidence Y]" · "[The rebuttal]: [X does not follow]"

"Having conceded the speaker's grief, the essay rebuts the reading that he is merely victim: the syntax of self-blame ('I am the one who…') relocates agency, and with it guilt." — counter-argument

IB Tip The engine of dialectic. A rebuttal must meet the opposing claim on its own ground with evidence, not assertion. Pair concede→rebut as one move (words 11–12). Links to Day 1's subvert and undermine — you undermine the counter-claim's logic. In Paper 2, a concede–rebut beat shows you command the global argument, not just your thesis.

Common collocations: rebut a claim · direct rebuttal · rebut the argument · rebuttal paragraph · forcefully rebut

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: qualify vs. concede

Both soften a position, but they soften in opposite directions:

qualify — Limits the scope of your own claim ('true, but only to this extent').
→ "The ending is hopeful, though only as survival."

concede — Grants a point to the opposing view before resuming yours.
→ "Admittedly the speaker grieves; yet that grief indicts the silence."

The test: are you refining your claim (qualify) or yielding ground to beat it back (concede)? In IB, both appear in a mature paragraph — qualify early to show precision, concede mid-way to show fair-mindedness, then rebut to show control. Used together they are the architecture of a 7.

✏️ Practice — Name the Level, Frame the Claim

Four drills. Write short answers; the model notes follow each.

  1. Level-matching. For each feature, name the linguistic level it belongs to (phonological / syntactic / semantic / pragmatic / discourse):
    a) the recurring chain of 'he… he… he' pronouns → discourse (cohesion); b) the shift to 'I / here' inside the child's voice → pragmatic (deixis); c) the rhyme scheme of the closing couplet → phonological; d) the repeated 'light is… light is…' parallelism → syntactic (foregrounding via over-regularity).
  2. Mini stylistic analysis. On the passage below, name at least three Day 22 features and state each one's effect:
    Passage "I am here. I have always been here. The walls remember me; the walls forgive no one. She left, and she left, and she left — but the room stays."
    Model: the deictic 'I / here' locks us in the speaker's centre (pragmatic, confines the reader's knowledge); the cohesive chain 'she left, and she left, and she left' uses polysyndeton (a Day 19 scheme) to accumulate absence; the personified 'walls remember' foregrounds entrapment through deviation. Each device is tied to an effect, not just named.
  3. Frame the claim. Turn this blunt thesis into a 7-band version by (a) qualifying it and (b) adding a concede–rebut beat:
    Blunt "The ending is happy because the family reunites."
    Model: Qualified — "The ending is happy to the extent that reunion marks survival, not the resolution of grief." Concede–rebut — "Admittedly the reunion soothes the surviving child; yet the mother's silence about what was lost rebuts any reading of full healing." The claim is now arguable and scoped.
  4. Distinguish on demand. In one sentence each, separate these two Day 22 pairs you will be tested on: (i) cohesion vs. coherence (page-level link vs. reader-built sense); (ii) thesis vs. contention (specific provable claim vs. broad debatable stance). Keep both pairs in your exam armour alongside the twelve Week 3 distinctions.

🎯 Study Tips — Making the Metalanguage Stick

  1. Name the level, then the feature. "Polysyndeton" tells the examiner what; "syntactic foregrounding via over-regularity" tells them how and why. Always push one step deeper than the device name — that step is the linguistic level, and it is where the top band lives.
  2. Cohesion is evidence; coherence is inference. When you claim a text 'holds together' or 'withholds connection', say which you mean and point to the link (a pronoun, a conjunction) or the gap the reader fills. This precision is what separates description from analysis.
  3. Foregrounding explains why a device matters. Every Week 3 term (metaphor, anaphora, parataxis) can be reframed as a foregrounding move. "The metaphor foregrounds the link between X and Y" is a complete analytical sentence; "there is a metaphor" is not.
  4. A thesis must be arguable and scope-limited. "Power corrupts" is a fact, not a thesis. "Power corrupts only the protagonists who refuse self-knowledge" is — and it invites a qualify. The narrower the claim, the easier to prove and the harder to rebut.
  5. Concede–rebut is the signature of a 7. A paragraph that grants the counter-view ('admittedly…') and then meets it with evidence ('yet…') shows command of the global argument. Practise the pair until it is automatic — it is the single highest-leverage move in Paper 2.
  6. These twelve words are the frame; Week 3's sixty are the evidence. Never let metalanguage sit empty. Every linguistic or argumentative term you use should hang a Week 3 device or a textual detail on it. The frame without evidence is scaffolding with no building.
