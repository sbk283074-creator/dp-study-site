Today you get the fourth pillar of technical terminology: rhetorical schemes — figures of arrangement, the shapes sentences take. A scheme is not about what a word means but how words are ordered: reversed, linked, stripped of conjunctions, balanced, inverted. This is the vocabulary for analysing style at the sentence level — the difference between "he left because she stayed" and "she stayed. He left."

🔁 Day 18 First Review — 12 Tone & Attitude Terms

Your first spaced pass on Day 18's tone set. Read each word, recall its precise definition before looking, then check.

connotation
The associations / feelings a word carries beyond its literal sense.
denotation
The literal, dictionary meaning of a word.
register
The level of formality / social variety of language.
subtext
The unspoken meaning the text itself implies.
inference
The conclusion the reader draws from evidence.
modality
The degree of certainty / necessity a text expresses.
equivocation
Strategic ambiguity used to dodge a position.
hedge
An honest softener that weakens a claim's force.
stance
The writer's position toward subject and audience.
sarcasm
Mocking words conveying the opposite of their sense.
innuendo
An indirect hint at something disparaging.
understatement
Deliberately representing something as less than it is.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Rhetorical Schemes

CONTINUING — "RHETORICAL SCHEMES (FIGURES OF ARRANGEMENT)"

From meaning to shape. Days 15-16 were tropes (a word used for something else — metaphor, irony). Today's twelve are schemes — the same words, rearranged for effect. chiasmus/antimetabole reverse order; asyndeton/polysyndeton play with conjunctions; litotes negates to affirm; isocolon/anadiplosis/climax build pattern; anastrophe inverts; parataxis/hypotaxis decide whether clauses are equal or nested. This is the vocabulary of stylistic analysis — the sentence-level craft that separates a 5 from a 7 in both Papers.

chiasmus /kaɪˈæzməs/ noun

A figure in which words, phrases, or concepts are repeated in reverse order (A–B–B–A), creating balance through mirrored structure.

Literary Usage Pattern "[ask not what your country can do for you] [— ask what you can do for your country]"

"Kennedy's 'ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country' is the textbook chiasmus: the second clause mirrors the first in reversed order." — Inaugural rhetoric

IB Tip Distinct from antimetabole (see box): chiasmus reverses concepts or structures; antimetabole repeats the exact same words. Connect to Day 15's juxtaposition (reversal) and Day 16's tricolon. Chiasmus produces symmetry and a sense of closure — name the turn and what it resolves. A staple of speeches and argumentative prose.

Common collocations: a perfect chiasmus · chiastic structure · chiastic reversal

zeugma /ˈzjuːɡmə/ noun

A figure in which one word (usually a verb) governs two or more others, to only one of which it strictly applies, yoking literal and figurative senses.

Literary Usage Pattern "[he] [opened the door and his heart]" · "[she lost her keys and her temper]"

"In Pope, 'he grasped the sceptre and his bride' — one verb grips both a thing and a person, the mismatch producing sudden wit." — Neoclassical satire

IB Tip Connect to Day 16's pun (wordplay) and Day 1's conflate. Zeugma yokes the literal and the figurative; the clash is the effect. Name which object the verb fits and which it doesn't — the gap is the meaning. Useful for poetry and satirical prose where compression carries wit.

Common collocations: a harsh zeugma · zeugmatic linkage · syntactic zeugma

asyndeton /eɪˈsɪndətɒn/ noun

The deliberate omission of conjunctions between coordinated words, phrases, or clauses.

Literary Usage Pattern "[veni, vidi, vici] [— I came, I saw, I conquered]"

"Caesar's 'veni, vidi, vici' is asyndeton: the missing 'and' accelerates the sentence into a staccato of triumph." — Historical epigram

IB Tip Distinct from polysyndeton (see box). Connect to Day 16's tricolon (asyndeton often accompanies it). Asyndeton speeds pace and implies inexhaustibility or urgency — name the tempo it creates. In non-literary texts it can signal efficiency, brute fact, or coldness.

Common collocations: deliberate asyndeton · stripped of conjunctions

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: asyndeton vs. polysyndeton

Both manipulate conjunctions — but in opposite directions, producing opposite rhythms:

asyndeton — Omission of conjunctions ("I came, I saw, I conquered"). Clauses run together; pace accelerates; energy feels staccato or urgent.
→ Effect: speed, triumph, breathlessness, inexhaustibility.

polysyndeton — Repetition of conjunctions ("and… and… and…"). Clauses pile up; pace slows; the list feels cumulative or obsessive.
→ Effect: accumulation, weight, childlike listing, litany.

The test: are the connectives stripped out (asyndeton — faster) or piled on (polysyndeton — slower)? The two are often mirror images in the same author's toolkit. In stylistic analysis, naming which one explains the rhythm a sentence imposes on the reader — and rhythm is half of tone.

polysyndeton /ˌpɒlɪˈsɪndətɒn/ noun

The deliberate use of many conjunctions between coordinated elements, where they could be omitted.

Literary Usage Pattern "[we laughed and we cried and we remembered and we wept]"

"In Woolf, 'she bought the gloves and the shoes and the hat and the coat' — polysyndeton slows the sentence into a litany of accumulation." — Modernist prose

IB Tip Distinct from asyndeton (see box). Connect to Day 16's enumerative feel. Polysyndeton slows pace and suggests abundance, obsession, or a child's indiscriminate listing — name the rhythm it imposes. The mirror of asyndeton; spotting both in one text reveals a writer modulating tempo on purpose.

Common collocations: heavy polysyndeton · relentless 'and's · cumulative polysyndeton

litotes /ˈlaɪtətiːz/ noun

A figure of understatement in which an affirmative is expressed by negating its contrary (a form of double negative).

Literary Usage Pattern "[not unhappy] [= rather pleased]" · "[no small achievement]"

"'He was not without influence' is litotes — the denial of the negative states the positive with restrained force." — Understated prose

IB Tip Connect to Day 18's understatement (litotes is its grammatical form) and Day 11's restrained register. Litotes is the polite, hedged affirmation — name the restraint and what it conceals. Common in British/understated English prose; a mark of register awareness and of Day 18's hedge family.

Common collocations: a studied litotes · litotic understatement

antimetabole /ˌæntɪməˈtæbəli/ noun

Repetition of words in successive clauses in reversed grammatical order (A–B–B–A) — a subtype of chiasmus that repeats the exact same words.

Literary Usage Pattern "[when the going gets tough, the tough get going]"

"'You forget what you want to remember, and you remember what you want to forget' turns memory into antimetabole — the words flip but stay identical." — Aphoristic prose

IB Tip Distinct from chiasmus (see box): antimetabole repeats the same words; chiasmus reverses concepts/structures (often with different words). Connect to Day 15's anaphora (repetition) and Day 16's tricolon. Name the reversal and the paradox it stages — a favourite of proverbs and aphorisms.

Common collocations: neat antimetabole · antimetabolic reversal

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: chiasmus vs. antimetabole

Both are A–B–B–A reversals — but the test is whether the words are identical:

chiasmus — Concepts or structures reverse, often with different words.
→ "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country." (you/country swap, but the wording shifts.)

antimetabole — The exact same words repeat in reversed order.
→ "When the going gets tough, the tough get going." (going/tough/tough/going — identical.)

The test: do the same words come back flipped (antimetabole) or do ideas mirror each other in new words (chiasmus)? Every antimetabole is a chiasmus, but not vice versa. In analysis, naming which one shows you can tell a structural rhyme from a verbal echo — a precise, impressive distinction.

isocolon /aɪˈsɒkəlɒn/ noun

A series of clauses or phrases of roughly equal length and parallel structure.

Literary Usage Pattern "[to err is human; to forgive, divine]"

"Pope's 'to err is human, to forgive divine' is isocolon — two balanced halves of matching weight and rhythm." — Epigram

IB Tip Connect to Day 16's tricolon (isocolon can have 2, 3, or 4 balanced units) and Day 2's sense of symmetry. Isocolon produces aphoristic balance and memorability — name the equilibrium and what it dignifies (truth feels inevitable when two halves balance). The backbone of memorable prose and argument.

Common collocations: balanced isocolon · parallel isocola

anadiplosis /ˌænədɪˈpləʊsɪs/ noun

Repetition of the last word (or phrase) of one clause at the beginning of the next, chaining clauses together.

Literary Usage Pattern "[the crime was blood; blood demands blood]"

"In Shakespeare, 'blood hath brought blood' — anadiplosis links clause to clause by repeating the hinge word." — Revenge tragedy

IB Tip Connect to Day 15's anaphora (head-repetition) — anadiplosis repeats at the tail-into-head, creating a chain. It builds momentum and causal linkage — name the chain it forges and the word it elevates to a theme. Powerful in argument and tragedy; the repeated word becomes the obsession.

Common collocations: iterative anadiplosis · a chain of repetition

anastrophe /əˈnæstrəfi/ noun

Inversion of the normal word order, usually for emphasis, metre, or effect.

Literary Usage Pattern "[great is the temptation] [← normal: the temptation is great]" · "[object that I love]"

"'Great is the guilt' inverts 'the guilt is great' — anastrophe throws weight onto 'great' and slows the line." — Biblical / epic style

IB Tip Connect to Day 1's foreground — inversion is syntactic foregrounding — and Day 5's emphasis. Anastrophe is defamiliarisation through order; name what the inversion highlights and why the writer broke the expected pattern. Common in poetry, older prose (Milton, the Authorised Version), and translation — a tool for sound and weight.

Common collocations: deliberate anastrophe · inverted word order

climax /ˈklaɪmæks/ noun

Arrangement of words or phrases in ascending order of importance, intensity, or scale (also called gradatio).

Literary Usage Pattern "[trial, then sentence, then death] [— each step intensifies]"

"'First the whisper, then the argument, then the screaming silence' — climax ratchets each item higher until the final paradox lands." — Rhetorical prose

IB Tip Connect to Day 16's tricolon (climax is often a rising tricolon) and Day 18's stance. Climax directs the reader's attention to the peak — name the escalation and its emotional payoff. The purposeful cousin of asyndeton's speed; both control how a sentence builds.

Common collocations: a rising climax · arranged in climax · gradatio

parataxis /ˌpærəˈtæksɪs/ noun

Coordination of clauses without subordination or connectives — short, equal, side-by-side statements placed next to one another.

Literary Usage Pattern "[he left. she stayed.] [— no 'because' or 'although']"

"Hemingway's 'He drank. He drove. He died.' is parataxis — blunt, equal clauses with no linking word to explain causality." — Modernist prose

IB Tip Distinct from hypotaxis (see box). Connect to Day 2's starkness and Day 11's laconic. Parataxis suggests objectivity, fatalism, or plainness — name the flatness and what it withholds (no cause is stated). The modernist sentence; essential for style analysis and for contrasting literary with non-literary registers.

Common collocations: blunt parataxis · paratactic style · paratactic listing

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: parataxis vs. hypotaxis

The fundamental choice in how clauses relate — equality vs. hierarchy:

parataxis — Clauses set side by side, equal, with no subordination ("He left. She stayed."). The reader supplies the link.
→ Effect: objectivity, fatalism, plainness, restraint.

hypotaxis — Clauses nested by subordination ("She stayed because he left."). One idea is made dependent on another.
→ Effect: complexity, nuance, qualification, a mind weighing conditions.

The test: are the clauses equal neighbours (parataxis) or one embedded inside another (hypotaxis)? This single choice encodes a writer's whole relationship to certainty — parataxis withholds causality; hypotaxis supplies it through because / although / if. Connect to Day 18's modality and hedge: hypotaxis is where nuance lives. Hemingway vs. Henry James is the canonical contrast.

hypotaxis /ˌhaɪpəˈtæksɪs/ noun

Subordination of clauses — embedding dependent ideas within main ones by means of conjunctions (because, although, if, when).

Literary Usage Pattern "[she stayed] [because] [he left]" · "[although tired, he worked]"

"Henry James's sentences pile clause within clause — hypotaxis that mirrors a mind qualifying everything it says before it says it." — Late-modern prose

IB Tip Distinct from parataxis (see box). Connect to Day 18's modality / hedge — hypotaxis carries nuance, condition, and caution. Hypotaxis suggests intellectual complexity or sophistication — name the nesting and what it implies about the thinker. The opposite of Hemingway's parataxis; together the pair explains almost any stylistic contrast you'll meet.

Common collocations: dense hypotaxis · hypotactic nesting · heavily subordinated

🎯 Building Your Device Toolkit

  1. Day 19 completes the "scheme" half of technical terminology. Devices (Days 15-16) are figures of meaning; schemes (today) are figures of arrangement. Together they let you analyse a text at both the word level and the sentence level — the full stylistic toolkit IB examiners look for.
  2. Three distinction pairs anchor the day. asyndeton/polysyndeton (conjunctions: speed vs. weight), chiasmus/antimetabole (reversal: ideas vs. same words), parataxis/hypotaxis (clause relation: equal vs. nested). These six terms let you explain why a sentence feels the way it does — the move that lifts stylistic analysis from description to explanation.
  3. Schemes are the grammar of rhythm and tone. Asyndeton accelerates; polysyndeton accumulates; parataxis withholds cause; hypotaxis supplies nuance. Once you can name the scheme, you can explain the tempo — and tempo is half of tone (Day 18). The two days are now linked.
  4. Zeugma and litotes are the witty/subtle ones. Zeugma compresses a joke or a shock into one verb; litotes says less to mean more (kin to Day 18's understatement and hedge). Both reward close readers who notice the economy of a line. Name them and you've caught the writer being cunning.
  5. Revisits tie the weeks together. juxtaposition (Day 15) → chiasmus; tricolon (Day 16) → isocolon/climax; anaphora (Day 15) → anadiplosis; foreground (Day 1) → anastrophe; understatement/hedge (Day 18) → litotes/hypotaxis. Your technical vocabulary is now a connected web across all three weeks, not isolated lists.
  6. Deploy these on a real sentence this week. Take any paragraph and force yourself to name: one scheme of arrangement, one of the three distinction pairs it illustrates, and the effect on rhythm/tone.
