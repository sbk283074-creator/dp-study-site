🔁 Day 10 Spaced Review — 12 Mood & Atmosphere Adjectives

A spaced-repetition pass on the mood & atmosphere toolkit from Day 10 — at the 15-day interval, the long-term consolidation point where a word either becomes permanent or slips. These twelve name the emotional weather of a text: sombre, spectral, haunting, elegiac, foreboding, melancholic, wistful, languid, ethereal, glacial, febrile, visceral. They are the direct ancestors of today's sound vocabulary — every sound pattern you learn today is a mechanism for producing one of these moods. Read each, recall its precise shade before looking, then check.

sombre
Dark, gloomy, serious in tone; heavy with gravity or sadness.
spectral
Ghostly, phantom-like; belonging to another, uncanny world.
haunting
Lingering in the mind; disturbingly memorable, not easily shaken off.
elegiac
Mournful, lamenting; sorrow for what is lost or passing.
foreboding
Ominous; suggesting something bad is about to happen.
melancholic
Deeply pensive, inwardly wistful-sad; given to thoughtful sorrow.
wistful
Sadly longing; gentle regret for something absent or past.
languid
Weak, sluggish, lacking energy; tiredly slow or indolent.
ethereal
Delicate, light, airy; too perfect or refined for this world.
glacial
Cold, hostile, emotionally frozen; lacking warmth or feeling.
febrile
Feverish, agitated, restless; barely-contained nervous energy.
visceral
Felt in the gut; raw, instinctive, bodily rather than intellectual.

If you can still separate the three Day 10 pairs (sombre/melancholic, spectral/ethereal, foreboding/haunting) without peeking, you have them for good. Today's sound devices give you the causal story — mood is sound made felt.

📖 Today's New Words — 12 Sound & Metre Terms

NEW CATEGORY — "SOUND & METRE"

From the sentence to the syllable. You can now name what language does (Days 22–23) and from which position you read it (Day 24). Today you learn to name how it sounds — the phonological and prosodic metalanguage that turns "I like the rhythm" into "the trochaic opening enacts the forge's hammer-stroke." We split the twelve into two families: sound-repetition devices (alliteration, assonance, consonance, onomatopoeia, sibilance, rhyme — the music of individual sounds) and prosody & line-structure (metre, iamb, trochee, caesura, enjambment, euphony — the architecture of the verse line). In IB, sound analysis is never decorative: the pattern is the argument, and the examiner rewards a candidate who can say why a sound matters, not just that it is there. These terms complete the stylistic toolkit before the Week 4 consolidation.

alliteration /əˌlɪtəˈreɪʃn/ noun

The deliberate repetition of an initial consonant sound in neighbouring words or stressed syllables — a sonic hook that binds a line and draws the ear (and thus the attention) to the words it links.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The] alliterative [chain] [binds] the line" · "[A tumbling] alliteration of t-sounds [drives] the clause" · "[Initial] p-alliteration [pounds]"

"In Beowulf, the alliterative half-lines — 'fyrst forð gewát; flota wæs on ýðum' — make the verse cohere by sound before sense: the Old English ear is led by the repeated consonant rather than by end-rhyme, the beat carrying the story." — Beowulf analysis

IB Tip Repetition of a sound at the start of words (contrast assonance = vowel, consonance = consonant anywhere). In IB, never just name it — say what the repeated sound does: plosives (p, t, k) = speed, violence, or menace; liquids (l, m, r) = comfort, flow, or lull. Link to Day 22's foregrounding: sound that draws the ear is meaning made audible. Root: Latin ad- + littera, 'letter'. It is the headline device of Anglo-Saxon verse and of every advert jingle you have ever remembered.

Common collocations: initial alliteration · alliterative chain · heavy alliteration · alliterative verse · plosive alliteration

assonance /ˈæsənəns/ noun

The repetition of a vowel sound in nearby words whose consonants differ — an internal rhyme of vowel music that knits a line beneath the surface, often carrying mood without the obviousness of end-rhyme.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A long o] assonance [sustains] the line" · "[The] vowel-echo [weaves] the stanza" · "[Held] a-vowels [open] the mouth"

"In Tennyson's 'the rhyme of the far-off bell / from the answering sea', the long o assonance slows the line into a tolling — the vowel itself seems to mourn, long before the sense declares the grief." — Tennyson analysis

IB Tip Vowel repetition (not consonant — that is consonance; not initial — that is alliteration). Assonance quietly carries mood — link to Day 10's feeling-family: a held open vowel (a, ah) = space or despair; a tight closed vowel (i) = tension or restraint. It is the subtle cousin of rhyme; deploy it for poems that refuse end-rhyme yet still sing. The examiner notices when you catch the half-heard vowel-music.

Common collocations: vowel assonance · sustained assonance · internal assonance · assonance and rhyme · euphonious assonance

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: alliteration vs. assonance

Both repeat sound, but the seam is consonant vs. vowel and position:

alliteration — Repetition of an initial consonant sound across words ("peter picked a peck").
→ "The fortnight's frost framed the field." — the ear is caught at every word-start.

assonance — Repetition of a vowel sound inside words, regardless of initial consonant ("the light of the fire").
→ "The tiny mine's bite." — the vowels chime while the consonants change.

The test: are the repeated sounds consonants at the start (alliteration) or vowels anywhere (assonance)? They often travel together, but naming the right one shows you hear the mechanism. In IB, the sophisticated move is 'the alliterative p-pound enacts panic, while the assonant long o simultaneously slows the line into dread' — you read both layers at once. Keep this pair beside today's consonance/sibilance in your sound-device shelf.

consonance /ˈkɒnsənəns/ noun

The repetition of a consonant sound at any position in nearby words (initial, medial, or final) — a subtler, less obvious cousin of alliteration that creates texture the reader feels but may not consciously name.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A muted] consonance of m-sounds [hums]" · "[The repeated] k [underpins] the dread" · "[Final] t-consonance [taps] shut"

"In Plath's 'the moon is no door', the repeated m and d consonance hums beneath the sense — a low, mechanical music that matches the poem's trapped, inward turning." — Sylvia Plath analysis

IB Tip Consonant repetition at any position (unlike alliteration, which is initial only). Sibilance (today) is a subset of consonance — the s-family specifically. In IB, noticing half-heard consonance shows sophistication: it is sound-level cohesion (Day 22) binding a passage you cannot quite explain by sense alone. Name the consonant and the effect (a held m = enclosure; a tapped t = closure or death).

Common collocations: repeated consonance · muted consonance · medial consonance · consonance and assonance · harsh consonance

onomatopoeia /ˌɒnəmætəˈpiːə/ noun

A word whose sound imitates the thing it names (buzz, crash, whisper, hiss) — language that performs what it denotes, collapsing the gap between signifier and signified.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The] onomatopoeic [crack] [enacts] the break" · "[A] hiss [that is] the snake" · "[The] word [sounds like] its thing"

"In Tennyson's 'the moan of doves in immemorial elms', the long, open vowels are near-onomatopoeic with mourning itself — the line sounds like the grief it describes, the word and the thing briefly the same." — Tennyson analysis

IB Tip The word that is its sound. Distinct from mere sound-patterning (alliteration) — onomatopoeia names the object in its own noise. Connect to Day 22's foregrounding and Day 26's semantic field (the word's meaning and its sound fuse). Greek: onoma (name) + poiein (to make) — 'name-making'. Watch: not every noisy word is onomatopoeic; it must echo its referent, not just be loud.

Common collocations: onomatopoeic word · near-onomatopoeia · onomatopoeic effect · sound-imitating · the word enacts its sense

sibilance /ˈsɪbɪləns/ noun

The heavy repetition of sibilant sounds (s, sh, z, soft c) — a hissing, whispering texture often used to evoke secrecy, menace, or intimacy, the s-specific subset of consonance with a strong affective charge.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] sibilant [hiss] [threads] the line" · "[The] soft s [conspire] / [whisper]" · "[S]ounds that [hush]"

"In Shakespeare's whispered confidences — 'so sweet, so soft, so slow' — the sibilance is a shared secret made audible; the hiss enacts the intimacy, or the menace, the line names." — Shakespeare analysis

IB Tip A specific, loaded form of consonance (the s-family). It carries affect: sibilance = stealth, menace, or intimacy (a confidence half-spoken). Contrast with the hard plosives of alliteration when a poem moves from threat to tenderness. Link to Day 24's othering — a hissed 's' can mark the alien 'other'. Name it precisely; examiners reward the specific over the vague 'repetition of sound'.

Common collocations: sibilant s · heavy sibilance · whispered sibilance · hissing sibilance · sibilant texture

rhyme /raɪm/ noun

The correspondence of terminal sounds between words — full/perfect (exact) or half/slant (near) — a structural music that closes lines and marks pattern, signalling order or, in its breaking, its absence.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A closed] rhyme [buttons] the stanza" · "[Slant] rhyme [destabilises] the closure" · "[End] rhyme [marks] the turn"

"In Wilfred Owen's Anthem for Doomed Youth, the near-rhymes — 'guns' / 'orisons', 'pall' / 'bugle' — are half-rhymes that refuse to close, the broken rhyme enacting the broken rites of war." — Owen analysis

IB Tip End-rhyme is structure; internal rhyme is texture. Distinguish full rhyme (exact) from slant/half rhyme (near) — the latter is the sophisticated choice, signalling unease or incompletion. Link to Day 23's coordination: rhyme is sound-level coordination of line-ends. In IB, a poet who 'should' rhyme but doesn't (blank verse, half-rhyme) is making an argument; name the broken pattern as a broken world.

Common collocations: full rhyme · half rhyme · slant rhyme · end rhyme · internal rhyme · rhyme scheme

metre /ˈmiːtə(r)/ noun

The regular rhythmic pattern of a line of verse, measured in feet (units of stressed and unstressed syllables) — the underlying pulse or grid against which meaning, and its disruptions, are played out.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Iambic] metre [carries] the argument" · "[A] disrupted [metre] [signals] the break" · "[The] default [pentameter] [breathes]"

"In Shakespeare's blank verse, the iambic pentameter is the default breath; when Macbeth's 'Out, out, brief candle' snaps the metre, the rupture performs his despair — the form breaks as the man breaks." — Macbeth analysis

IB Tip Metre = the system; the foot (iamb, trochee — next words) = its unit. Distinguish from rhythm (the actual, variable sound) — metre is the grid, rhythm is what the voice does to it. The IB payoff is metrical disruption: where a poet breaks the pattern, something breaks in the world. Link to Day 22's cohesion (expectation) and Day 23's fronting (emphasis by position).

Common collocations: iambic metre · trochaic metre · metrical pattern · disrupted metre · metrical foot

iamb /ˈaɪæm/ noun

A metrical foot of two syllables, unstressed then stressed (da-DUM) — the natural English beat, the pulse of everyday words like 'today' and 'behold', and the foundation of iambic pentameter.

Literary Usage Pattern "[An] iambic [line] [mimics] speech" · "[The] rising [iamb] [ascends] the sense" · "[Five] iambs [a line]"

"Shakespeare's 'Shall I / comPARE / thee TO / a SUM / mer's DAY' is iambic pentameter — five da-DUMs that sound like ordinary English speech elevated, the rising foot carrying argument as breath." — Shakespeare's sonnets

IB Tip Rising foot (weak-strong). The default of English verse; its familiarity makes it feel 'natural', so a poet's departures register sharply. Contrast with trochee (next box) — falling. In IB, iambic = ease, speech, the diurnal; a metrical substitution (a trochee where an iamb is expected) is emphasis by irregularity. Root: Latin iambus, from Greek iambos.

Common collocations: iambic foot · iambic pentameter · rising iamb · iambic line · substituted iamb

trochee /ˈtrəʊkiː/ noun

A metrical foot of two syllables, stressed then unstressed (DUM-da) — a falling, often march-like or incantatory beat, the mirror-image of the iamb.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] trochaic [opening] [strikes] the ear" · "[The] falling [beat] [weighs] each word" · "[Trochees] [hammer]"

"In Blake's 'Tiger Tiger burning bright', the trochee falls on every word — DUM-da, DUM-da — the descending beat mimicking the hammer-strokes of the forge, weight before release." — Blake, The Tyger

IB Tip Falling foot (strong-weak), the mirror of the iamb (box 2). Trochee feels urgent, incantatory, or solemn because it starts loud and drops — unlike the iamb's gentle rise. In IB, trochaic openings (or a line that begins on a trochee amid iambs) signal force, command, or ritual. Name the foot and the feeling; metre is never neutral. Root: Greek trokhaios, 'running'.

Common collocations: trochaic foot · trochaic opening · falling trochee · trochaic line · trochaic beat

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: iamb vs. trochee

Both are two-syllable metrical feet, but the seam is direction of stress — rising vs. falling:

iamb — Unstressed then stressed: da-DUM (rising). "beHOLD", "toDAY".
→ "Shall I comPARE thee TO a SUMmer's DAY" — the line rises into each stress, mimicking speech.

trochee — Stressed then unstressed: DUM-da (falling). "TIGer", "DOgwood".
→ "TIGer TIGer BURNing BRIGHT" — the line falls from each stress, mimicking the hammer.

The test: does the foot rise (iamb) or fall (trochee)? The iamb is the default English beat (natural, speech-like, diurnal); the trochee is the marked beat (urgent, incantatory, solemn). In IB, the analytical gold is the mixed line: an iambic poem that opens a line or a sentence on a trochee is emphasizing by irregularity — 'DO not / go GENtle' begins on a trochee to shout the command. Name the foot, then name the feeling it produces; the metre is the argument's pulse.

caesura /sɪˈzjʊərə/ noun

A deliberate pause or break within a line of verse (marked by punctuation, syntax, or both) — a breath that shapes sense and pace, controlling where the reader is made to stop.

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] central [caesura] [halts] the line" · "[The] mid-line [pause] [mirrors] the grief" · "[A] broken [caesura] [withholds] the name"

"In Beowulf, the caesura splits each long line into two balances — 'Hwæt! We gardena / in geardagum' — the pause itself a structural beat, the Old English line breathing at its centre." — Beowulf analysis

IB Tip Pause within the line (contrast enjambment, box 3 — run-over between lines). A caesura can be a full stop, a dash, or merely a syntactic break. In IB, the caesura is control of breath and thought: where a poet stops mid-line, the reader stops too — often to mark a turn, a grief, or a withheld name. Link to Day 23's fronting and ellipsis (what is left unsaid).

Common collocations: central caesura · mid-line caesura · marked caesura · syntactic caesura · the line breathes

enjambment /ɪnˈdʒæmbmənt/ noun

The running-over of a sentence or clause from one line (or couplet) to the next without terminal punctuation — syntax that refuses the line-break's pause, accelerating or suspending the sense.

Literary Usage Pattern "[The] enjambed [line] [accelerates] the sense" · "[Syntax] spills [across] the break" · "[Run-on] enjambment [delays] the closure"

"In Wordsworth's 'continuous as the stars that shine / And twinkle on the milky way', the enjambment lets the sentence spill over the line-end, the form mimicking the endless, unpunctuated sweep of the daffodils." — Wordsworth, Daffodils

IB Tip Run-on across the line-break (contrast caesura, box 3 — pause within). Enjambment = speed, surprise, or suspended sense; end-stopped lines (punctuated at the line-end) = closure, control. In IB, the tension between enjambment and end-stopping is a primary analytic: a poet who enjambs at a crucial word delays or destabilises meaning. Link to Day 23's coordination/subordination and Day 22's cohesion.

Common collocations: enjambed line · run-on enjambment · syntactic enjambment · enjambment and caesura · end-stopped vs enjambed

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: caesura vs. enjambment

Both are about the line-break, but the seam is pause within vs. run-over between:

caesura — A pause inside the line (mid-line breath, marked by punctuation or syntax).
→ "The woods are lovely, dark — / and deep." The dash halts you mid-line.

enjambment — A run-over between lines (no pause at the break; the sentence spills across).
→ "The woods are lovely, dark / and deep." The sense refuses to stop at the line-end.

The test: at the line-break, does the poet stop (caesura / end-stop) or carry on (enjambment)? They are the two poles of line-control. In IB, the sophisticated reading tracks the fluctuation: a stanza that enjambs furiously then suddenly end-stops has shifted from flux to finality — often at a death, a decision, or a closure. Pair this with today's metre: a caesura and an enjambment are where meaning meets the grid.

euphony /ˈjuːfəni/ noun

Pleasing, smooth, harmonious sound — the music of consonants and vowels arranged for beauty, ease, or comfort to the ear (and, by its shadow cacophony, the absence that marks rupture).

Literary Usage Pattern "[A] euphonious [line] [soothes] the ear" · "[The] soft [cadence] [lulls]" · "[Liquid] l-sounds [enchant]"

"In Keats's Ode to a Nightingale, the euphony — liquid l's, open vowels, gentle sibilants — is a sonic balm that enacts the poet's desire to flee the world's 'weariness' into song." — Keats analysis

IB Tip Sound that pleases (contrast its shadow, cacophony — harsh, clashing sound, which you should name when a text turns ugly). Euphony is not mere 'nice sound'; in IB it often encodes comfort, seduction, or the lure of art — and its absence marks rupture. Pair with Day 10's ethereal mood (both enchant) and Day 24's lenses (who gets the beautiful sound, and who is denied it?). Root: Greek eu- (good) + phōnē (sound).

Common collocations: euphonious line · liquid euphony · soft euphony · euphony vs cacophony · the music of the line

✏️ Practice — Hear the Argument

Four drills. Write short answers; the model notes follow each.

  1. Name the device. For each line, name the sound/metre term (and the family) that applies:
    a) "The fair breeze blew, the white foam flew" → alliteration (sound-repetition, initial f/b); b) "the silken, sad, uncertain rustling" → sibilance (consonance subset, s-family); c) "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day" → iambic (rising foot, metre); d) "Tyger Tyger burning bright" → trochaic (falling foot, metre); e) "the moan of doves … immemorial elms" → assonance (long o vowel-echo) + near-onomatopoeia.
  2. Pair sound with the Day 10 mood. Take one Day 10 mood word and explain, in one sentence, which sound device would build it:
    Model A sombre tone is built with low, repeated consonance (m, d, l) and end-stopped lines that refuse relief; a febrile atmosphere is built with plosive alliteration (p, t, k) and broken enjambment that will not let the breath settle. Sound is the cause; mood is the effect.
  3. Scan and mark. Scan this line — mark the feet and identify any caesura or enjambment:
    "My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun."
    Model Scansion: "my MIS / tress' EYES / are NO / thing LIKE / the SUN" — predominantly iambic pentameter (five da-DUM), with the opening trochaic substitution ("MY mis") emphasising the possessive. The line is end-stopped (full stop), so no enjambment; a caesura could fall after "eyes" for breath. The steady iambs mimic plain, reasoned speech — fitting a poem that refuses poetic exaggeration.
  4. Distinguish on demand. In one sentence each, keep these Day 25 pairs exam-ready: (i) alliteration vs. assonance (initial consonant vs. internal vowel); (ii) iamb vs. trochee (rising da-DUM vs. falling DUM-da); (iii) caesura vs. enjambment (pause within vs. run-over between lines). Stack them beside the fifteen Week 3 distinctions, the three Day 23 syntax pairs, and the four Day 24 theory seams.

🎯 Study Tips — Sound Is Sense Made Audible

  1. Sound is never decorative — it is the argument. Never write "the poet uses alliteration" without saying why. The repeated plosive enacts panic; the held vowel enacts grief; the broken rhyme enacts a broken world. The examiner rewards the candidate who moves from "I like the rhythm" to "the trochaic opening performs the forge's hammer-stroke." Link every device to an effect (and to a Day 10 mood).
  2. Keep the three repetition families straight by position. alliteration = initial consonant; assonance = vowel anywhere; consonance = consonant anywhere (with sibilance as its s-subset). They overlap constantly, but naming the precise layer — "alliterative p-pound and assonant long-o" — is what separates band 5 from band 7.
  3. Metre is the grid; rhythm is the voice. Metre is the underlying pattern (iambic pentameter); rhythm is what the actual voice does to it. The IB payoff is disruption: the line that breaks its own metre is the line where the world breaks. Always scan the default, then hunt for the exception.
  4. The iamb/trochee opposition is rising vs. falling affect. Iamb = natural, speech-like, diurnal ease; trochee = urgent, incantatory, solemn force. A poem that is mostly iambic but opens a line on a trochee is emphasising by irregularity. Name the foot, then the feeling — metre is never neutral.
  5. Caesura vs. enjambment = the line's breath-control. Pause within (caesura / end-stop) vs. run-over between (enjambment). Track the fluctuation across a stanza: furious enjambment that suddenly end-stops has shifted from flux to finality — often at a death or a closure. This is where meaning meets the grid.
  6. Euphony and its shadow name comfort vs. rupture. Euphony encodes beauty, seduction, or the lure of art; cacophony (its harsh twin) marks fracture. And bring Day 24's lenses: ask who is granted the beautiful sound and who is denied it — sound analysis becomes cultural critique the moment you notice the distribution.
