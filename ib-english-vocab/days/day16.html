🔁 Day 15 First Review — 12 Core Device Terms

Your first spaced pass on yesterday's device toolkit. Read each word, recall its precise definition before looking, then check. These twelve are the foundation everything else this week builds on — keep them instantly available.

metaphor
A figure asserting one thing IS another — implicit comparison, no "like/as".
simile
A comparison using "like/as" — explicit, hedged.
personification
Human traits, actions, or feelings given to non-human things.
symbolism
Objects, colours, or actions standing for deeper, abstract meaning.
irony
Gap between appearance and reality — verbal, situational, or dramatic.
motif
A recurring element that gains meaning through repetition.
juxtaposition
Placing things side by side for contrast or comparison.
hyperbole
Deliberate exaggeration for emphasis, effect, or humour.
allusion
An indirect reference to another text, myth, or event.
metonymy
Substitution by association — the crown = the monarchy.
oxymoron
Two contradictory words yoked in one phrase ("bitter-sweet").
anaphora
Repetition at the START of successive clauses ("I have a dream…").
Self-assessment: Could you give a one-line definition of all twelve without peeking? The three Day 15 distinction pairs (metaphor/simile, verbal/situational irony, metonymy/metaphor) are the ones most likely to blur — revisit the Day 15 document's boxes if any felt shaky. Today's distinction boxes all reach back to these words (synecdoche builds on metonymy, antithesis on juxtaposition, epistrophe on anaphora), so solid recall here makes Day 16 effortless.

paradox /ˈpærədɒks/ noun

A statement or proposition that, despite apparently sound reasoning from acceptable premises, leads to a conclusion that seems self-contradictory or logically unacceptable — yet may reveal a deeper truth.

Literary Usage Pattern "[Less is] [more]" · "[the only constant is] [change]" · "[War is] [Peace]"

"In Orwell's 1984, 'War is Peace. Freedom is Slavery. Ignorance is Strength' — the Party's slogans are paradoxes that invert truth to control thought, each contradiction a lever on the mind." — 1984 analysis

IB Tip Distinct from oxymoron (Day 15): a paradox is a contradictory idea or statement (it can span a sentence or a whole text); an oxymoron is two contradictory words in one phrase ("bitter-sweet"). Both signal a truth logic alone can't hold. Paradoxes are the spine of much unseen IB poetry — name the paradox, then show how it resolves. Connect to Day 5's ambivalent: a paradox often names an ambivalent response in a single line.

Common collocations: apparent paradox · central paradox · resolve the paradox · rhetorical paradox

synecdoche /sɪˈnekdəki/ noun

A figure of speech in which a part is made to represent the whole, or the whole to represent a part (e.g., "all hands on deck" = sailors; "fifty sails" = ships).

Literary Usage Pattern "[fifty sails] [in the harbour]" (sails = ships) · "[give us] [our daily bread]"

"In Hamlet, 'forty thousand brothers / Could not, with all their quantity of love, / Make up my sum' — 'brothers' stands for the love they could give, a synecdoche of kin for feeling." — Shakespeare analysis

IB Tip Distinct from metonymy (Day 15) — see the box below. Synecdoche = a part/whole relation (a piece stands for the totality); metonymy = association (the crown for the king). Both condense meaning; synecdoche is about scale, metonymy about adjacency. Confusing them is a classic IB slip — name the exact one. Connect to Day 1's foreground: a synecdoche foregrounds one telling detail of a larger whole.

Common collocations: synecdoche and metonymy · use of synecdoche

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: synecdoche vs. metonymy

Both substitute one term for another, but on different grounds (metonymy is from Day 15):

synecdoche — A part/whole relation: a piece stands for the totality (or vice versa). "All hands on deck" (hands = sailors); "fifty sails" (sails = ships).
→ The part literally belongs to the whole.

metonymy — Substitution by association / contiguity: the crown = the monarchy; the pen = writing. The two things sit next to each other in the real world, but one is not part of the other.
→ "The crown declared war."

The test: is the substituted thing a physical part of the whole (synecdoche) or merely associated with it (metonymy)? "Hands on deck" = synecdoche (hands are part of sailors); "the crown spoke" = metonymy (the crown is not part of the monarchy, only its symbol). Some cases overlap (a "roof" over a family = both), but in IB essays the distinction is worth naming — it shows you see how a text condenses the world. This pair anchors your Day 16 toolkit to Day 15.

imagery /ˈɪmɪdʒəri/ noun

Visually descriptive or figurative language that appeals to the senses (sight, sound, touch, taste, smell); the pattern of images in a text.

Literary Usage Pattern "[vivid] imagery" · "[the] [nature imagery] [recurs throughout]"

"In Ted Hughes's 'Hawk Roosting,' the imagery of 'the earth's face' and 'the air's buoyancy' renders the hawk's dominion through tactile, bodily detail — you feel the perch, not just see it." — Hughes analysis

IB Tip Go beyond "pictures in the mind." Imagery is multisensory — name which sense is invoked (aural, gustatory, kinesthetic, olfactory) and to what effect. Imagery is the raw material that symbols and metaphors are built from (link Day 15). Connect to Day 12's texture adjectives (luminous, palpable, evocative) — those words describe imagery's quality. A commentary weak on imagery is weak everywhere; trace a text's dominant image-cluster and you've found its theme.

Common collocations: vivid imagery · sensory imagery · nature imagery · recurring imagery · use of imagery

allegory /ˈæləgəri/ noun

A narrative in which characters, events, and settings stand for abstract ideas or moral qualities, operating simultaneously on a literal and a symbolic level.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [political allegory]" · "[the novel] [functions as] [an allegory of]…"

"In Golding's Lord of the Flies, the desert-island tale is an allegory of civilisation's fragility and the innate capacity for savagery — the boys figure ideas larger than themselves." — Golding analysis

IB Tip Revisited from Day 5's allegorical (you knew the adjective; now name the whole form). An allegory is sustained symbolism across an entire text — contrast Day 15's symbolism, which can be local. Ask what each character/event figures, and whether the allegory holds or breaks down by the end. Connect to Day 5's allegorical and Day 15's symbolism / motif. A text "is an allegory of X" — name X precisely, and watch for where the author lets the allegory slip.

Common collocations: political allegory · moral allegory · allegorical reading · function as allegory

antithesis /ænˈtɪθəsɪs/ noun

A figure of speech in which contrasting ideas are juxtaposed in balanced, parallel structure (e.g., "speech is silver, silence is golden"); also the direct opposite of something.

Literary Usage Pattern "[the] [antithesis] of X and Y" · "[thesis] [and] [antithesis]"

"In Dickens's 'It was the best of times, it was the worst of times,' antithesis opens A Tale of Two Cities by yoking opposites in parallel clauses — the world framed as a balance about to tip." — Dickens analysis

IB Tip Distinct from juxtaposition (Day 15) — see the box below. Antithesis is contrast expressed in balanced, parallel wording (a rhetorical structure); juxtaposition is the broader act of placing things side by side (visual, structural, or conceptual). Antithesis often carries the "rule of two." Connect to Day 5's reductive (antithesis resists reductive either/or by holding both) and Day 15's juxtaposition. In speeches, antithesis is the engine of memorable argument.

Common collocations: stark antithesis · sharp antithesis · antithesis of · rhetorical antithesis

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: antithesis vs. juxtaposition

Both involve contrast, but at different levels (juxtaposition is from Day 15):

antithesis — Contrast in balanced, parallel wording: "speech is silver, silence is golden." The opposition is built into the sentence's symmetry — a rhetorical structure.
→ "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times."

juxtaposition — The broader act of placing things side by side for comparison or contrast — can be visual, structural, or conceptual, not necessarily parallel in wording.
→ A rich banquet framed beside a beggar's hunger (Day 15 example).

The test: is the contrast wired into parallel phrasing (antithesis) or merely arranged side by side (juxtaposition)? Every antithesis is a kind of juxtaposition, but not every juxtaposition is an antithesis. In essays, name antithesis when the symmetry itself is doing work (it signals balance, tension, or a held contradiction); use juxtaposition for the wider placement of scenes, images, or texts — especially in Paper 2 comparative work. This pair ties Day 16 back to Day 15.

epistrophe /ɪˈpɪstrəfi/ noun

The repetition of a word or phrase at the end of successive clauses or sentences — the mirror image of anaphora.

Literary Usage Pattern "[…of the people, …by the people, …for the people]"

"In Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, 'that government of the people, by the people, for the people' closes with epistrophe that rings each clause to a settled, resonant end." — Lincoln analysis

IB Tip The twin of Day 15's anaphora (repetition at the start). Name which end the repetition sits at — the difference is structural and marks different rhythms: anaphora builds upward (rising urgency); epistrophe lands downward (finality, emphasis). Both are gold for non-literary texts (Paper 1, Section 2). See the distinction box below (epistrophe vs. anaphora) — this pair is the third that links Day 16 back to Day 15.

Common collocations: use of epistrophe · rhetorical epistrophe

⚠️ Crucial Distinction: epistrophe vs. anaphora

Both are repetition schemes; the only difference is where the repeated unit sits (anaphora is from Day 15):

anaphora — Repetition at the beginning of successive clauses: "I have a dream… I have a dream… I have a dream." Builds upward, accumulating momentum and rising urgency.
→ MLK's "I Have a Dream."

epistrophe — Repetition at the end of successive clauses: "of the people, by the people, for the people." Lands downward, sealing a thought with finality and emphasis.
→ Lincoln's Gettysburg Address.

The test: does the echo return at the start (anaphora) or the end (epistrophe)? Same device family, opposite rhythm — anaphora opens each clause like a wave; epistrophe closes each like a stamp. In IB, naming the position tells the examiner you hear the speech's architecture. A single text can use both (anaphora to rise, epistrophe to conclude). Master this pair and your rhetoric analysis of oratory and manifestos is complete.

apostrophe /əˈpɒstrəfi/ noun

A rhetorical figure in which the speaker addresses a dead, absent, or imaginary person, or an abstraction or personified object, as if present and able to reply.

Literary Usage Pattern "[O] [Death, where is thy sting?]" · "[Twinkle, twinkle, little star]"

"In John Donne's 'Death, be not proud,' the poet addresses Death directly as a person — apostrophe that dares mortality to answer back." — Donne analysis

IB Tip Not the punctuation mark! Apostrophe is a direct address to the absent or abstract. It reveals a speaker's emotional stance and often signals heightened feeling or a turn outward to the reader. Connect to Day 1's evoke and Day 15's personification (apostrophe frequently personifies its addressee). Vital for dramatic-monologue and lyric-poetry analysis. Name it precisely — it is one of the most misidentified devices in student essays.

Common collocations: use of apostrophe · apostrophic address · direct apostrophe

alliteration /əˌlɪtəˈreɪʃn/ noun

The repetition of initial consonant sounds in closely connected words, used for rhythm, mood, or emphasis.

Literary Usage Pattern "[peter piper] [picked]" · "[the] [silken, sad, uncertain]"

"In Poe's 'The Raven,' 'doubting, dreaming dreams no mortal ever dared to dream before' — alliteration on /d/ drums the obsession into sound." — Poe analysis

IB Tip A sound device, not a sense device. Always ask what the repeated sound does: harsh /k/ = menace; soft /s/ = hush; liquid /l/ = flow. Connect to Day 11's tone — alliteration shapes tone at the phonetic level. Distinct from assonance (vowel repetition) and from rhyme. In IB, never note alliteration without naming its effect on pace or mood. Pair it with Day 12's texture adjectives (jarring, scintillating) to describe what the sound feels like.

Common collocations: use of alliteration · initial alliteration · heavy alliteration

euphemism /ˈjuːfəmɪzəm/ noun

A mild or indirect word or expression substituted for one considered too harsh, blunt, or offensive to state directly.

Literary Usage Pattern "[passed away] [for died]" · "[let go] [for fired]"

"In Orwell's 1984, the Ministry of 'Love' (torture) and 'Peace' (war) are state euphemisms that invert atrocity into benign-sounding language." — Orwell analysis

IB Tip The noun behind Day 11's euphemistic — you already know the adjective. Euphemism is a power device: who gets to soften language, and why? Connect to Day 5's obfuscate (euphemism obscures) and Day 15's irony (euphemism can be ironically loaded). Essential for non-literary / advertising analysis (Paper 1, Section 2): spot the euphemism and name what it hides. A text that "euphemises" X is making a political choice about X.

Common collocations: use of euphemism · bureaucratic euphemism · clinical euphemism

pun /pʌn/ noun

A play on words exploiting the similarity of sound between two words with different meanings, or different senses of the same word.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [grave] [situation]" (grave = serious / burial) · "[time] [flies]"

"In Romeo and Juliet, 'Ask for me tomorrow and you shall find me a grave man' — Mercutio's dying pun on 'grave' (dead / serious) turns mortality into wordplay at the threshold of death." — Shakespeare analysis

IB Tip Often dismissed as a mere joke, but puns do serious work: they compress two meanings, signal wit or mental disintegration (see Day 5's subversive), or mark a character's state of mind. Connect to Day 5's satirical / playful and Day 15's wordplay family. In IB, a pun is evidence — name both senses and explain why the collision matters. Don't skip it as "just a joke"; a well-read pun is a constructed ambiguity.

Common collocations: make a pun · double pun · fond of puns · pun on

satire /səˈtaɪə(r)/ noun

A genre or mode that uses humour, irony, exaggeration, or ridicule to expose and criticise vice, folly, or stupidity — often with a reforming aim.

Literary Usage Pattern "[a] [sharp satire] [on]…" · "[the novel] [is a satire of]…"

"In Swift's A Modest Proposal, the speaker's calm recommendation to eat Irish infants is satire that weaponises irony to flay indifference to poverty." — Swift analysis

IB Tip The mode behind Day 5's satirical — now name the whole form. Satire = the text's stance; irony and hyperbole (Day 15) are its tools. Ask: what is being attacked, and what remedy is implied? Connect to Day 5's satirical / subversive and Day 15's irony / hyperbole. A text "is a satire of X" — name X. Distinct from parody (which mimics a style) and from comedy (broader); satire always bites toward a target.

Common collocations: savage satire · political satire · satire on · biting satire

tricolon /ˈtraɪkəlɒn/ noun

A series of three parallel words, phrases, or clauses — the "rule of three" — used for balance, rhythm, and rhetorical force.

Literary Usage Pattern "[veni, vidi, vici]" (I came, I saw, I conquered) · "[liberty, equality, fraternity]"

"In Churchill's 'blood, toil, tears and sweat' — the tricolon (here a four-part variation) builds accumulating resolve clause by clause, each unit darker than the last." — Churchill analysis

IB Tip The rhythm of persuasion. Three is the smallest series that establishes a pattern and feels complete — hence the "rule of three" in speeches, slogans, and proverbs. Connect to Day 15's anaphora / epistrophe (a tricolon can carry them) and Day 5's polemical. Name it in oratory and manifestos (Paper 1, Section 2): the tricolon signals climax and memorability. A high-yield, low-effort term for any rhetoric analysis — spot it and you've found the speech's beat.

Common collocations: use of tricolon · rhetorical tricolon · rule of three

🎯 Building Your Device Toolkit

  1. You now command a two-set device vocabulary. Day 15 gave you the core (metaphor, irony, symbolism, anaphora); Day 16 adds the adjacent terms — contrast (paradox, antithesis), part & sense (synecdoche, imagery), whole-text modes (allegory, satire), and sound/structure schemes (alliteration, epistrophe, tricolon, apostrophe, euphemism, pun). Together they span figurative language, interpretive concepts, and rhetoric. By Day 21 this is a complete, nameable toolkit.
  2. Three distinction pairs now link Day 16 to Day 15. synecdoche/metonymy (part-whole vs association), antithesis/juxtaposition (parallel wording vs side-by-side placement), epistrophe/anaphora (end vs start repetition). These six device-names form a tightly connected cluster — master the pairings and your device analysis is airtight across both days.
  3. Revisit, don't relearn. allegory, satire, and euphemism are the nouns behind adjectives you already carry from Day 5 (allegorical, satirical) and Day 11 (euphemistic). Naming the noun lets you discuss the whole device, not just its flavour — and it ties your evaluative vocabulary to your technical vocabulary.
  4. Sound devices need an effect, not just a label. alliteration (and, soon, assonance, rhyme) describe how a line sounds. In IB, "there is alliteration" earns nothing; "the repeated /s/ hushes the line into secrecy" earns the mark. Always pair the device with its phonetic effect (link Day 12's jarring / scintillating / muted).
  5. Rhetoric belongs to non-literary texts too. epistrophe, tricolon, euphemism, and satire are everywhere in speeches, adverts, and op-eds (Paper 1, Section 2). This week's device set works double duty: literary and non-literary. Spot three of today's terms in the news this week and you'll be ready for any text type.
  6. Deploy these in your next commentary. When you next write on a set text, force yourself to name at least four Day 15-16 devices precisely and trace one image-cluster or rhetorical scheme across the passage. By Paper 2, the right term should arrive without thinking — that's technical precision.
