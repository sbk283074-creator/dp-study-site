⚖️ The 12 Distinction Pairs (Your Week 3 Exam Armour)

IB markers reward precise discrimination. These are the pairs most often confused — know the exact seam between each.

① metaphor vs. simile

metaphor — asserts identity: "the city is a jungle" (no "like/as").
simile — asserts resemblance: "the city is like a jungle" (with "like/as").
Test: is the comparison stated as fact or signposted as similarity? Metaphors are stronger and riskier; similes are safer and more measured.

② irony vs. satire

irony — a device: a gap between saying and meaning (verbal, situational, dramatic).
satire — a mode / genre: uses irony + wit to expose vice or folly.
One is a single technique; the other is a whole posture. A text can be ironic without being satirical; satire almost always deploys irony.

③ symbolism vs. allegory

symbolism — a single object carries a deeper meaning within an otherwise realistic text.
allegory — the ENTIRE narrative is a sustained second-level system (characters = ideas).
Scale is the seam: one symbol vs. a whole symbolic world. Animal Farm is allegory; the green light in Gatsby is symbolism.

④ anaphora vs. epistrophe

anaphora — repetition at the START of units ("I have a dream… I have a dream…").
epistrophe — repetition at the END of units ("…of the people, by the people, for the people").
Same device, opposite position. Anaphora builds mounting urgency; epistrophe lands a hammering close.

⑤ anaphora vs. anadiplosis

anaphora — the same word repeats at the start of each unit, independently.
anadiplosis — the LAST word of one clause becomes the FIRST word of the next ("the crime was blood; blood demands blood").
Anaphora = parallel opens; anadiplosis = a chain linking end to beginning. The latter creates causal or escalating linkage.

⑥ synecdoche vs. metonymy

synecdoche — a PART stands for the WHOLE (or whole for part): "all hands on deck".
metonymy — a related term substitutes for the thing: "the crown declared war".
Synecdoche is a subset of metonymy based on part/whole; metonymy is broader association (not necessarily part/whole).

⑦ asyndeton vs. polysyndeton

asyndeton — conjunctions OMITTED ("I came, I saw, I conquered") → speed, staccato, urgency.
polysyndeton — conjunctions PILED ON ("and… and… and…") → slowness, accumulation, weight.
Opposite manipulations of the same connective. Name which one to explain the sentence's rhythm.

⑧ chiasmus vs. antimetabole

chiasmus — CONCEPTS/structures mirrored in reverse (A–B–B–A), not identical words.
antimetabole — the SAME WORDS repeated in reversed order ("when the going gets tough, the tough get going").
Antimetabole is the stricter, word-for-word form; chiasmus can swap synonyms. Both produce symmetry and closure.

⑨ parataxis vs. hypotaxis

parataxis — equal clauses joined by no subordinator ("he left. she stayed") → blunt, fast,并列.
hypotaxis — clauses nested by subordinators ("she stayed because he left") → nuanced, causal, hierarchical.
Clause relation is the seam: equal vs. nested. Parataxis withholds cause; hypotaxis supplies it.

⑩ connotation vs. denotation

denotation — the literal dictionary meaning.
connotation — the feelings / associations a word carries beyond that.
"Home" and "house" share a denotation; their connotations diverge entirely. Word-choice analysis lives in connotation.

⑪ subtext vs. inference

subtext — the unspoken meaning that resides IN the text (the author's implied layer).
inference — the conclusion the READER draws from that evidence.
Subtext is in the work; inference is in the reader. You infer the subtext. Don't confuse the two in an essay.

⑫ hedge vs. equivocation

hedge — an honest softener that modestly weakens a claim ("perhaps", "seems").
equivocation — strategic ambiguity that dodges taking a position at all.
Both reduce force, but hedge is fair caution; equivocation is evasive manoeuvring. Watch the speaker's intent.

🎯 Building Your Device Toolkit

  1. Week 3 is the metalanguage spine of the whole programme. Tropes (15–16) tell you what words mean; structure (17) tells you how the story is built; tone (18) tells you how it feels; schemes (19) tell you how sentences are shaped. Together they are the vocabulary for a complete Paper 1 commentary and a precise Paper 2 essay.
  2. Regroup, don't re-list. Today you met the same sixty words ordered by function. That interleaving is the difference between memorising five lists and owning one toolkit. When you analyse a real passage, reach for one term from each pillar rather than twelve from one.
  3. The twelve distinction pairs are where marks are won or lost. Saying "this is a metaphor" gets you the identification; saying "this is a metaphor, not a simile, because it asserts identity and so risks more" gets you the top band. Precision of discrimination is the IB differentiator.
  4. Tone and schemes are now linked. Asyndeton accelerates; polysyndeton accumulates; parataxis withholds cause; hypotaxis supplies nuance (Day 19) — and all of them are the grammar of rhythm, which is half of tone (Day 18). Name a scheme and you can explain a tempo; name a tempo and you can explain a tone.
  5. Structure terms let you discuss the whole before the sentence. Before you zoom into a scheme, use foreshadowing / frame narrative / non-linear narrative / unreliable narrator to orient the reader of your essay to the text's architecture. Top commentaries move between macro (structure) and micro (scheme) deliberately.
  6. Deploy these on a real text this week. Take any set-text paragraph and force yourself to name: one trope, one structural feature, one tone term, and one scheme — then show how they reinforce each other. By Day 21's assessment you'll have a complete, connected Week 3 toolkit ready to deploy under exam conditions.
