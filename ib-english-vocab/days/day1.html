📖 Today's Words — 12 New Terms

Category: Core Analysis Verbs (10) + Foundational Evaluative Adjectives (2)
These are the words you'll use constantly in IB essays to describe what authors do and how texts work. Master these first — they appear in almost every analytical paragraph.

subvert /səbˈvɜːt/ verb

To undermine, overturn, or deliberately undermine the authority, power, or established conventions of something.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author subverts [convention/expectation/stereotype] by [specific technique]…"

"Atwood subverts the conventions of the dystopian genre by framing the narrative through an unreliable, fragmented retrospective account." — The Handmaid's Tale analysis

IB Tip Use this when an author deliberately breaks or challenges a genre convention, social norm, or reader expectation. Don't use it for simple disagreement — it implies a systematic overturning.

Common collocations: subvert expectations · subvert conventions · subvert authority · subvert stereotypes · subvert the narrative

juxtapose /ˈdʒʌkstəpəʊz/ verb

To place two things side by side to highlight the contrast or relationship between them.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author juxtaposes [X] with [Y] to [reveal/highlight/emphasise]…"

"Fitzgerald juxtaposes the glittering opulence of West Egg with the desolate grey of the Valley of Ashes to expose the moral emptiness beneath the American Dream." — The Great Gatsby analysis

IB Tip This is one of the most useful verbs in literary analysis. Always state what is juxtaposed and why — the effect of the contrast is the analytical insight.

Common collocations: juxtapose A with B · juxtapose imagery · juxtapose scenes · juxtaposition (n.)

foreground /ˈfɔːɡraʊnd/ verb

To bring to prominence; to make something the central focus or most noticeable element.

Literary Usage Pattern "The narrative foregrounds [theme/idea/issue] through [technique]…"

"Miller foregrounds the theme of mass hysteria through the escalating stage directions, which physically crowd the courtroom scene." — The Crucible analysis

IB Tip "Foreground" is more precise than "show" or "highlight." It implies the author deliberately makes something central — use it when a theme or idea is given unusual prominence.

Common collocations: foreground a theme · foreground an issue · foreground the protagonist's · foreground the contrast

undermine /ˌʌndəˈmaɪn/ verb

To gradually weaken, damage, or erode the confidence, authority, or effectiveness of something.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [technique] undermines [the reader's confidence / the protagonist's authority / the assumption that]…"

"Iago's whispered asides undermine the audience's trust in Othello's perception, creating dramatic irony that intensifies the tragedy." — Othello analysis

IB Tip "Undermine" implies a gradual process, not a sudden destruction. Perfect for describing how irony, unreliability, or conflicting perspectives erode certainty.

Common collocations: undermine confidence · undermine authority · undermine the premise · undermine certainty

evoke /ɪˈvəʊk/ verb

To bring a feeling, memory, or image to mind; to elicit an emotional response from the reader.

Literary Usage Pattern "The imagery evokes a sense of [emotion/atmosphere]…"

"The opening lines of Eliot's 'The Waste Land' — 'April is the cruellest month' — evoke a pervasive sense of spiritual decay and disillusionment." — T.S. Eliot, poetry analysis

IB Tip "Evoke" is about emotional resonance, not explicit statement. Use it when imagery, sound, or atmosphere implies a feeling rather than naming it directly. Distinguish from "invoke" (to call upon).

Common collocations: evoke nostalgia · evoke sympathy · evoke a sense of · evoke an atmosphere · evocative (adj.)

elucidate /ɪˈluːsɪdeɪt/ verb

To make something clear; to explain or shed light on a complex idea or relationship.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [metaphor/symbol/scene] elucidates the relationship between [X and Y]…"

"The extended metaphor of the conch elucidates the fragility of civilised order when stripped of institutional authority." — Lord of the Flies analysis

IB Tip More sophisticated than "explain" or "show." Use it when a literary device makes an abstract concept concrete and understandable. Reserve for genuine clarification, not mere illustration.

Common collocations: elucidate a theme · elucidate the relationship · elucidate a concept · elucidation (n.)

problematize /ˈprɒbləmətaɪz/ verb

To present something as problematic; to complicate or question what was previously taken for granted.

Literary Usage Pattern "The novel problematizes the notion of [idea/concept] by [technique]…"

"Achebe problematizes the colonial narrative of 'civilising' Africa by centring the Igbo perspective and exposing the violence beneath the imperial project." — Things Fall Apart analysis

IB Tip A high-level analytical verb. Use it when a text doesn't simply reject an idea but shows it to be more complex, contradictory, or unstable than it first appears. Common in Paper 2 comparative essays.

Common collocations: problematize the notion · problematize identity · problematize assumptions · problematize the binary

conflate /kənˈfleɪt/ verb

To merge two distinct things into one; to combine elements that should remain separate.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author conflates [X] and [Y], suggesting that [insight]…"

"Orwell conflates surveillance and intimacy in 1984, where the telescreen watches not only actions but thoughts — love itself becomes a form of rebellion." — 1984 analysis

IB Tip Use this when a text deliberately blurs the boundary between two concepts (e.g., love and power, memory and identity). It's an analytical observation about how the text merges categories.

Common collocations: conflate X with Y · conflate personal and political · conflate memory and identity · conflation (n.)

obfuscate /ˈɒbfʌskeɪt/ verb

To deliberately make something unclear, obscure, or difficult to understand.

Literary Usage Pattern "The [narrative structure/unreliable narrator/ambiguity] obfuscates [the truth/motivation]…"

"The fragmented, non-linear chronology obfuscates the causal relationship between the narrator's trauma and her present reality, forcing the reader to reconstruct meaning." — Generic narrative analysis

IB Tip Implies deliberate obscuring, not accidental complexity. Use it when the text's difficulty is itself meaningful — a technique, not a flaw.

Common collocations: obfuscate the truth · obfuscate motive · obfuscate meaning · obfuscation (n.)

interrogate /ɪnˈterəɡeɪt/ verb

In literary analysis: to examine closely, question rigorously, or subject something to critical scrutiny. (Not the police-interview meaning.)

Literary Usage Pattern "The text interrogates [assumption/idea/social construct] by [technique]…"

"The play interrogates the nature of justice in a society where the legal system itself is corrupted by fear and mass hysteria." — The Crucible analysis

IB Tip A powerful analytical verb. "Interrogate" implies the text doesn't just show something — it subjects it to sustained critical questioning. Pairs well with concepts like "identity," "power," "justice," "truth."

Common collocations: interrogate assumptions · interrogate identity · interrogate power structures · interrogate the notion

nuanced /ˈnjuːɑːnst/ adjective

Characterised by subtle distinctions or fine gradations; not simplistic or black-and-white.

Literary Usage Pattern "The author's nuanced portrayal of [character/theme] avoids simplistic [binaries/judgements]…"

"Characterisation in the novel is nuanced — the protagonist is neither hero nor villain, but a figure whose moral compromises accumulate with devastating inevitability." — Generic novel analysis

IB Tip "Nuanced" is one of the highest-value adjectives in IB English. Examiners reward students who recognise complexity rather than reducing texts to simple messages. Use it to signal that you see layers.

Common collocations: nuanced portrayal · nuanced understanding · nuanced approach · nuanced perspective · nuance (n.)

didactic /daɪˈdæktɪk/ adjective

Intended to teach or instruct, often with a moral lesson; sometimes implies being overly preachy or moralising.

Literary Usage Pattern "The tone is didactic, suggesting [moral lesson / ideological stance]…"

"The fable's didactic purpose is evident in its concluding moral, yet the narrative's ambiguity complicates any single reading of its message." — Generic fable analysis

IB Tip Can be neutral (a text that teaches) or critical (a text that preaches too much). In IB, often used to identify when an author's moral agenda shapes the narrative. Ask: Does the text teach, or does it question?

Common collocations: didactic tone · didactic purpose · didactic message · didactic literature · didacticism (n.)

🎯 How to Use These Words in IB Essays

  1. Use analysis verbs in your topic sentences. Instead of "The author shows that…", try "The author juxtaposes X with Y to foreground…" This immediately signals analytical thinking to the examiner.
  2. Always link technique → purpose → effect. Don't just name what the author does. Write: "By [technique], the author [analytical verb] in order to [purpose], which [effect on reader]." This three-part chain is the backbone of IB literary analysis.
  3. Vary your analytical verbs. If every sentence uses "shows" or "highlights," your essay sounds repetitive. Today's 10 verbs give you alternatives that carry more precise meanings.
  4. Use evaluative adjectives to show nuance. Examiners reward students who recognise complexity. "The portrayal is nuanced" signals that you see layers — not just a simple message. This is worth marks in Criterion C (Organization) and Criterion D (Language).
  5. Don't force sophisticated vocabulary. Use these words where they fit. A precisely used "juxtapose" is worth more than five misused "obfuscates." Quality over quantity — always.
  6. Build a personal word bank. Keep a running list of analytical verbs and evaluative adjectives you encounter in model essays, critical commentary, or your teacher's feedback. Add to it weekly.

📚 Vocabulary & Literary Reading

Your vocabulary gap isn't just about essay-writing — it directly affects your reading comprehension of literary texts. Here's how to close the gap:

1. Active reading with a vocabulary journal. When you encounter an unfamiliar word in a set text, don't skip it. Record it with: the sentence it appeared in, your inferred meaning, the dictionary definition, and one sentence of your own using the word.

2. Read criticism alongside the text. Find one literary essay or review about your set text per week. Academic criticism models the exact vocabulary you need — and shows you how experienced critics use words like "problematize," "foreground," and "interrogate" in context.

3. Pay attention to authorial word choice. When an author uses an unusual word, ask: Why this word and not a simpler one? The gap between ordinary language and literary language is where meaning lives. Your growing analytical vocabulary helps you name what the author is doing and why it matters.

4. Read beyond your set texts. Read 15 minutes of quality English prose daily — novels, long-form journalism (The Guardian, The Atlantic), or literary essays. Exposure to sophisticated prose in context is how vocabulary moves from "recognised" to "owned."
