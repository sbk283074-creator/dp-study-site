The analysis toolkit & self-editing pass

The other pages teach the parts — the devices, the vocabulary, the question shapes, the model paragraphs. 1. The close-reading routine — before you write a word

Most weak analysis is lost in the reading, not the writing. A reader who misreads the situation, the genre or the speaker's stance then writes a fluent analysis of the wrong thing. The fix is a deliberate, repeatable reading routine. Do not skip it because you are in a hurry; the minutes you spend here are the highest-yield minutes you have.

1.1 The six passes

PassQuestion you answerWhat you mark or note
1 · Whole readWhat is this text, in plain terms?Genre, medium, situation, audience, purpose. One sentence.
2 · Stance readWho is speaking, to whom, with what attitude?Speaker / addressee / tone. The single most-missed feature.
3 · Feature readWhat specific choices does the text make?Underline / highlight concrete choices: words, images, structure, design.
4 · Pattern readWhich choices cluster or repeat?Group the marks into 3–4 thematic clusters (not a flat list).
5 · Meaning readWhat does it add up to?A one-line central claim the clusters support.
6 · Task readGiven the question / framing, what do I argue?The specific thesis or thread for this response.
The 3-minute first-read checklist

If you only have time for one discipline, make it this. On the first full read, force yourself to answer four questions: (a) What is the text for — to inform, persuade, satirise, mourn, sell? (b) Who is it addressed to? (c) What is the speaker's stance — complicit, detached, outraged, playful? (d) What is the single biggest structural or design move? A wrong answer to (a)–(d) quietly sinks the whole response; thirty seconds each is cheap insurance. (Under timed exam conditions, the course page on Paper 1 gives a windowed version of this routine.)

1.2 Why "clusters" beat "a list of devices"

Pass 4 is where weak and strong responses diverge. The weak reader makes a flat inventory — "there is a metaphor, and alliteration, and a short sentence, and a shift in tense" — and analyses each in isolation. The strong reader notices that the metaphors, the sentence lengths and the tense shift all serve one effect (say, the collapse of control) and groups them. Always argue the cluster, not the isolated device.

2. PEEL / TEAL as a logic check, not a sentence template

You will meet PEEL (Point · Evidence · Explanation · Link) or TEAL (Technique · Evidence · Analysis · Link) in many classrooms. Used well, it is a logic check: a way to confirm a paragraph actually argues rather than merely describes. Used badly, it becomes a straitjacket that produces the mechanical, formulaic writing good teachers warn against. This section shows you the difference.

2.1 The five analytical moves, mapped to the letters

LetterThe move it enforcesThe question it answers
P — PointState the claim this paragraph makes.What am I arguing in this paragraph?
E — EvidenceGround it in a concrete feature or quotation.What in the text proves the claim?
E — Explanation / A — AnalysisName the technique, explain how it works, state the effect.How does the choice produce that effect on the reader?
L — LinkTie back to the thesis, the question, or the next idea.So what — why does this matter to my whole argument?
(T — Technique)In TEAL, name the device explicitly up front.What specific technique am I analysing?
Trap — the template that kills the writing

PEEL is a check, not an opening formula. Writing that announces its own mechanics — "In this paragraph I will analyse the use of metaphor, which shows…" — or pads each paragraph with a ritual "This links to my point that…" reads as immature and wastes words. Write the paragraph so the point, evidence, analysis and link are present and coherent; do not perform the acronym. If a sentence exists only to display a letter, cut it. 2.2 A worked paragraph, annotated

The micro-extract below is an illustrative teaching construct, not a line from any set text or past paper. It exists only to show the logic of one paragraph.

"The door would not open. She counted the seconds — one, two, three — and the silence behind it grew teeth. Outside, the city kept its ordinary noise, indifferent as weather."

SegmentWhat it does (the letter)
"The door would not open" — the flat, curt opening denies the reader any context, dropping us mid-crisis rather than preparing us for it.P + E: the point (we are thrown into crisis) is made through the evidence, not stated separately.
The personification "the silence grew teeth" turns an absence into a threat…A (technique named): the device is identified.
…so that the character's fear is externalised as something actively hostile, and the reader feels confinement rather than merely being told of it.A (effect): the how and the effect on the reader are stated.
The final clause, "indifferent as weather," widens the frame: the city's indifference contrasts the intimate terror of the doorway, and the ordinary world continues unpriced by her panic.L: ties the local choice to the larger claim (isolation within an indifferent world) the whole response will develop.

Notice what is not there: no "I will now analyse," no separate "This links to my point." The logic is present; the scaffolding is invisible. That is the target.

3. The comparison method — hold two texts in one paragraph

Any comparison fails when it becomes two essays. The method is to plan on a grid so that every paragraph carries both works against a shared axis, plus the insight the contrast produces.

3.1 The four-column grid

Axis of comparisonText 1Text 2So what? (the insight)
How each presents isolation through settingConfinement is physical and claustrophobic.Confinement is psychological and diffuse.Both equate isolation with a loss of agency, but locate it differently — body vs mind.
How each uses a framing narrator………
How each resolves (or refuses) the central tension………

Use Text 1 and Text 2 as your real labels; the grid is transferable and is not keyed to specific works. Each row becomes one comparative paragraph. The fourth column — the "so what" — is the part weak plans omit: a comparison that only says "Text 1 does X, Text 2 does Y" describes; a comparison that says "and the difference reveals…" argues. For the pair-specific version (which pair answers which question), see the Paper 2 course page.

3.2 How to pick the axis

1
Start from the question, not from the works. Let the prompt dictate the axes.
2
Find a genuine divergence, not a similarity you invent. The richest paragraphs come from works that approach the same question differently.
3
Cap at three to four axes. More than four and the essay becomes a shallow survey; fewer than three and you cannot sustain the length with depth.
4
Write the "so what" before the paragraph. If you cannot state the insight in one clause, the row is not yet a comparison — it is two observations.

4. The self-editing pass — run it before you submit

This is the craft version of a checklist. Read your draft once, top to bottom, and confirm each move is present. It replaces any component-specific rubric checklist; the criteria are simply these moves, assessed.

Locate
Is every claim tied to a specific textual choice — a word, image, structure, design — not a vague "the text shows"?
Name
Have I named the technique precisely where it matters, rather than "it's effective"?
Explain
Have I said how the choice works and what effect it produces on the reader?
Meaning
Does each paragraph tie its effect to a larger claim, not just stop at the observation?
Thread
Does the essay hold one clear line from opening to close, with a conclusion that synthesises rather than repeats?
Language
Is the writing precise and controlled, with quotations embedded in my own syntax, not dropped?

5. Three craft errors to catch first

The full lists of exam-specific mistakes live on the course pages (Paper 1 and the Individual Oral each carry their ten). The three that most often sink otherwise-able writing, in craft terms:

#ErrorThe craft fix
1Describing instead of analysing. Summary of what happens, with no "and this matters because…"After any plot or content statement, force the effect: what does the choice make the reader see or feel?
2Feature-spotting. Naming devices without explaining their payoff.Never name a technique without its effect. Cluster devices by shared effect, not by a flat list.
3Dropped or un-integrated quotations. A colon, then a floating quote.Embed every quote in your own sentence; introduce it with a clause, not a leap.
A note on "transferable" examples

The grids, the model micro-extract in §2.2, and the comparison rows above are deliberately not keyed to any specific set work or past paper. The course pages under English A: Lang & Lit hold the work-specific studies; this section is the transferable method you apply to whatever text is in front of you, seen or unseen. Practise the routines on any text you read this week — an advert, a speech, a poem — and the exam becomes a familiar shape, not a new one.
