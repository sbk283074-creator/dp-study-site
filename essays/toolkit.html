The analysis toolkit & self-check checklists

The other pages in this section teach you the parts — the devices, the vocabulary, the question shapes, the model paragraphs. This page teaches you the method: a small set of reusable routines you run before and after writing so that the parts assemble into a response the criteria actually reward. Think of it as the workshop. You will not reinvent analysis from scratch in the exam; you will reach for the same five or six moves every time, then run a checklist to catch the marks you would otherwise lose to carelessness.

0. What is in the toolkit, and when you use it

Every strong response to any of the four tasks is built from the same analytical moves (recapped in §2). What separates a 6/7 candidate from a 4/5 candidate is rarely raw insight — it is process discipline: did you read the text properly before you wrote, did you plan, and did you check your own work against the criteria before the clock ran out. The toolkit is the process.

RoutineYou use it…It protects against
Close-reading passes (§1)Before you write a word, on the text in front of you.Misreading the text; analysing the wrong thing.
PEEL / TEAL logic check (§2)While drafting each paragraph, and when a paragraph feels thin.Description, feature-spotting, dropped quotes.
Comparison grid (§3)While planning Paper 2, before you write the essay.Two separate essays instead of one comparison.

Self-check lists (§5)In the last 5–10 minutes of every task.Forgetting a criterion, weak focus, weak close.

None of this is IB-specific secret knowledge. The routines are standard literary-studies and exam-method practice; the assessment facts they are tuned to (the four criteria, the task shapes) come from the IB Language A: language and literature guide. Where a routine depends on a number that changes between sessions, the page says so.

1. The close-reading routine — before you write a word

Most lost marks on Paper 1 and the HL Essay are lost in the reading, not the writing. A candidate who misreads the situation, the genre, or the speaker's stance then writes a fluent, well-expressed analysis of the wrong thing — and the criteria (A: Understanding & interpretation) penalise exactly that. The fix is a deliberate, repeatable reading routine. Do not skip it because the clock is ticking; the minutes you spend here are the highest-yield minutes in the paper.

1.1 The six passes

PassQuestion you answerWhat you mark or note
1 · Whole readWhat is this text, in plain terms?Genre, medium, situation, audience, purpose. One sentence.
2 · Stance readWho is speaking, to whom, with what attitude?Speaker / addressee / tone. The single most-missed feature.
3 · Feature readWhat specific choices does the text make?Underline / highlight concrete choices: words, images, structure, design.
4 · Pattern readWhich choices cluster or repeat?Group the marks into 3–4 thematic clusters (not a flat list).
5 · Meaning readWhat does it add up to?A one-line central claim the clusters support.
6 · Task readGiven the question / guidance, what do I argue?The specific thesis or thread for this response.
The 3-minute first-read checklist

If you only have time for one discipline, make it this. On the first full read, force yourself to answer four questions out loud (or in the margin): (a) What is the text for — to inform, persuade, satirise, mourn, sell? (b) Who is it addressed to? (c) What is the speaker's stance — complicit, detached, outraged, playful? (d) What is the single biggest structural or design move (a shift, a list, a layout, a gap)? A wrong answer to (a)–(d) quietly sinks the whole response; thirty seconds each is cheap insurance.

1.2 Why "clusters" beat "a list of devices"

Pass 4 is where weak candidates and strong candidates diverge. The weak candidate makes a flat inventory — "there is a metaphor, and alliteration, and a short sentence, and a shift in tense" — and then analyses each in isolation. The strong candidate notices that the metaphors, the sentence lengths, and the tense shift all serve one effect (say, the collapse of control) and groups them. Criteria B (Analysis & evaluation) rewards the second move: not "the device is present" but "these choices work together to produce an effect." Cluster first; the paragraph plan writes itself.

2. PEEL / TEAL as a logic check, not a sentence template

You will meet PEEL (Point · Evidence · Explanation · Link) or TEAL (Thesis/Technique · Evidence · Analysis · Link) in many classrooms. Used well, it is a logic check: a way to confirm that a paragraph actually argues rather than merely describes. Used badly, it becomes a straitjacket that produces the mechanical, formulaic writing examiners explicitly warn against. This section shows you the difference.

2.1 The five analytical moves, mapped to the letters

LetterThe move it enforcesThe question it answers
P — PointState the claim this paragraph makes.What am I arguing in this paragraph?
E — EvidenceGround it in a concrete feature or quotation.What in the text proves the claim?
E — Explanation / A — AnalysisName the technique, explain how it works, state the effect.How does the choice produce that effect on the reader?
L — LinkTie back to the thesis, the question, or the next idea.So what — why does this matter to my whole argument?
(T — Technique)In TEAL, name the device explicitly up front.What specific technique am I analysing?
Trap — the template that kills the mark

PEEL is a check, not a opening formula. Examiner reports across IB English sessions repeatedly flag writing that announces its own mechanics — "In this paragraph I will analyse the use of metaphor, which shows…" — or that pads each paragraph with a ritual "This links to my point that…" The criteria reward organisation and focus (C), not visible scaffolding. Write the paragraph so the point, evidence, analysis and link are present and coherent; do not perform the acronym. If a sentence exists only to display a letter, cut it.

2.2 A worked paragraph, annotated

The micro-extract below is an illustrative teaching construct, not a line from any set text or past paper. It exists only to show the logic of one paragraph.

"The door would not open. She counted the seconds — one, two, three — and the silence behind it grew teeth. Outside, the city kept its ordinary noise, indifferent as weather."

SegmentWhat it does (the letter)
"The door would not open" — the flat, curt opening denies the reader any context, dropping us mid-crisis rather than preparing us for it.P + E: the point (we are thrown into crisis) is made through the evidence, not stated separately.
The personification "the silence grew teeth" turns an absence into a threat…A (technique named): the device is identified.
…so that the character's fear is externalised as something actively hostile, and the reader feels confinement rather than merely being told of it.A (effect): the how and the effect on the reader are stated.
The final clause, "indifferent as weather," widens the frame: the city's indifference contrasts the intimate terror of the doorway, and the ordinary world continues unpriced by her panic.L: ties the local choice to the larger claim (isolation within an indifferent world) that the whole response will develop.

Notice what is not there: no "I will now analyse," no separate "This links to my point." The logic is present; the scaffolding is invisible. That is the target.

3. The comparison grid — Paper 2's backbone

Paper 2 asks you to compare and contrast at least two literary works studied in the course, answering one essay question on both. The most common Paper 2 failure is not weak analysis — it is writing two separate essays that happen to sit on the same page. The fix is to plan the comparison on a grid before you write, so that every paragraph holds the two works together against a shared axis.

3.1 The four-column grid

Axis of comparisonText 1Text 2So what? (the insight)
How each presents isolation through settingConfinement is physical and claustrophobic.Confinement is psychological and diffuse.Both equate isolation with a loss of agency, but locate it differently — body vs mind.
How each uses a framing narrator………
How each resolves (or refuses) the central tension………

Use Text 1 (your novel) and Text 2 (your novel) as your real labels; the grid is transferable and is deliberately not keyed to specific set works. Each row becomes one comparative paragraph. The fourth column — the "so what" — is the part weak plans omit, and it is the part that earns B (evaluation) marks: a comparison that only says "Text 1 does X, Text 2 does Y" describes; a comparison that says "and the difference reveals…" evaluates.

3.2 How to pick the axis

1
Start from the question, not from the works. The set question names a concept (power, identity, narrative method, ending). Let it dictate the axes.
2
Find a genuine divergence, not a similarity you invent. The richest paragraphs come from works that approach the same question differently.
3
Cap at three to four axes. More than four and the essay becomes a shallow survey; fewer than three and you cannot sustain the word count with depth.
4
Write the "so what" before you write the paragraph. If you cannot state the insight in one clause, the row is not yet a comparison — it is two observations.
Session note — verify the Paper 2 rubric

Paper 2 is marked /30 against criteria A–D (SL 35% / HL 25% of the final grade). Reports indicate a mark-scheme refinement was introduced for the 2026 session that reframes criterion A around direct engagement with the question and splits criterion B into B1 (analysis) and B2 (evaluation). Treat that as reported, not settled: confirm against the guide and specimen mark scheme for your examination session before you rely on it. The grid method above serves either version, because it forces question-engagement and evaluation regardless.

4. The 15-minute Paper 1 pre-write

Paper 1 (guided textual analysis of one or two unseen non-literary texts) is a timed sprint. The discipline that separates candidates is how they spend the first fifteen minutes. The guiding question is not compulsory — the criteria reward the analysis itself — but you must still choose a thread and plan. Below is a windowed routine.

WindowSL (one text, ~1h45 total)HL (two texts, ~2h15 total)
0–3 minFirst whole read; note genre, audience, purpose, stance.Same, on both texts; decide which to lead with if order is free.
3–7 minSecond read; underline concrete choices; build 3–4 clusters.Second read of both; cluster each separately, then note one cross-link.
7–11 minDraft the central claim / thread and the paragraph order.Draft the claim for each response and its paragraph order.
11–15 minWrite the opening sentence; confirm timing budget.Write both openings; confirm each response gets ~half the time.
Why a written opening matters

Spending the last two minutes of the pre-write writing your actual opening sentence does two things: it removes the blank-page paralysis when the clock starts, and it forces you to commit to a thread you can defend. A thread you can state in one sentence is a thread the criteria (C: Focus & organisation) can reward. A thread you are still "feeling out" when you start writing is the root of the rambling, unfocused response.

At HL, the two responses are marked independently — a strong response 1 cannot rescue a weak response 2. Budget the time as two separate sprints; do not let the first eat the second.

5. Per-task self-check checklists

These are the lists to run in the final 5–10 minutes of each task. Tick mentally; fix what you can. They are organised by the four criteria (A Understanding · B Analysis & evaluation · C Focus & organisation · D Language) so you can see exactly which mark you are protecting.

5.1 Paper 1 — guided textual analysis

A — Understanding
Have I identified the text type, audience and stance accurately? Is my reading of the situation right, not just plausible?
B — Analysis
Is every claim tied to a specific textual choice? Have I named techniques and explained effects — not just listed devices?
C — Focus
Does the response follow one clear thread from opening to close? Is the structure signposted, not meandering?
D — Language
Is my expression precise and controlled? Are quotations embedded in my own syntax, not dropped?
Task
Did I answer the guiding question or follow a stronger self-chosen thread? (The question is not compulsory — but a thread must exist.)

5.2 Paper 2 — comparative essay

A — Understanding
Have I shown I understand both works in relation to the set question, not in isolation?
B — Analysis & evaluation
Is the comparison real — same axis, both works, plus a "so what"? Or have I written two separate essays?
C — Focus
Does every paragraph hold both works together? Does the essay answer the question, not just "compare the books"?
D — Language
Is the comparative language precise ("whereas", "by contrast", "converges on") rather than vague?
Task
Have I referenced both works throughout, with specific textual support from each?

5.3 HL Essay — sustained investigation (1,200–1,500 words, /20)

A — Understanding
Is my line of inquiry clear and grounded in a specific work or body of work from the course?
B — Analysis & evaluation
Do I sustain argument across the word count, not pad? Is the thesis developed, not just stated once?
C — Focus
Is the essay organised around the line of inquiry, with a real introduction, body and conclusion?
D — Language
Is the register appropriately academic and precise? Are secondary ideas integrated, not name-dropped?
Task
Is it 1,200–1,500 words (HL only)? Is it an investigation, not a book report or a biography of the author?

5.4 Individual Oral — spoken analysis on a global issue (/40)

A — Understanding
Do I show how the extract and the stimulus connect to the global issue, not just to each other?
B — Analysis & evaluation
Is the analysis of both the literary extract and the non-literary stimulus detailed and evaluative?
C — Focus
Does the 10-minute talk hold a clear line from the global issue through both texts to a conclusion?
D — Language
Is the spoken language clear, varied and appropriate to an analytical register?
Task
Is the global issue named and does it have the three properties — wide significance, transnational nature, local application?

6. Ten most common pitfalls — and the fix for each

These are the errors that cost the most marks across the four tasks, with the routine that prevents each. They are ordered roughly by how often they appear in weaker responses.

#PitfallWhy it loses marksThe fix
1Retelling / describing instead of analysing.Criteria B rewards analysis of how and why, not summary of what happens.After any plot statement, force "and this matters because…" Tie every observation to an effect.
2Feature-spotting. Listing devices without explaining effect.B asks for evaluation of effect and meaning, not an inventory of techniques.Cluster devices by shared effect (§1.2); never name a technique without its payoff.
3Ignoring the set question / guiding question.A (Paper 2) and focus (C) reward direct engagement with the task.Write the question at the top of your plan; every paragraph must answer it.
4Dropped or un-integrated quotations.Signals weak control of the text (A) and weak language (D).Embed every quote in your own sentence; introduce it with a clause, not a colon and a leap.
5Generic thesis. "The author uses language to show X."Too vague to organise an argument; C cannot reward an unspecified claim.Name the specific divergence or controlling move (see the model paragraphs on the Examples page).
6Paper 2 as two separate essays.The task is compare and contrast; separated treatment fails B and C.Plan on the comparison grid (§3); every paragraph holds both works on one axis.
7HL Essay as a book report.The task is a sustained investigation with a line of inquiry, not a summary.Open with a question, not a plot outline; let the argument drive the selection of evidence.
8Weak global issue (IO). Too narrow, or not transnational.The global issue must have wide significance, a transnational nature, and local application.Stress-test it: could it apply in another country, and does it matter beyond the text?
9Formulaic openings. "In this essay I will…"Examiners warn against mechanical scaffolding; it wastes words and reads as immature.Use PEEL/TEAL as a logic check (§2), not a visible template. Start in the argument.
10Running out of time / no conclusion.An absent or rushed close weakens C (focus) and leaves the argument hanging.Reserve the last 5–10 minutes for the checklist (§5) and a one-paragraph synthesis close.
A note on "transferable" examples

The grids, the model micro-extract in §2, and the comparison rows above are deliberately not keyed to any specific set work or past paper. The course pages under English A: Lang & Lit hold the work-specific studies; this section is the transferable method you apply to whatever text is in front of you, seen or unseen. Practise the routines on any text you read this week — an advert, a speech, a poem — and the exam becomes a familiar shape, not a new one.
