The four essay situations and their registers

The same analytical muscle powers every IB English A writing task, but each situation asks for a different register — a different voice, opening and shape. The commentary, the comparative argument, the sustained investigation and the spoken analysis are four ways of sounding like a careful reader. Confusing two of them — writing the comparative as a commentary, or the investigation as a book report — is one of the most expensive mistakes available. This page is about voice and shape, not exam rules; for the rules of each task, see the course pages.

1. The throughline: what / how / why

Whatever you are writing, three questions organise every strong analytical paragraph:

QuestionWhat it produces
What is the text doing?An accurate account of content, situation and form.
How does it do it?Analysis of the specific choices — language, structure, technique, design.
Why does it matter?A claim about meaning, and (where the task calls for it) about the wider world or the framing issue.

The difference between the situations is not the throughline; it is where the weight sits and how openly you state it. A commentary keeps its claim implicit in the reading of one text; a comparative argument states the shared ground and the divergence up front; an investigation sustains a single question for many words; a spoken analysis returns to one issue constantly. Learn the throughline once; the rest of this page is the register each situation wraps around it.

2. The commentary register — stay inside the text

A commentary is a focused analysis of one text that argues a reading of how it works. Its register is immanent: it stays inside the text and resists generalising outward. The opening names the text type, audience and a controlling interpretive claim; the body paragraphs each build around a pattern of choices rather than a checklist of devices; the close synthesises, it does not summarise.

A workable commentary opening (generic, transferable)

"This [text type — e.g. opinion column] addresses a [target audience] in order to [purpose]. Beneath its surface argument, the writer's controlling move is to [central interpretive claim — e.g. fuse the personal with the political by…]. The following analysis traces that move through diction, structure and tone."

That single paragraph names the what (type, audience, purpose), commits to a why (the controlling interpretive claim), and previews the how (the three patterns). It is a thesis for a commentary: it argues a reading, it does not summarise. For the full Paper 1 drill and model commentaries, see the course page.

Common register error

Writing a themed essay ("this text is about climate anxiety") instead of a commentary. A commentary stays inside the text; it does not generalise outward. If your paragraph could be moved to a different text about the same topic, you are off register.

3. The comparative register — integrated, not sequenced

The comparative argument holds two works together on a shared axis and argues a divergence between them. Its register is relational: almost every sentence names both texts, or a contrast between them. The single most common failure is to write two essays that happen to sit on one page — "In Text A… [800 words]. In Text B… [800 words]" has not compared; it has listed. Open each paragraph with a shared concern and switch between the works within it.

A workable comparative thesis (generic, transferable)

"Both [Text A] and [Text B] stage the erosion of identity under [pressure], yet they diverge in method: where [A] externalises the loss through [device/structure], [B] internalises it through [device/structure], suggesting that [larger claim about the concept]."

That thesis names the shared ground and the divergence in the same sentence — the paragraph can now alternate between the two works as it develops. For the comparative-vocabulary bank and the pair-specific comparison grid, see the Paper 2 course page.

4. The investigative register — one sharp question, sustained

The sustained investigation (the HL Essay's shape) is a scholarly essay driven by a line of inquiry: a question, not a topic, narrow enough to sustain many words without padding. Its register is precise and self-directed: because you choose the question, the essay rewards focus above all. "How does X use Y?" is the productive shape; "What is the theme of X?" is not.

Common register error

A descriptive or over-broad essay. Strong investigations spend their words analysing specific passages against a sharp line of inquiry; weak ones summarise plot or gesture at themes. The line of inquiry should be visible in the introduction and returned to in the close — if you cannot state it in one sentence, it is not a line of inquiry yet. See the question bank for line-of-inquiry starters.

5. The spoken-analysis register — prepared, not scripted

The spoken analysis (the Individual Oral's shape) examines how two texts present a chosen issue, then answers questions. Its register is oral but disciplined: you may bring bullet points, not a script, so the voice is conversational yet tightly structured, returning to the issue constantly and going deep on a few moments rather than covering everything. For the issue's definition and the minute-by-minute script, see the course page.

6. Register: what "formal academic" actually means

Every situation is assessed on the quality of its language: clear, accurate, varied, in an appropriate register. "Appropriate register" is not "fancy". It means the prose of analysis — precise, tentative where judgement is uncertain, free of classroom tics. The table is the practical version.

Avoid (undermines the writing)Use instead (lifts it)
"I think / I believe the writer…""The writer positions the reader to…" (no first person needed)
"This shows that…" (a weak verb)"This foregrounds / subverts / destabilises…" (see the vocabulary bank)
"The effect is good / nice / interesting""The effect is to implicate / distance / ironise…"

8. Weak → strong: thesis moves for each situation

The same weakness appears in all four — a thesis that labels instead of argues. The fixes:

Commentary

Weak "This advert uses colour and layout to sell a product."

Strong "By desaturating everything but the product, the advert constructs the object as the only point of colour in an otherwise drained world — desire as survival."

Comparative

Weak "Both texts show that power corrupts."

Strong "Both texts locate corruption in language itself, yet A dramatises it through official decree while B leaks it through domestic dialogue, implying corruption is systemic in the first and intimate in the second."

Investigation

Weak "How does the novel explore memory?"

Strong "How does the novel use an unreliable, non-linear narrator to suggest that memory is a strategy of self-protection rather than a record of the past?"

Spoken analysis

Weak "Both texts are about gender inequality."

Strong "Both texts present gender inequality as enforced through everyday language — the slogan in the campaign and the repeated imperative in the poem — but the campaign normalises it while the poem indicts it."
