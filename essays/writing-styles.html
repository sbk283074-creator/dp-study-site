The four writing tasks and their registers

All four IB English A writing components reward the same analytical muscle, but they ask for four different registers — four different voices, openings and shapes. Paper 1 is a commentary; Paper 2 is a comparative argument; the HL Essay is a sustained scholarly investigation; the Individual Oral is a spoken analysis built on a global issue. Confusing two of them — writing Paper 2 as a commentary, or the HL Essay as a book report — is one of the most expensive mistakes available. This page sets out what each task is really asking and how its register differs.

Paper 1 · commentary Paper 2 · comparative HL Essay · investigation Individual Oral · spoken

1. The throughline: what / how / why

Whatever the task, three questions organise every strong response:

QuestionWhat it producesThe criterion it serves
What is the text doing?An accurate account of content, situation and form.A — Understanding
How does it do it?Analysis of the specific choices (language, structure, technique, design).B — Analysis & evaluation
Why does it matter?A claim about meaning, effect on the reader, and (where relevant) the global issue.B + C — Evaluation & focus

The difference between the tasks is not the throughline; it is where the weight sits. Paper 1 spends almost all its energy on how and why about a single unseen text. Paper 2 spends its energy on why across two texts at once. The HL Essay sustains why over 1,200–1,500 words on one work. The Individual Oral ties why to a stated global issue. Learn the throughline once; the rest of this page is about the register each task wraps around it.

2. Paper 1 — guided textual analysis (the commentary)

Paper 1 is a commentary, not a themed essay. You are given one or two unseen non-literary texts (SL: choose one of two; HL: respond to both, each separately), each with a guiding question. You write a focused analysis of how the text works.

Three things the rubric is explicit about

(1) The guiding question is not compulsory — the four criteria reward the analytical response itself, so a strong analysis that follows a different thread can still earn top marks; a diligent but descriptive answer to the guiding question cannot. (2) At HL the two responses are marked independently — a brilliant response 1 cannot rescue a weak response 2. (3) The criteria are identical at SL and HL: A Understanding & interpretation, B Analysis & evaluation, C Focus & organisation, D Language, each out of 5.

Structure that works: a short introduction naming the text type, audience and the central tension or interpretive direction; then three or four body paragraphs, each built around a pattern of choices rather than a checklist of devices; then a brief conclusion that synthesises, not repeats. The IB does not prescribe a five-paragraph form or a word count.

A workable Paper 1 opening (generic, transferable)

"This [text type — e.g. opinion column] addresses a [target audience] in order to [purpose]. Beneath its surface argument, the writer's controlling move is to [central interpretive claim — e.g. fuse the personal with the political by…]. The following analysis traces that move through diction, structure and tone."

That single paragraph names the what (type, audience, purpose), commits to a why (the controlling interpretive claim), and previews the how (the three patterns). It is a thesis for a commentary: it argues a reading, it does not summarise.

Common error

Writing a themed essay ("this text is about climate anxiety") instead of a commentary. A commentary stays inside the text; it does not generalise outward. If your paragraph could be moved to a different text about the same topic, you are off task.

3. Paper 2 — the comparative essay

Paper 2 is a comparative argument. You answer one of four prompts on two literary works studied during the course (closed book — no text in the room). The prompt is usually built on one of the seven concepts or a broad thematic question, and it almost always carries a command term (compare, examine, to what extent, discuss).

The comparison must be integrated, not sequenced. The mark scheme is explicit that Criterion B rewards "how effectively the candidate analyses similarities and/or differences between the two works." A response that writes "In Text A… [800 words]. In Text B… [800 words]" has not compared; it has listed. A response that opens each paragraph with a shared concern and switches between the two works within the paragraph has.

Paper 2 mark scheme (2021 baseline)

Criterion A — Knowledge, understanding & interpretation: /10 (the top band asks for "insightful interpretation of the similarities and differences"). Criterion B — Analysis & evaluation: /10. Criterion C — Focus & organisation: /5. Criterion D — Language: /5. Total /30. Note the 2026 revision reframes A around the question and splits B into B1 (analysis of choices) and B2 (comparison); confirm against your session.

A workable Paper 2 thesis (generic, transferable)

"Both [Text A] and [Text B] stage the erosion of identity under [pressure], yet they diverge in method: where [A] externalises the loss through [device/structure], [B] internalises it through [device/structure], suggesting that [larger claim about the concept]."

That thesis names the shared ground and the divergence in the same sentence — the paragraph can now alternate between the two works as it develops, which is exactly what Criterion B rewards.

4. The HL Essay — sustained investigation

The HL Essay is a sustained scholarly investigation: 1,200–1,500 words on one literary work or one non-literary body of work you have studied, driven by a student-chosen line of inquiry. It is not a Paper 2 (no comparison required) and not a book report. It is marked /20: A Knowledge & understanding /5, B Analysis & evaluation /5, C Focus, organisation & development /5, D Language /5.

Because it is untimed and you choose the question, the HL Essay rewards precision of focus above all. The line of inquiry is the backbone: it is a question, not a topic, and it must be narrow enough to sustain 1,500 words without padding. "How does X use Y?" is the productive shape; "What is the theme of X?" is not.

Common error

A descriptive or over-broad essay. Top responses spend their words analysing specific passages against a sharp line of inquiry; weak responses summarise plot or gesture at themes. The line of inquiry should be visible in the introduction and returned to in the conclusion — if you cannot state it in one sentence, it is not a line of inquiry yet.

5. The Individual Oral — spoken analysis on a global issue

The Individual Oral is a spoken analysis. In 10 minutes you examine how one literary work and one non-literary body of work present a common global issue you have chosen, then answer 5 minutes of teacher questions. You may bring 10 bullet points (not a script). It is marked /40: A Knowledge & understanding /10, B Analysis & evaluation /10, C Focus & organisation /10, D Language /10.

The global issue is the spine: every extract you choose must genuinely present it, and your 10 minutes should return to it constantly. The two extracts are short — up to 40 lines from the literary work and samples from the non-literary body of work — so the oral is about depth on a few moments, not coverage.

Extract choice

Pick extracts where the global issue is visible in the language, not just the subject matter. An extract "about" migration that contains no striking linguistic or structural choice gives you nothing to analyse. The issue must be present in content and form — that is the prompt wording, and it is also where the marks are.

6. Register: what "formal academic" actually means

Every component is assessed on Criterion D / AO4: language must be clear, accurate, varied and in an appropriate register. "Appropriate register" is not "fancy". It means the prose of analysis: precise, tentative where judgement is uncertain, and free of the spoken tics of the classroom. The table is the practical version.

Avoid (undermines D)Use instead (lifts D)
"I think / I believe the writer…""The writer positions the reader to…" (no first person needed)
"This shows that…" (a weak verb)"This foregrounds / subverts / destabilises…" (see the vocabulary bank)
"The effect is good / nice / interesting""The effect is to implicate / distance /ironise…"
Text-speak, contractions in excess, slangControlled syntax; terminology used correctly
Over-long sentences that lose the subjectVaried sentence length with a clear main clause

8. Weak → strong: thesis moves for each task

The same weakness appears in all four tasks — a thesis that labels instead of argues. The fixes:

Paper 1

Weak "This advert uses colour and layout to sell a product."

Strong "By desaturating everything but the product, the advert constructs the object as the only point of colour in an otherwise drained world — desire as survival."

Paper 2

Weak "Both texts show that power corrupts."

Strong "Both texts locate corruption in language itself, yet A dramatises it through official decree while B leaks it through domestic dialogue, implying corruption is systemic in the first and intimate in the second."

HL Essay

Weak "How does the novel explore memory?"

Strong "How does the novel use an unreliable, non-linear narrator to suggest that memory is a strategy of self-protection rather than a record of the past?"

Individual Oral

Weak "Both texts are about gender inequality."

Strong "Both texts present gender inequality as enforced through everyday language — the slogan in the campaign and the repeated imperative in the poem — but the campaign normalises it while the poem indicts it."
