Literary & stylistic devices

A device is a choice, not a decoration. This glossary exists to stop you at the name and push you to the function. For every entry the job is the same three steps from the overview: name the technique, then ask what it makes the reader see or feel, then tie that to meaning. The "typical effect" below is a starting hypothesis, not a law — the only correct effect is the one the specific text earns. Read the question column first; if you cannot answer it, you have not yet found the device's work.

The trap this page is built to avoid

Naming a device is worth nothing on its own. "The writer uses alliteration" is a label; "the repeated plosive b and p sounds batter the line, mimicking the blunt force the speaker describes" is analysis. Every entry below therefore ends with the question that turns the label into a paragraph.

1. Language and sound

Alliteration
Repetition of initial consonant sounds in nearby words. Ask: does the sound echo the sense (hissing for malice, plosives for impact)? Typical effect: draws attention to a phrase, creates mood, or mimics meaning through sound.
Assonance
Repetition of vowel sounds within words. Ask: where does the sound cluster sit and what feeling does it carry (long o for lament, short i for tension)? Typical effect: musicality or unease; links words the eye might keep separate.
Consonance
Repetition of consonant sounds at the end or middle of words. Ask: is the texture tight or jarring? Typical effect: cohesion or dissonance across a line or sentence.
Sibilance
Repetition of s / sh sounds. Ask: does it soothe or does it sneer? Typical effect: often used for menace, secrecy or a hush.
Onomatopoeia
A word whose sound echoes its meaning (hiss, crash). Ask: does the word do the sensory work the rest of the sentence avoids? Typical effect: immediacy; pulls the reader into the moment.
Rhyme / half-rhyme
Full or partial echo of ending sounds. Ask: does a half-rhyme (pararhyme) create a sense of something nearly resolved but not quite? Typical effect: order and closure, or — with half-rhyme — unease and incompletion.
Caesura
A pause within a line (marked by punctuation). Ask: where does the breath break and what does the break interrupt? Typical effect: hesitation, emphasis, or a shift in thought.
Enjambment
A line running on without pause into the next. Ask: does the meaning withhold its end to create suspense or speed? Typical effect: momentum, or a tension between syntax and line.

2. Imagery and figurative language

Imagery
Language that appeals to the senses (not only sight). Ask: which sense is engaged and what feeling follows? Typical effect: embodied experience; makes abstract ideas concrete.
Simile
A comparison using "like" or "as". Ask: what two things are yoked and what does the comparison reveal that literal language could not? Typical effect: clarifies or defamiliarises; controls how the reader sees the tenor.
Metaphor
A direct equivalence ("X is Y") without "like/as". Ask: what is being carried from the vehicle into the subject? Typical effect: compresses an argument; reshapes perception by merging two fields.
Extended metaphor
A metaphor sustained across a passage or whole text. Ask: does the governing comparison organise the text's logic? Typical effect: a controlling structure of meaning; unifies disparate moments.
Personification
Attributing human agency to the non-human. Ask: what does giving the thing a will allow the writer to imply? Typical effect: makes setting or abstraction an agent, often to implicate or accuse.
Symbolism
A concrete object standing for an abstract idea. Ask: does the object accrue meaning beyond itself, and is the meaning stable or contested? Typical effect: layers meaning; condenses theme.
Motif
A recurring element (image, phrase, object) across a text. Ask: what does each recurrence deepen or alter? Typical effect: ties the work together; tracks a developing idea.
Allegory
A sustained one-to-one mapping where characters/events stand for ideas. Ask: what system of meaning sits beneath the surface story? Typical effect: argues a thesis through narrative; invites decoding.
Metonymy / synecdoche
Using a related part for the whole (the Crown for the monarchy; "hands" for workers). Ask: what is gained by naming the part instead of the whole? Typical effect: reframes scale or focus; can generalise or particularise.
Oxymoron / paradox
A conjunction of opposites (bitter-sweet; "silent scream"). Ask: what contradiction is the writer refusing to resolve? Typical effect: registers complexity; resists simple judgement.
Hyperbole
Deliberate overstatement. Ask: what is the gap between claim and reality doing? Typical effect: emphasis, satire, or emotional intensity.
Understatement / litotes
Saying less than is meant ("not unimportant"). Ask: why is restraint more powerful here than assertion? Typical effect: irony, control, or a challenge to the reader to supply the weight.
Pun
A play on words with two meanings. Ask: what second sense is being activated and to what end? Typical effect: wit, double meaning, or a serious point delivered sideways.

3. Diction and tone

Diction
Word choice — formal/informal, abstract/concrete, Latinate/Anglo-Saxon. Ask: why this word and not a synonym? Typical effect: sets register; reveals attitude.
Tone
The writer's attitude toward subject and reader (ironic, solemn, playful). Ask: what stance does the language take and is it stable? Typical effect: tells the reader how to receive the content; can be undercut by irony.
Register
The level of formality suited to audience and purpose. Ask: is the level appropriate, and does a shift in register signal something? Typical effect: positions writer and reader; a register clash can expose hypocrisy.
Colloquialism
Everyday, informal language. Ask: does it create intimacy, authenticity, or a class signal? Typical effect: closeness or a specific social voice.
Archaism
Old-fashioned wording. Ask: does it evoke a period, a ritual, or false grandeur? Typical effect: distance in time; authority or pastiche.
Euphemism
A mild term for a harsh reality. Ask: what is being softened and who benefits from the softening? Typical effect: evasion, politeness, or sinister dilution of responsibility.
Jargon / cant
Specialised or in-group language. Ask: does it include or exclude the reader, and what does that say? Typical effect: authority, tribalism, or gatekeeping.
Concrete vs abstract
Specific, sensory words vs general concepts. Ask: where does the text switch between them and why? Typical effect: abstraction argues; concreteness embodies.

4. Syntax and sentence structure

Syntax
Word order and sentence shape. Ask: is it loose or tight, balanced or fractured? Typical effect: pace, clarity, and the psychology of the speaker.
Parataxis
Short clauses joined by "and" or nothing ("I came, I saw, I conquered"). Ask: does the equal weight create urgency or flatness? Typical effect: speed, accumulation, a refusal to rank.
Hypotaxis
Subordinate clauses showing cause, condition, concession. Ask: what is being made dependent on what? Typical effect: complexity, qualification, a layered argument.
Anaphora
Repetition of a word/phrase at the start of clauses ("We shall fight…"). Ask: what is being built up by the repetition? Typical effect: momentum, insistence, rallying force.
Epistrophe
Repetition at the end of clauses. Ask: what lands with accumulating weight? Typical effect: closure and resonance.
Antithesis
Balanced opposition of ideas ("It was the best of times…"). Ask: what contradiction is being held? Typical effect: dialectic tension; a controlled both/and.
Chiasmus
A criss-cross structure (ABBA: "when the going gets tough, the tough get going"). Ask: what reversal is being performed? Typical effect: symmetry that can imply equivalence or a turn.
Polysyndeton / asyndeton
Many connectives vs none. Ask: does piling "and" create breathlessness, or does stripping them create starkness? Typical effect: flood or severity.
Parenthesis
An inserted, set-off aside. Ask: what is being sidelined and why? Typical effect: a second voice; doubt or candour leaking through.
Fragment
An incomplete sentence. Ask: what is the break refusing to complete? Typical effect: shock, breathlessness, or a thought cut off.
Rule of three / triadic structure
A grouping of three. Ask: does the third item cap or overturn the first two? Typical effect: completeness, rhythm, rhetorical climax.

5. Structure and narrative

Narrative perspective / point of view
Who tells (first, second, third; limited or omniscient). Ask: what does this vantage allow us to know and what does it hide? Typical effect: controls sympathy, knowledge and reliability.
Focalisation
Through whose consciousness we see, distinct from the narrator. Ask: is the focaliser reliable or constrained? Typical effect: shapes interpretation by filtering events.
Unreliable narrator
A narrator whose account is partial or self-deceiving. Ask: where does the telling diverge from what is shown? Typical effect: irony; implicates the reader in detection.
Stream of consciousness
Unmediated flow of thought. Ask: what does the lack of ordering reveal about the mind? Typical effect: intimacy, disorder, or a specific psychology.
Non-linear narrative
Events out of chronological order. Ask: why this arrangement — what is withheld or revealed by the order? Typical effect: suspense, theme over plot, retrospective meaning.
Frame narrative
A story enclosing another. Ask: what does the outer frame add or undercut? Typical effect: distance, authority, or a questioning of the inner tale.
In medias res
Beginning in the middle of action. Ask: what context is withheld and to what effect? Typical effect: immediate engagement; delayed understanding.
Juxtaposition
Placing elements side by side for contrast. Ask: what is the collision producing? Typical effect: irony, tension, or a new meaning from the pairing.
Foil
A character contrasting another to heighten a trait. Ask: what does the contrast throw into relief? Typical effect: definition by opposition.
Intertextuality
Explicit or implicit reference to another text. Ask: how does the new text reinterpret the old? Typical effect: layers meaning; positions the work in a tradition.
Foreshadowing
Early hints of later events. Ask: does it create fate or preparation? Typical effect: cohesion; retrospective inevitability.
Setting
Time, place and social context. Ask: is setting active or backdrop — does it shape character? Typical effect: mood, constraint, or a projection of inner state.

6. Rhetoric and argument (non-literary and persuasive texts)

Rhetorical question
A question not meant to be answered. Ask: what assumption does it smuggle in? Typical effect: engages or manipulates; plants a premise.
Irony (verbal / dramatic / situational)
Saying one thing meaning another; a gap between appearance and reality; an outcome contrary to expectation. Ask: where is the gap and who sees it? Typical effect: critique, humour, or exposure.
Satire / parody
Attack by mockery / imitation. Ask: what is being held up to ridicule and through what mimicry? Typical effect: exposes vice or convention; can also merely entertain.
Ethos / pathos / logos
Appeals to credibility, emotion, reason. Ask: which appeal dominates and what does that reveal about the audience? Typical effect: persuasion through the chosen lever.
Concession / counter-argument
Acknowledging the opposing view. Ask: is it genuine or a setup to defeat? Typical effect: credibility, balance, or a stronger rebuttal.
Anecdote / exemplum
A brief illustrative story or example. Ask: what does the singular case stand in for? Typical effect: concreteness; can personalise or over-generalise.
Analogy
An extended comparison to explain. Ask: where does the analogy break down? Typical effect: clarifies the unfamiliar by the familiar.
Connotation vs denotation
Associative meaning vs literal meaning. Ask: which words carry charged associations and why those? Typical effect: persuasion and mood beneath the literal.

7. Non-literary: visual and design (essential for Paper 1)

Because Language and Literature Paper 1 is on non-literary texts, visual and design choices are as mark-bearing as words. Treat an image with the same three-step rigour.

Layout
The arrangement of elements on the page. Ask: what does the eye meet first and what is marginalised? Typical effect: hierarchy of importance; guides (or manipulates) attention.
Typography
Font, size, weight, spacing. Ask: does a heavy sans-serif shout authority while a thin serif whispers elegance? Typical effect: tone and credibility; signals seriousness or play.
Colour
Hue, saturation, contrast. Ask: what is coloured and what is drained, and what does the palette associate? Typical effect: mood, association, and symbolic contrast.
Salience
The degree to which an element stands out. Ask: what has been made prominent and at what cost? Typical effect: directs attention; implies value.
Gaze
Who looks at whom in an image. Ask: is the subject returning the gaze or objectified? Typical effect: power relations; invites or refuses viewer complicity.
Vector / direction
The lines that lead the eye (a pointing arm, a road). Ask: where does the composition push the viewer? Typical effect: narrative direction; subtle instruction.
Social distance
How close the framing is (intimate, social, public). Ask: what relationship with the subject does the distance create? Typical effect: intimacy or detachment.
Camera angle
High (diminishes), low (aggrandises), eye-level (neutral). Ask: from whose height is the subject shown? Typical effect: status and judgement.
Framing / cropping
What is included and what is cut off. Ask: what is excluded and what does the cut imply? Typical effect: selection as argument; omission as statement.
Modality
The perceived realism of an image (Kress & van Leeuwen). Ask: how "real" does it claim to be, and is that claim warranted? Typical effect: authority of the visual.
Interplay of text and image
How words and pictures cooperate or clash. Ask: does the image confirm, extend or contradict the headline? Typical effect: a combined message neither carries alone — or a revealing contradiction.
White space
The deliberate emptiness. Ask: what does the silence around an element do? Typical effect: focus, luxury, or a withheld answer.
How to use this glossary in an exam

Do not tick devices off a list. Read the text first, form an interpretive claim, then reach for the glossary to name the choice you already noticed and to sharpen the question you will answer. The device is evidence for your argument, not the argument itself. A top Criterion B paragraph uses two or three devices in service of one claim, not ten devices in search of one.
