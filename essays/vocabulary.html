The analytical vocabulary bank

The fastest way to move a response from description to analysis is to change the verb. "The writer shows" tells the examiner nothing; "the writer foregrounds / subverts / positions" tells them you are doing Criterion B. This bank is the analytical subset of vocabulary — the verbs, transitions and adjectives of argument. It is deliberately separate from the daily vocabulary programme, which builds broad B2–C1 reading vocabulary; here the words exist to be used in your writing, not recognised in theirs.

Criterion B + D Verbs · transitions · adjectives Command-term glossary

1. Why this page exists

Two criteria depend on word choice. Criterion D (Language) rewards "carefully chosen" words and "relevant terminology"; Criterion B (Analysis & evaluation) rewards explaining how choices shape meaning. A precise verb does both at once: it names the move and commits to an argument about effect. "This destabilises the reader's trust" is already an analytical claim; "this shows the reader doesn't trust" is not.

The weak-verb habit

The most common D-damaging habit is the weak verb followed by a noun: "the writer shows the theme of power", "this makes the reader feel sad", "the text gives a sense of…". These are grammatically fine and analytically empty. Replace the verb with one that carries an argument.

2. Strong analytical verbs

Use these to name what the choice does. Each carries a built-in claim about effect, which is exactly what B rewards.

A caution

These verbs are precise only if the analysis behind them is real. "This destabilises" with no following explanation is a heavier word doing the same empty job as "this shows". The verb opens the claim; the sentence must close it.

3. Verbs and phrases to retire

shows → reveals / demonstrates / constructs
tells us → argues / implies / positions
makes the reader feel → implicates / unsettles / distances
gives a sense of → evokes / encodes / performs
is about → interrogates / mediates / stages
tries to → seeks to / labours to / performs
the author wants to → the text positions / the form encodes
good / nice / interesting → precise evaluative adjectives (below)

4. Transitions and comparison signals

Paper 2 and the HL Essay live or die on integration. These frames keep two texts (or two moves) in the same sentence instead of two separate paragraphs.

Signalling similarity

"Like A, B…"; "This parallels…"; "Both texts converge on…"; "Where A establishes X, B likewise…"; "The same logic recurs in…"

Signalling difference

"Whereas A…, B…"; "The divergence lies in…"; "B inverts A's…"; "A externalises what B internalises"; "The contrast turns on…"

Signalling development

"Building on this…"; "This complicates the earlier claim that…"; "The passage shifts from… to…"

Signalling evaluation

"The effect is to implicate…"; "What emerges is…"; "The strategy ultimately serves to…"

5. Evaluative adjectives

Use these to judge (evaluate) rather than merely describe. Pair them with evidence.

pervasive present throughout, inescapable
tenuous weak, barely held
ambivalent holding two attitudes at once
resonant echoing with larger meaning
dissonant clashing, unresolved
equivocal deliberately unclear or double
strategic serving a calculated purpose
involuntary not chosen by the speaker/character
fraught loaded with tension or risk
loaded carrying charged association
restrained holding back; understated
contradictory working against itself

6. Command-term glossary

The IB builds its questions from a fixed set of command terms. Misreading one loses marks before you start, even with strong analysis. These are the definitions the IB publishes; the "what it demands" column is the English-A-specific translation.

Command termIB definition (abridged)What it demands in English A
AnalyseBreak down to bring out essential elements or structure.Show how parts (language, structure, form) create meaning. Not summary.
CompareGive an account of similarities, referring to both throughout.In Paper 2, keep both works present in every point; do not sequence.
ContrastGive an account of differences, referring to both throughout.Name the divergence as a claim, not a list.
Compare and contrastSimilarities and differences, both throughout.The richest Paper 2 prompt; reward is in the interplay.
CommentGive a judgement based on available evidence.A reasoned evaluation, not an opinion without proof.
DiscussConsidered, balanced review; clear conclusion supported by evidence.At least two sides, then your argued verdict.

ExploreUndertake a systematic process of discovery.Open investigation with a direction, not a wander.

7. Build your own phrasebook

The verbs above are a starting set, not a ceiling. The durable habit is to keep a running list of three or four verbs and two transitions you have actually used and had confirmed as strong, then deploy them deliberately in timed practice. A phrasebook you wrote is worth more than a list you read.

A minimal phrasebook template

My analytical verbs: 1. ________ 2. ________ 3. ________

My comparison frames: 1. "Whereas A…, B…" 2. "Both converge on…, but…"

My evaluation move: "The effect is to ________, which means…"

One sentence I am proud of: "________"

Fill these from your own drafts, not from this page. The point is ownership under the clock.
