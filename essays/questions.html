Question bank & model plans

A bank of practice prompts with model comparative plans, HL Essay line-of-inquiry starters, and the recurring shapes of Paper 1 guiding questions. Everything here is generic and transferable: it uses "Text A / Text B" and universal themes rather than the four set works, because those live in the course pages and you should learn to generate the plan for any pair. The value is in the method of planning, not in memorising answers — Paper 2 is closed book and Paper 1 is on texts you have never seen.

How to use this bank

Pick a prompt, write the comparative thesis in one sentence before you touch evidence, then test it: does it name both works and a divergence? If yes, the body paragraphs can alternate between the texts. If no, rewrite it. The model plans below are examples of that test passed.

1. Paper 2 prompts grouped by concept

Paper 2 prompts are frequently built on one of the seven concepts. For each concept below, three prompts and a model comparative thesis for the first. Write your own theses for the other two before checking the pattern.

Identity

PromptModel comparative thesis
"To what extent do the works you have studied present identity as shaped more by society than by the individual?" Both texts construct identity as socially imposed — A through institutional labelling, B through domestic expectation — yet A leaves a margin for resistance that B withholds entirely, suggesting identity is inescapable in the second and contested in the first.
"Compare the ways in which two works explore the relationship between language and identity."(Plan: both tie identity to a lost or imposed language; diverge on whether the loss is mourned or weaponised.)
"How do your two works represent the self under pressure?"(Plan: A externalises pressure through physical setting; B internalises it through fractured narrative voice.)

Culture

PromptModel comparative thesis
"To what extent do the works question the values of the cultures they depict?" Both texts expose a cultural value as coercive — A satirises consumer culture, B indicts filial duty — but A distances the critique through irony while B implicates the narrator, so the reader is complicit in the second and safe in the first.
"Compare how two works use setting to construct a culture."(Plan: A's setting is a character; B's is a vacuum. Diverge on whether culture sustains or erases the individual.)
"How do your works represent tradition and change?"(Plan: both stage a clash of generations; diverge on whether the change is redemptive or corrosive.)

Creativity

PromptModel comparative thesis
"To what extent do the works present creativity as a form of resistance?" Both texts frame making as defiance — A through a marginal artist, B through storytelling under censorship — yet A celebrates the individual while B shows creativity as survival for a community, shifting the value from self to collective.
"Compare the ways in which two works blur the boundary between maker and made."(Plan: both use a self-referential form; diverge on whether the blurring liberates or traps.)
"How do your works represent the imagination under constraint?"(Plan: A's imagination is a refuge; B's is a record of what was forbidden.)

Communication

PromptModel comparative thesis
"Compare the ways in which two works expose the limits of communication." Both texts locate meaning in what is withheld — A through silence between lovers, B through official euphemism — but A mourns the failure while B reveals it as a strategy of power, so silence is tragic in one and tactical in the other.
"To what extent do the works show language controlling as much as expressing?"(Plan: both show language as action; diverge on whether the speaker is victim or agent.)
"How do your two works represent the audience's role in making meaning?"(Plan: A demands an active reader; B supplies a passive one. Diverge on who holds authority.)

Perspective

PromptModel comparative thesis
"To what extent do the works suggest that perspective determines truth?" Both texts make truth relative to vantage — A through an unreliable narrator, B through competing testimonies — yet A traps the reader in one deception while B offers a route out, implying perspective is a prison in the first and a choice in the second.
"Compare how two works use narrative voice to shape interpretation."(Plan: A's voice is intimate and partial; B's is detached and partial. Diverge on what each hides.)
"How do your works represent the bias embedded in memory?"(Plan: both show memory as edited; diverge on whether the edit protects or deceives.)

Transformation

PromptModel comparative thesis
"To what extent do the works show individuals transformed by the worlds they inhabit?" Both texts treat transformation as coercive — A through war, B through migration — but A returns the character changed and refusing, while B leaves them changed and assimilated, so transformation is resisted in one and absorbed in the other.
"Compare the ways in which two works rewrite an earlier text or tradition."(Plan: both are intertextual; diverge on whether the rewriting honours or subverts the source.)
"How do your works represent the reader's transformation?"(Plan: A changes the reader's sympathy; B changes their certainty. Diverge on what is left behind.)

Representation

PromptModel comparative thesis
"To what extent do the works reveal the politics of representation?" Both texts show representation as an act of power — A by omitting a group, B by stereotyping one — yet A's omission is exposed as a gap while B's stereotype is reproduced uncritically, so the politics are named in one and concealed in the other.
"Compare how two works construct a social group through language."(Plan: both define a group by a repeated label; diverge on whether the label is reclaimed or imposed.)
"How do your works represent reality — as found or as constructed?"(Plan: A presents reality as given; B presents it as staged. Diverge on the reader's trust.)

2. HL Essay — line-of-inquiry starters

The HL Essay begins with a line of inquiry: a question, not a topic. The table moves each from a weak topic-statement to a sharp, sustainably narrow inquiry. Use the right-hand column as the shape; substitute your own work.

#Weak (topic)Strong (line of inquiry)
1"How does the novel explore memory?""How does a non-linear, unreliable narrator suggest memory is self-protection rather than record?"
2"The theme of power in the play.""How does the play locate corruption in everyday language rather than in acts of state?"
3"Gender in the poetry collection.""How does the speaker use the imperative to expose how gender is enforced through daily instruction?"
4"The use of imagery.""How does the sustained marine metaphor reframe grief as an environment rather than an event?"
5"Colonialism in the novel.""How does the text's use of an imperial language ironise the narrator's claim to liberation?"
6"The film's visual style.""How does a fixed, high camera angle turn the protagonist into a specimen, implicating the viewer in surveillance?"
7"The campaign's persuasion.""How does the interplay of text and desaturated image construct desire as a form of survival?"
8"The short story's ending.""How does the withheld resolution force the reader to co-author the story's moral?"
9"The speech's rhetoric.""How does anaphora build collective identity while excluding a named Other?"
10"The memoir's voice.""How does a child's limited syntax perform the erosion of agency under authority?"
11"The graphic novel's form.""How does the gutter (the space between panels) stage the impossibility of representing trauma?"
12"Nature in the poems.""How does personification of landscape indict human custody of the environment rather than celebrate it?"
The HL Essay planning test

A good line of inquiry passes three checks: (1) it is a question, not a statement; (2) you can answer it in 1,200–1,500 words without summarising the plot; (3) it points at specific formal choices, not a theme. If your inquiry fails (2) or (3), narrow it until it does.

3. Paper 1 — the recurring shapes of guiding questions

Paper 1 guiding questions (which are optional but a useful foothold) cluster into a small set of shapes. Knowing the shapes means you have a routine before you open the unseen text. The examples below are drawn from past Language and Literature Paper 1 papers (e.g. May 2021, May 2022) and show the pattern; the exact texts change every session.

ShapePast-paper example (illustrative)Your routine
Text + image interplay"How do text and image work together to characterise the writer's lifestyle?" (2022)Map which meaning each mode carries; find the contradiction or reinforcement.
Features → impression"Discuss how different features are used on this webpage to create an impression of Indian culture."Name 3 features, each tied to one part of the impression.
Diction / imagery → effect"How and to what effect are diction and imagery used in this text?"Pick a cluster of words; state the effect, then the meaning.
Tone → opinion"How and to what effect is tone used to express the author's opinion in this column?"Locate the tonal shift; tie it to the argument's turn.
Narrative voice → immediacy"How does the narrative voice create immediacy while involving the reader?"Identify the voice (first/second); show the involvement device.
Structure → idea"How is the narrative structure used to explore ideas about identity?" (2021)Describe the structure; explain what the shape argues.
Visual features → message"Discuss how visual features are used in this text to underline a serious message."Treat each visual choice (colour, gaze, framing) as evidence.
Audience → appeal"Comment on how the interplay of text and images is used to appeal to multiple audiences."Show the layered address; who is spoken to by which mode.
Remember

The guiding question is not compulsory and is identical at SL and HL. A strong analysis on a different thread scores as well as a compliant one; a descriptive answer to the guiding question scores nothing extra. Use the question to find an entry point, then follow the analysis where the text leads.

4. Weak → strong: the global issue (for the Individual Oral)

The Individual Oral prompt asks you to examine how a global issue is presented through the content and form of one literary work and one non-literary body of work. The issue must have wide-scale significance, transnational nature, and local application. The move from theme to issue:

Theme (too broad — a noun)Issue (arguable and specific)
"Gender""How institutional and domestic language enforces gender norms"
"Power""How the abuse of political power operates through censorship and propaganda"
"Migration""How borders are constructed as natural rather than political through everyday rhetoric"
"Technology""How algorithmic feed design manufactures consent by concealing selection"
"Racism""How ordinary, polite language normalises racial hierarchy"
