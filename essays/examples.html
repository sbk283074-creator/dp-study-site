Annotated model paragraphs

Models beat advice. Each passage below is generic and transferable — built on placeholder works ("Text A", "the campaign", "the poem") so you can lift the structure of the thinking, not the content. After each model, the commentary names the analytical move each sentence performs — reading, analysis, focus, prose — and says why. Then a set of weak/strong pairs shows the single most common gap: the distance between naming a device and analysing its work.

1. How to read these models

Treat each model as a sentence-level demonstration of the five moves from the overview: locate a choice → name the technique → explain the effect → tie to meaning → (where required) tie to the framing issue. The commentary after each model is the examiner's view, not part of the student's answer. Notice that none of the models is long; analysis is won in the middle sentences, not by word count.

2. Model comparative paragraph (Paper 2)

Both Text A and Text B stage identity as something imposed rather than chosen, but they diverge sharply in the room they leave for resistance. In A, the protagonist is defined by an official label — the repeated bureaucratic noun "ward" that strips the character of a name across three chapters — so identity is administered from outside. B makes the same point through the domestic instead of the institutional: the protagonist is "taught" who to be by a sequence of imperatives from a parent. The divergence is the payoff: A's ward can refuse by going silent, a small but real withholding the text honours, whereas B's child has no silent space at all, so the imposition is total. Both, then, indict the societies that author identity — but A implies the self can retreat, and B implies it cannot. — illustrative model paragraph (placeholder works A and B)

Reading

References are specific and support the claim (the repeated "ward", the imperatives). The reading of both texts is accurate and interpretive, not plot summary.

Analysis

This is where the work is. Each device is tied to an effect ("strips… of a name", "no silent space") and then to meaning ("identity is administered", "imposition is total"). The comparison is integrated — both works appear in every sentence.

Focus

One claim (divergence in room for resistance) runs the whole paragraph; the topic sentence states it and the close returns to it.

Prose

Verbs do the work: "administered", "imposed", "withholding", "indict". Register is consistent and academic.

3. Model Paper 1 commentary excerpt (non-literary)

The campaign's controlling move is to render an act of consumption as an act of survival. The product occupies the only saturated colour in an otherwise drained, grey composition: every element around it is desaturated, so the eye is led — by salience, not by argument — to treat the object as the single point of life in a deadened world. The headline performs the same trick in words: "You need this" reframes a luxury as a necessity, and the low camera angle on the model turns a person into a specimen the viewer inspects rather than meets. Together the image and the text construct desire as need, which is precisely the claim a critical reader should resist — the technique is most powerful where it appears most natural. — illustrative Paper 1 excerpt (non-literary advertisement)
Why it scores on Paper 1

It reads the non-literary text with the same rigour as literature: colour and salience are named as choices, each is tied to an effect on the viewer, the image–text interplay is analysed rather than described, and the conclusion judges the strategy. The guiding question was optional; this answer followed its own thread and would score regardless.

4. Model HL Essay introduction + first body paragraph

This essay asks how the novel uses a non-linear, unreliable narrator to suggest that memory is a strategy of self-protection rather than a record of the past. The line of inquiry matters because the text is repeatedly read as a trauma narrative when its form argues the opposite: that what the narrator cannot sequence, she cannot bear. The first chapter establishes the pattern — events arrive out of order, and each return to the "same" memory changes a detail, so the reader learns to distrust the teller before pitying her. The effect is to relocate the injury from the event to the act of telling: the fracture in syntax is the wound, and the novel's refusal to resolve it declines the comfort of closure the genre usually offers. — illustrative HL Essay opening (placeholder novel)

Reading

The line of inquiry is stated as a question and the text is understood on its own terms (non-linear, unreliable).

Analysis

Analysis is sustained: narrative form is tied to meaning (syntax = wound) and to an evaluative claim (declines closure).

Focus

The line of inquiry returns in the body; the paragraph develops it rather than drifting.

Prose

Controlled academic register; terminology (line of inquiry, unreliable narrator, genre) used correctly.

5. Weak → strong pairs

The same move, done two ways. The weak version names; the strong version analyses. Read the right-hand column first to see what is missing on the left.

The one-sentence test

After you write any analytical sentence, ask: "If I removed the work's name, would this still say something specific?" If the sentence survives as "the writer uses X to show Y is bad," it is feature-spotting. If it collapses without the specific choice you named, it is analysis. This single test catches most B-marks lost.

6. A note on transferability

None of the models above is keyed to a set work, on purpose. The course pages hold the work-specific studies; this page teaches the shape of a scoring paragraph so you can produce your own on any text, seen or unseen. The durable skill is the structure: a claim that names a divergence or a controlling move, followed by choices tied to effects tied to meaning — not the particular example.
