Writing for IB English A: Language and Literature

IB English is not a talent test, it is a method test. Every mark on every writing component — Paper 1, Paper 2, the HL Essay and the Individual Oral — comes from the same small set of moves: locate a deliberate choice, name the technique, explain the effect, tie it to meaning, and (for two of the four components) tie it to a global issue. This section is the skills layer that drills those moves: essay types and registers, a device glossary, an analytical vocabulary bank, a question bank with model plans, annotated example paragraphs, and a toolkit of checklists. It sits beside the English A course pages, which walk you through each paper in detail and hold the work-specific studies.

2. The assessment at a glance

Language and Literature has four writing components. Two are external exams (Paper 1, Paper 2), one is written coursework (the HL Essay, HL only), and one is a recorded spoken response (the Individual Oral). The table is the spine of everything else on this site.

ComponentSL weightingHL weightingTimeMarksWhat it is
Paper 1 — Guided textual analysis 35%35% SL 1h 15m · HL 2h 15m SL /20 (one response) · HL /40 (two responses, /20 each) A guided analysis of unseen non-literary passage(s). SL chooses one of two; HL analyses both, each as a separate response.
Paper 2 — Comparative essay 35%25% 1h 45m (both levels) /30 Answer one of four prompts as a comparative essay on two literary works studied during the course (closed book).
HL Essay —20% Coursework (not timed) /20 A 1,200–1,500 word essay on one literary work or one non-literary body of work studied, driven by a student-chosen line of inquiry.
Individual Oral 30%20% 15 min (10 + 5) /40 A prepared spoken response on how one literary work and one non-literary body of work approach a common global issue, followed by 5 minutes of teacher questions.
Two facts students routinely get wrong

Paper 1 in Language and Literature is on non-literary texts. In Literature it is literary passages; in Language and Literature it is advertisements, speeches, articles, blogs, comics, infographics and the like. So your Paper 1 drill is in reading non-literary rhetoric, not poems. Paper 2 is always on two literary works in both subjects. The HL Essay is 20 marks, not 40, and the Individual Oral is 40 marks, not 30 — the two are easy to transpose.

3. The four assessment objectives

Every component is marked against the same four assessment objectives (AO1–AO4). They are not a separate rubric — they are the meaning behind the four criteria letters (A–D) you will see on every mark scheme. Learn them as a single chain:

ObjectiveWhat it asksThe criterion letter it becomes
AO1 · Knowledge, understanding & interpretation Do you understand what the text says and means, and can you support your reading with well-chosen references? Criterion A (Understanding & interpretation)
AO2 · Analysis & evaluation Do you explain how language, structure, technique and form shape meaning and effect — and judge why it matters? Criterion B (Analysis & evaluation)
AO3 · Organisation, focus & development Is your response coherent, balanced and sustained, with a clear line of argument and an appropriate register? Criterion C (Focus & organisation)
AO4 · Language Is your own writing clear, accurate, varied and in an appropriate academic register, with correct terminology? Criterion D (Language)

Notice the pattern: A and D are about the text and your prose; B and C are where most marks (and most lost marks) live. The single most common failure is a response that is strong on A and D but never leaves description to do B — it names devices and paraphrases plot but never explains how a choice operates. The annotated examples page is built around exactly this gap.

4. The seven concepts and three areas of exploration

The concepts are not directly assessed, but they organise the whole course and surface constantly in Paper 2 prompts and as lenses for the HL Essay and the Individual Oral. Treat them as "touching points" you return to, not as essay headings you copy out.

Identity

How texts construct and present individuals, groups and voices — and how your own identity shapes interpretation.

Culture

How texts reflect or challenge the values, beliefs and traditions of their time and place.

Creativity

Imagination, originality, the writer's choices, and the reader's role in making meaning.

Communication

The relationship between writer, text and reader: audience, purpose, assumption, multiple meanings.

Perspective

How meaning shifts with position — narrative voice, bias, reliability, context.

Transformation

Change and intertextuality — how texts borrow from, adapt and reinterpret other texts.

Representation

How reality is depicted; what is emphasised or omitted; how social groups and ideas are constructed.

Three areas of exploration

(1) Readers, writers, texts · (2) Time and space · (3) Intertextuality: connecting texts. Every text is studied through one or more of these.

5. The global issue

The global issue is the through-line of the Individual Oral (and a productive lens everywhere else). The Language A guide defines it by three properties — a strong issue must have all three:

  • Wide-scale significance — it matters on a large social scale, not as an isolated incident.
  • Transnational nature — it crosses national boundaries; it is felt in more than one country or culture.
  • Local application — it is experienced on a local, everyday basis, by communities and individuals.

Students are guided to derive issues from five fields of inquiry: Culture, Identity and Community · Beliefs, Values and Education · Politics, Power and Justice · Arts, Creativity and the Imagination · Science, Technology and the Environment. The recurring mistake is to pick a theme ("power", "gender") instead of an issue ("how institutional language normalises racial hierarchy", "how the abuse of political power operates through censorship and propaganda"). An issue is arguable and specific; a theme is a noun. The question bank shows the weak→strong move in practice.

Why this section exists

Because the four writing tasks are not four different subjects. They are four applications of one procedure. A student who revises by re-reading set works is preparing for the 35% of the course that is predictable and neglecting the 65% that is procedural. Paper 1 is on texts you have never seen; the HL Essay and the IO reward a method, not content. So this section teaches the method once, in one place, and lets the course pages apply it to specific papers and works.

6. The scoring mindset: the five moves

If you remember nothing else, remember this chain. It is what separates a 5/5 on Criterion B from a 2/5:

  • Locate a choice. The writer did not have to write it this way. Find the specific word, structure, image or design decision.
  • Name the technique. Use precise terminology (a metaphor, a chiastic reversal, a low camera angle) — not "it's effective".
  • Explain the effect. What does the choice make the reader see, feel or question? This is the step most responses skip.
  • Tie it to meaning. What larger claim about the text, the world, or the global issue does this choice support?
  • (Where required) Tie it to the global issue. For the IO especially: how does this choice present the issue you have chosen?
    • The trap: feature-spotting

    "The writer uses a metaphor to show that war is bad" is feature-spotting: it names a device and states a theme. It earns nothing on Criterion B. "The metaphor fuses the battlefield with a kitchen — 'the front line simmered' — which domesticates mass death and implicates the comfortable reader as a bystander at the stove" is analysis: choice → technique → effect → meaning. The second sentence is longer, but the marks are not for length; they are for the link between the choice and its work. See the examples page for the full weak/strong contrast.
