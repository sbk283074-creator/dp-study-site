The craft of writing analytical essays

This section is about writing, not about the exam. It drills the transferable skill of building a convincing analytical essay — how to read a text, locate what is doing work, name it, and argue its effect — using the kinds of questions IB sets and the essay shapes IB rewards as the training ground. The companion English A course pages handle the exam machinery: rubrics, weightings, the set works and their studies. Here we learn the method once, in one place, so it transfers to any text you are handed, seen or unseen.

2. The craft spine — the five moves

If you remember nothing else, remember this chain. It is what separates a paragraph that analyses from one that merely describes:

1
Locate a choice. The writer did not have to write it this way. Find the specific word, structure, image or design decision.
2
Name the technique. Use precise terminology (a metaphor, a chiastic reversal, a low camera angle) — not "it's effective".
3
Explain the effect. What does the choice make the reader see, feel or question? This is the step most responses skip.
4
Tie it to meaning. What larger claim about the text — or, where the task calls for it, the world — does this choice support?
5
(Where required) Tie it to the framing issue. For the Individual Oral especially: how does this choice present the issue you have chosen?
The trap: feature-spotting

"The writer uses a metaphor to show that war is bad" is feature-spotting: it names a device and states a theme. It earns nothing. "The metaphor fuses the battlefield with a kitchen — 'the front line simmered' — which domesticates mass death and implicates the comfortable reader as a bystander at the stove" is analysis: choice → technique → effect → meaning. The second sentence is longer, but the marks are not for length; they are for the link between the choice and its work. See the examples page for the full weak/strong contrast.

3. The four essay situations

IB English A asks you to write in four situations. The craft on this site applies to all four; what changes is the register and where the weight sits. We use them here as the training ground, not as exam briefs — for the rules of each, see the course pages.

SituationWhat the writing is doingWhere the craft weight sits
Commentary (unseen non-literary text)A focused analysis that stays inside one text and argues a reading of how it works.Effect and meaning on a single text; no generalising outward.
Comparative argument (two works)One essay that holds two works together on a shared axis and argues a divergence.The comparison itself — integrated, not two essays side by side.
Sustained investigation (one work / body of work)A scholarly essay driven by a sharp line of inquiry, sustained over many words.Precision of focus; depth on specific passages.
Spoken analysis (on a framing issue)A prepared, not scripted, talk linking two texts to a chosen issue.Constant return to the issue; depth on a few moments.

The essay types & registers page works through the voice and shape of each; the question bank gives you prompts in each situation to practise on.
