Paper 2 — the comparative essay

One hour forty-five minutes. A list of six questions. You pick one and write an essay comparing two of the four works you studied. This is 35% of your grade and it is the component where confident, fluent students most often underperform — because they write two mini-essays glued together with the word "similarly". A comparative essay is not two essays. It is one argument that could not exist without both texts. This page gives you the machinery for that argument.

1. What the exam is really testing

There is one question underneath every Paper 2 question on the paper: can you hold two texts in your head at the same time? Not "can you write about Othello" — you had two years to prepare that. The examiner wants to see a mind moving between two works and using each to illuminate the other.

Criterion (5 marks)What it rewardsThe band-7 behaviourThe band-3 behaviour
A — Knowledge and understanding Secure, specific knowledge of both works, used to support an argument rather than displayed. Precise references — act and scene, poem title, the exact moment in the novella — woven into claims. Knowledge of context is attached to a choice in a single clause. Long plot summary at the start of each paragraph. Vague recall ("in the play, Iago tricks Othello"). Confuses the two works' plots because neither is really known.
B — Analysis and evaluation Authorial choices analysed and evaluated, in both texts. Technique → effect → meaning, in both works, in the same paragraph. Compares the choices, not just the themes: "where García Márquez withholds, Barnes over-shares." Theme comparison with no technique: "both texts are about revenge." Feature lists from one work followed by feature lists from the other.
C — Focus and organisation A comparative thesis sustained through every paragraph. Both texts appear in every body paragraph. The essay develops: paragraph 3 builds on paragraph 2. The conclusion states what the comparison revealed. Two halves: paragraphs 1–4 on text X, paragraphs 5–8 on text Y, one sentence of comparison in the introduction and one in the conclusion. This is the classic band-3/4 structure.
D — Language Clarity, precision, register, control. Comparative vocabulary used flexibly; varied sentence length; thesis language ("whereas", "by contrast", "more radically") doing structural work. "Both texts… Also… Similarly…" repeated. Clunky, over-long sentences with ideas bolted on with "and".
The comparative emphasis

Examiners are explicitly instructed to reward sustained comparison. In practice this means one test: open your essay at any body paragraph. Are both works in it? If you can find a paragraph with only one work in it, you have written two essays and your C mark is capped. The fix is mechanical, not intellectual — see §4.

2. Choosing a question in five minutes

Six questions. You get about five minutes to decide, and the decision is worth more than any single paragraph you will write. A wrong question costs you 10 marks; a clumsy paragraph costs you one.

How to use it

Take each question in turn and mark it with three highlighter passes. This takes about 40 seconds per question.

  1. Circle the concept word. Every question contains one: identity, culture, creativity, communication, perspective, transformation, representation — or a near-synonym (reputation, memory, authority, voice). If you cannot circle one, the question is probably about theme and probably weak.
  2. Underline the authorial-choice demand. Look for how, in what ways, through what techniques, to what effect. Better questions ask how; weak questions only ask what. If there is no how, you must supply one yourself.
  3. Box the comparison demand. Compare, contrast, to what extent, discuss the ways in which… differ. "To what extent" demands a measured judgement (largely / only partially). "Compare" genuinely means both similarity and difference.
  4. Then, and only then, test your works. Ask: can I name four specific moments — two from each work — that answer this? Four is your minimum; you need four body paragraphs.
  5. Discard on evidence, not on interest. The most interesting question with three moments loses to the less interesting one with eight. Write down the moments before you commit.
Common trap

Do not choose a question because it contains a word you recognise. "Compare the presentation of conflict" looks safe and is a trap: it is so broad that every paragraph becomes a different essay. The best questions are usually the narrowest ones: how does the text use silence, how does it control what the reader knows and when, how does it present the authority to tell a story. Narrow questions produce specific evidence, and specific evidence produces marks.

3. Two architectures, and the honest trade-off

Point-by-pointBlock
Shape Every paragraph takes one idea and treats it in both works. Work A then Work B, every time. Section 1 treats work A across three or four ideas; section 2 treats work B across the same ideas, in the same order.
Advantage Comparison is structural: the examiner cannot accuse you of writing two essays. Easier to keep both texts alive. Difference emerges naturally inside each paragraph. Allows you to develop a longer, subtler argument about a single work's technique before moving. Good when one work is doing something genuinely intricate.
Danger Can become mechanical ping-pong — "A does this. B does that." — with no judgement about which is more effective. The two sections drift apart. Without explicit signposting, the examiner reads two essays, and C collapses.
Verdict for you Use this by default. It is safer, and safe is what you need under time pressure. Use only if you can write a linking sentence at the end of every block paragraph that names the corresponding move in the other work.
Exam technique

Whatever architecture you choose, the order of works must be constant. If you discuss Othello first in paragraph 2, discuss it first in every paragraph. Examiners read for patterns; a consistent order makes your comparison legible in seconds and costs you nothing.

4. The comparative thesis

A Paper 2 thesis must contain both a similarity and a difference. A thesis with only similarity ("both works show that honour is destructive") produces a list. A thesis with only difference ("the two works are completely opposed") produces two essays. You need the tension.

Although [Work A] and [Work B] both [shared function or concern], they differ in that [A] [verb of method] whereas [B] [contrasting method], and this difference reveals [the insight the comparison produces].

Four versions, written for your four works:

  • "Although Chronicle of a Death Foretold and Othello both present honour as a public property administered by others, García Márquez disperses the guilt across an entire town that narrates in the first-person plural, whereas Shakespeare concentrates it in a single manipulator who speaks in the intimate second person — and the difference reveals that collective honour kills through inertia while individual malice kills through design."
  • "Although both The Sense of an Ending and The World's Wife rewrite an inherited story in order to reclaim it, Barnes gives the revision to a narrator who does not realise he is revising, whereas Duffy gives it to speakers who know exactly what they are doing — so that Barnes produces dramatic irony where Duffy produces testimony."
  • "Although Othello and The World's Wife are both preoccupied with the authority to describe a woman, Shakespeare withholds Desdemona's own account almost entirely, while Duffy's entire collection consists of women who were previously only described; the contrast shows that silence and monologue are two different strategies for the same political end."
  • "Although Chronicle of a Death Foretold and The Sense of an Ending both depend on a narrator reconstructing the past, García Márquez's investigator knows the ending and cannot prevent it, whereas Tony Webster does not know the ending and cannot prevent it — a distinction that turns the first into tragedy and the second into self-indictment."
Why this works Why a thesis must carry both similarity and difference
  1. The similarity creates the grounds for comparison. Without it, why are these two texts in the same essay? It also generates your paragraph topics: each paragraph is one aspect of the shared concern.
  2. The difference creates the argument. A comparison that finds only resemblance has not told the reader anything they could not get from either text alone. The difference is the new knowledge, and new knowledge is what criterion A and B reward.
  3. The insight clause stops you writing a book report. "…and this reveals that" forces you to say what the comparison produced. If you cannot finish that clause, your two works are not yet doing comparative work — change one of them.
  4. Practical test: read your thesis aloud. If it sounds like two separate sentences joined by "and", rewrite it. It should sound like one claim with a hinge in the middle.

5. Paragraph anatomy and the comparison vocabulary bank

How to use it

One body paragraph, six sentences, in this order. This is the single most valuable pattern on this page — memorise it and your C mark stops being a lottery.

  1. Claim — one sentence stating the aspect of the argument this paragraph handles. "Both texts locate honour not in the individual but in the audience watching him."
  2. Evidence A — a precise moment from the first work, with a short quotation.
  3. Technique + effect in A — name the choice and say what it does.
  4. Transition — one comparative word or phrase that signals the move. This is where most students fail, because they use "similarly" when they mean "by contrast".
  5. Evidence B + technique + effect in B — same shape, ideally echoing the syntax of the A half so the comparison is visible on the page.
  6. Mini-synthesis — one sentence judging which is more effective, or why the difference matters. "Where the town's passivity reads as fate, Iago's activity reads as evil — and the reader is left more disturbed by the first."
Use when you mean…Words and frames
Difference (contrast)whereas · by contrast · conversely · in a move that reverses this · where X does ___, Y instead ___ · X opts for ___, while Y refuses ___
Similarity (with a twist)in a comparable move · likewise · X's equivalent strategy is · both writers resort to · the parallel is not exact, however
Degree (this earns judgement marks)more radically · far less explicitly · to a greater extent · only partially · superficially similar but fundamentally · X goes further than Y in ___
Concession (sophisticated)although · granted that · admittedly · for all this · it would be too simple to say
Synthesis (end of paragraph)the effect of this divergence is · what this contrast exposes is · read together, the two works suggest · the comparison therefore reveals
Structural signpostinghaving established ___ in X, the same question in Y · the second point of contact is · the pattern recurs, differently,
Common trap

"Similarly" is the most abused word in Paper 2. Students use it to mean "and now, the other text". If the two points are not actually similar, use "in the other work", "the equivalent moment in Y", or — best — "where X…, Y…". Precision here costs nothing and signals control of criterion D.

6. Six original exam-style questions with full plans

These are written for your four works. Each plan gives you the best pairing and the reason, a thesis, four body paragraphs and a conclusion. Use them as drills: write the introduction and paragraph 1 in 20 minutes, timed.

Question 1 Community and the individual

Question. Compare the ways in which two of the works you have studied present the pressure of a community's expectations on an individual's choices.

Best pairing: Chronicle of a Death Foretold + Othello. Both are built on a public code of honour that is administered by witnesses rather than by the self, and both end with a killing that the community could have prevented. The comparison is genuinely productive because the mechanism differs completely: diffusion versus concentration.

Thesis. Although both texts locate honour in the gaze of others, García Márquez distributes responsibility across a town that narrates itself in the first-person plural, whereas Shakespeare funnels it through one manipulator who controls what each character sees — and the difference shows that a community can murder by omission far more efficiently than an individual can by design.

  1. Paragraph 1 — honour as public property. In Chronicle, the narrator's census-like catalogue of the town establishes honour as an audit conducted by neighbours; in Othello, the Venetian senate scene (1.3) establishes reputation as something granted by an institution. Compare: bureaucratic collective versus institutional hierarchy.
  2. Paragraph 2 — the failure to warn. The town's "we all knew" and the series of non-warnings (Clotilde Armenta, the milk shop, the priest) against Iago's calculated half-warnings ("Ha, I like not that"). Contrast inertia with engineering.
  3. Paragraph 3 — the language of the code. García Márquez's flat, documentary register and repeated formulas of honour against Iago's shifting register — blunt prose with Roderigo, elevated verse with Othello. Both show a code that is linguistically available to everyone, but only Iago weaponises register.
  4. Paragraph 4 — the ending as verdict. Santiago's death at his own door, witnessed by everyone, against Othello's final self-narration and suicide before the ambassadors. Compare what each community does afterwards: the town disperses into exile and amnesia; Venice simply takes the house.

Conclusion. Both works end not with justice but with administration — a town that files the death away, a state that records the "heavy act". The comparison suggests that a community's honour code is survivable only because no individual in it is ever required to own a decision.

Question 2 Accounts the reader doubts

Question. Discuss how two of the works you have studied use a narrator or speaker whose account the reader is led to mistrust.

Best pairing: The Sense of an Ending + Chronicle of a Death Foretold. Both texts are retrospective reconstructions by a first-person narrator with an interest in the version he produces. Crucially they fail in different directions — Barnes's Tony under-reports, García Márquez's narrator over-documents — which makes for a genuine analytical contrast.

Thesis. Both narrators reconstruct a death they were implicated in, but where Tony Webster's unreliability lies in what his defensive register omits, the narrator of Chronicle is unreliable through saturation: he supplies so much contradictory testimony that documentation itself becomes the evasion. Barnes therefore makes us distrust a memory, and García Márquez makes us distrust an archive.

  1. Paragraph 1 — the pose of the investigator. Tony's "I can't remember" hedges and his legalistic phrasing against the Chronicle narrator's interview method and his claim to have reconstructed everything "over the course of many years". Both adopt the idiom of inquiry; both are protecting themselves.
  2. Paragraph 2 — the gap in the record. Tony's missing letter — the one he wrote, the one Adrian has — against the town's missing warnings. In both texts the decisive absence is documentary, and in both the narrator is the person holding it.
  3. Paragraph 3 — register as self-defence. Tony's clipped, reasonable, faintly aggrieved register ("That was about it, really") against the narrator's folk-legal, proverb-laden style. Compare how each masks culpability: modesty versus authority.
  4. Paragraph 4 — the structural betrayal. Part Two's retroactive destruction of Part One against the novella's circular structure, which reveals the ending in the first sentence. Barnes's structure punishes the narrator; García Márquez's structure punishes the reader for hoping.

Conclusion. In both texts distrust is not a flaw in the narrator but the subject of the book. The comparison reveals that memory and testimony are the same technology: a way of editing oneself into a witness rather than a participant.

Question 3 Rewriting an inherited story

Question. Compare the ways in which two of the works you have studied rewrite an existing story in order to question who has the authority to tell it.

Best pairing: The World's Wife + The Sense of an Ending. Duffy rewrites myth and scripture from the woman's side; Barnes makes his narrator rewrite his own youth and then has the text rewrite it again. Both are exercises in intertextuality, and both hinge on the question of who gets the last word.

Thesis. Both texts contest an authorised version, but Duffy's speakers knowingly seize the narrative from a male predecessor, whereas Tony Webster does not know he is misremembering — so The World's Wife produces deliberately revisionist testimony while The Sense of an Ending produces dramatic irony at its narrator's expense.

  1. Paragraph 1 — the intertextual premise. "Mrs Midas" and "Mrs Darwin" assume the reader knows the husband's story and supply the domestic cost; Barnes assumes the reader knows the conventions of the schoolboy memoir and supplies a narrator who thinks he is living in one.
  2. Paragraph 2 — who speaks, and to whom. Duffy's dramatic monologues address a silent or absent listener (Midas, Aesop, Icarus) — the husband becomes the audience for the first time; Tony addresses an implied reader he is trying to convince. Contrast confidence with anxiety.
  3. Paragraph 3 — the volta as a seizure of power. In "Mrs Midas" and "Medusa" the turn converts complaint into control; in Barnes the turn is structural — Part Two — and it takes power away from the narrator. This is the sharpest contrast in the essay: the same device pointed in opposite directions.
  4. Paragraph 4 — what the revision costs. Duffy's speakers gain voice but often lose the relationship (Mrs Midas sleeps separately, Circe keeps her distance); Tony gains self-knowledge and loses the comfort of his own life-story. Both texts insist that revised knowledge is expensive.

Conclusion. Read together, the two works suggest that authority over a story is always contested after the fact, and that the revision is only ever partial — Duffy's wives still define themselves against husbands, Tony still cannot feel what he now knows.

Question 4 Language as an instrument of power

Question. Examine the ways in which two of the works you have studied present language itself as an instrument of power.

Best pairing: Othello + The World's Wife. This is the strongest pairing on the paper for a question about language, because both texts are obsessed with who is permitted to speak, in what register, and with what consequence. One is a play about a man who loses his command of language; the other is a collection that hands language to women who previously had none.

Thesis. Both works treat eloquence as power, but Shakespeare dramatises its theft — Iago dismantles Othello's language until the general is speaking broken, image-choked prose — while Duffy dramatises its seizure, giving monologue and therefore authority to figures who had been silent objects in someone else's story.

  1. Paragraph 1 — verse and prose as status. Othello's opening "Keep up your bright swords, for the dew will rust them" (1.2) as controlled, elevated verse, against Duffy's free verse that deliberately refuses the elevated register — the choice of a plain, colloquial line is itself a claim about who gets to be poetic.
  2. Paragraph 2 — register collapse as tragedy. Track Othello from "My parts, my title and my perfect soul" to the fragmented "Goats and monkeys!" (4.1). Compare with "Mrs Icarus", where the speaker's flat, commonsensical register is not collapse but superiority.
  3. Paragraph 3 — the language of persuasion. Iago's rhetoric — anaphora, rhetorical questions, the planted aposiopesis at "Ha, I like not that" — against Duffy's speakers' use of bathos and understatement to deflate masculine grandeur. Both use the same devices for opposite ends.
  4. Paragraph 4 — silence as the ultimate power. Desdemona is silenced permanently; Duffy's "Pilate's Wife" and "Eurydice" are poems about women whose words were not believed and who now take the lines for themselves. Compare enforced silence with chosen speech.

Conclusion. The two works read together propose a blunt political claim: power is not what happens, it is who gets to narrate what happened — and the moment the narration changes hands, the hierarchy does too.

Question 5 Gender and the expectations placed on women

Question. Compare the presentation of the expectations placed on women in two of the works you have studied.

Best pairing: The World's Wife + Chronicle of a Death Foretold. García Márquez gives you honour culture from the inside — Angela Vicario returned by her husband for not being a virgin, her mother beating her, the whole economy of female reputation; Duffy gives you the same economy in myth after myth. The comparison is almost too easy, which is why you must make it precise: compare mechanisms, not attitudes.

Thesis. Both texts present female reputation as a currency owned by men, but Chronicle shows the system working perfectly and without protest, whereas The World's Wife dismantles it by giving the traded woman the microphone — so García Márquez produces indictment through documentary calm and Duffy produces it through comic aggression.

  1. Paragraph 1 — the economy of virginity. Angela Vicario's returned marriage and the town's forensic interest in her body, against "Queen Herod" or "Salome", where female sexuality is spectacularised. Compare: a small town's gossip versus myth's permanence.
  2. Paragraph 2 — the mother as enforcer. Pura Vicario's beating of her daughter — internalised patriarchy made physical — against Duffy's women who inherit and then rewrite their mothers' stories (Demeter, "Little Red-Cap").
  3. Paragraph 3 — voice and its denial. Angela is never really interviewed; the narrator reports her. Duffy's entire project is the reversal of that: the woman speaks, and the man is the silent addressee. This is the single strongest contrast available to you.
  4. Paragraph 4 — how each text resolves it. Angela survives into letter-writing and stubborn love; Duffy's speakers survive into autonomy that is often lonely. Neither offers a happy resolution, and that refusal is itself an argument.

Conclusion. Placed side by side, the texts suggest that the oppression of women in story and the oppression of women in a town operate by the same rule: a woman's value is decided in a conversation she is not invited to join. Both texts are the invitation.

Question 6 Self-knowledge arrived at too late

Question. Discuss how two of the works you have studied present self-knowledge as something a character reaches too late to act on.

Best pairing: Othello + The Sense of an Ending. Both texts are tragedies of timing: the truth arrives, and it arrives after the damage. Othello's is catastrophic and final; Tony's is quieter but perhaps crueller, because he must go on living with it.

Thesis. Both works structure themselves so that recognition follows destruction, but Shakespeare makes the recognition absolute and terminal — Othello's "one that loved not wisely but too well" comes with a sword — whereas Barnes makes it partial, provisional and survivable, which is a more damning verdict: Tony knows, and knowing changes almost nothing about how he lives.

  1. Paragraph 1 — the moment of knowing. 5.2, "O fool, fool, fool!" and the collapse into the third person, against Tony's receipt of Adrian's diary and his slow, hedged admission. Compare a single scene of total collapse with a gradual, resisted realisation.
  2. Paragraph 2 — what the text withheld from the character. Emilia's delayed revelation about the handkerchief against Veronica's delayed letter — in both, a woman holds the evidence and hands it over too late.
  3. Paragraph 3 — self-narration after the fall. Othello's final speech re-authors himself as a romantic figure ("like the base Judean"); Tony's Part One has already done the same thing, and Part Two exposes it. Both men write their own alibis; Shakespeare lets his character finish the speech, Barnes takes the pen away.
  4. Paragraph 4 — judgement on the reader. Othello kills himself, closing the account; Tony ends with the Severn bore and the phrase "There is accumulation. There is responsibility. And beyond these, there is great unease." Compare resolution with irresolution as a moral strategy.

Conclusion. Both texts insist that self-knowledge is not redemptive. Othello's arrives at the speed of a dagger; Tony's arrives at the speed of a letter in a drawer. The comparison suggests the crueller tragedy is the survivable one.

Two model body paragraphs, written out in full

Original model writing

These are model paragraphs written for this page. They quote only short phrases, as your exam must.

Model A — point-by-point, from Question 1 (Chronicle then Othello)

Both texts stage honour as something that exists only where it can be seen, and both therefore depend on a public that watches and declines to intervene. García Márquez makes the point structurally: the narrator reconstructs the murder as a series of sightings, and nearly every chapter supplies someone who saw Santiago Nasar and said nothing — the milk-shop owner who "knew" and assumed someone else had warned him, the priest who receives the letter and forgets the errand. The technique is accumulation without agency: by distributing the knowledge across twenty witnesses in a flat, investigative register, the novella produces a community in which responsibility has been so thoroughly diluted that it has become weather. Shakespeare, by contrast, concentrates the same mechanism into one man who understands exactly what a witness is for. Iago does not merely fail to warn Othello; he manufactures the visual evidence — "Look where he comes… Note, note, note your own suspicion" — and converts seeing into proof. Where García Márquez's town is passive because everyone assumes someone else has acted, Iago is active precisely because he knows everyone will assume that. The divergence matters: inertia kills Santiago while the entire town is sorry for him, whereas design kills Desdemona while her killer believes he is doing justice. Read together, the two texts suggest that a shared code of honour is more lethal than a single villain, because the first can be sustained by people who think of themselves as good.

Model B — point-by-point, from Question 3 (The World's Wife then The Sense of an Ending)

Both works turn on a seizure of narrative authority, yet the seizures are pointed in opposite directions. In "Mrs Midas", Duffy hands the story to a woman who has been a footnote in her husband's myth, and the poem's humour is part of the strategy: the golden touch is deflated into a domestic nuisance, the pear tree becomes a suburban embarrassment, and the elevated register of the original myth is replaced by a flat, put-upon marriage voice. The volta arrives when the speaker reclaims desire on her own terms and sends Midas upstairs — a reversal in which the woman does not merely complain about the story but takes the pen. Barnes constructs the same reversal and then takes the pen back. Tony Webster spends Part One confidently authoring his youth, and the register gives him away: the clipped, reasonable, faintly aggrieved sentences, the repeated "That was about it, really", the habit of summarising other people's feelings rather than his own. Part Two does to him what "Mrs Midas" does to Midas — it removes the right to the version — but it does so without his knowledge, which converts revision into exposure. The contrast is the essay's engine: Duffy's speakers gain authority and the reader's sympathy at the same moment, whereas Tony loses both. Where The World's Wife suggests that retelling can be liberation, The Sense of an Ending insists that most retelling is defence, and that the defence usually fails.
What earns the marks here

Count the moves. Each paragraph contains: one claim sentence, evidence from work A with a named technique and an effect, a comparative hinge ("by contrast" / "yet"), evidence from work B treated in the same way, and a mini-synthesis that judges the difference. Both works appear in both paragraphs. Neither paragraph summarises plot for more than a clause. That is 5/5 on B and C.

8. Eight more practice questions with thesis outlines

  1. Compare how two works present the consequences of a failure to communicate.

    Show thesis outline

    Pairing: Chronicle + Othello. Thesis: Both texts stage death as the product of messages that were sent to the wrong person, but García Márquez presents a town whose channels are clogged by assumption — everyone knows, so everyone assumes the message was delivered — whereas Shakespeare presents a single man who intercepts and redirects every channel: the difference is between a broken network and a hijacked one. Paragraphs: (1) the non-warnings in Chronicle; (2) Iago's management of who hears what; (3) objects as failed messages (the note pushed under the door, the handkerchief); (4) the ending's verdict on both networks.

  2. Discuss how two works use objects as symbols of a relationship.

    Show thesis outline

    Pairing: Othello + The World's Wife. Thesis: The handkerchief and the golden pear are both objects that carry a woman's fate in a man's hands, but Shakespeare's strawberry-spotted napkin is a token of possession that Othello destroys, while Duffy's objects are usually the residue of a wish the woman's husband made without consulting her — the contrast separates jealous possession from careless transformation. Paragraphs: (1) the handkerchief's provenance and its circulation; (2) Midas's gold and its domestic consequences; (3) who holds the object at the climax; (4) what the object's destruction achieves.

  3. Compare the ways two works present the relationship between the individual and the state or institution.

    Show thesis outline

    Pairing: Chronicle + Othello. Thesis: Both texts locate justice in an institution that arrives after the event, but the Colombian novella presents the church and the authorities as absent or comic — the autopsy is botched, the bishop sails past — while Venice is efficient and still useless, suggesting that institutional failure is a matter of timing rather than competence. Paragraphs: (1) the botched autopsy; (2) the Venetian senate's efficiency in 1.3; (3) the law's arrival after death in both; (4) what each ending says about the state.

  4. Examine how two works use a first-person voice to control the reader's sympathy.

    Show thesis outline

    Pairing: The World's Wife + The Sense of an Ending. Thesis: Both texts use the first person to solicit agreement, but Duffy's speakers earn sympathy by confessing unflattering truths on purpose, whereas Tony Webster loses sympathy by confessing them accidentally — the reader's trust is won by candour in one text and withdrawn by self-deception in the other. Paragraphs: (1) the confessional monologue and the silent listener; (2) Tony's hedging and modesty topoi; (3) where each text exposes its own speaker; (4) the reader's final position.

  5. Compare the presentation of jealousy or possessiveness in two works.

    Show thesis outline

    Pairing: Othello + The World's Wife. Thesis: Shakespeare presents jealousy as a poison manufactured by rhetoric and felt in the body, while Duffy repeatedly diagnoses it as a husband's professional vanity — Midas's greed, Darwin's theory, Freud's interpretation — so that the emotion is relocated from love to ego. Paragraphs: (1) the "green-eyed monster" and Othello's physical collapse; (2) "Frau Freud" and the comedy of possession; (3) the role of interpretation in creating jealousy; (4) which text offers the more damning account.

  6. Discuss how two works use humour or comedy to make a serious point.

    Show thesis outline

    Pairing: The World's Wife + Chronicle of a Death Foretold. Thesis: Both texts use bathos — Duffy to deflate male myth, García Márquez to deflate official solemnity — and in both the joke is a way of making an unbearable subject discussable, though Duffy's comedy attacks upwards while the novella's black comedy implicates everyone including the narrator. Paragraphs: (1) bathos in "Mrs Midas"; (2) comedy in the autopsy and the bishop's boat; (3) who is the butt of the joke in each; (4) whether humour softens or sharpens the critique.

  7. Compare how two works present the passage of time and its effect on understanding.

    Show thesis outline

    Pairing: The Sense of an Ending + Chronicle of a Death Foretold. Thesis: Both narrators look back across decades, but Barnes uses time to expose a narrator who believed time had settled his account, whereas García Márquez's narrator returns after twenty-seven years precisely because nothing has been settled — one text weaponises elapsed time, the other is defeated by it. Paragraphs: (1) the twenty-seven-year return and the ruined house; (2) Tony's forty-year gap and Adrian's diary; (3) the Severn bore image as a theory of time; (4) what each structure implies about closure.

  8. Examine how two works present a character who is defined by what they refuse to say.

    Show thesis outline

    Pairing: Othello + The World's Wife. Thesis: Silence in these texts is never emptiness: Iago's refusal to finish a sentence is the most productive silence in literature, while Duffy gives speech to women whose silence was historical — Pilate's Wife, Eurydice — so the comparison distinguishes silence as a weapon from silence as an imposed condition. Paragraphs: (1) Iago's aposiopesis, "Ha, I like not that"; (2) "Pilate's Wife" and the dream nobody heeded; (3) "Eurydice" and the refusal to follow; (4) who benefits from the silence in each text.
