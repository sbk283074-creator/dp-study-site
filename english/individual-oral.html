English A: Language and Literature SL · Assessment

The individual oral

Fifteen minutes. Two extracts — one from a literary work you studied, one from a non-literary text in your learner portfolio — linked through a global issue. Up to ten minutes of prepared presentation, then about five minutes of questions from your teacher. It is 30% of your grade, it is the component students fear most, and it is also the most controllable, because unlike Paper 1 nothing here is unseen. You can prepare this to the sentence. This page makes you do exactly that.

15 min · 30% · 20 marks 1 literary extract (≤40 lines) + 1 non-literary extract 8 original portfolio texts · 4 exemplar outlines

Confirm this with your teacher

The description here follows the current Language A: Language and Literature guide (first assessment 2021): 15 minutes total, up to 10 minutes of prepared presentation followed by about 5 minutes of teacher questions, 30%, marked out of 20 across A knowledge and understanding, B analysis and evaluation, C focus and organisation, D language. Confirm the exact timings, the 40-line limit and the extract rules with your own teacher, since schools apply them with some variation.

Copyright — read this once

All eight non-literary texts below are written by me for you. They are original practice material, not real published texts, so you may print, annotate and rehearse with them freely — and your teacher can verify that you are not passing off a found text as your own analysis. In the real oral you will of course use texts you collected yourself; these exist so that you can rehearse the method on material that has been designed to be analysable.

1. What happens in the room, and what is being listened for

You walk in with your two extracts. You may not read from a script; you may use brief notes — most teachers allow a single side of A4 or a small card with bullet points, but check. You speak for up to ten minutes. Then your teacher asks questions for about five. Then you leave. That is the whole event.

Why this exists

The oral is not a memory test and it is not a performance. It is the only place in the course where you must produce analysis without the luxury of revision. That is why the marks are structured the way they are: the examiner is asking whether your analytical habit has become automatic. If you have rehearsed the method twenty times, the oral is the easiest 30% you will ever earn. If you have rehearsed a script twenty times, it is the most fragile — because the questions will break it.

The four criteria, as behaviour

Criterion (5 marks)What a band 7 sounds likeWhat a band 3 sounds like
A — Knowledge and understanding The extract is placed precisely inside the whole work: "this comes twenty pages before the murder, after the narrator has already told us the ending." The non-literary text's source, date and audience are named. Context is attached to a choice in one clause. The extract is treated as if it were a free-standing passage. No sense of where it sits. The non-literary text is described without any sense of who produced it or why.
B — Analysis and evaluation Every point is choice → technique → effect → meaning. Short quotations, spoken naturally. At least one point evaluates: "the informality is strategic, because…" Feature-spotting out loud: "there is also alliteration here…" with no effect, or plot summary delivered as analysis.
C — Focus and organisation A global issue stated in one arguable sentence in the first minute. Signposted structure ("having looked at the literary extract, I now want to turn to…"). Both extracts genuinely linked, not just mentioned in turn. Two separate mini-presentations with "and now my second text" in between. No stated global issue, or a slogan masquerading as one.
D — Language Technical vocabulary used precisely and without hesitation; sentences completed; register formal but spoken, not written-aloud. "This shows that…" repeated; long written sentences collapsing mid-way; filler ("like, you know, basically") filling the silence.
Exam technique

The most under-used mark on this component is C. It is worth 5 and it is almost purely mechanical. If you (a) state one arguable global issue in the first 60 seconds, (b) say out loud when you are moving between extracts, and (c) use the word "because" when you link them, you have done most of what criterion C asks. Most students never do any of the three.

2. Choosing a global issue and writing one you can actually argue

A global issue is a concern that transcends the page and is genuinely contested in the world. The IB gives five categories — culture, identity and community; beliefs, values and education; politics, power and justice; art, creativity and the imagination; science, technology and the environment — but the category is not the issue. The issue is your one sentence.

How to use it

Test your global issue statement against four checks. If it fails any one, rewrite it.

  1. Is it arguable? State the opposite. If the opposite is nonsense ("war is bad"), you have a slogan. If the opposite is a real position someone holds ("permanent digital records protect the vulnerable by preventing denial"), you have an issue.
  2. Is it specific? "Gender inequality" is a category. "The assumption that a woman's account needs a man's corroboration before it counts as evidence" is an issue.
  3. Does it contain a mechanism? The best statements contain a because or a when: "communities that treat honour as public property produce violence that everyone can foresee and no one stops."
  4. Do both extracts genuinely engage it? Not "could be made to". If you have to strain to attach the non-literary text, change the text or the issue.

Slogan (loses marks)

"Honour is important in society."
"Women have been treated badly throughout history."
"Memory is unreliable."
"Social media affects people."

Issue (earns marks)

"Communities that locate honour in a public audience produce violence that everyone can foresee and no one is individually required to prevent."
"The authority to narrate an event has historically depended on gender, and the recovery of that authority changes what counts as fact."
"A culture's record of the past is shaped less by what happened than by who is permitted to keep the archive."

Common trap

Do not choose a global issue first and then hunt for texts. Choose your pair first — the two extracts that genuinely illuminate each other — and let the issue emerge from the connection. An issue you found by looking at two texts is always sharper than one you found in a list.

3. Choosing the two extracts

The literary extract may be up to 40 lines (check the exact rule with your teacher: for prose it is usually a passage of about 40 lines' equivalent, for poetry 40 lines). The non-literary extract is short by nature.

How to choose
  1. Density beats length. A twelve-line passage with six analysable choices gives you more to say than forty lines of narrative you end up summarising. Students who choose long extracts almost always run out of analytical material halfway and start retelling.
  2. Choose a passage with a shift in it. A tonal turn, a register change, a volta, a move from verse to prose. A shift gives you structure for free: before, during, after.
  3. Choose a passage you can place. If you cannot say in one sentence where it sits in the work and why that position matters, pick another.
  4. Check the global issue survives contact. Say the issue, then say the extract. If the connection needs three sentences of explanation, it is too loose.
  5. Avoid the famous speech. Everyone picks the "green-eyed monster" speech or the handkerchief scene. A less obvious passage shows knowledge of the whole work and lets you sound like yourself.

4. The minute-by-minute script

Do not memorise an essay and recite it. Memorise this shape, and speak inside it. The times below assume a ten-minute presentation; if you are slower, compress the analysis to two points per extract rather than three.

0:00–0:45
Open with the global issue, not with your name. State the issue in one arguable sentence, name its category, and state the two texts you will use. Frame: "The global issue I want to explore is ___, which falls under ___. I'll be looking at it through ___ and ___."
0:45–1:15
Situate the literary extract. Work, author, where in the work it comes, and what is happening. Two sentences maximum. Frame: "The literary extract comes from ___, ___. It occurs ___, and it matters because ___."
1:15–4:30
Three analytical points on the literary extract. Each: name the choice → quote briefly → name the technique → explain the effect → connect to the global issue. Order them so they build: language, then structure, then implication.
4:30–5:10
The bridge. This is the most important forty seconds in the whole oral. Do not say "now I will talk about my second text." Say what the two texts share and where they will differ. Frame: "What links these two texts is ___. But they approach it from opposite directions: ___."
5:10–8:00
Three analytical points on the non-literary extract. Name the source, the audience and the purpose first — that is what makes it non-literary analysis and not just reading. Then: visual/typographic choices, diction, structure, rhetorical appeals, and what the text assumes about its reader.
8:00–9:15
Comparative conclusion. Return to the global issue and say what putting the two texts together revealed. Use the frame: "Read together, these texts suggest ___." Add one wider implication beyond the texts.
9:15–10:00
Buffer, not panic. If you have time left, add one more short point to the non-literary text or restate the issue more sharply. If you are already at ten minutes, stop cleanly: "That's where I'd like to stop." Do not trail off.
10:00–15:00
Questions. Answer in 45–75 seconds each. Add something new; never repeat what you already said. See §6 — this is where band 7 is often decided.
Exam technique

Practise to nine minutes, not ten. Every oral I have seen fail did so because the student ran over and had to be stopped mid-point, which truncates criterion C. Finishing at 9:15 with a clean conclusion is worth more than reaching 10:00 without one. Record yourself on your phone weekly from term 2 of year 2 — hearing your own filler words is the single fastest improvement available to you.

Sentence frames for every move

MoveFrame
State the global issue"The global issue I want to explore is ___, which sits within the category of ___."
Place an extract"This extract comes ___ of the work, at the point where ___."

5. Eight original non-literary texts for portfolio practice

Each is annotated with the global issues it can serve and the authorial choices worth analysing. Use them as rehearsal material and as models for what to look for in texts you collect yourself.

Text 1 — Advertisement: outdoor poster

EVERYONE SAW. NO ONE CALLED.
Full-bleed photograph of a busy high street at night, shot from inside a shop window so the crowd is blurred and slightly distorted. Bottom left, white type on black: "In 61% of emergencies, witnesses assume someone else has already acted. Call anyway." Bottom right: a phone number, a small logo, and the words Citywide Safety Partnership. No image of a victim.
  • Global issues: politics, power and justice (collective responsibility, bystander intervention); culture, identity and community (what a community owes a stranger).
  • Choices to analyse: the cropped, blurred photograph, which makes the crowd anonymous and therefore implicates the viewer rather than a named individual; the two-sentence headline whose parallelism ("EVERYONE… NO ONE…") turns a statistic into an accusation; the statistical authority of "61%" and what it conceals (sample, year, definition of emergency); the imperative "Call anyway", which relocates the burden onto the individual; the decision to show no victim, denying the reader the route of pity; the typographic hierarchy that makes the accusation unmissable and the sponsor nearly invisible.

Text 2 — Opinion column

The past is no longer something we survive. It is something we store.
Every photograph I have ever taken is still somewhere. Every careless sentence I wrote at nineteen is retrievable in under a second. We were told this would make us wiser. What it has made us is permanent: a generation of people who cannot shed an earlier version of themselves, because the earlier version has a username and a date stamp.
There is a name for the opposite of this. It is called forgetting, and for most of human history it was considered a mercy.
  • Global issues: science, technology and the environment (the permanence of digital record); beliefs, values and education (what a society chooses to remember and to forgive).
  • Choices to analyse: the opening antithesis ("no longer… it is"), which establishes the argument as a before/after structure; the hyperbole of "Every… Every…" and the anaphora that follows; the italicised single word permanent, isolated for emphasis and redefined in the colon that follows; the neologism of "a username and a date stamp" making identity sound like admin; the late personification of forgetting as a "mercy", which reframes a technological issue as a moral one; the closing short sentence, which gives the column the rhythm of a verdict.

Text 3 — Speech extract

From the opening address at a national museum redevelopment, 2024.
Walk through this building with me and count. Count the women. Count the ones who are named and the ones who are simply present — the figure at the edge of the painting, the body that holds up the plinth, the face that a catalogue lists as "unknown".
I am not asking you to remove anyone. I am asking you to notice that the building has already made a decision about whose life is a story and whose life is furniture.
A gallery is an argument with walls.
  • Global issues: culture, identity and community (whose history is preserved); art, creativity and the imagination (the politics of representation and of the canon).
  • Choices to analyse: the imperative anaphora "Count… Count… Count…", which turns the audience into auditors and makes the argument self-discovering; the distinction between "named" and "present" plus the italicised present, which exposes a hierarchy inside visibility itself; the tricolon of diminishing roles ("the figure at the edge… the body… the face"); the scare-quoted "unknown", which attributes the erasure to an institution rather than to chance; the concession "I am not asking you to remove anyone", which pre-empts the accusation of censorship; the closing metaphor "an argument with walls", which relocates the museum from neutral container to active author.

Text 4 — Blog post

Returning: notes from a town I used to know — posted to a personal travel blog, 2024.
I came back after twenty years expecting to feel something and mostly I felt the admin of it: the bus route had changed twice, the school had been flattened and rebuilt with a different name, and the shop where my mother bought bread is now a vape outlet with the same yellow door. I kept photoshopping the old street over the new one in my head and getting the scale wrong.
The strange part was not that the town had changed. It was how certain I had been that it wouldn't.
  • Global issues: culture, identity and community (belonging, migration, the myth of return); beliefs, values and education (how nostalgia edits history).
  • Choices to analyse: the deflation of "expecting to feel something" into "the admin of it" — bathos used to signal emotional avoidance; the tricolon of mundane change (bus, school, shop) which makes loss measurable; the verb "photoshopping", a deliberately anachronistic technological metaphor for memory; the admission "getting the scale wrong", which concedes that memory is quantitatively inaccurate, not just selective; the closing antithesis, which relocates the problem from the town to the speaker's own arrogance.

Text 5 — Memoir extract

My grandmother could not read. She kept letters anyway — eleven of them, in a biscuit tin, tied with the same piece of red string for forty years. She could tell you what each one was about without opening it, because she had paid a neighbour to read them to her, once each, and had then memorised the sound of them.
When she died we opened the tin. Two were from a man nobody in the family recognised.
  • Global issues: culture, identity and community (literacy, class, gender and access to the written record); art, creativity and the imagination (who is allowed to be an author).
  • Choices to analyse: the blunt opening sentence — four words, no qualification — which makes illiteracy a fact rather than a tragedy; the concrete triad "eleven of them, in a biscuit tin, tied with the same piece of red string", where the domestic container dignifies the archive; the detail "for forty years" converting objects into duration; the paradox of memorising "the sound" rather than the sense, which makes the letters oral rather than written; the final two-sentence paragraph, whose shortness and whose bland word "recognised" deliver the revelation without comment.

Text 6 — Satirical piece

Notice from the Department of Civic Standing.
From next month, every resident will receive a quarterly Standing Report. The Report will consolidate neighbourly observations, workplace feedback and transport conduct into a single figure between 0 and 100. Citizens scoring below 40 will be invited to a voluntary conversation.
"People have always judged each other," said a spokesperson. "We are simply making the arithmetic honest."
Residents may dispute a Report for a fee of £35.
  • Global issues: politics, power and justice (surveillance, bureaucratic power, the quantified citizen); science, technology and the environment (data as a moral instrument).
  • Choices to analyse: the official register and the invented departmental name, which borrows the authority of the state; the euphemism chain — "Standing Report" for a score, "neighbourly observations" for denunciation, "invited to a voluntary conversation" for a summons; the reduction of a life to "a single figure between 0 and 100"; the spokesperson's quotation, which uses the rhetoric of honesty to justify surveillance; the final line, the satirical payoff, in which the remedy is monetised and the fee is precise.

Text 7 — Infographic (described in words)

Who gets quoted? — a single-page graphic produced by a media watchdog.
Top band: a row of 100 small human icons, 76 grey and 24 blue. Caption: "Of every 100 people quoted in national news, 76 are men."
Middle band: four horizontal bars labelled Experts, Politicians, Victims, "Ordinary people". The "Ordinary people" bar is the only one where the blue segment exceeds half.
Bottom band, in larger type: "Women are quoted most often when they are not qualified."
Footer: "Sources on request."
  • Global issues: culture, identity and community (gender and voice); politics, power and justice (who counts as an authority).
  • Choices to analyse: the use of a hundred identical icons, which converts a percentage into something countable and visible; colour coding restricted to two hues, forcing a binary; the categorisation itself as an argument — "Victims" is a role, "Experts" is a status; the single bar in which women dominate, which is the graphic's real claim; the final sentence's word "qualified", which reframes representation as a question of legitimacy rather than fairness; the withholding of sources in the footer, which performs transparency while preventing verification.

Text 8 — Social-media campaign

#StillOnDuty — a men's mental health campaign, three posts.
(1) Image: an empty kitchen at 3 a.m., one light on. Caption: "He was fine. He was always fine."
(2) Video, 12 seconds: a man in work boots sitting in a parked van, not speaking. On-screen text only: "Reporting for duty on yourself counts as duty."
(3) Final post, text only: "The strongest thing you can do is be the first to say it out loud. #StillOnDuty"
  • Global issues: culture, identity and community (masculinity, and the cost of a culture that equates silence with strength); beliefs, values and education (what boys are taught about vulnerability).
  • Choices to analyse: the repetition of "fine" in post 1, which turns a reassurance into an accusation; the domestic setting and the single lit window, which locate the problem at home rather than in public; the military lexis ("duty", "reporting", "strongest") deliberately repurposed against itself; the 12-second silence, where the absence of a voice is the argument; the choice of on-screen text over speech, which suits a platform habit and also signals that the audience may not want to be heard; the imperative in post 3, which makes the ask small, concrete and repeatable.

6. The three question types, and how to answer each

TypeWhat it sounds likeHow to answer (45–75 seconds)
1. Extension "You mentioned ___. Can you develop that?" / "What else is going on in that passage?" Add a new technique, never more of the same. Best structure: "Yes — and the other thing happening there is ___, because ___, which means ___." Never repeat your presentation point.
2. Challenge "But couldn't it be read the other way?" / "Isn't that a bit of a stretch?" Concede precisely, then re-frame. "It could, and there's a reading in which ___. But the text makes that reading hard, because ___." Conceding costs nothing and shows you understand that interpretation is contested — that is exactly what the top band rewards.
3. Connection / global issue "How do your two texts speak to each other?" / "How does this issue appear outside the texts?" Return to the mechanism, not the topic. "They share a mechanism rather than a subject: both make ___ visible by ___." Then one sentence on the real world. One is enough.
Exam technique

If you do not understand a question, do not improvise an answer. Say: "Could you rephrase that?" — this is permitted, costs about five seconds, and is far better than 60 seconds of drift. If you genuinely do not know, say "I'd need to think about that further, but my first response would be ___" and then attempt it. Partial, honest analysis beats confident waffle.

7. Four exemplar IO outlines, one per set work

Copyright

Only short quotations are given below, as your own oral must use. Each outline pairs a set work with one of the original non-literary texts above.

IO outline 1 Chronicle of a Death Foretold + Text 1 (advertisement)

Global issue (category: politics, power and justice). "Communities that treat honour as a public possession produce violence that everyone can foresee and no one is individually required to prevent."

Literary extract. The passage in which the narrator lists the people who saw Santiago Nasar on the morning of the murder and did not warn him — including the milk-shop owner who "thought he had already been warned".

  • The extract sits early, but after the first sentence has already told us Santiago will die — so the reader reads the list knowing its outcome, which converts information into dread.
  • Technique: paratactic catalogue of named witnesses. Effect: each name is a person who chose not to act; the accumulation makes negligence statistical. Meaning: responsibility is diluted to nothing by being shared.
  • Technique: the passive and reflexive constructions — "it was thought", "he assumed". Effect: no grammatical subject performs the failure. Meaning: the syntax itself performs the evasion the town is guilty of.
  • The first-person plural ("we all knew") appears later in the novel; note here that the narrator is himself one of the witnesses, so the investigation is also a confession.

Non-literary extract. Text 1, the "EVERYONE SAW. NO ONE CALLED." poster.

  • Source, audience, purpose: a city safety partnership targeting commuters; the purpose is behavioural change, not information.
  • The blurred crowd photograph anonymises the witnesses — the viewer cannot identify a culprit, which forces self-implication rather than blame of others.
  • "61%" lends scientific authority to a moral accusation; the absence of a victim image refuses pity and offers only guilt.
  • The imperative "Call anyway" relocates duty onto the individual — which is precisely the move the town in Chronicle never makes.

Bridge. Both texts stage the same failure — knowledge without action — but the novella shows a community accepting diffusion while the poster fights it, and the comparison reveals that diffusion of responsibility is not ignorance but comfort.

IO outline 2 Othello + Text 8 (#StillOnDuty campaign)

Global issue (category: culture, identity and community). "Cultures that define masculinity as control leave men no vocabulary for fear, and the silence is then mistaken for strength."

Literary extract. Othello's speech at 3.3 beginning "Farewell the tranquil mind…", where he bids farewell to "the plumed troop and the big wars / That make ambition virtue".

  • Where it sits: immediately after Iago has planted the suspicion, but before Othello has committed to it — the moment his identity begins to be dismantled.
  • Technique: anaphora of "Farewell" across four objects — the mind, the troop, the wars, "the neighing steed". Effect: the repetition performs a stripping-away, as though he is undressing himself. Meaning: his identity is entirely composed of public military roles; there is no private self underneath to retreat to.
  • Technique: the oxymoron "make ambition virtue". Effect: it admits that the value system he is losing was itself a rationalisation. Meaning: soldiering was how he converted ambition into respectability — a man of service rather than a man of appetite.
  • The verse here is still controlled; note that his later collapse into prose ("Goats and monkeys!") is the real tragedy, because the loss of register is the loss of status.

Non-literary extract. Text 8, the #StillOnDuty campaign.

  • Source, audience, purpose: a health campaign targeting men; the purpose is to make help-seeking legible as strength.
  • The repurposed military lexis — "duty", "reporting", "strongest" — borrows the exact register that Othello's identity is built from, and points it inward.
  • The repetition "He was fine. He was always fine." makes a reassurance into an indictment: the campaign argues that the phrase itself is the symptom.
  • The 12-second silence is the campaign's bravest choice — it reproduces, on screen, the silence the campaign is trying to end.

Bridge. Othello's tragedy is that the only language available to him is the language of the troop; the campaign's argument is that the same language can be turned inward and used for survival. Both texts show that masculinity is a register before it is a set of beliefs.

IO outline 3 The Sense of an Ending + Text 2 (opinion column)

Global issue (category: science, technology and the environment, crossing into beliefs, values and education). "A permanent record of the past is often defended as justice and experienced as imprisonment — what a culture chooses to store determines what its members are permitted to become."

Literary extract. Tony Webster's reflections on memory, including the line "I remembered… or perhaps I only remembered remembering", together with his account of "the sense of an ending" as something that accumulates backwards.

  • Where it sits: the methodological section of Part One, where Tony establishes his credentials as a careful rememberer — which Part Two will dismantle.
  • Technique: hedged repetition — "I remembered… or perhaps I only remembered remembering". Effect: the syntax performs the very instability it describes; the reader cannot tell which clause is the fact. Meaning: memory is an activity, not an archive.
  • Technique: Tony's defensive register throughout — "That was about it, really", the modest qualifiers, the refusal of strong verbs. Effect: he sounds reasonable, and that reasonableness is the disguise. Meaning: restraint is how he protects himself from his own past.
  • The Severn bore image — a wave travelling upstream against the current, "a great sloshing trajectory" — reframes time as something that can move backwards and thereby rewrite what is already known.

Non-literary extract. Text 2, the column on digital permanence.

  • Source, audience, purpose: a broadsheet-style opinion column for a general educated readership; the purpose is to reframe a technology story as an ethical one.
  • The opening antithesis ("no longer something we survive… something we store") establishes survival and storage as opposites, which is the column's whole argument in one sentence.
  • The isolated italicised word permanent, redefined in the colon, converts a neutral property into a condition imposed on a person.
  • The closing personification — forgetting as "a mercy" — is the rhetorical masterstroke: it makes forgetting sound like an act of kindness, which is precisely the thing Tony Webster has been denied.

Bridge. Tony is destroyed by a record he did not know existed (Adrian's diary, his own letter); the column argues that we are all now living in Tony's condition permanently. The comparison suggests that the tragedy of the novel is not unusual — it is simply the one case where the archive was paper.

IO outline 4 The World's Wife + Text 3 (speech extract)

Global issue (category: art, creativity and the imagination, crossing into culture, identity and community). "The authority to narrate has historically determined whose life counts as a story, and recovering that authority changes not only the record but the meaning of the past."

Literary extract. "Mrs Midas", the closing movement in which the speaker describes the golden husband and her separation from him — "It's a woman's lot / to be the one who stays" — together with the earlier line "Believe me, I'd not wanted gold."

  • Where it sits: near the opening of the collection, so it establishes the whole project: the woman who was a footnote in a man's myth takes the microphone.
  • Technique: the dramatic monologue form with a silent, addressed listener — "Believe me" presupposes a doubting audience. Effect: the reader is cast as the person who needs convincing. Meaning: the poem stages the historical disbelief of women's accounts and answers it.
  • Technique: bathos — the mythic golden touch is reduced to domestic nuisance (the pear tree, the bathroom, the "sick of the smell of money" register). Effect: deflation of male grandeur. Meaning: the myth was only ever impressive from his side.
  • Technique: the volta into "It's a woman's lot / to be the one who stays", where the tone shifts from comedy to a generalising bitterness. Effect: the personal grievance becomes a structural claim about gender.

Non-literary extract. Text 3, the museum director's speech.

  • Source, audience, purpose: an institutional figure addressing donors and the public at a redevelopment launch; the purpose is to justify a change of display policy without alienating the audience.
  • The imperative anaphora "Count… Count… Count…" makes the audience produce the evidence themselves, which is far more persuasive than being told.
  • The distinction between being "named" and merely "present" exposes a hierarchy inside visibility — exactly the condition Duffy's wives are in.
  • The concession "I am not asking you to remove anyone" pre-empts the charge of censorship, and the closing metaphor "a gallery is an argument with walls" makes the institution an author rather than a container.

Bridge. Both texts argue that representation is not decoration but authorship: Duffy reassigns the narrator, the museum director admits the building has always been narrating. The comparison reveals that the recovery of a voice changes the genre of the past, not just its contents.

8. The ten most common IO failures

Common trap
  1. A slogan instead of a global issue. "Gender inequality" is not an issue statement. One arguable sentence, with a mechanism in it.
  2. Two unrelated presentations. If you cannot say in one sentence what the texts share, the examiner cannot either.
  3. Summarising the extract instead of analysing it. You have ten minutes; every sentence of plot is a sentence stolen from analysis.
  4. Reading aloud from a script. It is not permitted, it sounds terrible, and it means the question section will destroy you because you cannot adapt.
  5. Ignoring the non-literary text's audience and purpose. An advertisement is not a poem. Say who it is for and what it wants.
  6. Treating the non-literary text as the "easy half". It is half the marks. Analyse typography, layout, image and what is absent.
  7. Running over ten minutes. Practise to nine. Truncation destroys criterion C.
  8. No signposting. Say out loud when you are moving between texts; the examiner cannot see your plan.
  9. Answering the questions by repeating yourself. Every answer must add a new technique or a new qualification.
  10. Never having said it out loud before. Analysis that works on paper dies in the mouth. Record yourself. Minimum eight full run-throughs before the real thing.
