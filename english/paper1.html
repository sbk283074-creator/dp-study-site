Copyright — read this once

Every practice text on this page is written by me for you. None of it is copied from a published source, so you may annotate, print, rewrite and rehearse with it freely. The same rule you must follow in your exam applies here: quote shortly. A line, a phrase, a single image — never a paragraph. Long quotation is not analysis; it is padding, and examiners punish it.

1. What you are actually being asked to do

A textual analysis is not a summary, not a review and not a list of features. It is an argument about how a text produces meaning. You are claiming something like: this text makes the reader feel X, and it does so deliberately, through choices A, B and C. Everything else in the essay exists to prove that claim.

The word guided is the part students ignore. Printed under the passage is a guiding question. It is not a topic suggestion and it is not optional decoration. It tells you the lens through which the whole 20 marks will be read.

How to use it

The guiding question does four jobs for you. Do all four in the first ten minutes.

  1. It chooses your material. A question about how the writer presents the relationship between the individual and the community means every paragraph must serve that relationship. A brilliant paragraph on imagery that does not serve it earns close to nothing.
  2. It tells you the direction of travel. Questions are usually built as how / to what effect / in what ways. Those words demand method, not content. If you answer with "the writer shows that…" you have answered a different question.
  3. It defines the axis of your thesis. Whatever contrast the question contains — public and private, individual and collective, reason and emotion, appearance and reality — becomes the although X, Y of your thesis.
  4. It sets the order of your paragraphs. Most guiding questions contain two or three named elements. Give each element a paragraph. That is your plan, done.
Common trap

Two failures destroy more Paper 1 scripts than any other. The first is ignoring the guiding question and writing a general appreciation of the passage — this caps you around band 4 no matter how elegant the writing. The second is answering it like a comprehension question: "the writer uses a metaphor when he says…" with no attempt at why. Criterion B is called analysis and evaluation of authorial choices. The word evaluation is where band 7 lives.

2. The four criteria as behaviour

Do not memorise the level descriptors. Memorise behaviour: what a band-7 script physically does that a band-3 script does not. The table below is the whole difference. Read the band-3 column as a description of what you must stop doing.

Criterion (5 marks)What it really asksWhat a band 7 doesWhat a band 3 does
A — Understanding and interpretation Do you get the text, including what is implied rather than stated? Reads against the grain. Notices irony, gaps, contradictions, what the text refuses to say. Interprets the silences: "the absence of any named speaker makes the guilt collective." Treats the text as intentional but not simple. Retells. "The writer describes a village and then a drowned boy." Paraphrases the passage in prettier words and calls the paraphrase interpretation. Misses tone entirely and takes everything at face value.
B — Analysis and evaluation of authorial choices Can you name the technique, show it in the words, and say what it does? Every point is choice → technique → effect → meaning. Quotes are short and embedded. Evaluates: "the metaphor is deliberately domestic, which makes the violence harder to dismiss as foreign." Says why this choice and not another. Spotlights features without effect: "there is a metaphor, there is alliteration, there is a rhetorical question." The technique is named and then abandoned. No link between technique and what the text is arguing.
C — Focus and organisation Is there a thesis, and does every paragraph serve it? One arguable thesis in the first 100 words. Each paragraph opens with a claim, not a quotation, and closes by returning to the thesis. Development is accumulative — paragraph 4 knows what paragraph 2 established. Five paragraphs that could be shuffled into any order. Each begins with a quote and wanders. The conclusion introduces a new idea, which proves the essay never had one.
D — Language Is your English precise, varied, and controlled? Technical vocabulary used because it is needed: asyndeton, free indirect discourse, bathos. Syntax varied for emphasis. Register is consistently formal-analytical. Almost no errors. Repetition of "shows", "uses", "this makes the reader feel". Everyday vocabulary doing technical work. Errors frequent enough to interrupt meaning — including punctuation splice errors in embedded quotation.
Exam technique

Criterion D is the cheapest 5 marks on the paper and the most often thrown away. You cannot fix it in the last five minutes. You fix it by owning twelve analytical verbs and using them on purpose: constructs, positions, undercuts, destabilises, legitimises, ironises, distances, implicates, elevates, collapses, reframes, withholds. Every one of those does more work than "shows". Practise replacing "shows" in your last three essays and your D mark moves without any new ideas.

3. The method: 10 / 60 / 5

Seventy-five minutes. You do not get more by hoping. Here is the split, and it is not negotiable, because the single most common cause of a band-3 Paper 1 is a beautiful unfinished essay.

BlockMinutesExactly what you doWhat you must have at the end
1 — Choose0–2 Read only the two guiding questions and skim the first and last lines of each text. Ask: which passage do I already have three ideas about? One text chosen. Circle it. Never look at the other again.
2 — Read and annotate2–6 Read once fast for gist. Read again with a pen: mark every shift, every repetition, every image, every word that feels weighted. Underline, do not highlight. A passage with 15–25 marks on it and three or four clusters of observations.
3 — Plan6–10 Write the thesis sentence in full. Then write four or five paragraph claims as short sentences. Then number the evidence you will use in each. A plan you could hand to someone else. If you cannot write the thesis in the plan, you do not yet have an argument — go back to the annotations.
4 — Write10–70 Introduction (thesis + context in three sentences). Four or five body paragraphs at roughly 180–220 words each. Conclusion that answers the guiding question in new words, not by repeating the introduction. About 900–1,100 words. Do not stop to admire a paragraph.
5 — Proof70–75 Read only for (a) sentences that make no sense, (b) subject-verb agreement, (c) missing words, (d) quotations that are not integrated into a sentence of your own. A clean script. Never leave the last question blank to re-write paragraph 2.
How to annotate in four minutes

You are looking for clusters, not features. A single metaphor is not a paragraph; four metaphors from the same semantic field are. Use these five marks:

  1. Box the shifts. Draw a vertical line where the tone, tense, addressee or argument changes. Shifts are the skeleton of your essay — they give you paragraph order for free.
  2. Circle the repetitions. Any word, image or structure that returns is a deliberate motif. Two returns is a pattern; three is an argument.
  3. Star the odd word. The register break, the unexpected adjective, the colloquialism in a formal text, the Latinate word in a plain one. Oddness is always a choice.
  4. Arrow the end back to the beginning. Does the closing image answer the opening one? Circular structure is a two-minute paragraph and most students never look for it.
  5. Write the opposites in the margin. Public/private, then/now, us/them, control/chaos. The binaries you list are the raw material of your thesis.
Timing reality check

Some teachers recommend 20 minutes of reading before you write. Fine — but if you take twenty, take the extra ten out of the writing block and protect the five-minute proof block completely. A script with uncorrected nonsense sentences loses D marks faster than a slightly thinner argument loses B marks. Also: 900 words with a thesis beats 1,400 words without one, every single time.

4. The analytical toolkit

Three layers, in this order: form and structure (what kind of thing is this, and how is it built?), content and context (what is being said, to whom, and from where?), and style (which verbal choices carry the weight?). Band-7 essays move between all three and never stay in one.

4.1 Form and structure

Form is the type of text and the conventions that come with it. Structure is the order of its parts. Both are choices: no writer accidentally writes a sonnet, and no columnist accidentally opens with an anecdote.

If the text is…Form questions to askStructural moves to look for
Prose fiction / memoirFirst or third person? Past or present tense? Is the narrator reliable, and what do they gain by telling it this way?In medias res opening; flashback; circular return; a final reversal; a withheld detail released at the end.
PoetryFree verse, sonnet, dramatic monologue, ballad? What does the chosen form promise the reader?Stanza breaks as argument steps; volta (turn); enjambment versus end-stopping; first line / last line echo; a shift in addressee.
SpeechWho is the audience, and what does the text assume they already believe? Is it transcribed, so do stage directions or applause matter?Exordium (opening rapport) → narrative → argument → refutation → peroration (call to action); anaphora across paragraphs.
Opinion column / editorialWhat is the publication and what does its readership expect? Where does the writer place themselves on the spectrum?Hook → concession → thesis → evidence → rebuttal of the opposing view → closing kicker.
Advertisement / campaignWhat is the single action being requested? Who is the target and who is being excluded?Headline / image / body / logo hierarchy; the placement of the imperative; white space as a class signal; the absence of the product.
Infographic (described in words)Who produced it, and why is data being presented as a picture rather than a table?Order of the statistics; scale and cropping; colour coding; what has been left out of the visual field entirely.

4.2 Content and context

Content is what is said; context is everything outside the page that makes it mean what it means: who produced it, when, where, for which audience, against which other texts. In Language and Literature, context is not background colour — it is often the whole argument. A speech about curfew means one thing in a school hall and another in a parliament.

How to use it

Never write a paragraph of biography. Instead, attach context to a choice in one clause: "by invoking the language of occupation, the speaker draws on a post-war European context in which curfew carries a single, damning referent." One clause, one effect, done. Context that is not attached to a choice is a band-3 habit.

4.3 Style: the seven features you must be able to define and use

Each of these has a definition you could say out loud in the exam, and each is shown below in a short original example sentence written for this page.

Diction (word choice)
The deliberate selection of vocabulary for register, connotation and class. Ask: is this word Latinate or Anglo-Saxon, technical or domestic, abstract or concrete? Example: "The committee relocated the residents; the residents were moved out." Two dictions, one event — the first bureaucratically euphemistic, the second physical and involuntary. The clash is the point.
Imagery (including figurative language)
Language that makes a picture or maps one thing onto another — metaphor, simile, personification, pathetic fallacy. Always ask what domain the image comes from. Example: "Grief arrived like a parcel — signed for, set down in the hall, and not opened for weeks." The domestic-absurd domain makes grief manageable and therefore more damaging.
Syntax (sentence structure)
Sentence length, order, subordination and punctuation as meaning. Short sentences accelerate or close down; long periodic sentences qualify and defer; asyndeton (no conjunctions) creates accumulation; anaphora (repeated openings) builds rhythm. Example: "We waited. We waited in the rain. We waited until the rain became the only thing we were doing." The third sentence's awkward expansion performs the stagnation it describes.
Tone
The writer's or speaker's attitude to the subject and to the reader — ironic, elegiac, bureaucratic, intimate, sneering. Tone is inferred, never stated, and it can shift inside a text. Trace it with adverbs: how would this sentence be said? Example: "The building was, of course, regrettably unsafe." Triple hedge produces a tone of institutional self-protection rather than sorrow.
Point of view
Whose consciousness filters the text, and how much they know. First person gives intimacy but also bias; free indirect discourse borrows a character's voice inside third-person narration; second person implicates the reader. Example: "You have heard this story before. You have already decided she was asking for it." The second person converts the reader from judge into accomplice.
Sound devices
Alliteration, assonance, sibilance, plosive consonants, rhyme, rhythm and caesura. In prose these work subconsciously; in poetry they carry argument. Example: "the salt silted and stiffened on the step" — sibilance produces a hissing, drying sound that mimics the evaporation it describes.
Rhetorical devices
Persuasive structures: rhetorical question, tricolon, antithesis, hyperbole, understatement, apostrophe, inclusive "we", anaphora, concession-and-refutation. They are never decorative; each one makes a claim about what the audience already believes. Example: "We do not protect young people by moving them out of sight. We protect them by seeing them." The antithesis makes the opposition sound self-evident and therefore unarguable.
Common trap

Do not write "the writer uses imagery to create a vivid picture." Vivid is not an effect — it is a compliment. The effect is always a response in a reader: discomfort, complicity, false security, grief deferred. Name the response, then say who benefits from it. That is evaluation, and it is criterion B.

5. The thesis and the paragraph that earns the marks

5.1 The thesis formula

A Paper 1 thesis must be arguable, specific and directional. It names the text's purpose, the means, and the effect. Use this template until it becomes instinct:

Through [structural move / stylistic cluster], the [text type] [verb of purpose] by [means], which [effect on the reader], ultimately suggesting [thematic claim about the guiding question].

Three worked versions of that formula:

  • "Through its adoption of the idiom of a police report, the memoir fragment transforms a private drowning into public evidence, implicating the reader in the village's arithmetic of guilt and suggesting that collective memory is a form of self-exculpation."
  • "By opening with an anecdote and closing with a statistic, the speech converts an abstract policy debate into a matter of bodily risk, positioning the student audience as potential victims rather than potential offenders."
  • "The poem's volta from inventory to revelation reframes the dead woman's bus tickets as evidence of a secret interior life, using the domestic image of travel to argue that care-work renders women's desires illegible even to their own children."
Exam technique

Put the thesis in the first 100 words, and make the guiding question's own vocabulary appear in it. If the guiding question says to what extent, your thesis needs an extent word: "largely", "only partially", "far more than it first appears". If it says how, your thesis needs a mechanism. Matching the question's command term in your first sentence is the cheapest way to secure criterion C.

5.2 The paragraph: five moves, in order

  1. Claim — one sentence answering part of the guiding question. No quotation yet. ("The text's most consequential choice is its refusal to name a single speaker.")
  2. Evidence — a short quotation or a precise paraphrase, embedded grammatically into your own sentence. Two to eight words is usually enough.
  3. Technique — name it exactly. Not "language" but "asyndetic list", "free indirect discourse", "the passive construction".
  4. Effect — what it does to a reader, and why this technique rather than another. This is where band 7 is won.
  5. Link — one clause returning to the thesis or connecting to the next paragraph. Never "in conclusion".
Why this works The anatomy of one band-7 paragraph, line by line
  1. Claim: "The speaker's refusal of the word 'curfew' is the pivot on which the whole address turns." — answers the question directly, no evidence yet.
  2. Evidence: "She defines it instead as what 'an occupying army imposes on a conquered city', then immediately offers the alternative she can accept." — eight quoted words, integrated into the syntax of the sentence.
  3. Technique: "This is redefinition by extreme analogue, followed by a tricolon of uncontroversial precedents (cigarettes, driving, motorways)."
  4. Effect: "The extremity is strategic: by making the familiar word unlivable she forces the audience to abandon a position they had already taken up, while the tricolon reassures them that she is not arguing for unlimited freedom. The reader is managed into agreement before the argument is formally made."
  5. Link: "Having dismantled the term, she can then dismantle the policy that hides inside it."

Notice what is absent: no plot summary, no "this shows that", no biography, no feature list. Every sentence is doing analytical work. That is what 5/5 on criterion B looks like at the level of a single paragraph.

6. The ten most expensive mistakes

Common trap
  1. Summarising instead of analysing. If a sentence of yours could be replaced by a one-line plot summary without loss, delete it.
  2. Quoting too much. A quotation longer than about eight words is usually hiding the fact that you have not decided what to say about it.
  3. Dropping quotations in without grammar. "This is shown when 'the sea gives back what it wants'." A quote must be part of your sentence, with a verb after it.
  4. Feature-spotting. "There is a metaphor… There is also alliteration…" — a list with no argument earns band 2–3 on B no matter how long it is.
  5. Ignoring the guiding question after the introduction. Re-read the question after every paragraph. If the paragraph does not answer it, cut it.
  6. Writing about the author's feelings. "García Márquez feels…" — you cannot know. Write "the narrator's account implies…" or "the text positions…".
  7. Confusing tone with mood. Tone belongs to the writer/speaker; mood belongs to the reader. Do not swap them.
  8. One text, one idea, five times. Four paragraphs that all say "the writer creates sympathy" is one paragraph. Plan four different mechanisms.
  9. No conclusion, or a conclusion with a new idea. The conclusion answers the guiding question in fresh words and states the implication. Nothing new.
  10. Running out of time. An unfinished essay loses C and D catastrophically. Finish, always, even if paragraph 5 becomes two sentences.

Text A — literary prose (original)

Original practice material

The following text was written for this page. It is not an extract from any published work.

The Salt Line

Everyone in São Bento knew the tide would take him. That is the thing no one in the town will admit now, and the thing the fishermen repeat every year on the sixth of November, when the water comes up as far as the church step and the women set candles along the wall.

Benedito was my brother. He was eleven and I was nine and the sea was not a place to us, it was a fact, like the price of ice or the way our mother's hands smelled of limes. On the morning it happened he had been sent to fetch salt from Dona Ilka, who kept it in a tin by the door and never counted. He took the long way, past the nets.

I have asked each of them separately, over forty-one years, and each of them has told me the same thing in a different order. Seu Afrânio saw him at the boats at seven. Dona Ilka saw him at nine and gave him the salt and told him to run. Joana, who was sweeping, saw him at ten, walking the other way, towards the point, with the salt still in his hand. The priest, who does not like to be questioned, says he saw him at noon, already up to his knees, and that he called out and the boy turned and smiled.

There are four accounts and there is one boy and the arithmetic does not close. My mother kept the salt. It sat on the shelf for years, in the tin, going hard, and when she died I found that she had written the date on the lid in pencil: 6 xi. Nothing else. No name.

The sea gives back what it wants to and keeps the rest. Every year on the sixth the water comes up the street to the church step, and the candles are lit, and the town stands in a line along the wall, watching the place where he went in, and nobody says the thing that everyone in São Bento knows: that he was walking towards it for three hours, in full view of us all, and that we have spent forty-one years calling that an accident.

Guiding question. Discuss how the writer uses the conventions of testimony and reportage to present the community's relationship to guilt.

Model commentary A ~830 words · with examiner's marginal comments

Introduction and thesis. The fragment adopts the machinery of an inquiry — dated sightings, named witnesses, a documented object — in order to show a community that has spent four decades manufacturing testimony as a substitute for intervention. Its first sentence is the thesis of the whole piece: "Everyone in São Bento knew the tide would take him." Knowledge is attributed not to a person but to a collective, and the verb "knew" is placed before any event has been narrated, so that guilt is established as a condition prior to the death rather than a consequence of it. Through the cold arithmetic of witness statements, the first-person plural of the closing lines, and the symbolic hardening of the salt, the text argues that reportage, in this community, is not a route to truth but a technology of self-exculpation.

Paragraph 2 — the reportage frame. The middle section is built from the conventions of police evidence: four witnesses, four times of day, each introduced with the flat, documentary sentence pattern "X saw him at…". The syntactic repetition is the point. Each statement is grammatically identical, which produces the impression of reliability while simultaneously exposing its fragility, because the accumulation of four incompatible times produces not a timeline but a contradiction. The narrator names this explicitly — "There are four accounts and there is one boy and the arithmetic does not close" — and the movement from a numerical to a mathematical register ("does not close" is the language of an audit) turns grief into a failed reconciliation. The effect is to make the reader perform the community's own evasion: we count, we check, and only then do we notice that nobody acted.

Paragraph 3 — the collective pronoun and the shifting "we". The text's most consequential choice is its management of the first-person plural. The narrator begins with "Everyone… knew", distances into "the fishermen" and "the women" as though observing a species, and only in the final sentence admits the pronoun that matters: "in full view of us all, and… we have spent forty-one years calling that an accident." The delayed confession is structurally prepared by the earlier phrase "I have asked each of them separately" — a claim of investigator's detachment that the closing line demolishes. The effect is a slow collapse of the boundary between witness and participant: the reader realises, in the last clause, that the narrator has been building a case file against himself. Guilt here is not denied; it is administered.

Paragraph 4 — object symbolism and the refusal of naming. The salt functions as the text's most compressed symbol. Sent for as a domestic errand, it remains in the boy's hand at ten, is kept by the mother for years, "going hard" — a physical change that mirrors the community's emotional calcification — and is finally inscribed only with the date, "6 xi", and "No name". The refusal of the name is devastating precisely because the passage is otherwise saturated with names (Benedito, Dona Ilka, Seu Afrânio, Joana): the one word withheld is the one word that would constitute an acknowledgment. The Roman numeral is a further chill, borrowing the formality of an official record for what is, in fact, an erasure. Diction does the work here — "kept", "sat", "hard" are plain monosyllables among the more discursive sentences, and their bluntness prevents any sentimental reading of the object.

Paragraph 5 — cyclical structure and fatalism. The piece opens and closes with the same ritual, and the repetition is exact enough to be structural: "the water comes up as far as the church step" becomes "the water comes up the street to the church step", and "the women set candles along the wall" becomes "the town stands in a line along the wall". The substitution of "the town" for "the women" widens responsibility from a gendered ritual to the whole settlement. This circularity enacts fatalism as form: the community has converted a preventable death into an annual liturgy, and the reader is made to feel the suffocating comfort of that conversion. The final chiasmus — "gives back what it wants to and keeps the rest" — gives the sea a will while denying it a morality, which is precisely how the town prefers to think of itself.

Paragraph 6 — tone and the reader's position. Tone is controlled throughout by a deliberate flatness. Even the most emotive material is delivered in declarative, almost bureaucratic sentences; the single moment of softness — "the way our mother's hands smelled of limes" — is embedded in a simile that compares the sea to "the price of ice", so that intimacy is immediately re-categorised as inventory. The effect on the reader is discomfort of a specific kind: we are never permitted to mourn, because to mourn would be to accept the town's own framing of the event as tragedy rather than negligence. The text therefore withholds catharsis as a moral strategy.

Conclusion. By borrowing the apparatus of testimony — witnesses, times, exhibits — and then demonstrating that the apparatus produces four irreconcilable accounts, the writer shows a community using documentation to dissolve responsibility rather than to establish it. The reportage is immaculate and the report is worthless. That is the text's final, cold insight: a town can know everything about a death and still call it an accident, provided it knows collectively rather than individually.

Clearing Out

For three days we sorted her into boxes:
keep, charity, bin. My mother did it
by rote — plates, towels, the good scissors —
until she opened the second drawer
and found them: forty-one bus tickets,
each one dated in her own small hand,
each one the same route, the same stop,
the same three pennies' worth of going
to a street we never once visited.

We sat on the floor. The kettle
went on, went off. Nobody spoke; then
she said — and this is the part I keep —
She was travelling somewhere all those years
and we thought she was only shopping.

Outside, the bins lid-lid-lid in the wind.
I have kept one ticket. It says nothing,
it says 4th March, it says 1979,
it says: she went. She went. She went.

Guiding question. Analyse how the poet uses structure and imagery to present the relationship between domestic routine and a hidden inner life.

Model commentary B ~800 words · with examiner's marginal comments

Introduction and thesis. "Clearing Out" is built on a single structural irony: a poem about disposal becomes a poem about discovery. Duffy-like in its domestic free verse and in its use of a quiet object to carry an enormous retrospect, it moves from the bureaucratic triage of a dead woman's possessions to the recognition that her interior life was conducted entirely inside the mundane. Through the poem's shift from list to lyric, its conversion of the ticket from evidence into voice, and its closing anaphora, the poet argues that domestic routine is not the opposite of a secret life but its camouflage.

Paragraph 2 — the opening inventory. The first stanza is deliberately prosaic. The colon after "boxes" delivers a tricolon — "keep, charity, bin" — which reduces a lifetime to a waste-management decision, and the blunt monosyllables refuse any elegiac tone. "by rote" is the stanza's key phrase: it establishes that the daughter is performing grief as a procedure, so that the poem's later disruption is felt physically. The list that follows ("plates, towels, the good scissors") uses asyndeton to suggest the mechanical speed of sorting, and the definite article in "the good scissors" is the only moment of affection — a small, telling admission that these objects were once persons' possessions. The effect is to position the reader as a co-conspirator in a disrespectful efficiency, which makes the drawer opening feel like a trespass.

Paragraph 3 — the volta and the evidence. The poem turns on "until", a hinge word that converts administration into revelation. The tickets are quantified with absurd precision — "forty-one" — and the number is the poem's central device, because a precise count proves that the journeys were deliberate and repeated rather than accidental. The anaphora of "each one… each one… the same… the same" mimics the uniformity of the tickets themselves: same route, same stop, same fare. Yet the repeated "same" is exactly what makes them extraordinary, and the poet exploits that paradox. The most sophisticated line is "the same three pennies' worth of going": by making "going" a purchasable commodity, the line both measures how little the freedom cost in money and how much it cost in subterfuge. The destination — "a street we never once visited" — completes the stanza's argument: the family's ignorance was total and voluntary.

Paragraph 4 — the mother's voice and its framing. The second stanza is the poem's still point. Its syntax collapses into fragments ("We sat on the floor. The kettle / went on, went off") with caesura doing the work of silence, and enjambment across "The kettle / went on" creating a small domestic rhythm that stands in for the absence of speech. Into that silence the italicised line arrives as reported speech, and the parenthesis "and this is the part I keep" is crucial: the speaker tells us explicitly that she is curating her own memory, which quietly aligns her with her grandmother's secrecy. The mother's sentence is itself structured as a correction — "She was travelling… and we thought she was only shopping" — an antithesis that reframes a whole life in eleven words. Travelling and shopping are the same physical act described from the outside and the inside, and the poem's whole argument is contained in that gap.

Paragraph 5 — the closing anaphora and the object as voice. The final stanza performs the transformation of the ticket from evidence into testimony. "Outside, the bins lid-lid-lid in the wind" is the poem's only overtly poetic line: the hyphenated onomatopoeia reproduces the mechanical, indifferent sound of the world continuing, and its placement immediately before the speaker's assertion isolates her against that indifference. Then the tricolon "It says nothing, / it says 4th March, it says 1979" builds through anaphora to give a mute object a voice, and the climax — "it says: she went. She went. She went" — uses three short, end-stopped sentences in place of a single continuous one. The repetition is a refusal of past-tense closure: each "went" is a separate act, restored to the grandmother as agency. Effect on the reader: we end not mourning a woman who is gone but witnessing a woman who repeatedly left.

Paragraph 6 — tone and the reader's complicity. The tone is restrained to the point of severity until the very last line, which is why that line lands. There is no adjective of grief anywhere in the poem; grief is expressed entirely through structure — through what is counted, what is withheld, and what is repeated. The reader is denied emotional guidance and so must perform the same interpretive labour the granddaughter performs, which is the poem's ethical move: it makes us responsible for noticing.

Conclusion. The poem's structure enacts its argument. A text that opens as an inventory and closes as an anthem demonstrates, rather than asserts, that a life of domestic routine can conceal a sustained, deliberate act of selfhood. The ticket is the perfect vehicle: the smallest possible piece of paper, and the only one that can still speak.

Text C — non-literary: a speech (original)

Original practice material

Written for this page. It is an invented speech by an invented councillor.

Address to the City Youth Assembly — Councillor M. Adeyemi, 14 March, speaking to the motion "that this city should restrict the movement of under-eighteens between eleven at night and five in the morning".

Thank you. Thank you. And thank you for turning up on a Thursday, which in my experience is the single bravest thing a sixteen-year-old can do.

Let me tell you about a boy called Daniel. Daniel was fifteen. Daniel was walking home from the leisure centre at 11.40 on a Tuesday in October, and Daniel is now a file in a drawer in this building, and the file is one inch thick, and that is the whole of what is left of him.

The motion before you tonight is simple: that this city should restrict the movement of under-eighteens between eleven at night and five in the morning. I want to be honest with you about what that means. It means I am asking for a power over you. It means I am asking you to hand me, and the people in this room, the right to say where your body may be at midnight. That is not a small thing. I would not insult you by pretending it is.

So let us have the argument, not the slogan. You have been told this is a curfew. A curfew is what an occupying army imposes on a conquered city. What I am describing is closer to what we already accept without complaint: we do not let eleven-year-olds buy cigarettes, we do not let fourteen-year-olds drive, we do not let anyone, of any age, walk onto a motorway. We have never believed that freedom means the absence of every limit. We have only ever argued about where the line goes.

But — and this is the part my colleagues begged me to cut — I do not think the line is where I once thought it was. I have read the data. The data says that of the serious incidents involving young people in this city last year, sixty-one per cent happened between three and six in the afternoon, on the walk home from school. Not at midnight. Not in the dark. At half past three, on a Wednesday, in the rain.

So here is my position, and you may boo it if you like. Do not give me a curfew. Give me the three-thirty. Give me the streetlights on Estwick Road that have been broken for two years. Give me the bus that stops running at nine so that a boy has to walk. Give me the thing that is actually killing you, and I will stand here and vote for it and take the consequences.

We do not protect young people by moving them out of sight. We protect them by seeing them. That is not a curfew. That is a city.

Guiding question. Analyse how the speaker uses rhetorical and structural choices to position the audience against the very motion she has been asked to support.

Model commentary C ~860 words · with examiner's marginal comments

Introduction and thesis. This is a speech of refutation disguised as a speech of introduction, and every choice in it serves that disguise. Adeyemi has been asked to argue for a curfew and instead dismantles it, but she does so by appearing to concede every point that matters to her audience first. Through the strategic use of an anecdotal opening, the redefinition of a contested term, a public reversal staged as candour, and a closing antithesis, the speaker converts a hostile room into a constituency and positions opposition to the motion not as rebellion but as the only adult position available.

Paragraph 2 — the exordium and the management of status. The opening is a triple anaphora — "Thank you. Thank you. And thank you…" — which is both a conventional gesture of gratitude and a subtle assertion of control, since repetition of this kind establishes rhythmic authority before any argument begins. The joke that follows ("the single bravest thing a sixteen-year-old can do") performs flattery while quietly flattering herself: she is the adult who understands teenagers. This is captatio benevolentiae with an edge, and it matters because the rest of the speech depends on the audience granting her the right to disagree with her own brief.

Paragraph 3 — the anecdote as body-count. The Daniel passage is the emotional engine, and its technique is the paratactic repetition of the name — "Daniel was fifteen. Daniel was walking… Daniel is now a file…" — which produces a drumbeat that is abruptly undercut by the shift from a boy to "a file in a drawer… one inch thick". The effect is devastating because the reduction is factual rather than rhetorical: the sentence does not describe death, it administers it. Clause accumulates on clause with "and… and… and…" until the rhythm itself feels like an inventory of loss, and the flatness of "that is the whole of what is left of him" refuses the audience any catharsis. Crucially, the detail "11.40 on a Tuesday" appears to support the curfew — the boy was out late — which makes the later statistical reversal far more powerful, because the speaker has first granted the audience their own emotional premise.

Paragraph 4 — candour as a weapon. The frank passage ("I am asking for a power over you… the right to say where your body may be at midnight") is the speech's riskiest and most effective move. By naming the policy in the most coercive available language, she achieves two things at once: she demonstrates respect for the audience's intelligence, and she makes the word "curfew" untenable before she has formally rejected it. The phrase "I would not insult you by pretending it is" constructs an in-group of honest people — speaker and audience — against unnamed others who would dissemble. The reader is positioned not as a subject of policy but as a co-judge of it.

Paragraph 5 — redefinition and the tricolon of precedent. Here the rhetorical work is most deliberate. "A curfew is what an occupying army imposes on a conquered city" is definition by extreme analogue: the term is not argued against but contaminated, so that anyone still using it seems to endorse military occupation. Immediately after, she offers a tricolon of uncontroversial restrictions (cigarettes, driving, motorways) staged as an inclusive "we", which performs moderation and prevents the audience from suspecting libertarianism. The pivot sentence — "We have never believed that freedom means the absence of every limit. We have only ever argued about where the line goes" — shifts the entire question from whether to where, which is the real victory of the speech: the debate has been reframed before the data has even appeared.

Paragraph 6 — the staged reversal. The dash construction ("But — and this is the part my colleagues begged me to cut —") is a piece of theatre. It converts a change of policy into an act of conscience, and by naming her colleagues as censors she gives the audience an enemy to enjoy. The statistics that follow are delivered in the plainest possible register — "The data says…" — repeated as anaphora, and fragmented into three short negative sentences ("Not at midnight. Not in the dark.") before landing on the deflatingly ordinary "At half past three, on a Wednesday, in the rain." The bathos is deliberate: danger is relocated from the melodramatic night to the banal afternoon, and the tricolon of "three / Wednesday / rain" makes neglect sound like weather.

Paragraph 7 — the imperative anaphora and the peroration. The closing sections are built on anaphora of the imperative — "Do not give me… Give me… Give me… Give me…" — which does two things: it makes the speaker sound like a supplicant rather than an authority, and it relocates agency entirely with the audience. The repetition of "Give me" against concrete, unglamorous objects (streetlights, a bus route) strips the debate of grandeur and recasts it as municipal maintenance. The final antithesis — "We do not protect young people by moving them out of sight. We protect them by seeing them" — resolves the speech in a balanced pair that sounds like common sense, and the closing fragment "That is not a curfew. That is a city" redefines the term one last time, substituting a word of belonging for a word of coercion. The effect is to make agreement feel like patriotism.

Conclusion. Adeyemi wins by conceding everything except the conclusion. She grants the fear, grants the need for limits, grants the audience's intelligence, and then demonstrates that the proposed limit addresses the wrong hour. The speech is a reminder that in persuasive non-literary texts, structure is argument: every apparent digression is a stepping stone placed before the audience has noticed where it is being led.

8. Six further practice texts

Each is original and short. Do a 10-minute plan only: write a thesis and four paragraph claims. Then check the model thesis.

  1. Advertisement (original). Full-page charity poster. Headline, in large white type on black: YOUR PHONE HAS SEEN MORE OF THE WORLD THAN YOU HAVE. Beneath, a photograph of a pair of hands cupped around a lit screen, taken from above so that the face is not visible. Small print bottom left: "The average person checks their device 144 times a day. Children in the Sahel walk 6 km for water." Logo bottom right. No image of a child, no image of water, no request for money anywhere on the page.

    Guiding question. How does the advertisement use visual and verbal choices to provoke guilt rather than pity?

    Show model thesis

    Thesis: By withholding both the suffering child and the appeal for money, the poster refuses the reader the two exits that pity normally provides, and the juxtaposition of a luxury statistic with a survival statistic converts the private act of checking a phone into an indictment of the reader's attention rather than their wallet.

    Paragraph claims: (1) the cropped photograph denies the reader a face to feel sorry for and forces identification instead; (2) the typographic hierarchy makes the accusation unmissable and the charity's name nearly invisible; (3) the statistical juxtaposition performs an implicit moral arithmetic; (4) the absence of a call to action is the advert's most aggressive choice, since it leaves guilt unresolved.

  2. Opinion column opening (original). "There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a staff room when a student's name is mentioned, and every teacher knows it. It is not the silence of forgetting. It is the silence of a shared decision not to say the thing that would have to be reported if it were said out loud. I have been that silence for nineteen years, and I am done with it."

    Guiding question. Analyse how the writer establishes a position of authority and complicity in the opening of an opinion column.

    Show model thesis

    Thesis: The columnist builds credibility by confessing to it — the movement from an anthropological description of institutional silence to the first-person admission "I have been that silence" reframes the writer as both expert and accomplice, so that the coming accusation carries the weight of self-incrimination rather than of criticism from outside.

    Paragraph claims: (1) the abstract noun "silence" is concretised into a physical event in a named room; (2) the distinction between two kinds of silence establishes the writer's diagnostic authority; (3) "nineteen years" converts opinion into experience and pre-empts the charge of naivety; (4) "I am done with it" uses a colloquial, final register to signal a break with the professional in-group the column has just described.

  3. Social-media campaign (original, three posts). (1) "£4. This is not a typo." (2) A photo of a garment with the price tag visible, caption: "The woman who made this earned less than your coffee." (3) "We're not asking you to stop buying clothes. We're asking you to ask one question. #WhoMadeMyClothes"

    Guiding question. To what extent does the campaign's informality strengthen or weaken its argument?

    Show model thesis

    Thesis: The campaign's colloquial register and meme-scale brevity make the moral claim portable and therefore shareable, but the same choices reduce structural exploitation to an individual consumer's question, so the informality that gains reach also quietly removes the manufacturer from the frame.

    Paragraph claims: (1) the fragment opening mimics platform vernacular to avoid the tone of a lecture; (2) the comparison to coffee domesticates an abstract supply chain into something held in the hand; (3) the hashtag converts a political demand into an individual, low-cost action; (4) the concession "We're not asking you to stop buying" protects the audience's identity while narrowing the campaign's ambition.

  4. Infographic described in words (original). A government health campaign. Top third: a large red circle reading "8 HOURS". Middle: a bar chart showing average sleep by age, with the 16–24 bar in dark red and all others in pale grey. Bottom strip: three icons in a row — a brain, a heart, a graduation cap — each with one word beneath: Memory. Mood. Marks. Footer in small grey type: "Self-reported data, 2025." No source named, no sample size given.

    Guiding question. How does the infographic use design choices to present opinion as fact?

    Show model thesis

    Thesis: Through colour hierarchy, the removal of qualifying data and the placement of unverified claims beside a single authoritative number, the infographic borrows the visual grammar of science to make a behavioural exhortation look like a measurement, presenting a recommended target as though it were an observed fact.

    Paragraph claims: (1) the isolated red circle gives "8" the status of a discovered constant rather than a guideline; (2) selective colouring directs the reader's conclusion before they read any label; (3) the tricolon of icons uses alliteration to make an argument from resemblance; (4) the grey footer performs transparency while withholding the sample size that would allow it to be checked.

  5. Memoir extract (original). "My grandfather arrived at the port with one suitcase and no language. He used to say that the first English word he learned was 'queue', and that he learned it from a policeman's hand on his shoulder. He was proud of this. He told it at every family dinner for thirty years, and we all laughed, and only much later did I understand that he was telling us the story of a hand."

    Guiding question. Discuss how the writer uses a single anecdote to present the relationship between national identity and personal dignity.

    Show model thesis

    Thesis: By retelling an apparently comic immigration anecdote and then, in the final clause, stripping it of its comedy, the memoirist exposes the way assimilation narratives are built: a humiliating contact is recast as a humorous initiation so that a family can be proud of the very thing that diminished its patriarch.

    Paragraph claims: (1) "one suitcase and no language" uses a balanced, biblical rhythm to establish the grandfather as a type rather than a person; (2) the word "queue" is chosen because it is both quintessentially English and an instruction to wait; (3) "He was proud of this" is the pivot — pride attached to submission; (4) the closing reduction of thirty years of dinners to "a hand" converts memory into evidence.

  6. Satirical piece (original). "Following the discovery that the new stadium will be named after a bank, the city has announced that from next month all residents will also be known by their sponsor's name. 'It's about identity,' said a spokesperson. 'People already introduce themselves by what they do, what they earn and where they shop. We are simply making the transaction visible.' Residents may opt out for a one-off fee of £40."

    Guiding question. How does the writer use satire to criticise the language of corporate sponsorship?

    Show model thesis

    Thesis: By extending the logic of naming-rights from buildings to people, the piece exposes the metaphor already latent in corporate language, and the deadpan official register — including the final, perfectly reasonable-sounding fee — implicates the reader, who has already accepted the first transaction without objection.

    Paragraph claims: (1) the extension from stadium to person is a reductio that reveals the premise; (2) the spokesperson's "It's about identity" uses corporate euphemism to describe commodification in the language of selfhood; (3) the tricolon "what they do, what they earn and where they shop" accepts a wholly materialist definition of the person; (4) the opt-out fee is the satirical climax, monetising the only remaining form of refusal.
