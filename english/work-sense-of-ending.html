The Sense of an Ending

Julian Barnes, 2011. About 150 pages. A retired man receives a small legacy and a dead friend's diary, investigates his own past, and discovers that the account he has been living on for forty years is false. This is the shortest and most structurally cunning of your four works, and it is the one that most rewards a student who reads for method: the novel is built so that Part Two destroys Part One. If you can explain how that is engineered, you have a band-7 paragraph on almost any question.

1. Author and context

Julian Barnes (b. 1946) is an English novelist closely associated with European-influenced, formally playful fiction — Flaubert's Parrot, A History of the World in 10½ Chapters, Elizabeth Finch. He writes about history, memory and the difficulty of knowing anything for certain, and he is a specialist in the first-person narrator who is quietly, devastatingly wrong. The Sense of an Ending won the Booker Prize in 2011.

Context worth a single clause in an essay: the novel is set across the 1960s (a grammar-school sixth form in suburban London, the era's sexual and philosophical confidence) and the early 2000s (retirement, divorce, the internet). Barnes uses the first period to show a young man certain that he is the hero of a rational story, and the second to dismantle that certainty. The title itself is borrowed from Frank Kermode's criticism about how humans impose endings on time in order to make it mean something.

Why this text matters

Most novels ask you to notice what a character learns. This one asks you to notice the machinery by which a person avoids learning. Tony Webster does not lie to the reader; he lies to himself with such consistency that the narration itself is the evidence. That makes it the most efficient text you have for any question about perspective, memory, or narrative reliability.

2. Form and structure: two parts, and the destruction

Part One — a memoir nobody should trust

Part One is the autobiography Tony has been quietly composing for decades: school, the four friends, the arrival of Adrian Finn (brilliant, quiet, philosophical), Tony's relationship with Veronica, the humiliating weekend at her family's house, the break-up, Adrian's request for permission to see Veronica, Tony's letter, and finally Adrian's suicide — which Tony narrates as a philosophical decision he more or less admires. Throughout, Tony presents himself as moderate, decent, slightly hard done by, and above all reasonable.

Part Two — the demolition

A legacy arrives: £500 and Adrian's diary, left by Veronica's mother. Tony, who claims he has "no curiosity", immediately begins an obsessive investigation. He emails Veronica, stalks her on the internet, meets her repeatedly, and slowly the frame moves: Veronica is not the cold, punishing girl of Part One; Tony is not the reasonable man. He obtains a copy of the letter he wrote forty years earlier, and it is vicious — he had wished harm on them and on any child they might have. And the final revelation: Adrian had later had an affair with Veronica's mother, she had become pregnant, and the disabled man Tony saw at a care home with Veronica — the man he had casually dismissed — is Adrian's son.

How to use it

The structural claim you must be able to make in the exam, in one clean sentence: Part Two does not add information to Part One; it reclassifies it. Every event in Part One stays exactly as it was reported; everything about Tony's account of it is altered. That is the definition of dramatic irony at the level of an entire book. Practise saying it without hedging.

Part One, §1
Sixth-form memories, offered as reliable: "I can picture…", lists of what he remembers. Establishes Tony as a careful rememberer — which is precisely the claim Part Two destroys.
Part One, §2
Adrian arrives; the philosophy of suicide; Tony's relationship with Veronica and the weekend at Chislehurst; the break-up and the letter. Adrian's suicide is reported at a cool distance.
Part Two, §1
The legacy, the diary, the emails, and Tony's growing — and unflattering — obsession. The register begins to betray him.
Part Two, §2
The letter, the reunion, the truth about Adrian and Veronica's mother, the damaged son, and the Severn bore. The novel ends not with resolution but with unease.

3. Plot in brief

Tony Webster, retired and twice-divorced in the loosest sense, recalls his sixth form, his friendship with the intellectually formidable Adrian Finn, and his awkward relationship with Veronica Ford. After they split, Adrian asks Tony's permission to take up with her; Tony writes Adrian a letter whose contents he will later describe as generous. Adrian and Veronica become a couple; Adrian, some years later, kills himself with a reasoned argument about the value of life. Decades later Mrs Ford leaves Tony £500 and Adrian's diary. Tony pursues it, reconnects with a wary Veronica, and is forced to read the letter he actually wrote and to learn the truth about Adrian's later relationship with Veronica's mother. He ends the book understanding more than he can bear and feeling less than he should.

5. Themes

Memory as self-serving fiction
The novel's central proposition. Tony's memory does not decay; it edits. He remembers the version of events in which he was kind, and the letter is the physical object that disproves him. Barnes's point is that memory is not a store but a production, produced for a purpose — usually self-justification.
Responsibility and its avoidance
Tony's life is a study in the minimisation of responsibility: "I wanted life not to bother me too much." The novel's closing sentence — the accumulation, the responsibility, the great unease — is the moment he stops minimising and still cannot act.
Time and how it is misrepresented
Tony's theory of "the sense of an ending" argues that endings are imposed from inside the middle of things, and the Severn bore image extends it: a wave travelling upstream, so that a consequence arrives before its cause seems possible.
History versus memory
The novel's working definition — history as the certainty produced where the imperfections of memory meet the inadequacies of documentation — is Barnes's joke at both his narrator's and the historian's expense.
Regret without redemption
Tony ends knowing more and feeling less. The refusal of catharsis is the book's most adult and most examinable quality.

6. Language and style

Tony's defensive register

This is the single most important technical feature, and the one students most often miss because it looks like plain writing. Tony's register is characterised by:

  • Hedging — "I think", "I suppose", "probably", "more or less", "or perhaps". Hedging reduces the narrator's liability for every claim he makes.
  • Dismissive closures — "That was about it, really", "and so on", "You get the picture". These close off areas he does not want to examine; they are structural avoidance in a clause.
  • Mock modesty — he presents himself as unremarkable, which is a claim to being inoffensive, and therefore to being blameless.
  • Abstract, methodological vocabulary — he prefers "the sense of an ending", "history", "responsibility" to any concrete emotion. Abstract nouns let him discuss his life without feeling it.
  • Reported rather than quoted speech — he summarises what other people felt, which lets him control their testimony without ever having to produce it.
Exam technique

When you quote Tony, choose a hedge rather than a revelation. "That was about it, really" is worth more to you than any dramatic line in the book, because you can spend a whole paragraph on what the sentence is doing: closing a subject, performing modesty, and protecting the narrator. Three techniques from five words.

The Severn bore

Near the end, Tony recalls watching the Severn bore — a tidal wave that travels upstream, against the current of the river. It is the novel's master image and it does three things at once. It figures time as something that can run backwards, so that a consequence can overtake its cause. It figures Tony's own investigation, which is himself travelling upstream into a past he had assumed had flowed away. And it is the only moment of genuine wonder in a book largely written in a flat, aggrieved key — which is how you know Barnes means it.

Repetition and the return of phrases

Phrases from Part One recur in Part Two with their meaning inverted. This is the novel's structural technique at sentence level, and it is what makes the book re-readable: a casual claim early on — that he wrote a decent letter, that he has no curiosity, that Veronica was cold — returns later as a proved lie. Reading the novel a second time is not optional; it is where the evidence is.

Register shifts in Part Two

As Tony becomes obsessed, his control loosens: the emails become pushy, the hedges thin out, and the vocabulary turns acquisitive (hunt, obtain, track down, find out). The prose becomes less moderate precisely when it should be more honest. That inversion is Barnes's cruellest joke on his narrator.

8. Possible global issues

  • Beliefs, values and education: "A society that treats memory as evidence will systematically mistake self-justification for truth."
  • Culture, identity and community: "Identity is a story a person tells about themselves, and the story is edited to minimise responsibility."
  • Science, technology and the environment: "A permanent record — a diary, an email, a letter kept for forty years — can undo a life's account of itself."
  • Politics, power and justice: "Holding the evidence — as Veronica does — is a form of power over the person who produced it."

9. How this work behaves in the exam

Paper 2

Best on questions about memory, perspective, unreliable narration, structure, time, regret, intertextuality and self-deception. It is also strong on gender where the question is about evidence and voice, because Veronica holds all three. Avoid questions about a large cast or social panorama — this is an intensely narrow novel.

Individual oral

Two extracts work: the Severn bore passage (dense, metaphorical, and thematically central in about thirty lines) and the moment the letter is produced (a shift you can track sentence by sentence). 10. Six practice essay questions

  1. Discuss how Barnes presents memory as a form of self-protection.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Tony Webster's memory is not failing but functioning perfectly for a purpose: it preserves the facts and edits the blame, so that a man who wrote a cruel letter can remember himself as a reasonable one for forty years.

    Paragraphs: (1) hedging and dismissive closure as techniques; (2) the letter as the physical disproof; (3) Veronica as the archive Tony cannot control; (4) the ending's refusal to let knowledge become amendment.

  2. Analyse the significance of the novel's two-part structure.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Part Two does not continue Part One; it reclassifies it. Because no event is changed but every interpretation is, the structure itself performs the novel's argument that the past is not a record but a reading.

    Paragraphs: (1) Part One as a self-authored memoir; (2) the legacy as the device that breaks it; (3) the return of Part One's phrases with inverted meanings; (4) the ending as an anti-resolution.

  3. Explore the presentation of Tony Webster's unreliability.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Tony is not lying; he is honest about everything except himself, and the gap between the two is where the novel lives. His unreliability is produced by register rather than by invention.

    Paragraphs: (1) hedges and qualifiers; (2) reported speech as control of others' testimony; (3) his abstract vocabulary as emotional avoidance; (4) the moments his control fails.

  4. Discuss the significance of the Severn bore as an image.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: The bore is the novel's theory of time made concrete: a wave travelling against the current, it figures consequence arriving before its cause is visible, and licenses Tony's investigation as an upstream journey into a past he had assumed was settled.

    Paragraphs: (1) the image itself and its physical wrongness; (2) its relation to "the sense of an ending"; (3) how it reframes Tony's own narration; (4) why the novel needs an image rather than an explanation.

  5. Compare the presentation of the past in this novel and one other studied work.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Pair with Chronicle of a Death Foretold: both narrators are implicated in a death and reconstruct it decades later, but Tony's reconstruction is defensive under-reporting whereas García Márquez's narrator over-documents — so the novel turns memory into an alibi and the novella turns an archive into one.

    Paragraphs: (1) the investigator's pose; (2) what each account omits; (3) register; (4) how the structure betrays each narrator.

  6. To what extent does the novel offer its protagonist any redemption?

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Only in knowledge, and not in feeling. Tony ends able to state what he did and unable to feel it proportionately — and Barnes's refusal of catharsis is the novel's most serious moral claim.

    Paragraphs: (1) the final tricolon; (2) the failure of emotion at the revelation; (3) the damaged son as the irreducibility of consequence; (4) the reader's judgement as the only resolution.

Connections — which pairings work, and why
  • → Chronicle of a Death Foretold (strongest pairing): both narrators reconstruct a death they were implicated in, decades later. The contrast is the most productive one available to you: defensive omission versus documentary saturation. Use for any question on memory, testimony, guilt or time.
  • → The World's Wife: both texts are about the authority to tell a story. Barnes gives the revision to a narrator who does not realise he is revising; Duffy gives it to speakers who know exactly what they are doing. Use for intertextuality, voice and perspective questions — the volta-as-power contrast is excellent.
  • → Othello: both protagonists reach self-knowledge too late and immediately begin narrating themselves as better men. Othello's is absolute and terminal; Tony's is partial and survivable. Use for questions on regret and self-deception — but remember to compare form (play versus first-person novel), not just character.
  • → Paper 1: Tony's hedged, defensive register is the clearest example you have of tone being inferred rather than stated. Use him as your mental reference when an unseen narrator sounds reasonable.
  • → Individual oral: pair the Severn bore passage with the opinion column on digital permanence, or the letter scene with any text about holding evidence.
