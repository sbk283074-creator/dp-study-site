The Sense of an Ending

Julian Barnes, 2011. Roughly one hundred and fifty pages, two parts, and one of the most efficient trapdoors in modern fiction. An elderly retired man called Tony Webster is left five hundred pounds and a dead friend's diary by a woman he barely knew, and spends the rest of the book discovering that the account of his own life — the one he has been living on, calmly, for forty years — is false. This is the shortest work on your list and the most structurally cunning: Part Two does not continue Part One, it reclassifies it. If you can explain exactly how that hinge is engineered, you have a band-7 paragraph on almost any question about memory, perspective, time or responsibility.

Copyright and wording

Only short quotations are reproduced, with analysis, as the IB expects. Barnes's prose is quoted in fragments of a few words, which is exactly how you should use it in an exam. Check every phrase against your own edition before you memorise it: page and wording vary slightly between printings, and a handful of the phrases below are given as close paraphrase where the exact wording is uncertain. The safe habit is to memorise the shape of a quotation — a hedge, a tricolon, a repeated word — rather than hunting for a sentence you may have misremembered.

1. Why this novella rewards close reading — and why it is dangerous

Every work on your list asks you to notice something. This one asks you to notice the machinery by which a person avoids noticing. Tony Webster is not a liar in the ordinary sense. He does not invent events; the events in Part One are largely accurate and Part Two never retracts a single one of them. What Part Two destroys is the framing: the tone, the emphasis, the implications, the small grammatical choices that quietly assign blame. That means the evidence for your essay is not in the plot at all — it is in the hedges, the dismissive closures, the abstract nouns, the shift into the language of hunting and acquisition in Part Two. A student who reads for event will find a very short book. A student who reads for method will find that every page is doing something.

It is dangerous for the opposite reason too. Because the book is short, plain and apparently modest, it invites the two worst habits in the subject: summary and sympathy. Students summarise because there is so little plot to summarise, and they sympathise because Tony is so careful to present himself as a decent, slightly put-upon man. He is not a decent man — or rather, he is, and that is the problem. Barnes's bet is that an ordinary, moderate, mildly self-pitying person can do real damage and then misremember it as restraint, and that the misremembering will be more interesting than a villain would be.

What students expect to write about

Memory. Regret. Nostalgia. "The past is a foreign country." Tony looked back on his youth. All true, all worthless on their own, because they are true of dozens of novels. They are topics. None of them contains a mechanism, and a paragraph without a mechanism is a paragraph of plot summary with a quotation stapled to it.

What actually earns marks

A device, tracked. How does four-word phrase — "That was about it, really" — perform modesty, close off an area of enquiry and protect a narrator, all at once? How does a first-person narrator who is scrupulous about facts become unreliable about himself without ever telling a falsehood? Answer in those terms and every body paragraph has something to point at on the page.

Why this text matters for a Language and Literature course

This is not a Literature course. The course wants you to be able to move between a novel and a speech, an advertisement, a piece of reportage, and to say how each of them produces a version of reality. The Sense of an Ending is the work in your list most explicitly about that production: Tony is a writer of his own life, and the book shows you his drafts. Once you can hear a hedge doing defensive work in a novel, you can hear the same hedge doing defensive work in a politician's apology or a brand's statement on Paper 1. That transferability is why this is the work I would reach for on any question containing the word perspective.

The one-sentence claim to memorise

Tony Webster is not unreliable because he misremembers; he is unreliable because he grammaticalises his own innocence. Everything else on this page is evidence for that sentence. It answers questions on memory, narration, responsibility, identity, self-deception and time, and it is specific enough that no examiner will have read it before.

2. Author and context that actually earns marks

Context in an IB answer is a condiment, not a meal. One clause attached to a specific choice; never a paragraph of biography before you reach the words. Below is the context that genuinely changes what an examiner thinks of your analysis, and then — in §2.4 — the context that students use as filler.

2.1 Barnes and the shape of a career

Julian Barnes (b. 1946) is an English novelist whose work has been consistently concerned with the same question from different angles: how much of what we call knowledge is really a story we have agreed to tell. Flaubert's Parrot (1984) sends a widower hunting through the documentary remains of a dead writer and finds only contradictory evidence; A History of the World in 10½ Chapters (1989) retells the same flood myth in ten incompatible registers; Arthur & George is about a miscarriage of justice reconstructed from paperwork. Read as a sequence, the career is an argument: the archive never delivers the certainty it promises.

That is the useful context, and it is one clause long: Barnes has spent forty years writing novels in which documents fail to settle the question they were produced to settle. In this novel the failed document is a diary, a letter and a memory, and the failure is not accidental but structural — the documents survive, and it is the reader of them who is exposed.

2.2 The title, and Frank Kermode

The title is taken from Frank Kermode's The Sense of an Ending: Studies in the Theory of Fiction (1967), a work of criticism arguing that human beings impose fictional shapes — beginning, middle, end — on time in order to make it feel meaningful. Kermode's key move is the distinction between chronos (empty, sequential time) and kairos (the significant moment, the point at which time seems to mean something). Novels, on his account, exist because we cannot bear time that merely passes.

This is the single most valuable piece of context you have, because Tony's narration is a demonstration of Kermode's thesis in action. Tony does not experience his life as a sequence; he experiences it as a plot with a shape, and he edits the sequence to fit the shape. Note also that the book is not about Kermode's idea; it uses it, and then quietly shows that the imposition of an ending is a moral act rather than a literary one. The endings Tony imposes on other people — on Veronica, on Adrian, on his own youth — are the ones that cost him.

Connection

Kermode's distinction is also the best tool you have for Chronicle of a Death Foretold, where a whole community treats kairos — the significant, fated moment — as something that has already happened before it does. Two narrators, two failures to live in sequence: one under-reports, one over-documents. See §11 for how to write that comparison.

2.3 Two periods, one man

The novel moves between the early 1960s and the early 2000s, and it needs both. The 1960s sections are set in the sixth form of a London grammar school and in the Fords' suburban house: a world of intellectual confidence, sexual negotiation conducted in code, class anxiety expressed as knowingness about other people's carpets, and young men who believe that arguing well is the same as being right. The 2000s sections are set in retirement, divorce, email, and the strange flatness of a life that has stopped generating events.

Two consequences worth using. First, the younger Tony's confidence is philosophical, and the novel is very funny about it: he and his friends discuss suicide, responsibility and history as though these were puzzles to be solved in a school magazine. The older Tony has inherited the vocabulary without the confidence, which is why he reaches for abstraction whenever feeling threatens. Second, the internet arrives in Part Two as an instrument. Tony stalks Veronica online, finds her, and uses the technology of the second period to pursue a grievance from the first — a nice, precise point about how much of the novel is about the will to know rather than the difficulty of knowing.

2.4 The Booker, and the context that is filler

The novel won the Booker Prize in 2011, after Barnes had been shortlisted three times. It is a very short book to win a prize often given to very long ones, and part of the interest at the time was how much it achieved in so little space. That single fact is usable: "Barnes compresses a life into a hundred and fifty pages, and the compression is itself the argument — a whole existence reduced to the summary its owner can bear."

Common trap — biography and theory as padding

Three pieces of context lose marks here. (1) Barnes's biography. He is not Tony; the novel is not disguised memoir. Nothing in your essay should depend on the author's life. (2) Long explanations of Kermode. One clause defining "the sense of an ending" is excellent; a paragraph on Kermode's career is a paragraph in which you are not analysing the text. (3) Quoting the blurb. "A novel about memory and regret" is not analysis. The moment context stops doing analytical work, cut it.

How to use this in a paragraph

Do not write "Barnes wrote this book in 2011 after a long career". Write: "the novel's two periods are not backdrop but equipment: the 1960s supply Tony with a philosophical vocabulary for avoiding guilt, and the 2000s supply him with the technology for pursuing everyone but himself." That is context doing analytical work in a single clause, which is criterion A at band 7.

3. Form, structure and narrative technique

Three formal facts decide everything about this book, and all three are examinable: it is told in retrospective first person by a narrator with an interest; it is built in two parts, of which the second reclassifies the first; and its central objects are documents — a diary, a letter, a bequest, a memory — whose authority is contested. Get these three straight and you can answer almost any question the exam asks about it.

3.1 The two-part hinge: Part Two does not extend Part One

Part One is the memoir Tony has been quietly composing for decades. It covers the sixth form, the friendship group, the arrival of Adrian Finn, the relationship with Veronica, the humiliating weekend at her family's house, the break-up, Adrian's request for permission to take up with her, Tony's letter, and Adrian's suicide — which young Tony narrates as a philosophical decision he more or less admires. The governing impression Tony works hard to produce is that he is moderate: not clever like Adrian, not successful like Veronica's brother Jack, but decent, sane, and slightly hard done by.

Part Two begins with a letter from a solicitor. Sarah Ford — Veronica's mother, whom Tony remembers as a comic sketch — has left him five hundred pounds and Adrian's diary. Veronica holds the diary and does not want to hand it over. Tony, who claims he was never a curious man, immediately begins an obsessive investigation: emails, internet searches, meetings, a visit to a care home. What he finds dismantles every load-bearing claim in Part One. The letter he wrote was not generous but vicious. Adrian's relationship was not with Veronica alone but, later, with her mother. The disabled man Tony saw with Veronica and casually dismissed is Adrian's son. And Tony's own account of himself — the reasonable man — is the least reliable document in the book.

How to use it — the structural claim, in one sentence

Part Two does not add information to Part One; it reclassifies it. Every event stays exactly as it was reported; everything about Tony's account of it changes. That is dramatic irony operating at the level of an entire book, and it is also the novel's epistemology made structural: the past is not a record, it is a reading. Practise saying this without hedging, in one clean sentence, at the top of a paragraph — then spend the rest of the paragraph proving it with phrases that return inverted.

3.2 "But wait" — the hinge inside the hinge

Part One does not end with a conclusion. It ends with an interruption. Having produced a settled, slightly complacent account — the suicide explained, the ex-girlfriend dismissed, the narrator exonerated — Tony stops himself with a self-interrupting "But wait", and offers the reader an image of "the little grey circles" left behind by wet glasses, as though a detail too small to matter were nevertheless the thing he cannot stop seeing. (Check the wording in your edition; the effect is what matters.) Why does this matter so much?

  1. It performs the book's method at the moment of transition. The narrator has just finished an act of self-editing and immediately reopens it. The "but wait" is the sound of a man catching himself mid-justification — and it is placed precisely where a reader would otherwise close the book satisfied.
  2. It makes the trivial load-bearing. The grey circles are nothing: marks on a surface. Their importance is entirely a function of what the narrator cannot say. Barnes's point is that memory attaches itself to detail precisely when the feeling attached to the detail is unmanageable.
  3. It licenses Part Two. If Part One ended cleanly, the investigation would be a character's whim. Because it ends with an unresolved, over-precise image, the reader arrives in Part Two already suspecting that the surface is where the truth is buried — and we are right, but not in the way we expect.
  4. It is the first of the book's self-corrections. Part Two is full of them: "I think", "or perhaps", "let me start again". The narration is revision made visible, and the hinge teaches you to read every revision as an admission.
Common trap — calling the hinge a twist

Students describe the end of Part One as a "plot twist" and then write about suspense. This novel has no suspense; it has retroactive reclassification. The correct formulation is: "the reader's second reading of Part One is the experience the novel is built to produce, and the hinge exists to guarantee that there will be a second reading." If your paragraph could apply equally to a detective story, it is describing the wrong book.

3.3 Time: retrospection, prolepsis and the bore

The narration is retrospective throughout, but it is not simply "looking back". Tony's retrospective voice does three distinct things with time, and separating them gives you a vocabulary most students do not have.

  • Prolepsis that he does not know he is making. The opening pages contain the image of a river running "nonsensically upstream" and of bathwater gone cold behind a locked door — both of which are the ending, planted before the beginning. He is not foreshadowing on purpose; the images have escaped his own editing. That is the important difference between a narrator who plants clues and a narrator who is haunted.
  • The Severn bore. A tidal wave that travels against the current of the river. It figures time as capable of running backwards, so that a consequence can arrive before its cause is visible; it figures Tony's investigation as an upstream journey; and it is the only moment of genuine wonder in a book otherwise written in a flat, aggrieved key — which is how you know Barnes means it. The image is planted on the first page and paid off at the end.
  • The undertow. Where the bore is spectacular, the undertow is insidious: the pull that takes your feet from under you when you thought you were standing safely. Tony reaches for it to describe how the past keeps reclaiming him. The pairing matters — one image for the dramatic return of the past, one for its quiet, continuous drag — and it gives you two different answers to "how does the novel present time?".

3.4 The documents: diary, letter, bequest

The plot is driven entirely by papers, and each document does a different job. This is the most under-exploited area of the novel in student essays, and the most rewarding, because it connects directly to the course's interest in how texts function as objects in the world.

DocumentWhat Tony believes it isWhat it turns out to beWhat it does structurally

Why the letter matters more than any other object

A diary is evidence about its author; a letter is evidence about its writer and its recipient. Tony's letter is the one object in the book that Tony himself produced, and he has been misremembering its contents for four decades — which means the unreliability is not a failure of storage but an act of authorship. He did not forget what he wrote. He rewrote it. That distinction is the whole argument of the novel, and it is worth a paragraph on its own.

3.5 How the unreliability is engineered

Wayne Booth's useful distinction is between a narrator who reports unreliably and one who interprets unreliably. Tony does the second, which is harder to spot and much more interesting to write about. Barnes installs five mechanisms, and each is quotable:

  1. Hedging. "I think", "I suppose", "probably", "or perhaps", "more or less". Every hedge reduces the narrator's liability for the claim it qualifies.
  2. Dismissive closure. "That was about it, really"; "and so on"; "you get the picture". These shut down areas he does not want examined. Avoidance in a clause.
  3. Reported rather than quoted speech. He summarises what other people felt. That lets him control their testimony without ever producing it — and it means the two most important people in the book, Adrian and Veronica, are heard almost entirely through his paraphrase.
  4. Abstract nouns in place of feelings. "Responsibility", "history", "the sense of an ending". Abstraction lets him discuss his life without inhabiting it.
  5. Narrative gaps he does not notice. He skips the years, elides his marriage, and summarises his own cruellest acts in a subordinate clause. The silences are shaped like a man protecting something.
Exam technique

When you quote Tony, quote a hedge rather than a revelation. "That was about it, really" is worth more to you than any dramatic line in the book, because a five-word throwaway gives you three techniques — colloquial closure, performed modesty, structural avoidance — and therefore three sentences of analysis. Quote the boring sentence. It is where the narrator is working hardest.

Part One, §1
The famous list of remembered fragments — a shiny inner wrist, a wet sink, a river running upstream, bathwater gone cold — followed by the admission that "what you end up remembering isn't always the same as what you have witnessed". Establishes Tony as a careful rememberer, and immediately disqualifies that claim in the same breath.
Part One, §2
Adrian arrives; the philosophy of suicide; Veronica; the weekend at her family's house; the break-up; Adrian's request; the letter. Adrian's death is reported at a cool distance and filed as a position rather than a loss.
Part Two, §1
The bequest, the diary, the emails, the internet searches, the meetings. The register begins to betray him: the vocabulary turns acquisitive — hunt, obtain, track down, find out — exactly as his claims to disinterest intensify.
Part Two, §2
The letter produced, Veronica's "You still don't get it, do you?", the truth about Adrian and Sarah Ford, the damaged son, the exchange about "blood", and the Severn bore. The novel ends not with resolution but with accumulation, responsibility and great unease.

4. Plot, characters and relationships

You need the plot only as scaffolding — but you need it accurately, because a misplaced moment undermines an otherwise good paragraph. Here it is in the order in which it is revealed, which is not the order in which it happened, and that difference is the book.

The 1960s
A London grammar-school sixth form; Tony, Colin and Alex, and then Adrian Finn, who arrives cleverer than all of them and unfashionably serious. Tony begins seeing Veronica Ford. The relationship is conducted largely in argument and innuendo, and it founders on the weekend he spends at her family's house in the suburbs — a weekend he remembers as humiliation at the hands of her brother Jack and her faintly comic parents, and which Veronica clearly remembers as something else: his failure to defend her. They split. Later, Adrian writes to ask Tony's permission to take up with Veronica. Tony replies — and the letter is the hinge of his life. Adrian and Veronica become a couple; some years later Adrian kills himself, in the bath, having written a reasoned argument for doing so.
The middle, which Tony skips
Marriage, a daughter, a divorce, a career of modest arts administration, retirement. He summarises this in a few pages, and the disproportion is deliberate: the years in which he was actually living are the years he has nothing to say about. Whatever else the novel is, it is a study of a man for whom the only interesting decade was the one he got wrong.
The bequest
Sarah Ford — Veronica's mother, decades on — leaves Tony five hundred pounds and Adrian's diary. Veronica, named as executor or simply as the person holding the papers, is reluctant. Tony, who has just told the reader that he is not a curious man, becomes obsessed.
The pursuit
Emails, a meeting in a café, a walk, an online search that finds Veronica's brother's business, a visit to a care home. Tony's manner in these sections is the book's cruellest achievement: it becomes pushier, more entitled and more self-pitying precisely as he becomes more certain that he is the injured party.
The letter
Veronica produces the letter Tony wrote forty years earlier and returns it to him. It is worse than he has remembered, and worse than he is able to absorb: it wishes harm on Adrian and Veronica, and on any child they might have. He reads it and the sentence he produces is a masterpiece of understatement.
The revelation
Adrian later had an affair with Veronica's mother, Sarah; she became pregnant; the child — the disabled man Tony saw at the care home and dismissed, the man he noted only as an obstacle to his own curiosity — is Adrian's son. Sarah's bequest was not a kindness but an act of transmission. Tony's closing exchange with Veronica turns on the single word "blood", which he cannot interpret and she refuses to explain.
The ending
The Severn bore, revisited; and then the famous close: "There is accumulation. There is responsibility. And beyond these, there is great unease." No apology is made, no amendment is offered, nobody is comforted. The book refuses catharsis, and the refusal is its most serious moral claim.

4.2 Relationships — and how to write about them

The IB loves a question about relationships, and students answer it by describing who married whom. The useful move is always the same: identify the asymmetry of interpretation in the relationship. Who is allowed to define whom? Who holds the record? Every relationship in this novel is a contest over who gets to write the account.

Tony and Veronica

In Part One this is a story about a cold girl who punished him for nothing. In Part Two it is a story about a woman who has spent forty years holding a document that proves what he is, waiting to see whether he will ever become the kind of person she could hand it to. Two details to use: she refuses to explain herself — "You still don't get it, do you?" — and she withholds the diary, which converts her from character into authority. The relationship is the novel's argument about evidence: the person who holds the document holds the power, and Tony has spent forty years assuming he was the one with the pen.

Tony and Adrian

Adrian is everything Tony is not: cleverer, more serious, braver, and — decisively — dead. That last fact is what makes the relationship unresolvable. You cannot apologise to a suicide, which is precisely the point: the novel chooses the one death that forecloses amendment. Note too that Tony admires Adrian's suicide in Part One as an intellectual position, and only in Part Two, once he knows what Adrian suffered, does it become what it always was — a young man destroying himself. The reinterpretation of a death is the novel's largest single act of revision.

Tony and his younger self

The most important relationship in the book, and the one students ignore. The narration is a continuous negotiation between a man who is writing and a man who did. Sometimes he defends the young Tony ("we were young", "it was the sixties"); sometimes he disowns him ("I don't know what I was thinking"); and the shifting between those two positions is where the self-deception lives. A strong essay point: Tony treats his younger self as a separate person when he needs to disown him and as the same person when he needs to excuse him. That is a mechanism, and it is quotable.

Tony and Sarah Ford — or rather, Tony and people he considered minor

The bequest is the novel's structural joke: the person Tony remembered least fondly and thought least about is the person who turns out to have been running the plot. Her act of giving him the diary is also the novel's one moment of genuine moral agency — a dead woman arranging for the truth to reach the man who needs it, at the cost of exposing herself. Note the irony that Tony responds by obsessing about the document and never once considering the woman.

How to use this in a paragraph

Do not write "Tony and Veronica had a difficult relationship". Write: "the relationship is a forty-year contest over who is permitted to author the other: Tony spends Part One writing Veronica as cold because that is the only version in which he is blameless, and Part Two discovers that she has held the one document capable of disproving him, and has been waiting forty years for him to become worth showing it to." Same information, but the second version is an argument about power, evidence and narration.

5. Themes in detail — and how to write about them without being vague

A theme is not a subject. "The novel is about memory" is a subject. "The novel presents memory not as a store that decays but as a production that is continuously revised in the interests of the person doing the remembering" is an argument. Each entry below gives you where to find it, the precise formulation worth memorising, and the trap to avoid.

5.1 Memory as production, not storage

Find it: the opening list, "I remember, in no particular order", and its closing admission that "what you end up remembering isn't always the same as what you have witnessed"; the repeated "I don't remember"; the letter, misremembered for forty years; the phrase "perhaps I only remember remembering".

Write it like this: Tony's memory does not decay — it edits. He has not lost the letter; he has rewritten it, and rewritten it in the direction of innocence. Barnes's proposition is that memory is not a warehouse but a workshop, and that its output is shaped by what the rememberer needs. The measure of this is that nothing in Part One is factually retracted: the facts were always right and the emphasis was always wrong. Formulation: "memory in this novel is not the past surviving into the present but the present recruiting the past".

Common trap

Do not write that Tony "has a bad memory" or that memory is "unreliable" as a general truth. Both are band-3 moves. His memory is excellent; it is his honesty that is unreliable, and the novel is precise about the difference. The sentence to avoid is "this shows that memory can play tricks on us".

5.2 Responsibility, and the machinery of its avoidance

Find it: "I wanted life not to bother me too much", offered as a throwaway and the most honest sentence he writes; the way he files Adrian's death as a philosophical position; the closing tricolon, "There is accumulation. There is responsibility. And beyond these, there is great unease."

Write it like this: the novel's moral subject is not a crime but a disposition: the preference for a life in which nothing is ever one's fault. Tony's technique is minimisation by abstraction — he converts actions into "questions", failures into "the way things were", cruelties into "what you did then". The closing tricolon is devastating precisely because it is grammatical and empty: he can now name accumulation and responsibility without being able to feel them, and the third term — "great unease" — is the residue of a conscience that has understood and not moved.

5.3 The gap between intention and consequence

Find it: the letter, and what it actually says about "any child"; the son in the care home; Tony's later realisation that Adrian's suicide may have had nothing to do with the philosophical position Tony assigned to it.

Write it like this: Barnes rigs the plot so that one small act of spite — a letter written in an afternoon — turns out to have been aimed, unknowingly, at a child who exists. That is the gap in its purest form, and it is why the revelation lands as horror rather than as plot. The formulation worth memorising: "the novel's most frightening claim is not that we do harm, but that we do harm whose shape we will never be shown." Consequences exceed intentions in this book by forty years, and the arithmetic is never completed.

5.4 "How we edit ourselves" — narrative identity and self-deception

Find it: the return of Part One's phrases with their meanings inverted; "I was not, I think, a bad person"; "a small, mean part of me"; Tony's self-description as unremarkable.

Write it like this: identity in this novel is a text under continuous revision, and the reviser is the person with the most to gain. The technical term you can use is self-serving bias; the literary one is the unreliable narrator; the IB-friendly version is this — Tony does not lie to the reader, he edits for the reader, and editing is the more insidious act because it can be defended as honesty. Notice that he can diagnose his own pettiness ("a small, mean part of me") and still minimise it: confession becomes another form of defence.

5.5 Suicide, and the tally of a life

Find it: Adrian's reasoned argument for suicide, which Tony summarises and admires; the bathwater image in the opening list; Tony's own return to the question late in the book, where he concedes that the argument was a young man's and the death was a man's.

Write it like this: suicide is the novel's test case for interpretation, because it is the one act that forecloses revision by its subject. Adrian dies; everyone else gets to write about why. Young Tony files it under philosophy, which is a way of not grieving and not being implicated. The related idea — that a life can be totalled up, that there comes a point, somewhere around fifty, when the accounts can be audited — is Tony's, and the novel's demolition of it is total: the totals are never in, the audit never closes, and the closing tricolon refuses to produce a figure. Formulation: "the book rejects the idea of a life as a balance sheet while never releasing its narrator from the habit of keeping one".

5.6 History, documents and the failure of the archive

Find it: "History is that certainty produced at the point where the imperfections of memory meet the inadequacies of documentation"; the diary; the school debates in which the young men congratulate themselves on their rigour; Tony's later admission that he used to say glibly that history is written by the victors.

Write it like this: the definition is a joke at two targets at once. It is a joke at the historian — certainty is what you get when two unreliable sources coincide, which is not certainty at all — and it is a joke at Tony, who has built his entire life on exactly that coincidence: his imperfect memory meets the inadequate documentation of a letter he wrote, and the result is forty years of false certainty. This is the novel's epistemology in one sentence, and it is the best single quotation in the book.

5.7 Regret without redemption

Find it: the ending's refusal of scenes — no confrontation, no apology, no letter back, no meeting with the son; the flatness of Tony's final paragraphs; the decision to end on a tricolon of abstract nouns.

Write it like this: the novel is unusually adult about what knowledge does. Tony learns everything and becomes barely different. Barnes denies the reader every conventional payoff — no reconciliation with Veronica, no scene of atonement, no access to the diary — and that denial is the argument: some damage is not redeemable by the person who did it, only survivable by the people it was done to. Formulation: "the ending offers the reader a verdict in place of a resolution, and makes the reader deliver it."

Which theme for which question

Questions about memory, time or perspective → §5.1 and §5.6. Questions about identity or self-representation → §5.4. Questions about responsibility, guilt or justice → §5.2 and §5.3. Questions about structure or ending → §5.7 plus §3.2. Do not try to use all seven; a Paper 2 essay needs three themes treated deeply, not seven treated thinly.

6. Language and style in detail

Barnes's prose in this novel is deliberately unshowy, which is a trap: students read it as "plain" and then find themselves writing about theme instead of technique. Every plainness here is a choice. This section is the toolkit; §7 applies it to four passages.

6.1 The defensive register — five features

FeatureWhat it looks likeWhat it is doing
Hedging"I think", "I suppose", "probably", "or perhaps", "more or less" Reduces the narrator's liability for every claim. Note that hedges cluster around the claims that matter most — which is how you know he knows.
Dismissive closure"That was about it, really"; "and so on"; "you get the picture" Shuts down an area he does not want examined. It is structural avoidance compressed into a clause, and it is always followed by a change of subject.
Mock modestyInsisting he was unremarkable, unambitious, "not one of life's high achievers" Modesty is a claim to harmlessness, and harmlessness is a claim to blamelessness. The three moves are one move.
Abstract nouns"Responsibility", "history", "the sense of an ending", "accumulation" Lets him discuss his life without inhabiting it. Abstraction is the novel's emotional anaesthetic.
Reported speech"She said she had felt…", "he made it clear that…" Controls other people's testimony without producing it. Adrian and Veronica are heard almost entirely in Tony's paraphrase, which is why Part Two — where Veronica finally speaks — is so destabilising.
Exam technique — quote the hedge, not the thunder

The instinct is to quote the dramatic revelation. Resist it. A five-word hedge gives you three techniques and three sentences of analysis; a dramatic line gives you one adjective ("devastating") and then you are stuck. When you revise, make a list of ten throwaway sentences from this novel and learn what each is defending against.

6.2 Syntax: short declaratives, aphorism, and the closing tricolon

Tony's sentences are predominantly short, paratactic and declarative: subject, verb, full stop, next sentence. That is not simplicity, it is a pose — the pose of a man reporting plain facts. Three places where the syntax changes are worth learning.

  1. The opening list. "I remember, in no particular order: — a shiny inner wrist; — steam rising from a wet sink…" Fragmentary, unpunctuated, apparently random. The form performs the claim: memory arrives as fragments, not as narrative. And the list is not random — two of its five items are the ending.
  2. The aphorism. "History is that certainty produced at the point where the imperfections of memory meet the inadequacies of documentation." Balanced, abstract, authoritative. Aphorisms are how Tony converts an opinion into a law of nature, and they are the most quotable evidence of his habit of generalising away from himself.
  3. The closing tricolon. "There is accumulation. There is responsibility. And beyond these, there is great unease." Three sentences, each with the same subject-verb shape, the third an addition rather than a conclusion. The grammar is complete; the emotion is absent. That mismatch is the novel's final technique, and it is worth an entire paragraph: asyndeton and parallel structure used to deliver a verdict the speaker cannot feel.

6.3 Repetition and the return of phrases

The novel's structural technique at sentence level: a casual claim in Part One returns in Part Two with its meaning inverted. This is what makes the book re-readable, and it is also the easiest high-scoring observation available to you, because you can put it in a table.

Phrase or claim in Part OneWhat it means thereWhat Part Two does to it
"I wrote them a decent letter"Self-congratulation; proof of his generosity The letter is produced and is vicious — the single most destructive reversal in the book
"I am not a curious man"A modest disclaimer Becomes a lie within three pages, as the vocabulary turns acquisitive
"Veronica was cold"A description of her Re-reads as a description of his own inability to read her
"Adrian chose to die"An intellectual position, admired Re-read as a young man destroying himself, with Tony's letter among the causes
"I can't remember"An admission of forgetting By sheer frequency, becomes a confession of what he does remember
"That was about it, really"Closure Becomes the signature of everything he refuses to reopen

6.4 Water: the bore, the undertow, the bath

The novel's imagery is dominated by water, and the cluster has a direction. The bathwater gone cold behind a locked door is the image of a death Tony did not witness and cannot stop re-entering; the river running upstream is time behaving impossibly; the Severn bore is consequence travelling against the current; the undertow is the past's quiet, continuous drag on a man who thought he was standing still. What the cluster does collectively is reclassify the past from something behind Tony to something beneath him — which is why the imagery is the answer to any question about time in this novel. Add the closing monosyllable "blood", and the pattern completes: blood is what you cannot argue with, the one fact no narrative can edit.

6.5 The register shift in Part Two

As Tony becomes obsessed, his control loosens in a way he does not notice. The emails become pushy, the hedges thin out, and the vocabulary turns acquisitive: hunt, obtain, track down, find out, pursue. He begins to describe Veronica in the language of a case file. The inversion is Barnes's cruellest joke on his narrator: the prose becomes less moderate precisely when it should be more honest. The measure of Tony's moral condition in Part Two is that his sentences get shorter and his claims get larger. If you want one technical observation that will lift an essay above everyone else's in the room, it is this one — and it is provable, because you can count the hedges.

Common trap

Do not describe the style as "simple" or "easy to read" and move on. Plainness is this novel's instrument, not its limitation. The sentence to write is: "Barnes gives Tony a plain, moderate, faintly aggrieved register, and then lets the register itself betray him — the plainer it sounds, the more work it is doing."

7. Close-reading worked examples

Four passages, each taken through the full chain: feature → technique → effect → meaning. This is the core of the page, and the part to practise out loud. Say the chain in one breath — you will need to in the oral.

Worked example 1 The opening list — "I remember, in no particular order"
I remember, in no particular order: — a shiny inner wrist; — steam rising from a wet sink as a hot frying pan is laughingly tossed into it; — a river rushing nonsensically upstream… — bath water long gone cold behind a locked door. This last isn't something I actually saw, but what you end up remembering isn't always the same as what you have witnessed.Tony Webster, The Sense of an Ending, Part One, opening

Feature 1 — the list form. Technique: asyndetic listing with dashes, no connecting grammar. Effect: memory is presented as fragments rather than as narrative, which flatters the narrator as a scrupulous reporter who refuses to tidy things up. Meaning: the apparent randomness is the alibi — a list looks like evidence precisely because it has not been arranged into an argument.

Feature 2 — "in no particular order". Technique: pre-emptive disclaimer. Effect: disarms the reader's suspicion of selection before it can form. Meaning: it is of course a selection, and a heavily loaded one — the claim of disorder is the first of the narrator's defences, placed at the very top of the book.

Feature 3 — the final admission. "This last isn't something I actually saw, but what you end up remembering isn't always the same as what you have witnessed." Technique: concessive self-correction, framed as an aphorism. Effect: the narrator appears to confess a limitation and in doing so establishes a general law that excuses him in advance. Meaning: the book's epistemology is stated on page one: remembering is not the same as witnessing, and Tony will spend a hundred and fifty pages discovering that he has been living on the difference.

Feature 4 — the planted ending. The river running upstream is the Severn bore; the bathwater behind a locked door is Adrian's suicide. Technique: prolepsis. Effect: the reader is given the ending before the beginning without realising it. Meaning: Tony's own unconscious has arranged the list after all, which undoes "in no particular order" — and proves that the editing is happening below the level of his awareness.

Why this is worth knowing: it gives you, in one paragraph, the list form, the disclaimer, the aphorism and the prolepsis. It is also the best single extract you could choose for the individual oral, because it is under forty lines, self-contained, and changes meaning the moment the reader finishes the book.
Worked example 2 The hinge — "But wait" and the little grey circles
But wait. … You see the little grey circles …Tony Webster, The Sense of an Ending, end of Part One (wording varies by edition — check your copy)

Feature 1 — the self-interruption. Technique: aposiopesis plus direct address to the reader. Effect: the settled account is reopened at the precise moment the reader was going to accept it, and the reader is addressed as a confidant. Meaning: Tony cannot stop talking, and the inability is diagnostic: a narrator who is satisfied does not need to add "but wait".

Feature 2 — the trivial image. The grey circles — the marks left by wet glasses — are nothing. Technique: bathetic concrete detail placed at a structural seam. Effect: the novel's most dramatic formal moment is marked by the smallest possible image, which deflates the narrator's self-importance exactly as Part Two will. Meaning: memory attaches to detail when the feeling is unmanageable. He cannot say what hurt him, so he describes a stain.

Feature 3 — the direct address. "You see…" Technique: second-person apostrophe. Effect: the reader is asked to corroborate a memory they do not have, and is therefore recruited. Meaning: this is the moment the reader becomes the prosecutor. We are invited to look for something the narrator cannot name, and the entire second half of the novel is us doing it.

Why this is worth knowing: most students treat the hinge as a plot twist. It is a formal instruction to re-read. "Barnes places the re-reading inside the text" is a band-7 sentence about structure.
Worked example 3 The letter scene
You still don't get it, do you?Veronica Ford, The Sense of an Ending, Part Two

Feature 1 — the production of the document. The letter is not described by Tony; it is handed to him, and then to us. Technique: embedded document — the text within the text. Effect: the reader's control of the narrative is transferred to Veronica for the length of the scene. Meaning: the archive has an owner, and it is not the man who has been narrating for a hundred pages. Authority in this novel belongs to whoever holds the paper.

Feature 2 — the smallness of Tony's response. He does not react; he minimises. Technique: understatement and the deflected confession — "a small, mean part of me". Effect: the disproportion between the document and the response is appalling, and the reader supplies the missing feeling. Meaning: he can name his pettiness and still reduce it by an adjective. Confession becomes a further form of defence, which is the novel's bleakest observation about self-knowledge.

Feature 3 — Veronica's question. "You still don't get it, do you?" Technique: rhetorical question with the adverb "still" doing the damage. Effect: it reframes forty years as a single continuous failure of attention, and it addresses the reader as much as Tony. Meaning: interpretive authority moves decisively to the woman he spent Part One describing. The sentence is also a verdict on the reader, who has just finished Part One and, like Tony, "didn't get it".

Why this is worth knowing: it is the passage to use for any question about power, evidence or gender. Note that Veronica wins not by arguing but by holding something back and then producing it — the same structure as Emilia's revelation in Othello, which makes it an excellent comparative point.
Worked example 4 The ending — accumulation, responsibility, unease
There is accumulation. There is responsibility. And beyond these, there is great unease.Tony Webster, The Sense of an Ending, final lines

Feature 1 — the tricolon of abstract nouns. Technique: three parallel existential constructions, the third extended by "beyond these". Effect: the cadence promises a conclusion and delivers a further term rather than a resolution. Meaning: the sentence is grammatically closed and morally open — which is the whole novel's shape in one line.

Feature 2 — the abstraction. Not "I did this" but "there is responsibility". Technique: impersonal construction, agentless. Effect: the removal of a grammatical subject removes the person who acted. Meaning: even at the end, having understood everything, Tony's syntax is still avoiding the first person. The evasion has survived the knowledge — and that is the novel's final, unfashionable claim about what self-knowledge can and cannot do.

Feature 3 — the choice of "unease". Not guilt, not remorse, not grief. Technique: careful, deflationary lexis. Effect: the word is smaller than the situation, and the mismatch is the point. Meaning: Barnes refuses catharsis. The reader is left holding the emotion the narrator cannot feel, which is why the ending is often described as cold and why it is so difficult to forget.

Why this is worth knowing: if a question asks how a text ends, this is one of the best endings available to you. The formulation to memorise: "the ending offers a verdict in place of a resolution, and hands the verdict to the reader."

9. Possible global issues

Each of these is a single arguable sentence containing a when or a because. That is the test: if your global issue cannot be disagreed with, it is a topic, not an issue.

  • Beliefs, values and education: "A society that treats memory as evidence will systematically mistake self-justification for truth."
  • Culture, identity and community: "Identity is a story a person tells about themselves, and the story is edited to minimise responsibility."
  • Politics, power and justice: "Holding the evidence — as Veronica holds the letter — is a form of power over the person who produced it."
  • Science, technology and the environment: "A permanent record — a diary, an email, a letter kept for forty years — can undo a life's account of itself."
  • Art, creativity and the imagination: "The endings we impose on our own lives are moral acts disguised as literary ones."

10. How this work behaves in the exam

Paper 1 — the transfer

You never write about this novel in Paper 1, but you use it constantly. Tony's hedged, defensive register is the clearest model you have of tone being inferred rather than stated. When an unseen narrator sounds reasonable, ask the Tony questions: who is being paraphrased, what is being closed down, what abstraction is standing in for a feeling? See Paper 1.

Paper 2

Strongest on memory, perspective, unreliable narration, structure, time, regret, self-deception and intertextuality. Strong on gender where the question is about evidence and voice, because Veronica holds all three. Weak on anything requiring a large cast, a social panorama, or public/political events — this is an intensely narrow novel, and that narrowness is the point.

Individual oral

Outstanding. The opening list is under forty lines, self-contained, and changes meaning once the book is finished; the letter scene is a shift you can track sentence by sentence. Pair either with any non-literary text about records, memory, reputation or digital permanence.

Opening list — best all-round choice
Dense with technique, short, and thematically central. Pair with an opinion column about digital memory, or an infographic about how much of our lives is recorded. Global issue: "When a life is reduced to a permanent record, the record competes with the memory — and the person who controls the record controls the story."
The letter scene — best for power and gender
Veronica producing the document Tony wrote. Pair with a text about whistleblowing, leaked emails, or reputation management. Global issue: "Evidence held by one party is power over the party who produced it."
The Severn bore — best for time and environment
The most figurative passage in the book. Pair with a text about climate, irreversible change, or intergenerational responsibility. Global issue: "Consequences can travel backwards through a life, arriving long after the decision that caused them."
How to make the pairing work in the oral

State the global issue in the first sixty seconds, in one arguable sentence containing a when or a because. Then — the part students skip — make the mechanism the link, not the topic. "Both texts are about memory" is worth nothing. "Both texts present memory as something produced rather than retrieved: Tony's narration revises as it goes, and the campaign text addresses the reader as someone whose memory can be managed" is worth five marks on criterion C. Full method on the oral page.

11. Writing the essay: question types, moves, and a weak thesis beside a strong one

Question typeTypical wordingUse this novel when…
Narrative perspective and reliability"How does the text use a narrator to shape the reader's response?" Always. This is the safest choice you can make and the one you should be most rehearsed on.
Time and space"How is time presented?" / "How does the text treat the past?" Strong, because the two-part structure and the bore give you structural and figurative evidence in the same paragraph.
Identity and self-representation"How is identity constructed?" Strong, provided you write about editing rather than about "finding yourself".
Conflict, violence, or social panorama"How is conflict presented?" Avoid. There is no conflict here in any conventional sense — the damage is all retroactive and internal. Choose Chronicle or Othello instead.

11.1 Four moves that produce a line of argument

  1. Convert the topic into a mechanism. Not "Tony misremembers" but "Tony misremembers selectively and in a single direction — towards his own innocence — and the novel measures the direction by returning his own phrases to him with their meanings inverted."
  2. Give the mechanism a direction. Every strong thesis has a trajectory: certainty becomes unease; a narrator becomes a defendant; a hedge becomes a confession.
  3. Build in a "however". "Granted that memory is genuinely unreliable for everyone, the novel insists that Tony's failures are moral rather than mechanical — he is not forgetting, he is editing."
  4. End with what the text does, not what it says. "By making the reader re-read Part One, Barnes makes us complicit in the revision we have just performed" beats "Barnes shows us that memory is unreliable."

Weak — band 3/4

"In The Sense of an Ending, Julian Barnes explores the theme of memory through Tony Webster, who looks back at his life and realises he made mistakes. This shows that we should learn from the past."

Why it fails: plot summary with the word "theme" attached; the moral is true of everything; no technique; no direction; nothing anyone could disagree with.

Strong — band 6/7

"Barnes makes his narrator unreliable without letting him lie: Tony Webster is scrupulous about facts and dishonest about emphasis, so that Part Two can reclassify Part One without altering a single event. The novel's method is grammatical — hedges, dismissive closures and agentless constructions do the work that invention would do in a lesser book."

Why it works: it redefines the concept, names a mechanism, has a direction, and is provable — every body paragraph can point at something countable on the page.

12. Common traps

  1. Calling Tony a liar. He is not. He is honest about everything except himself, and the distinction is the entire novel. "Unreliable" here means "interested", not "false".
  2. Plot summary. The biggest mark-loser on a book with this little plot. Test every sentence: does it contain a word from the text and a claim about how that word works? If not, it is summary.
  3. "Memory is unreliable" as a conclusion. It is a starting point. The interesting claim is what memory is unreliable for.
  4. Ignoring Veronica. She is the archive, the accuser and the only adult in the book. An essay on perspective that never mentions her control of the documents is missing the design.
  5. Treating Part Two as a detective plot. Tony's investigation is not an investigation; it is a symptom. Write about it as self-exposure, not as detection.
  6. Expecting redemption. The ending is deliberately unsatisfying. Do not try to rescue Tony in your conclusion; the novel refuses to, and the refusal is the argument.
  7. Quoting too much. Four words embedded in your own sentence beats a full line dumped into a paragraph. Integration is what is rewarded.

13. Eight practice questions

  1. Discuss how Barnes presents memory as a form of self-protection.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Tony's memory is not failing but functioning perfectly for a purpose: it preserves the facts and edits the blame, so a man who wrote a cruel letter can remember himself as a reasonable one for forty years.

    Paragraphs: (1) hedging and dismissive closure as techniques; (2) the letter as physical disproof — he did not forget, he rewrote; (3) Veronica as the archive he cannot control; (4) the ending's refusal to let knowledge become amendment.

  2. Analyse the significance of the novel's two-part structure.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Part Two does not continue Part One; it reclassifies it. Because no event changes and every interpretation does, the structure itself performs the argument that the past is a reading rather than a record.

    Paragraphs: (1) Part One as a self-authored memoir; (2) the bequest as the device that breaks it; (3) the return of Part One's phrases with inverted meanings; (4) the "But wait" hinge as an instruction to re-read.

  3. Explore the presentation of Tony Webster's unreliability.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Tony is reliable about facts and unreliable about himself, and the gap between the two is where the novel lives. His unreliability is produced by register, not by invention.

    Paragraphs: (1) hedges and qualifiers; (2) reported speech as control of others' testimony; (3) abstract nouns as emotional avoidance; (4) the moments the control fails — the acquisitive vocabulary of Part Two.

    Marks come from: distinguishing reporting unreliably from interpreting unreliably. That single distinction is usually the band-6 move.

  4. Discuss the significance of the Severn bore as an image.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: The bore is the novel's theory of time made concrete — a wave travelling against the current figures consequence arriving before its cause is visible, and licenses Tony's investigation as an upstream journey into a past he had assumed had flowed away.

    Paragraphs: (1) the image's physical wrongness; (2) its relation to "the sense of an ending"; (3) the undertow as its quiet counterpart; (4) why the novel needs an image rather than an explanation.

  5. Compare the presentation of the past in this novel and one other studied work.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Pairing: Chronicle of a Death Foretold. Thesis: Both narrators reconstruct a death they were implicated in, decades later — but Tony's reconstruction is defensive under-reporting whereas García Márquez's narrator over-documents, so the novel turns a memoir into an alibi and the novella turns an archive into one.

    Paragraphs: (1) the investigator's pose in each; (2) what each account omits; (3) register as self-defence; (4) how the structure betrays each narrator in the end.

  6. To what extent does the novel offer its protagonist any redemption?

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Only in knowledge, and not in feeling. Tony ends able to state what he did and unable to feel it proportionately; Barnes's refusal of catharsis is the novel's most serious moral claim.

    Paragraphs: (1) the final tricolon and its agentless grammar; (2) the failure of emotion at the revelation; (3) the damaged son as the irreducibility of consequence; (4) the reader as the only available judge.

  7. How does Barnes use documents — the diary, the letter, the bequest — to shape the reader's response?

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Documents in this novel are never neutral: each one is owned by someone, and the plot is a contest over who is permitted to produce them. Authority belongs to the holder of the paper.

    Paragraphs: (1) the bequest as engine; (2) the diary as the withheld archive, and the reader's exclusion from it; (3) the letter as the document that convicts its own author; (4) the closing "blood" as the fact that resists documentation.

  8. Which extract would you choose for the individual oral, and which non-literary text would you pair it with? Justify the pairing through a global issue.

    Show model answer

    Extract: the opening list. Pairing: an opinion column or campaign text about digital permanence — the argument that a life leaves a record that outlives the person's account of it.

    Global issue: "When a life is reduced to a permanent record, the record competes with the memory — and whoever controls the record controls the story."

    Mechanism to compare: both texts present remembering as production. Tony's list looks unedited and is heavily edited; the column addresses the reader as a person whose past can be curated. Both use a pretended neutrality — the list's "in no particular order", the column's appeal to "the facts" — to make a selection look like evidence.

Connections — which pairings work, and why
  • → Chronicle of a Death Foretold (strongest pairing): two first-person narrators reconstructing a death they were implicated in, decades later. The contrast is the most productive one available to you — defensive omission versus documentary saturation. Use for any question on memory, testimony, guilt or time.
  • → The World's Wife: both texts are about the authority to tell a story. Barnes gives the revision to a narrator who does not realise he is revising; Duffy gives it to speakers who know exactly what they are doing. Excellent for intertextuality, voice and perspective — the "who authors whom" contrast is the essay.
  • → Othello: both protagonists reach self-knowledge too late and immediately begin narrating themselves as better men. Othello's is absolute and terminal; Tony's is partial and survivable. Compare form as well as character, or the essay collapses into summary.
  • → Paper 1: Tony's hedged register is your model for inferring tone in an unseen text. Ask of every unseen narrator: what is being hedged, and what is being protected?
  • → Paper 2: this work is strongest on narration, memory, time and identity; weakest on conflict, society and public action.
  • → Individual oral: pair the opening list with a text on digital permanence, or the letter scene with any text about holding evidence.
Vocabulary
unreliable narrator — a narrator whose account the reader must not take at face value
first-person retrospective — an "I" narrator looking back at their own earlier life
hedging — softening a claim with "I think", "perhaps", "probably"
qualifier — a word that limits the force of what is said
dismissive closure — a phrase that ends a subject to avoid examining it
understatement — describing something as smaller or less serious than it is
aphorism — a short sentence stating a general truth as if it were a law
tricolon — three parallel elements in a row, for rhythm and weight
agentless construction — a sentence with no stated doer ("there is responsibility")
prolepsis — referring to something before it properly happens
dramatic irony — when the reader knows more than the narrator does
aposiopesis — breaking off mid-sentence, as if unable to continue
register — the level of formality a speaker or writer chooses
