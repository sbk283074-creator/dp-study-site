English A: Language and Literature SL · Set work 1 of 4

Othello

Shakespeare, first performed in 1604. A general who is also an outsider is persuaded, in roughly three days, that his wife has been unfaithful, and he kills her. What makes the play endlessly examinable is that the murder is achieved almost entirely with language: no evidence is ever produced. There is no letter, no witness, no scene of adultery — only a handkerchief and a man who knows how to leave a sentence unfinished. If you want a text that lets you write about how words do things to people, this is your text; if you want a text you can get away with summarising, it is the worst one on your list.

Copyright and line numbers

Only short quotations are reproduced, with analysis. Act, scene and line numbers are given as a finding aid; lineation varies between editions (Arden, Riverside, Folger, Norton and the Folger Shakespeare all differ), and the 1622 quarto and 1623 First Folio are genuinely different texts — the Folio is roughly 160 lines longer. Check every reference against the edition your school uses before you memorise it, and in the exam quote short: three or four words, embedded in your own sentence, is worth more than a full line pasted in.

1. Why this play rewards close reading — and why it is hard

Othello is the most concentrated study of persuasion in English. Iago never lies about anything anyone can check. He supplies no facts; he supplies frames — the interrupted sentence, the loaded word, the invitation to "note" something. Almost every technique in the rhetorical toolkit is in this play, deployed by a character who understands that the most convincing evidence is the evidence the victim assembles for himself. That is also why the play is dangerous for a student.

It is dangerous because the plot is memorable. Everyone knows what happens, so the temptation is to write about what happens. But in this play, what happens is nearly always reported rather than shown: we never see Desdemona and Cassio alone together in a compromising situation, we never see the handkerchief given to Cassio, and we are never once given a fact about the alleged affair that we can verify. Everything decisive happens in conversation. A plot-based answer therefore has almost nothing to attach itself to, whereas a language-based answer has everything.

What students expect to write about

Jealousy. Trust. Betrayal. Race. Othello was a great man brought down by a villain. These are true and they are worth nothing on their own, because they are true of a hundred plays. Every one of them is a topic, not an argument.

What actually earns marks

A mechanism. How does a five-word fragment — "Ha, I like not that" — move a secure general to a vow of murder within four hundred lines? Answer that, with the words of the scene in front of you, and you have an essay. Everything on this page is aimed at that answer.

Why this text matters for your course

This is a Language and Literature course, not a Literature course. Othello is the work in your list that most nakedly displays language doing something — manufacturing a reality, then manufacturing the evidence for it — which is exactly what the non-literary texts on Paper 1 do. Once you can write about Iago's management of what Othello hears, you can write about a speechwriter's management of what an audience hears. The skill transfers directly, which is why this is the work I would put at the centre of any question containing the word rhetoric.

2. Context that actually earns marks

Context in an IB answer is a condiment, not a meal. One clause attached to a specific choice; never a paragraph of biography before you reach the words. Below is the context that genuinely changes what an examiner thinks of your analysis — and then the context that students use as filler.

2.1 The source, and what Shakespeare changed

The source is a tale in Giraldi Cinthio's Hecatommithi (1565), about a Moorish captain in Venice who is persuaded by his ensign that his wife has been unfaithful. In the source, as you would expect from a moral novella, the wife is beaten to death with a sand-filled stocking and the ceiling is then brought down on the body to conceal the crime; the Moor is later tortured to death by the wife's family. Shakespeare made four decisions that you should be able to name, because each one is a choice with an effect:

  1. He compressed the time. In Cinthio the suspicion develops slowly and the Moor's jealousy grows over months. Shakespeare gives the temptation roughly seventy-two hours, and 3.3 alone carries the whole conversion. Compression makes the speed itself part of the meaning: a man with a lifetime of judgement is dismantled in an afternoon.
  2. He invented the handkerchief's career. Cinthio has a handkerchief, but Shakespeare gave it the strawberry spots, the Egyptian origin story and the occult provenance, and made it the play's only physical "proof". The object is therefore not inherited material but an engineered plot device — which is why writing about it as a "symbol of love" misses the point entirely (see §7.2).
  3. He named and enlarged the ensign's wife into Emilia. She is the play's structural solution: the person who steals the token without knowing why and who tells the truth too late. She is also the play's most explicit feminist voice, which is not an accident of characterisation but a plot requirement.
  4. He made the villain a rhetorician and the hero an orator. Cinthio's ensign is a straightforward malcontent. Shakespeare's Iago is given more lines than Othello, more soliloquies than any other character, and the best verse in the play — while Othello is given the grandest public style in Shakespeare and then has it taken away. That exchange is the tragedy.

2.2 Venice and Cyprus

The play moves from one location to another and the move is structural, not decorative. Venice was imagined in England as a sophisticated, mercantile, oligarchic republic — a society of laws, councils, procedure and paperwork, where a foreign soldier of fortune could in principle rise on merit. Cyprus was its most remote and most threatened possession, a garrison island; Venice held it until 1571, when it fell to the Ottoman Empire, and the play's Turkish fleet is drawn straight from that real conflict. Cyprus is presented as a masculine, enclosed military world with none of Venice's civil restraints: no senate, no fathers, no procedure, only a commander and his officers.

Two consequences to use. First, the storm in Act II destroys the Turkish fleet and with it the external plot, removing the only thing Iago's world had to do — which is why the play turns inward so violently. Second, the people who could have restrained Othello are all in the other city. Desdemona's father, the Duke and the law are in Venice; in Cyprus the only counsel available is Iago's. The geography is the argument.

How to use this in a paragraph

Do not write "Shakespeare set the play in Venice and Cyprus". Write: "by removing the senate, the Duke and Brabantio to Venice, Shakespeare leaves Othello on an island with one adviser, and the tragedy is therefore a study of what happens to a man whose only source of interpretation is the person destroying him." That is context doing analytical work in a single clause, which is criterion A at band 7.

2.3 Race, militarism and the early modern imagination

"Moor" was a loose early modern term covering North African, Arab and sub-Saharan African peoples, and Othello deliberately keeps its protagonist's origins imprecise — he mentions "Anthropophagi" and men whose heads do grow beneath their shoulders, and his own account of his life is a travel narrative rather than an ethnicity. What is precise is his position: he is Venice's indispensable military asset and simultaneously the object of its obscene jokes. Both halves of that sentence matter and dropping either one is the most common error in essays on this play.

Useful, checkable context: there was a real Moroccan ambassador, Abd el-Ouahed ben Messaoud, in London in 1600 with an entourage; black residents of England were few but visible enough that Elizabeth I's government drafted orders in 1601 concerning the removal of "blackamoores". Early modern humoral and medical writing frequently linked climate, complexion and temperament; and the period's travel literature supplied the exotic vocabulary the play uses about Othello's past. None of this needs to be stated at length — one clause, attached to a line, is what earns.

Common trap — the two failures of nerve

There are two opposite ways to lose marks on race in this play. The first is to ignore it: to write about Othello purely as a soldier and treat "black" as decoration. The second is to write a modern politics essay in which Othello is simply a victim of Venetian racism and Iago simply its spokesman. The play is more uncomfortable than either. Venice promotes, defends and employs Othello while its language about him is contemptuous; and the tragedy's crucial move is the moment Othello adopts that language himself — "Her name… is now begrimed and black / As mine own face" (3.3, approximate lineation). The word does the damage because he accepts the scale of value it carries.

2.4 Tragic form

Othello sits between two traditions and uses both. From the medieval de casibus tradition — the fall of princes — it takes the shape of a great man brought low, and the audience's conventional expectation that a tragedy will end with a body on stage and a restored political order. From Aristotelian and Senecan practice it takes a protagonist with a decisive error of judgement, a recognisable peripeteia (reversal) and anagnorisis (recognition). Locate those two moments precisely and you have located the play's spine: the reversal is the vow at the end of 3.3; the recognition is 5.2, when Emilia names the handkerchief's theft.

The formally interesting fact — and it is a good essay point — is that the play begins as a comedy. Act I offers an elopement, an angry father, a night-time arrest, and a Senate scene in which the lovers are reunited and approved. Shakespeare borrows the machinery of romantic comedy and then runs it backwards, which is why the ending feels like a violation rather than a climax.

3. Plot and structure, act by act

You need this only as scaffolding — but you need it accurately, because a wrong placing of a moment undermines an otherwise good paragraph. Here is the whole play with the turning point located exactly.

Act I — Venice
Exposition at speed, in three scenes. 1.1: Iago tells Roderigo that he has been passed over for promotion in favour of Cassio, "a great arithmetician… that never set a squadron in the field", and the two wake Brabantio with animal imagery — "an old black ram / Is tupping your white ewe". 1.2: Othello is brought before the Duke's officers and stops a fight with a sentence: "Keep up your bright swords, for the dew will rust them." 1.3: the senate scene — Othello defends the marriage, Desdemona confirms she married him freely, Brabantio warns "She has deceived her father, and may thee", and the council ships Othello to Cyprus. The act ends with Iago alone: "I have't. It is engender'd. Hell and night / Must bring this monstrous birth to the world's light." Note that at the end of Act I the audience knows the plot in full and Othello knows nothing.
Act II — Cyprus
The storm destroys the Turkish fleet, so the war ends and Iago's only remaining work is on Othello. 2.1: reunions, and Iago's first soliloquy — "I do suspect the lusty Moor / Hath leap'd into my seat" — plus his promise to "turn her virtue into pitch". 2.3: the drunken brawl. Iago gets Cassio drunk, Roderigo provokes him, Montano is wounded, and Othello strips Cassio of his lieutenancy. Cassio's lament is the act's thesis: "I have lost my reputation! I have lost the immortal part of myself, and what remains is bestial." Iago then advises Cassio to sue for reinstatement through Desdemona — the single most important piece of engineering in the play, because it makes a wife's intercession look like a wife's interest. The act closes with Iago's "I'll pour this pestilence into his ear."
Act III — the temptation
The heart of the play and its turning point. 3.3 is one of the great scenes in drama: in roughly four hundred and eighty lines Othello moves from "My noble Moor is true of mind" through "Ha, I like not that" and "Why did I marry?" to a kneeling vow of vengeance shared with the man destroying him. Within the same scene Desdemona drops the handkerchief and Emilia picks it up for Iago. Before 3.3 Othello is certain; after 3.3 he is committed. 3.4 is the test: Othello demands the handkerchief, Desdemona cannot produce it, and he supplies it with magic provenance — "there's magic in the web of it… it was dyed in mummy… conserved of maidens' hearts" — an escalation that shows how much of the "evidence" he is manufacturing.
Act IV — the degradation
Everything after 3.3 is consequence. 4.1: Iago plants the handkerchief in Cassio's lodging, and Othello, watching from hiding, misreads a comic exchange with Bianca as bawdy confirmation; he collapses into an fit and later strikes Desdemona before the Venetian ambassadors — "Goats and monkeys!". 4.2: the "brothel" scene, Othello calls his wife a whore to her cousin Lodovico's face, and Desdemona asks Emilia the terrible question "Am I that name, Iago?" — actually addressed to Emilia: "Am I that name, Iago?" is 4.2, spoken to Emilia about the word Iago has used. 4.3: the willow scene. Desdemona, undressing, sings the song her mother's maid Barbary sang, and asks to be shrouded in her wedding sheets. It is the last fully human moment she is given, and Shakespeare places it immediately before the murder.
Act V — the catastrophe
5.1: Cassio is wounded and Bianca is arrested on suspicion — the double standard made procedural. 5.2: the murder. Othello enters with "It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul", smothers Desdemona, then learns the truth from Emilia in the play's second reversal. Emilia is killed by her husband. Othello kills himself after a final speech in which he asks to be recorded as "one that loved not wisely but too well". Iago is led away refusing speech: "Demand me nothing. What you know, you know." Cassio is left governing.
Locating the turning point — the single most useful structural fact

The killing is decided at the end of 3.3, not in 5.2. Everything in Acts IV and V is the working out of a decision already made. Students who treat the murder as the climax produce essays in which Othello "gradually becomes jealous" across five acts; he does not. He is converted in one scene, and then the play shows you what a converted man looks like. That distinction — sudden conversion, slow consequence — is a band-6/7 structural observation and it takes one sentence to make.

4. The characters

Each entry below gives you four things: the motivation the character states, the arc they actually follow, the speeches to learn, and the critical argument around them. The fourth item is the one that lifts an answer above description — a critic's disagreement gives you a "however" for free.

4.1 Othello

Stated motivation: to be, and to be seen to be, a man of absolute public worth — "My parts, my title and my perfect soul / Shall manifest me rightly" (1.3). Arc: from the most self-possessed speaker in the play to a man reduced to a monosyllable about himself in the third person. Learn: "Keep up your bright swords, for the dew will rust them" (1.2); "She loved me for the dangers I had passed, / And I loved her that she did pity them" (1.3); "Othello's occupation's gone" (3.3); "Farewell the tranquil mind! farewell content! / Farewell the plumèd troop and the big wars / That make ambition virtue" (3.3); "It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul" (5.2); "one that loved not wisely but too well" (5.2).

The most useful single idea for writing about him: Othello's identity is entirely composed of roles granted by other people. He is Venice's general, Brabantio's son-in-law, a Christian convert, a man whose life-story is a travel narrative told to audiences. There is almost no private self underneath. That is why the marriage matters so catastrophically: it is the one relationship in which he was chosen for himself rather than for his function. When that is questioned, he has nothing to retreat into, and he destroys the question.

The critical debate. A. C. Bradley, in Shakespearean Tragedy (1904), read Othello as fundamentally noble — open, trusting, self-commanding — and argued that the tragedy is what happens to a great nature when it is made to doubt. T. S. Eliot, in a deliberately hostile essay, argued the opposite: that Othello's final speech is self-dramatisation, a man more interested in making himself interesting than in facing what he has done. F. R. Leavis pushed further, calling the last speech an exercise in self-pity and moral evasion. You do not have to settle this — but "Bradley reads the last speech as repentance; Leavis reads it as self-dramatisation; the text supports both, because Othello's final act is to author himself" is a genuine analytical position, and it is the kind of sentence that moves an essay from band 4 to band 6.

4.2 Iago

Stated motivation: several, and none sufficient. He claims Cassio was promoted over him; he claims to believe Othello has slept with Emilia — "And it is thought abroad that 'twixt my sheets / He has done my office"; he claims Othello may have slept with her too ("I hate the Moor…"). He also claims attraction to Desdemona. He gives a different motive to every listener, including to himself. Arc: none. He is the only major character who does not change, which is itself the observation to make. Learn: "I am not what I am" (1.1); "I'll pour this pestilence into his ear" (2.3); "Trifles light as air / Are to the jealous confirmations strong / As proofs of holy writ" (3.3); "Demand me nothing. What you know, you know" (5.2).

The critical debate. Samuel Taylor Coleridge's phrase for Iago — the "motive-hunting of motiveless malignity" — is the most quoted critical remark on the play, and it is worth understanding precisely: Coleridge's point is not that Iago has no motive but that Iago is constantly looking for one, and that the searching is the character. He invents a reason each time he needs one. The modern reading is less metaphysical: Iago is a technician, a man who treats other people's minds as instruments and who appears to be motivated by the experiment itself. Either way, the exam-safe formulation is the same — Iago's instability of motive is the point, because a villain with a sufficient motive would be explainable, and Shakespeare wants something that cannot be reasoned with.

Exam technique

Do not write "Iago is evil". Evil is not a technique. Write about what he does with sentences: he withholds the completion, so his victim supplies it; he answers his own questions, so no one else needs to agree; he refuses to speak, so he cannot be quoted against himself; he tells the truth in a form that makes it unusable. That is a paragraph on rhetoric, and rhetoric is examinable.

4.3 Desdemona

Stated motivation: love, stated plainly and publicly in the senate — "I saw Othello's visage in his mind", and "My heart's subdued / Even to the very quality of my lord". Arc: from the most eloquent and self-possessed woman in the play, who defends her own marriage in a council chamber, to a woman who cannot get a straight answer and finally asks whether she is the word she has been called. She has roughly a quarter of Othello's lines and dies with "Nobody; I myself. Farewell" on her lips — a line that has been argued over for four hundred years because it could be a lie protecting him or a refusal to accuse him.

Learn: "She loved me for the dangers I had passed" is his line about her; hers are "Am I that name, Iago?" (4.2), the willow song (4.3) and "A guiltless death I die" (5.2).

The critical debate. Older criticism treated Desdemona as passive, and the fact that she has so few lines made that easy. The strongest modern reading reverses it: her persistence is a form of resistance. She refuses to accept a definition she did not author — "Am I that name?" is not confusion, it is a challenge to the word itself. And her final defence of Othello, if read as deliberate, makes her the only character in the play who chooses a story rather than being given one.

4.4 Emilia

The most under-written character in student essays and the most rewarding to write about. Stated motivation: loyalty to her mistress, and a desire to please her husband — she steals the handkerchief because, as she says, "he begg'd of me to steal it" with an earnestness far beyond the object's worth. Arc: from a woman who says "I nothing know wherefore" to the play's most explicit moral voice. Learn: "Let husbands know / Their wives have sense like them: they see, and smell, / And have their palates both for sweet and sour, / As husbands have" (4.3); "The ills we do, their ills instruct us so" (4.3); "I will not charm my tongue; I am bound to speak" (5.2).

Why she matters structurally. She is the revealer. Shakespeare needs the truth to arrive after the murder, and the only way to make that plausible is to have the evidence in the hands of someone who did not know she was holding it. Ignorance is the mechanism, and it is gendered: the woman who carried the plot's only object was never told what it was for. That is an essay sentence, not a summary sentence.

4.5 Cassio

Florentine, courteous, literate, handsome, a little vain, and — crucially — a theoretician of war rather than a soldier: Iago's contempt for him is that he "never set a squadron in the field". His function in the plot is to be a body of evidence: he can be placed near Desdemona, made to hold the handkerchief, wounded, and removed, all without ever doing anything wrong. His function in the argument is to articulate the play's theory of reputation: "I have lost my reputation! I have lost the immortal part of myself, and what remains is bestial" (2.3). Note the logic — reputation is the immortal part, more real than the body, and its loss literally animalises him. Compare with Iago's cynical counter-phrase, "Reputation is an idle and most false imposition" (2.3). The play puts both statements in the same scene and lets you choose.

4.6 Roderigo

A gulled gentleman who pays Iago to pursue Desdemona and is used as a tool, a witness and finally a victim. He is important for one reason: he shows Iago's method from outside. Everything we understand about Iago's management of Othello, we see first in a cruder form with Roderigo — promises, invented grievances, money, and the instruction to do something violent. Roderigo's prose is also a social marker: he is the dupe, so he speaks prose. His death, killed by the man he funded, is the play's clearest statement about what a client is worth to a manipulator.

4.7 Bianca

Cassio's lover, a woman in Cyprus widely assumed to be a prostitute. She appears in two scenes and is easy to skip, which is a mistake. Bianca is the play's control case: she is what a woman publicly suspected of sexual irregularity is treated like when she has no rank to protect her. Iago uses her name to make Othello laugh about Cassio's "fit"; Othello's misreading of the scene with the handkerchief depends on Bianca being assumed available; and she is arrested on suspicion at the end. When she says "I am no strumpet, but of life as honest / As you that thus abuse me" (5.1, approximate), she is making the double standard explicit in the only register the play allows her.

5. Themes — and how to write about them without being vague

A theme is not a subject. "The play is about jealousy" is a subject. "The play presents jealousy not as a feeling but as a method of reading, which converts nothing into proof" is an argument. For each theme below: what the play says, where to find it, and the precise formulation that turns it into a paragraph.

5.1 Jealousy as interpretation, not emotion

Find it: "Trifles light as air / Are to the jealous confirmations strong / As proofs of holy writ" (3.3); "O, beware, my lord, of jealousy; / It is the green-eyed monster which doth mock / The meat it feeds on" (3.3); "Excellent wretch! Perdition catch my soul / But I do love thee! and when I love thee not, / Chaos is come again" (3.3).

Write it like this: jealousy in this play is a hermeneutic — a way of reading the world — rather than a passion that overcomes a man. Iago's formulation is precise and worth quoting whole: trifles do not support suspicion, they become "confirmations strong as proofs of holy writ", which grants a rumour the authority of scripture. The effect is that Othello is never presented as irrational; he is presented as a man applying a rigorous evidential standard to material that was manufactured for him. That is why the tragedy is not "he was jealous" but "he was reasoning correctly from false premises he had been given".

5.2 Appearance and reality

Find it: "I am not what I am" (1.1); "Men should be what they seem" (3.3); "O, you are well tuned now! / But I'll set down the pegs that make this music, / As honest as I am" (2.1); and the entire structure of 4.1, where Othello watches a scene and understands everything wrongly.

Write it like this: the play's central ironic formulation is that the honest man is called honest and is the villain, while the man who says "Men should be what they seem" is himself made to seem something he is not. The most sophisticated version of this point is structural rather than verbal: Shakespeare gives the audience complete knowledge from 1.1 — we know the plot before we meet the hero — and the play is therefore not a mystery but a demonstration. We are not asked "will he be deceived?" but "watch how".

5.3 Race, otherness and representation

Find it: "an old black ram / Is tupping your white ewe" and "making the beast with two backs" (1.1); "Her name, that was as fresh / As Dian's visage, is now begrimed and black / As mine own face" (3.3); "Haply, for I am black / And have not those soft parts of conversation / That chamberers have, or for I am declin'd / Into the vale of years" (3.3); the repeated, unadorned noun "the Moor".

Write it like this: the play's most disturbing move is not the abuse Iago and Brabantio direct at Othello but Othello's own adoption of it. At 3.3 he uses his own blackness as the standard by which his wife's name is defiled — the measure of her shame is his face. That is internalised otherness used as evidence, and it is the moment the play stops being about a villain and starts being about a system of value. Avoid the two failures of nerve described in §2.3.

5.4 Gender and misogyny

Find it: Emilia's 4.3 speech on wives having "sense like them"; Iago's catalogue of women's faults in 2.1; Othello's "O, thou weed, / Who art so lovely fair and smell'st so sweet / That the sense aches at thee" (4.2); the Brothel scene; the contrast between Cassio's reputation for fornication, which costs him a promotion, and Desdemona's reputation for adultery, which costs her her life.

Write it like this: the play's economy of female reputation is asymmetrical and the asymmetry is measurable. A man's sexual conduct is gossip; a woman's is evidence in a capital case. Two details make this precise rather than general: Desdemona is tried and condemned without ever being formally accused aloud, and Bianca is arrested for a crime nobody saw. The strongest essays notice that Emilia is given the argument and then killed for making it — the play's feminism is spoken by a woman who is murdered within a hundred lines of saying it.

5.5 Reputation

Find it: Cassio, 2.3: "I have lost my reputation! I have lost the immortal part of myself, and what remains is bestial." Iago, 2.3: "Reputation is an idle and most false imposition." Othello, 1.3: "My parts, my title and my perfect soul / Shall manifest me rightly." Othello, 5.2: "Speak of me as I am; nothing extenuate, / Nor set down aught in malice".

Write it like this: reputation is the play's currency and it is the only thing in the play that is simultaneously described as immortal and shown to be infinitely fragile. Cassio loses his in a brawl he was tricked into; Desdemona loses hers without ever being told the charge; Othello destroys his own in order to defend it. The closing move is the sharpest: a man who has just murdered his wife asks to be recorded accurately — the last thing Othello cares about is the thing the play has spent five acts showing to be controlled by whoever is doing the recording.

5.6 Manipulation and persuasion

Find it: "Ha, I like not that" (3.3); "Note, note, note your own suspicion" (3.3, approximate); "Though I am bound to every act of duty, / I am not bound to that all slaves are free to — / Utter my thoughts?" (3.3); "I'll pour this pestilence into his ear" (2.3).

Write it like this: Iago's technique is to make Othello the author of his own destruction. He never states the accusation; he produces conditions under which Othello states it. The consequence is that the victim cannot be warned, because a warning would have to come from outside a reasoning process he believes is his own. This is the single richest theme for a Paper 2 essay, because every one of your other works has a version of the same question about who controls interpretation.

5.7 The handkerchief

Find it: 3.3 (origin and loss — "That handkerchief / Did an Egyptian to my mother give", "spotted with strawberries"); 3.4 (the test and the invented magic — "there's magic in the web of it… dyed in mummy… conserved of maidens' hearts"); 4.1 (the "ocular proof", planted with Cassio); 5.2 (Emilia's revelation that she "found by fortune and did give my husband").

Common trap

Do not write about the handkerchief as a symbol of love, fidelity or Othello's mother. That is a band-3 answer, and it is wrong twice over: the object's meaning is changed rather than expressed, and Othello's account of its origin is probably invented on the spot — he inflates it with occult provenance only in 3.4, after it has become useful to him. Write about it as a plot mechanism that converts a token into evidence through misreading, and note that the object never changes: only the interpretation does. The play is about who controls interpretation.

6. Language and form

6.1 Verse, prose and what they signal

This is the most reliable technical route into any question about status, power or identity in this play, and it is the easiest place to earn marks because the evidence is measurable.

ModeWho uses itWhat it signals
Blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter Othello early on, the Duke, the senators, Desdemona in 1.3 Rank, education, public office, self-command. In this play regular metre is social position: a man who can complete a pentameter is a man in control of himself.
Prose Iago with Roderigo; letters; the clown; the drunken scene Low status, intimacy, manipulation, or the breakdown of ceremony. Iago's prose is his workshop — it is where he is accidentally honest and where he does his planning.
Broken verse, exclamation, fragments Othello in 4.1 and 5.2 The collapse of self-command. When he can no longer finish a line, he can no longer command anything else. This is the single most examinable formal feature in the play.
Riming couplets Scene-closings; Othello's final speech; Iago's soliloquy endings Formality and closure — and, in 5.2, Othello's attempt to re-impose order on himself through form. A man who cannot govern a marriage still governs a couplet.

6.2 Iambic pentameter and its disruptions

The base line is ten syllables alternating weak and strong: da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM. You do not need to scan a whole scene; you need to notice where the pattern breaks and say why. The three disruptions that matter in Othello:

  1. Shared lines and interruptions. By 3.3 Iago and Othello are completing each other's pentameters — one man starts a line, the other finishes it. Formally this is intimacy; dramatically it is possession. Iago is literally inside Othello's line, and therefore inside his thinking. If you want one technical observation that will lift an essay above everyone else's, it is this one.
  2. Enjambment versus end-stopping. Early Othello is end-stopped: each line closes, each statement is complete. Late Othello runs over, with caesurae mid-line — "Lie with her? lie on her? We say lie on her when they belie her. Lie with her! Zounds, that's fulsome" (4.1) — where the syntax stumbles and repeats as if the sentence cannot find a resting place.
  3. Silence as metre. Iago's final "Demand me nothing. What you know, you know. / From this time forth I never will speak word" (5.2) is a refusal that has the shape of a decision. The villain's last act is to withhold the one thing a court needs, and the play ends with a man who has not explained himself — which is, structurally, the same crime as the one he committed.

6.3 Imagery clusters

Animals and bestiality

"Making the beast with two backs" (1.1) launches it; "the green-eyed monster" (3.3) names jealousy as a creature that eats its host — "doth mock the meat it feeds on"; "Goats and monkeys!" (4.1) is Othello reducing human beings to livestock. The trajectory matters: animals arrive before the violence and license it. Dehumanisation precedes the killing in every case.

Hell and the demonic

Iago opens the play's plot with "Hell and night / Must bring this monstrous birth to the world's light" (1.3), calls his plan "divinity of hell" and is repeatedly called a devil — by Othello, who examines his feet for cloven hooves in 5.2. The cluster gives the audience a moral vocabulary for a character who offers no motive, and it lets the play present manipulation as possession.

Sight, seeing and evidence

The word "proof" recurs; Othello demands "the ocular proof"; Iago promises to make him "see" it. The play's joke is savage: the one moment of seeing (4.1) is the moment Othello is most comprehensively deceived. Sight is the sense the play distrusts most, and the ear — "I'll pour this pestilence into his ear" — is where the damage is done.

Disease and poison

"Pestilence into his ear" (2.3); "Not poppy nor mandragora / Nor all the drowsy syrups of the world / Shall ever medicine thee to that sweet sleep" (3.3). Language is imagined as a liquid infection entering a body through a sense organ. The consequence for an essay: persuasion is presented as something done to a person, not chosen by them.

6.4 Irony, soliloquy and the audience's position

Dramatic irony is the engine. From 1.1 the audience knows more than any character except Iago, which converts suspense into horror: we watch a demonstration, not an investigation. The sharpest instance is Iago warning Othello against jealousy — "O, beware, my lord, of jealousy" — while manufacturing it. The warning is effective because it is ironic: it appears to be reluctant candour.

Soliloquy does two different jobs depending on who speaks it. Iago's soliloquies are workshop scenes in which the audience is made his accomplice: he tells us the plan, so we are implicated in watching it succeed. Othello's are public-formal even when he is alone — he addresses himself as an audience would, which is precisely his problem. Note that Desdemona is given no soliloquy at all. The distribution of direct address in this play is a map of who is permitted to explain themselves.

6.5 Othello's register collapse — the five-stage version

Memorise this progression and you have a ready-made essay on identity, power or language:

  1. 1.2 — "Keep up your bright swords, for the dew will rust them." Controlled, musical, unhurried; an imperative that does not need to be repeated. Total authority.
  2. 1.3 — "My parts, my title and my perfect soul / Shall manifest me rightly." A balanced tricolon in formal verse: public self-definition by a man confident that the world will confirm it.
  3. 3.3 — "Farewell the tranquil mind! farewell content! / Farewell the plumèd troop and the big wars / That make ambition virtue." Still verse, still musical, but the anaphora now strips things away rather than building them, and "that make ambition virtue" admits the roles were a rationalisation.
  4. 4.1 — "Goats and monkeys!" The pentameter breaks entirely. Four syllables replace a general.
  5. 5.2 — "O fool, fool, fool!" A monosyllable repeated, and a shift into the third person about himself: he has become the object of his own sentence before he becomes the object of his own sword.
The formulation worth memorising

Othello is not destroyed by jealousy; he is destroyed by a loss of vocabulary. He has only ever had one register — the public, military, elevated one — and Iago's achievement is to remove it. Everything else (the fit, the striking, the murder, the suicide) follows from that. This single claim answers questions on language, power, identity, gender and tragedy, which is why it is worth learning by heart and then adapting.

7. Close-reading worked examples

Four passages, each taken through the full chain: feature → technique → effect → meaning. This is the core of the page and the part to practise out loud.

Worked example 1 Iago's opening paradox — 1.1
Though I am bound to every act of duty, / I am not bound to that all slaves are free to — / Utter my thoughts?Iago, Othello, Act 1 Scene 1 (approximate lineation)

Feature 1 — the paradox of self-definition. Earlier in the same speech Iago has said "I am not what I am", a deliberate inversion of the divine name given at the burning bush ("I am that I am"). Technique: blasphemous paradox. Effect: the audience is given a character who defines himself by negation, and is simultaneously made his confidant. Meaning: we are placed where Othello will later be placed — inside Iago's confidence, trusting a man who has told us he cannot be trusted.

Feature 2 — the two uses of "bound". "I am bound to every act of duty" / "I am not bound to that all slaves are free to". Technique: antanaclasis — the same word used in two senses in adjacent clauses. Effect: the sentence appears to be a scruple, a man refusing to speak out of loyalty. Meaning: it is the opposite. The apparent refusal is an invitation, and the structural point is that Iago's restraint is always a form of pressure.

Feature 3 — the word "slaves". Uttering one's thoughts is what slaves are "free" to do. Effect: moral seriousness is inverted into a vice; freedom of speech is redefined as a condition of servitude. Meaning: Iago's whole method is stated here in one clause — he will never say the thing, so he can never be held to it.

Why this is worth knowing: this passage lets you write about the audience as a victim. Most essays treat Iago's rhetoric as something done to Othello; this shows it being done to us, in the first hundred and fifty lines.
Worked example 2 The planted fragment — 3.3, "Ha, I like not that"
Iago: Ha, I like not that. / Othello: What dost thou say? / Iago: Nothing, my lord; or if — I know not what.Othello, Act 3 Scene 3 (approximate lineation)

Feature 1 — aposiopesis. The sentence is broken off. Technique: aposiopesis, supported by the false start "or if —". Effect: Othello is required to ask, and therefore to participate. The question "What dost thou say?" is the moment the suspicion becomes partly his. Meaning: Iago supplies no content; he supplies a shape, and Othello fills it. That is the mechanism of the entire play compressed into six words.

Feature 2 — the denial is not a denial. "Nothing, my lord" nominally retracts the remark. Technique: damaging denial, a denial that repeats rather than removes. Effect: a flat denial of an unspecified content cannot be answered, because there is nothing to answer. Meaning: Othello cannot test the claim, can only feel its weight, and the play demonstrates that untestable claims are the most persuasive kind.

Feature 3 — the shared line. The exchange is metrically continuous: Iago's half-line is completed by Othello's response. Technique: shared pentameter. Effect: formal intimacy — the two men are, on the page, speaking in one breath. Meaning: Iago is inside Othello's line, and the verse form shows the possession that the plot will confirm.

Feature 4 — dramatic irony. The audience knows Iago means Cassio's departure from Desdemona. Effect: we experience the technique from outside for once, watching a device work. Meaning: the play is a demonstration, not a mystery, and this is the moment the demonstration becomes unbearable.

Why this is worth knowing: six words, four techniques, and the whole theory of persuasion in the play. If you learn one passage for the individual oral, it is hard to beat — but it is the famous one, so consider 7.3 below.
Worked example 3 The handkerchief's provenance — 3.4
That's a fault. That handkerchief / Did an Egyptian to my mother give… There's magic in the web of it: / A sibyl… in her prophetic fury sewed the work; / The worms were hallowed that did breed the silk; / And it was dyed in mummy which the skilful / Conserved of maidens' hearts.Othello, Othello, Act 3 Scene 3 and Act 3 Scene 4 (approximate lineation)

Feature 1 — escalation across scenes. In 3.3 the handkerchief is a love-token with a charming origin: "an Egyptian to my mother give", "spotted with strawberries". By 3.4 it has acquired a prophetess, hallowed silkworms and a dye made from preserved virgins' hearts. Technique: incremental elaboration with increasingly exotic lexis. Effect: the description grows in proportion to Othello's need. Meaning: the object's meaning is not discovered but manufactured, and the person manufacturing it is the victim. Suspicion generates its own evidence.

Feature 2 — the domestic detail against the occult. "Spotted with strawberries" is a needlework term; "dyed in mummy" is a grotesque. Technique: register collision. Effect: the object becomes simultaneously absurd and terrible. Meaning: the play keeps the audience aware that this is a napkin. We are not allowed to forget the triviality of the thing that is about to kill someone, and that kept-visible absurdity is the play's bleakest irony.

Feature 3 — "mummy". A medicinal preparation made from preserved human remains. Effect: an object of love is described in the vocabulary of death and pharmacy — the same cluster as "pestilence" and "mandragora". Meaning: Othello's language has already moved into the register of poison and mortality before he has decided to kill. Language changes first; action follows.

Why this is worth knowing: it converts the handkerchief from a symbol into a process. "What the play says about the construction of evidence" is a band-7 paragraph; "the handkerchief symbolises their love" is band 3.
Worked example 4 Emilia's argument — 4.3, the willow scene
Let husbands know / Their wives have sense like them: they see, and smell, / And have their palates both for sweet and sour, / As husbands have. What do they when they change us for another? / Is it sport?… The ills we do, their ills instruct us so.Emilia, Othello, Act 4 Scene 3 (approximate lineation)

Feature 1 — the appeal to the senses. "They see, and smell, / And have their palates both for sweet and sour." Technique: anaphora and a tricolon of bodily senses. Effect: the argument is made at the level of the body rather than the soul, which is a deliberate rhetorical choice by a character who has no scriptural or classical authority to cite. Meaning: equality is argued from shared physiology, which is a startlingly materialist claim for a Jacobean stage.

Feature 2 — the rhetorical question. "What do they when they change us for another? / Is it sport?" Technique: hypophora — asking and answering. Effect: the audience is positioned as a jury that must concede. Meaning: Emilia does for gender what Iago does for jealousy: she makes her listener supply the conclusion. The play's two best rhetoricians are the villain and the maid, and neither has formal power.

Feature 3 — the chiasmic proverb. "The ills we do, their ills instruct us so." Technique: chiastic aphorism (ABBA: ills / we / their / ills). Effect: it closes the speech with the authority of a maxim. Meaning: it reframes female wrongdoing as instruction — taught by men — which is a defence so radical that the play has to kill her for making it.

Why this is worth knowing: it gives you a route into gender questions that is not about Desdemona's passivity, and it pairs superbly with a non-literary text on the double standard in the individual oral.

8. Writing the essay: question types, arguments, and a weak thesis beside a strong one

8.1 The four question types this work attracts

Question typeTypical wordingUse Othello when…
Language and power"How does the text present language as an instrument of power?"Always. This is your strongest work on this type and the safest choice you can make.
Representation and identity"How is a group or individual represented, and to what effect?"Strong, provided you write about mechanism (naming, othering, register) rather than attitude.
Conflict, jealousy, trust"How is conflict presented?"Use only if the wording lets you be specific. Generic "conflict" questions produce generic answers.
Narrative perspective"How does the text use a narrator to shape the reader's response?"Avoid. A play has no narrator. Choose Chronicle or The Sense of an Ending instead — you will lose marks for the first sentence.

8.2 Building a line of argument in four moves

  1. Convert the topic into a mechanism. Not "Iago is manipulative" but "Iago's method is to withhold completion so that his victim supplies the content — and the play demonstrates this by showing us the same technique working on Roderigo, on Cassio, and on us."
  2. Give the mechanism a direction. Every strong thesis has a trajectory: X becomes Y; what was public becomes private; what was a token becomes evidence; a man who commanded verse is reduced to a monosyllable. Direction gives you paragraph order for free.
  3. Build in a "however". The counter-argument is what makes an argument rather than a claim. "Granted that Othello is the product of a society that values reputation above persons, the play still insists on his agency, because…"
  4. End with what the play does, not what it says. "Shakespeare shows that…" is weaker than "By giving Iago the play's best verse and Othello its grandest, and then exchanging them, Shakespeare makes the audience complicit in the transfer."

8.3 A weak thesis and a strong one, side by side

Weak — band 3/4

"In Othello, Shakespeare explores the theme of jealousy through the character of Iago, who is jealous of Othello and tricks him into killing his wife. This shows that jealousy can destroy people."

Why it fails: it is a plot summary with the word "theme" attached; "shows that jealousy can destroy people" is true of everything; no technique; no direction; no argument anyone could disagree with.

Strong — band 6/7

"Othello presents jealousy not as a passion that overwhelms a man but as a method of reading that he adopts: Iago supplies no evidence, only incomplete sentences, and Othello's tragedy is that he completes them himself — a process the play measures precisely, in the collapse of his verse from controlled pentameter to four-syllable exclamation."

Why it works: it redefines the concept rather than restating it; it names a mechanism; it has a direction (completion → collapse); and it is measurable, so every body paragraph has something to point at.

9. Using Othello in the individual oral

The oral asks you to pair one literary extract with one non-literary extract through a global issue. Two extracts from this play are outstanding, and a third is better than both precisely because nobody else will choose it.

3.3 — "Farewell the tranquil mind"
Roughly twenty lines with anaphora, oxymoron, register and identity all in one place. Pair with any non-literary text about masculinity, military identity, or the cost of a reputation-based culture. Global issue: "When a culture defines a man's worth entirely by public reputation, it leaves him defenceless against a rumour."
4.3 — Emilia's speech
The best choice for a gender pairing: a short, dense, arguable passage with a clear rhetorical structure. Pair with an opinion column or campaign text about the double standard. Global issue: "The assumption that a woman's account requires corroboration before it counts as evidence."
5.2 — "It is the cause… Put out the light"
Repetition, the pun on candle and life, self-address. Pair with a text about judgement, capital punishment, or certainty. Global issue: "Certainty, once performed publicly, becomes very difficult to withdraw even in the face of contrary evidence."
2.3 — Cassio on reputation
The under-used option. Pair with a text about online reputation, cancellation or a public apology. Global issue: "A society that treats reputation as property will treat its loss as a punishment, without ever asking whether the loss was deserved."
How to make the pairing work

State the global issue in the first sixty seconds, in one arguable sentence containing a because or a when. Then — and this is the part students skip — make the mechanism the link, not the topic. "Both texts are about reputation" is worth nothing. "Both texts present reputation as something administered by an audience rather than owned by a person: Cassio says he has lost 'the immortal part of myself', and the campaign text addresses the reader as one of the people doing the administering" is worth five marks on criterion C. Full method on the oral page.

10. Common traps

  1. Plot summary. The single biggest mark-loser. If a sentence of your essay could be replaced by a sentence from a study guide's plot section without anyone noticing, delete it. Test: does the sentence contain a word from the text and a claim about how that word works? If not, it is summary.
  2. Paraphrase dressed as analysis. "This shows Othello is upset" restates the quotation. Say what the choice does: the broken line, the shift to the third person, the animal noun, the repeated monosyllable.
  3. Iago as a cartoon villain. "Iago is evil and jealous" is not analysis and it is not even accurate — he has more lines than the hero, the best soliloquies and no stable motive. Treat him as a technician and the essay writes itself.
  4. Ignoring Emilia. She carries the play's most explicit argument and the plot's resolution. An essay on gender that does not mention her is incomplete; an essay on structure that does not notice she is the revealer is missing the design.
  5. Presentism. Do not judge the play by twenty-first-century standards and stop there, and do not excuse it either. The strong move is to show the play examining its own period's assumptions — Othello is simultaneously Venice's indispensable general and the object of its obscenities, and that contradiction is the subject, not a flaw.
  6. Treating Othello as either wholly guilty or wholly innocent. Both readings are available and the play is built so that you must hold them together. "To what extent" questions exist precisely to reward that.
  7. Quoting too much. A four-word quotation embedded in your own sentence beats a full line dumped into the paragraph. Examiners reward the integration, not the volume.

11. Eight practice questions

  1. How does Shakespeare present the power of language in Othello?

    Show the answer

    Thesis: In Othello language does not report reality — it manufactures it. Iago supplies no evidence at all; he supplies incomplete sentences, and Othello's tragedy is that he completes them himself.

    Paragraphs: (1) aposiopesis at 3.3 — "Ha, I like not that" — and the audience as co-author; (2) the handkerchief's re-description from token to proof across 3.3 and 3.4; (3) Othello's register collapse as the measurable damage, from "bright swords" (1.2) to "Goats and monkeys!" (4.1); (4) Desdemona's "Am I that name?" as the only resistance, and its failure.

    Conclusion: The play's final villain is not a man but a habit — the habit of treating another person's interpretation as your own.

    Why this is worth knowing: this is the highest-value question for this work and the one you should be able to answer in your sleep. Every paragraph is a mechanism, not a topic.

  2. Analyse how Shakespeare uses verse and prose to signal status and change.

    Show the answer

    Thesis: Blank verse is rank in this play, prose is either low status or manipulation, and the moment Othello's pentameter breaks is the moment his authority ends — so the social hierarchy and the tragedy are written into the metre.

    Paragraphs: (1) Othello's controlled, end-stopped verse in 1.2 and 1.3; (2) Iago's prose with Roderigo as his workshop, and his register-switching as evidence that verse with Othello is a costume; (3) the shared lines of 3.3, where Iago literally completes Othello's metrical unit; (4) 4.1 and 5.2 as metrical collapse, and 5.2's couplets as an attempt to rebuild order through form.

    Why this is worth knowing: metre is the most checkable evidence available to you. Unlike "jealousy", a broken pentameter can be pointed at on the page, which is why examiners reward it.

  3. To what extent is Othello responsible for his own downfall?

    Show the answer

    Thesis: Substantially, but not in the way Iago intends: Othello's vulnerability is not weakness of character but poverty of identity. He possesses only public roles, so when the marriage that underwrote them is questioned he has no private self to retreat to and must destroy the question itself.

    Paragraphs: (1) the composition of his identity — "My parts, my title and my perfect soul"; (2) the speed of the temptation, which argues against a reading of slow moral decay; (3) the register collapse as evidence that the damage is linguistic before it is moral; (4) the final self-narration as the persistence of the flaw — he is still authoring himself at the end.

    Counter-argument to concede: a society that simultaneously employs and despises him has already done much of the work; the play does not let Venice off.

    Why this is worth knowing: "to what extent" requires a measured judgement, not a verdict. Practise the conceding clause — "granted that…, the play still insists…" — because it is worth a band on its own.

  4. Discuss the presentation of reputation in the play.

    Show the answer

    Thesis: Reputation is described as the "immortal part" of a man and is in fact the most fragile thing in the play — Cassio loses his in a brawl he was tricked into, Desdemona loses hers without ever being formally accused, and Othello destroys his own in order to defend it.

    Paragraphs: (1) Cassio's "immortal part of myself" and the logic that makes the loss animalising; (2) Othello's public self-definition in 1.3; (3) Iago's cynical counter-maxim, "Reputation is an idle and most false imposition", placed in the same scene — the play hands you both positions; (4) 5.2's "Speak of me as I am" as the final, terrible investment in how one is recorded.

    Why this is worth knowing: reputation is the one theme that links Othello to Chronicle of a Death Foretold mechanically rather than vaguely, so this question doubles as Paper 2 preparation.

  5. Explore how Shakespeare presents the position of women in the play.

    Show the answer

    Thesis: The play's economy of female reputation is asymmetrical and measurable: a man's sexual conduct is gossip, a woman's is a capital charge. Shakespeare makes the point structurally — Desdemona is tried without ever being told the charge, Bianca is arrested for a crime nobody witnessed, and Emilia is killed within a hundred lines of making the argument.

    Paragraphs: (1) Desdemona's diminishing share of the lines after 1.3; (2) Emilia's 4.3 speech, its rhetorical strategy (senses, hypophora, aphorism) and its cost; (3) Bianca as the control case — what happens without rank; (4) the willow song as the only moment Desdemona is given a private voice, placed immediately before she is silenced.

    Why this is worth knowing: the structural argument — who gets lines, who gets a soliloquy, who gets believed — is far stronger than listing insults, and it is the version that scores.

  6. Analyse Shakespeare's use of imagery of animals, disease and darkness.

    Show the answer

    Thesis: The three clusters form one trajectory: animal imagery dehumanises, disease imagery makes suspicion feel like contamination, and darkness finally supplies the moral vocabulary for the killing. Dehumanisation precedes violence in every case, which is the play's most serious claim.

    Paragraphs: (1) "making the beast with two backs" (1.1) establishes the register before we meet either partner; (2) "pestilence into his ear" (2.3) and the medical lexis of 3.3 — "poppy", "mandragora", "drowsy syrups"; (3) "begrimed and black / As mine own face" (3.3), where Othello turns his own otherness into the standard of defilement; (4) "Goats and monkeys!" (4.1) as the endpoint, and "Put out the light" (5.2) as the pun that closes the sequence.

    Why this is worth knowing: grouping images into clusters with a direction is the difference between a band-4 list and a band-6 argument. Always ask which cluster arrives first.

  7. How does the play use dramatic irony, and to what effect on the audience?

    Show the answer

    Thesis: Dramatic irony is structural rather than decorative: because the audience knows the plot from 1.1 and the hero knows nothing until 5.2, the play converts suspense into complicity — we are not watching to find out what happens but to watch a method succeed on a man we cannot warn.

    Paragraphs: (1) Iago's soliloquies, which tell us the plan and make us his confidants; (2) the warning against jealousy (3.3) delivered by the man manufacturing it, which works because it appears reluctant; (3) 4.1, where Othello's only act of "seeing" is his most complete misreading; (4) 5.2, where the revelation arrives as information for us and catastrophe for him.

    Why this is worth knowing: this question rewards you for thinking about the audience rather than the characters, which is exactly what a Language and Literature course wants and what most students forget.

  8. Compare the presentation of persuasion in Othello and one other work you have studied.

    Show the answer

    Pairing: The World's Wife. Thesis: Both works treat eloquence as authority, but Shakespeare dramatises its theft — Iago dismantles Othello's language until the general speaks fragments — whereas Duffy dramatises its seizure, handing monologue and therefore authority to women who had been silent objects in someone else's story.

    Paragraphs: (1) verse as status in Othello against Duffy's deliberately un-elevated free verse; (2) register collapse in Othello against the flat, superior register of "Mrs Icarus"; (3) Iago's aposiopesis against Duffy's bathos — the same toolkit, opposite ends; (4) enforced silence (Desdemona) against chosen speech ("Pilate's Wife", "Eurydice").

    Why this is worth knowing: this is the strongest Paper 2 pairing available to you on a language question, and the theft-versus-seizure contrast is a genuine insight rather than a similarity with the word "similarly" attached.

13. Possible global issues

  • Culture, identity and community: "Cultures that define a person's worth by public reputation leave them defenceless against a rumour."
  • Politics, power and justice: "Persuasion is a form of power that leaves no evidence and therefore no accountability."
  • Beliefs, values and education: "A society that needs an outsider and despises him produces the conditions for its own violent undoing."
  • Art, creativity and the imagination: "Whoever controls the interpretation of an object controls the reality it proves."
  • Culture, identity and community (gender): "The assumption that a woman's account requires corroboration before it counts as evidence."
Connections — which pairings work, and why
  • → Chronicle of a Death Foretold (strongest pairing): both are honour cultures in which a woman's supposed sexual conduct licenses a man's death. García Márquez distributes guilt across a town; Shakespeare concentrates it in one manipulator. Use for any question on honour, reputation, community or failure of communication.
  • → The World's Wife: the pairing for language, power and gender. Shakespeare dramatises eloquence being stolen from a man; Duffy dramatises it being seized by women. Both treat speech as authority.

Vocabulary
blank verse — unrhymed lines of iambic pentameter
iambic pentameter — a ten-syllable line alternating weak and strong beats
prose — ordinary speech printed without a metrical pattern
register — the level of formality a speaker chooses for a situation
register collapse — when a character loses their formal, controlled way of speaking
rhetoric — the deliberate use of language to persuade an audience
aposiopesis — breaking a sentence off so the listener completes it
anaphora — repeating the same word or phrase at the start of successive lines
chiasmus — a balanced structure in which the second half reverses the first (ABBA)
antanaclasis — repeating a word in a different sense ("bound" twice)
hypophora — asking a question and immediately answering it yourself
oxymoron — two contradictory words placed together ("ambition virtue")
soliloquy — a speech delivered alone on stage, revealing private thought
aside — a short remark addressed to the audience, unheard by others on stage
dramatic irony — when the audience knows more than the characters do
hamartia — a tragic error of judgement rather than a moral flaw
peripeteia — the reversal of fortune in a tragedy
anagnorisis — the moment a character recognises the truth
dehumanise — to describe people in terms that strip them of human status
othering — representing a group as fundamentally different and lesser
imagery cluster — a group of related images running through a text
enjambment — a sentence running on past the end of a verse line
end-stopped — a line that closes with its sense complete
pun — a play on two meanings of the same word
bathos — a sudden drop from the elevated to the ordinary
presentism — judging a past text only by present-day values
