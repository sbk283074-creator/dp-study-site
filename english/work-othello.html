Othello

Shakespeare, around 1603–04. A general who is also an outsider is persuaded, in about three days, that his wife has been unfaithful, and kills her. What makes the play endlessly examinable is that the murder is achieved almost entirely with language: no evidence is ever produced. There is no letter, no witness, no scene of adultery — only a handkerchief and a man who knows how to leave a sentence unfinished. If you want to write about how words do things to people, this is your text.

1. Author and context

Shakespeare wrote Othello early in James I's reign, shortly after Hamlet, for a company performing at the Globe and at court. The source is a story by the Italian Giraldi Cinthio (1565), in which a Moorish captain is persuaded by his ensign to beat his wife to death; Shakespeare compressed the timescale, invented the handkerchief and Emilia, and made the murderer a figure of enormous eloquence and public dignity.

Context that matters for exam purposes: Venice was imagined in England as a sophisticated, mercantile, cosmopolitan state — a place where a foreign soldier could rise on merit — while Cyprus, the play's second setting, was a frontier garrison, a masculine military world with none of Venice's civil restraints. The movement from Venice to Cyprus is a movement from a society with rules to one with only codes, and that movement is structural, not decorative.

Why this text matters

The play is the most concentrated study of persuasion in the language. Iago never lies about anything anyone can check. He supplies no facts; he supplies frames — the interrupted sentence, the loaded word, the invitation to "note" something. Every technique in the rhetorical toolkit is in this play, deployed by a man who knows that the most convincing evidence is the evidence the victim assembles for himself.

2. Form and structure: five acts, and the handkerchief

The five-act architecture

Act I — Venice
Exposition at speed. Iago's grievance (Cassio promoted), the waking of Brabantio, and the senate scene (1.3) where Othello defends his marriage with controlled, elevated verse. By the end of the act we are on our way to Cyprus, and Iago has told us exactly what he intends.
Act II — Cyprus
The storm destroys the Turkish fleet, removing the external plot and leaving nothing for Iago to do but work on Othello. The drunken brawl destroys Cassio's reputation — reputation being, as Cassio says, "the immortal part of myself".
Act III — The temptation
The heart of the play. 3.3 is one of the great scenes in drama: in about 480 lines Othello moves from "My noble Moor is true of mind" through "Ha, I like not that" to a vow of vengeance. The handkerchief is dropped and picked up in this act.
Act IV — The degradation
Othello's public fit (4.1), his striking of Desdemona before the Venetian ambassadors, and the "brothel" scene (4.2) where he calls his wife a whore in front of her cousin. The willow song (4.3) gives Desdemona her most human moment, and it is the last one we get.
Act V — The catastrophe
The murder, Emilia's delayed revelation, and Othello's final self-narration and suicide. Note how little time passes on stage between the decision and the act — the speed is part of the horror.

The handkerchief as plot engine

The handkerchief is the single most efficient object in Shakespeare. It appears in four places, and each appearance does a different job:

  1. 3.3 — origin and loss. Othello claims it was an Egyptian charm given by his mother, "spotted with strawberries", and Emilia picks it up for Iago at her husband's insistence. It enters the play as a love-token and leaves the scene as evidence.
  2. 3.4 — the test. Othello demands it; Desdemona cannot produce it; the object becomes proof by absence. Othello then inflates it with occult provenance — "there's magic in the web of it… it was dyed in mummy… conserved of maidens' hearts" — an escalation that reveals how much of the "proof" he is supplying himself.
  3. 4.1 — the seeing. Iago has planted it in Cassio's lodging and Othello watches Cassio handle it, misreading a comic scene with Bianca as a bawdy confirmation. The handkerchief now performs what Iago calls "ocular proof".
  4. 5.2 — the unravelling. Emilia reveals the theft, and the object that carried the entire plot is exposed as a piece of linen. The tragedy's mechanism is disclosed as trivial, which is precisely the point: it was always trivial. The play needed no evidence because Othello was the one manufacturing it.
Exam technique

Do not write about the handkerchief as a "symbol of love". That is a band-3 answer. Write about it as a plot mechanism that converts a token into proof through misreading. The object does not change; the interpretation changes, and the play is about who controls interpretation.

3. Plot in brief

Iago, passed over for promotion in favour of Cassio, hates Othello. He engineers a drunken brawl that costs Cassio his lieutenancy, then advises Cassio to sue for reinstatement through Desdemona — which gives Iago the appearance of a wife's undue influence. He plants suspicions in Othello's mind with hints and interruptions, uses a handkerchief Desdemona has dropped as fabricated proof, and drives Othello to an epileptic fit and finally to murder. Desdemona is smothered. Emilia, realising what her husband has done, exposes the plot and is killed by Iago. Othello kills himself. Cassio inherits Cyprus; Iago is led away to be tortured.

4. Characters

CharacterFunctionWhat to say about them
OthelloTragic protagonistA public man with almost no private self. His identity is entirely composed of roles granted by Venice — general, son-in-law, Christian — and when he doubts his marriage, he has nothing to fall back on. His tragedy is a linguistic one: he loses his command of language before he loses his wife.
IagoAntagonist and rhetoricianThe most complete rhetorician in the language. He has no single convincing motive — passed over, cuckold-rumour, racism, sheer experimentation — and that instability is the point: he is a pure engine of method.
DesdemonaThe accusedEloquent in the senate scene and almost silenced thereafter. She has roughly a quarter of Othello's lines. Her persistence — "Am I that name, Iago?" — is not weakness but a refusal to accept a definition she did not author.
EmiliaThe revealerSteals the handkerchief without knowing why, and tells the truth too late. She is the play's most explicit feminist voice, and the structural device that exposes the plot.
CassioThe instrumentCourteous, literate, a little vain; his reputation is what he calls his "immortal part", and the play tests exactly that claim.
RoderigoThe dupeShows Iago's method from the outside: a man managed entirely through promises and prose.
BiancaThe mirrorA woman publicly suspected of prostitution; her treatment shows what Venice's sexual double standard does without the protection of rank.

5. Themes

Reputation and identity
Cassio's "I have lost my reputation… the immortal part of myself" and Othello's "My parts, my title and my perfect soul" define selfhood as something held in other people's estimation. The play's horror is that reputation can be taken away by a rumour.
Jealousy as interpretation
Iago's "Trifles light as air / Are to the jealous confirmations strong / As proofs of holy writ" is the play's thesis: jealousy is not a feeling but a hermeneutic — a way of reading the world that converts nothing into everything.
Race, otherness and representation
Othello is simultaneously Venice's indispensable general and the object of its obscene jokes ("an old black ram is tupping your white ewe"). The play shows a state that needs him and despises him, and shows him internalising the vocabulary of his own otherness — "Her name… is now begrimed and black / As mine own face."
Gender and the double standard
Desdemona is killed for a suspicion; Othello is admired for killing her. Emilia's speech on wives' equality is the play's most direct statement, and its placement moments before her death is Shakespeare's own comment.
Public versus private
Almost everything consequential happens in front of witnesses or through reports. There is no private Othello; the moment he stops performing is the moment he begins to break.

6. Language and style

Verse and prose as a status signal

This is the single most important technical fact about the play, and it is the easiest way to earn marks on any question about status, power or identity.

ModeWho uses itWhat it signals
Blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter)Othello at the start, the Duke, the senators, Desdemona in 1.3Rank, education, public office, self-command. Regular metre is the character's social position.
ProseIago with Roderigo; the clown; letters; the drunken scene Intimacy, low status, manipulation, or a breakdown of ceremony. Iago's prose is his workshop — it is where he is honest by accident.
Broken verse / exclamationOthello in 4.1 and 5.2The collapse of self-command. When Othello can no longer complete a pentameter line, he can no longer command anything else either.
Riming coupletsClosings of scenes; Othello's final speech Formality, closure, and — in 5.2 — Othello's attempt to re-impose order on himself through form.

Iago's rhetoric

  1. Aposiopesis — the broken-off sentence. "Ha, I like not that." He never completes the thought; Othello completes it. The audience finishes the sentence, and therefore owns it.
  2. Anaphora and repetition — "Note, note, note your own suspicion"; "Think, my lord?" Repetition wears down resistance like a physical force.
  3. Rhetorical questions and hypophora — he asks and answers, controlling both sides of the exchange, so Othello never has to agree out loud.
  4. Concession and refusal — "I am bound to every act of duty, I am not bound to that all slaves are free to" — he appears to resist telling, which makes the telling irresistible.
  5. Antithesis and balance — "The Moor is of a free and open nature / That thinks men honest that but seem to be so." Balanced, quotable, and therefore memorable as a "truth".
  6. Register switching — verse with Othello, prose with Roderigo, bawdy with the audience. He is a different speaker for every listener, which is the definition of rhetoric.

Imagery: darkness, monsters, poison

Darkness and blackness

Used in three competing ways: as Othello's self-description, as the accusation against Desdemona ("Her name… is now begrimed and black"), and as the moral vocabulary Iago plants ("O, beware the foul fiend"). The word black is the play's most contested term — it is the mechanism by which Othello's own otherness is turned against his wife.

Monsters and bestiality

"Making the beast with two backs" (1.1) launches it; "the green-eyed monster" (3.3) names jealousy as a creature that eats its host; "Goats and monkeys!" (4.1) is Othello's reduction of human beings to livestock. The progress is a progress of dehumanisation — the animals arrive before the violence.

Poison and infection

"I'll pour this pestilence into his ear" (2.3) — the murder of a reputation is imagined as a liquid administered through the ear, and the play is saturated with disease imagery thereafter. Language itself is treated as an infection entering a body.

Light and fire

"Put out the light, and then put out the light" (5.2) — the pun on candle and life is also the play's final argument about knowledge: Othello believes he is extinguishing corruption and is extinguishing illumination.

Othello's register collapse

Track this progression and you have a ready-made essay on identity:

  1. 1.2 — "Keep up your bright swords, for the dew will rust them." Controlled, musical, unhurried. A man whose authority is total.
  2. 1.3 — "My parts, my title and my perfect soul / Shall manifest me rightly." Balanced tricolon; public self-definition in formal verse.
  3. 3.3 — "Farewell the tranquil mind! farewell content! / Farewell the plumèd troop…" Still verse, still musical, but now the anaphora is stripping things away rather than building them.
  4. 4.1 — "Goats and monkeys!" The pentameter breaks entirely. Four syllables replace a general.
  5. 5.2 — "O fool, fool, fool!" Repetition of a monosyllable, and a shift into the third person about himself. He has become the object of his own sentence.
Exam technique

The most valuable formulation for this play: Othello is not destroyed by jealousy; he is destroyed by a loss of vocabulary. He has only ever had one register — the public, military, elevated one — and Iago's achievement is to remove it. Everything else (the fit, the violence, the suicide) follows from that.

8. Possible global issues

  • Culture, identity and community: "Cultures that define a man's worth by public reputation leave him defenceless against a rumour."
  • Politics, power and justice: "Persuasion is a form of power that leaves no evidence and therefore no accountability."
  • Beliefs, values and education: "A society that needs an outsider and despises him produces the conditions for its own violent undoing."
  • Art, creativity and the imagination: "Whoever controls the interpretation of an object controls the reality it proves."

9. How this work behaves in the exam

Paper 2

Best on questions about language and power, persuasion, reputation, jealousy, gender and representation, and register. It is the strongest of your four works on any question containing the word rhetoric. Weaker on questions about narrative perspective, because a play has no narrator — if a question is about narrators, choose Chronicle or The Sense of an Ending and leave Othello alone.

Individual oral

Two extracts are outstanding: 3.3.347 "Farewell the tranquil mind" (anaphora, oxymoron, identity, register in twenty lines) and 5.2.1–22 "It is the cause… Put out the light" (repetition, pun, light/dark). Both pair beautifully with any non-literary text about masculinity, reputation, or persuasion — including the #StillOnDuty campaign on the oral page.

10. Six practice essay questions

  1. Discuss Shakespeare's presentation of the power of language in Othello.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: In Othello language does not report reality — it manufactures it. Iago supplies no evidence at all; he supplies incomplete sentences, and Othello's tragedy is that he completes them himself.

    Paragraphs: (1) aposiopesis at 3.3.34 and the audience as co-author; (2) the handkerchief's re-description as proof; (3) Othello's register collapse as the measurable damage; (4) Desdemona's "Am I that name?" as the only resistance, and its failure.

  2. Analyse how Shakespeare uses verse and prose to signal status and change.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Blank verse is rank in this play, prose is either low status or manipulation, and the moment Othello's pentameter breaks is the moment his authority ends — so the play's social hierarchy and its tragedy are written in its metre.

    Paragraphs: (1) Othello's controlled verse in 1.2 and 1.3; (2) Iago's prose with Roderigo as his workshop; (3) the handover in 3.3 where Othello begins to speak Iago's fragmented lines; (4) 4.1 and 5.2 as metrical collapse.

  3. Explore the presentation of reputation in the play.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Reputation is described as the "immortal part" of a man and is in fact the most fragile thing in the play — Cassio loses his in a brawl, Desdemona loses hers without ever being accused aloud, and Othello destroys his own to defend it.

    Paragraphs: (1) Cassio's lament; (2) Othello's "My parts, my title and my perfect soul"; (3) Iago's cynical aphorism; (4) Othello's final speech as an attempt to recover reputation in death.

  4. To what extent is Othello responsible for his own downfall?

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Substantially, but not in the way Iago intends: Othello's vulnerability is not weakness of character but poverty of identity — he possesses only public roles, so when the marriage that underwrote them is questioned, he has no private self to retreat to and must destroy the question itself.

    Paragraphs: (1) the composition of his identity; (2) the speed of the temptation; (3) the register collapse as evidence; (4) the final self-narration as the persistence of the flaw.

  5. Compare the presentation of women in Othello and one other studied work.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Pair with The World's Wife: Shakespeare withholds Desdemona's account almost entirely, while Duffy's entire collection consists of women who were previously only described — so the same political point is made by silence in one text and by monologue in the other.

    Paragraphs: (1) Desdemona's diminishing share of the lines; (2) Emilia's speech and its cost; (3) "Mrs Midas" / "Medusa" as the recovery of voice; (4) which strategy is more effective, and for whom.

  6. Discuss the use of imagery of darkness, animals and disease.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: The three image-clusters form a single trajectory: animal imagery dehumanises, disease imagery makes suspicion feel like contamination, and darkness imagery finally provides the moral vocabulary for the killing — dehumanisation precedes violence in every case.

    Paragraphs: (1) "the beast with two backs"; (2) "pestilence into his ear" and infection; (3) "begrimed and black"; (4) "Goats and monkeys!" as the endpoint.

Connections — which pairings work, and why
  • → Chronicle of a Death Foretold (strongest pairing): both are honour cultures in which a woman's supposed sexual conduct licenses a man's death. García Márquez distributes guilt across a town; Shakespeare concentrates it in one manipulator. Use for any question on honour, reputation, community or failure of communication.
  • → The World's Wife: the pairing for language, power and gender. Shakespeare dramatises eloquence being stolen from a man; Duffy dramatises it being seized by women. Both texts treat speech as authority.
  • → The Sense of an Ending: both protagonists reach self-knowledge too late and both immediately begin narrating themselves as better men. Excellent for questions on memory, self-deception and regret — but remember to compare form as well as character.
  • → Paper 1: Iago is the unseen-speech writer's dream. Every rhetorical device in the toolkit on that page is something he does; Text C (the curfew speech) uses the same concession-and-reversal structure.
  • → Individual oral: pair 3.3's "Farewell the tranquil mind" with the #StillOnDuty campaign, or 5.2 with any text about reputation and judgement.
