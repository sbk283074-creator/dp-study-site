English A: Language and Literature SL

You are strong at content subjects and you think you are bad at English. What that usually means is this: nobody has ever shown you the machine. Language A is not a talent test, it is a method test. Every mark on every component comes from the same five moves — identify a choice, name the technique, explain the effect, tie it to meaning, tie it to a global issue. Learn the moves and the subject stops being mysterious. This hub is the map; the other seven pages are the drills.

1. What this subject actually is

Literature A studies literary works: novels, plays, poems. Language and Literature studies language itself — and it studies it in two modes at once. You read literature (your four set works) and you read non-literary texts (advertisements, speeches, opinion columns, blogs, memoirs, infographics, campaigns). The exam then asks you to do the same analytical work on both.

That changes how you read in one important way. A Literature student asks "what does this work mean?". You ask a second question at the same time: "how is this piece of language trying to do something to me, and by what deliberate choices?" Meaning and method are never separated. In this subject, no effect is an accident. That is the single belief the whole course rests on: if a text made you feel something, a writer built it.

Literature A asks

What does the work mean? How does it work as art? How do form, genre and tradition shape it? The corpus is literary throughout.

Language & Literature asks

How does this text — literary or not — construct meaning, position a reader, and act in the world? Who is the audience, what is the purpose, what is the context, and which choices carry the weight?

Why this exists

In real life you will read far more advertising, policy, journalism and social-media argument than you will read novels. The IB built this subject so that you can read a charity appeal and a Shakespeare soliloquy with the same question in your head: what is being done to me here, and how? That is also why the individual oral makes you pair a novel with an advertisement. It is not a trick. It is the point.

Exam technique

Notice the shape. 65% of your grade is comparative or unseen. You cannot memorise your way to a 7. You can only method your way there. That is good news for someone who is "bad at English": method is learnable in a way that "natural flair" is not. Every page here gives you a numbered procedure, not an opinion.

3. The three areas of exploration

Everything you study sits inside one — usually two — of these. They are not topics to memorise; they are angles of approach. When a Paper 2 question or an oral plan feels thin, check which area you are working in, because the area tells you what kind of evidence to look for.

Time and space
Where and when does this text sit, and how does that shape it? Includes the historical moment (1981 Colombia, Jacobean England, 1960s Britain), the physical setting (a Caribbean town, Venice and Cyprus, a Thames-side school), and the manipulation of time inside the text. García Márquez scrambles chronology; Barnes splits his book in two and lets part two rewrite part one; Shakespeare compresses days; Duffy rewrites myth so that ancient time and modern time sit on top of each other.
Intertextuality: connecting texts
No text is alone. Texts quote, answer, parody and rewrite each other. Duffy's whole collection is built on this — each poem takes a story you already know (Midas, Icarus, Darwin, the Bible) and re-tells it from the woman's side. Barnes quotes Adrian's diary and Veronica's letter. Shakespeare reworked Cinthio. García Márquez writes a novel that behaves like a newspaper report. Whenever you notice "this is in conversation with something else", you are in this area.
Readers, writers and texts
Who is speaking, to whom, for what purpose, and what does the text assume about us? This is the area that covers audience, purpose, tone, register, bias, and the position a text invites you to take. It is the area that most directly serves non-literary analysis: an advertisement is nothing but a writer with a target and a purpose.
How to use it

Use the areas as a diagnostic when you are stuck, not as decoration in an introduction. If your essay is only listing techniques, you have probably fallen out of all three areas and into "feature-spotting". Ask yourself: is my real interest here about where/when (time and space), about what this is answering (intertextuality), or about who is being addressed and how (readers, writers, texts)? Name the area in your head and the argument usually appears.

4. The seven concepts

Seven words. They come up constantly because they are the vocabulary the IB uses for "what a text is about at the abstract level". You are expected to use them naturally, not to bolt them on.

ConceptThe question it asksIn your four works
IdentityWho is this person, and how is that identity made, performed or taken away?Othello's self-image as a soldier; Santiago Nasar's name and honour; Tony Webster's self-justifying autobiography; Duffy's wives defining themselves against their husbands.
CultureWhat shared beliefs and rules govern this world?Machismo and honour on the Caribbean coast; Venetian military society; English middle-class reserve; myth and Judeo-Christian story as culture.
CreativityHow does the text make something new out of old material?Duffy's re-visioning; García Márquez's journalism-turned-myth; Barnes's recycling of his own narrator's memory; Shakespeare's reworking of a source.
CommunicationWho says what to whom — and who fails to say it?The failure of the whole town to warn Santiago; Iago's management of what each character hears; Tony's letters; the silent listener in a Duffy monologue.
PerspectiveWhose version are we getting, and what does that cost us?The narrator-investigator in Chronicle; the unreliable Tony; Iago's control of Othello's point of view; Duffy's deliberate shift to the woman's angle.
TransformationWhat changes — a person, a meaning, a genre?Santiago's last walk; Othello from "perfect soul" to killer; Tony's small, devastating revision of his past; Mrs Midas's golden husband; Circe's transformations.
RepresentationHow is a group, an idea or a body pictured — and is that picture fair?Women in all four; race in Othello; the town as a collective character; myth as a way of representing (and misrepresenting) women.
Common trap

Do not write "this relates to the concept of identity" and stop. That sentence scores nothing. The concept is a door, not a destination: "identity" only matters once you say whose identity, built by which technique, and to what effect on the reader. Band 3 essays name concepts. Band 7 essays use them as the spine of an argument.

5. The learner portfolio

Your portfolio is the collection of non-literary texts you gather over the two years. It is not formally submitted or marked on its own — but it is the raw material for the individual oral, which is 30% of your grade. A thin portfolio means a rushed, generic oral. A rich portfolio means you walk in with eight texts you already know how to analyse.

How to use it

Build it like this, and stop improvising in year two:

  1. Collect one text a week from the real world: a print or online advertisement, an opinion column, a speech, a charity campaign, a blog post, a government infographic, an Instagram or TikTok campaign, a memoir extract, a satirical piece. Screenshot or print it; keep the source and date — context is examinable.
  2. File it under a global issue from the five categories below. Ten texts, five categories, two each. That way any oral brief is already covered.
  3. Annotate it immediately: purpose, audience, tone, three authorial choices, and one line on what it assumes about the reader. Do this the day you find it — annotation memory decays fast.
  4. Pair it with a literary work in your head. Every time you file a text, write one sentence: "This would sit next to ___ because ___. That sentence is a future oral.
  5. Keep it to 20–30 texts, not 100. Depth beats volume. You need texts you could speak about for five minutes without notes.
Connection

The portfolio is also your Paper 1 insurance. Analysing non-literary texts weekly is exactly the skill Paper 1 tests on an unseen passage. See Paper 1, and the eight original portfolio texts on the Individual oral page if you want a starter set.

6. The five global issue categories

A global issue is a concern that matters beyond the page and is argued about in the world. The IB groups them into five. In the oral you must show how both extracts engage the same issue — so you need to be able to state the issue in one precise, arguable sentence.

  1. Culture, identity and community — belonging, migration, language, gender roles, tradition versus modernity, nation.
  2. Beliefs, values and education — religion, ideology, morality, schooling, what a society chooses to teach or to hide.
  3. Politics, power and justice — authority, law, corruption, protest, inequality, human rights, institutional failure.
  4. Art, creativity and the imagination — how art works, who gets to tell stories, censorship, myth-making, the value of the imagination.
  5. Science, technology and the environment — the natural world, progress, technology's effects, the body, medicine, ecology.
Exam technique

A global issue statement must be arguable, not a slogan. "Honour is important" is not a global issue; it is a bumper sticker. "Communities that prize collective honour over individual life produce violence that everyone can foresee and no one stops" is an issue, because someone could disagree. Test yourself: can you state the opposite? If the opposite is absurd, you have a slogan, not an issue.

The honest bit

The students who get 7s are not the ones with the best English. They are the ones who did twenty timed practices and read their own feedback. You are doing HL Maths, HL Physics and HL Computer Science. Your English time is limited. So spend it on timed writing, not on more reading about the subject. Ten minutes of writing beats an hour of highlighting.
