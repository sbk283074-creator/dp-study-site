English A: Language and Literature

You are strong at content subjects and you think you are bad at English. What that usually means is this: nobody has ever shown you the machine. Language A is not a talent test, it is a method test. Every mark on every component comes from the same five moves — identify a choice, name the technique, explain the effect, tie it to meaning, tie it to a global issue. Learn the moves and the subject stops being mysterious. This hub is the map and it is also a workshop: close reading, the analytical paragraph, global issues, the criteria, and eight worked practice questions are all on this page. The other seven pages are the drills.

1. What this subject actually is

Literature A studies literary works: novels, plays, poems. Language and Literature studies language itself — and it studies it in two modes at once. You read literature (your set works) and you read non-literary texts (advertisements, speeches, opinion columns, blogs, memoirs, infographics, campaigns). The exam then asks you to do the same analytical work on both.

That changes how you read in one important way. A Literature student asks "what does this work mean?". You ask a second question at the same time: "how is this piece of language trying to do something to me, and by what deliberate choices?" Meaning and method are never separated. In this subject, no effect is an accident. That is the single belief the whole course rests on: if a text made you feel something, a writer built it.

The practical consequence is that you are never allowed to stop at content. "The advert wants people to donate" is content, and it is worth nothing on its own, because it is true of every charity advert ever printed. "The advert refuses the word homeless and substitutes a spatial phrase — around the corner — which relocates the problem from a social category to a place the reader physically passes, so that avoidance becomes a decision rather than an absence of information" is analysis, and it is worth marks. The difference between the two sentences is not vocabulary and it is not fluency. It is that the second one has located a choice and asked what the choice is for.

Literature A asks

What does the work mean? How does it work as art? How do form, genre and tradition shape it? The corpus is literary throughout, and the questions tend to stay inside the work.

Language & Literature asks

How does this text — literary or not — construct meaning, position a reader, and act in the world? Who is the audience, what is the purpose, what is the context, and which choices carry the weight?

Why this exists

In real life you will read far more advertising, policy, journalism and social-media argument than you will read novels. The IB built this subject so that you can read a charity appeal and a Shakespeare soliloquy with the same question in your head: what is being done to me here, and how? That is also why the individual oral makes you pair a literary extract with a non-literary one. It is not a trick. It is the point.

There is a harder-edged version of this argument, and it is the one that should change how you spend your revision time. Both courses reward the same skill — noticing a verbal choice and explaining its effect — but only one of them examines it on material you have never seen, under a clock, twice. That makes Language and Literature a transfer subject: you cannot prepare content for Paper 1, because you do not know what the content is. You can only prepare a procedure. Students who revise this subject by re-reading their set works are preparing for 35% of it and wondering why the other 65% does not move.

2. The course at a glance

The guide describes the course in three interlocking ways, and you need all three because they answer different questions. The areas of exploration tell you what kind of evidence to look for. The seven concepts give you the abstract vocabulary for what a text is about. The framing elements — learners, texts, contexts — describe who is in the room and what they bring. Learn them as three lenses on the same object, not as three lists to memorise.

2.1 The three areas of exploration

Everything you study sits inside one — usually two — of these. They are not topics to memorise; they are angles of approach. When a Paper 2 question or an oral plan feels thin, check which area you are working in, because the area tells you what kind of evidence to look for.

Time and space
Where and when does this text sit, and how does that shape it? Includes the historical moment (1981 Colombia, Jacobean England, 1960s Britain), the physical setting (a Caribbean town, Venice and Cyprus, a Thames-side school), and the manipulation of time inside the text. García Márquez scrambles chronology; Barnes splits his book in two and lets part two rewrite part one; Shakespeare compresses days; Duffy rewrites myth so that ancient time and modern time sit on top of each other.
Intertextuality: connecting texts
No text is alone. Texts quote, answer, parody and rewrite each other. Duffy's whole collection is built on this — each poem takes a story you already know (Midas, Icarus, Darwin, the Bible) and re-tells it from the woman's side. Barnes quotes Adrian's diary and Veronica's letter. Shakespeare reworked Cinthio. Whenever you notice "this is in conversation with something else", you are in this area.
Readers, writers and texts
Who is speaking, to whom, for what purpose, and what does the text assume about us? This is the area that covers audience, purpose, tone, register, bias, and the position a text invites you to take. It is the area that most directly serves non-literary analysis: an advertisement is nothing but a writer with a target and a purpose.
How to use it

Use the areas as a diagnostic when you are stuck, not as decoration in an introduction. If your essay is only listing techniques, you have probably fallen out of all three areas and into "feature-spotting". Ask yourself: is my real interest here about where/when (time and space), about what this is answering (intertextuality), or about who is being addressed and how (readers, writers, texts)? Name the area in your head and the argument usually appears.

2.2 The seven concepts

Seven words. They come up constantly because they are the vocabulary the IB uses for "what a text is about at the abstract level". You are expected to use them naturally, not to bolt them on.

RepresentationHow is a group, an idea or a body pictured — and is that picture fair?Women in all four; race in Othello; the town as a collective character; myth as a way of representing (and misrepresenting) women.
Common trap

Do not write "this relates to the concept of identity" and stop. That sentence scores nothing. The concept is a door, not a destination: "identity" only matters once you say whose identity, built by which technique, and to what effect on the reader. Band 3 essays name concepts. Band 7 essays use them as the spine of an argument. A quick test: if you can delete the sentence containing the concept word and the essay still says the same thing, the concept is decoration and it is costing you words you needed elsewhere.

2.3 The framing elements: learners, texts, contexts

There is a third way the guide describes the course, and it is the one that explains why two students in the same class can legitimately disagree about a passage. Every act of reading has three participants.

ElementWhat it contributesWhat this means for your writing
Learners (you)Your own linguistic and cultural history is part of the reading event. A text about curfew lands differently on a student who has lived under one. You are allowed to register a response — "the text assumes I will agree" — as long as you then show the verbal evidence that produced it. Response without evidence is opinion; evidence without response is feature-spotting.
TextsThe verbal artefact itself: its form, its choices, its traditions, and the other texts it answers.Everything you claim must be traceable to something on the page — a word, an omission, an order, a repetition. This is the element students skip when they panic.
ContextsProduction and reception: who made it, when, for whom, and against what.Attach context to a choice in a single clause. Never write a paragraph of biography before you get to the words.

4. The two courses compared

Two Language A courses exist and they are genuinely different, not the same course with different reading lists. Choosing wrongly — or, more commonly, writing about one as if it were the other — costs marks in a way that is invisible until the results come out.

Core concepts you must know

The close-reading toolkit below is procedural; this section is the vocabulary it runs on. These are the terms whose absence is most often the reason a paragraph stays at band 3: the student can see what the text is doing but has no precise word for it, so they write "the writer uses language" and stop. Each entry here is defined in one sentence and used in full on Paper 1, Paper 2, the individual oral and the four work pages.

tone — the writer's or speaker's attitude towards the subject and the reader — ironic, elegiac, bureaucratic, intimate. Tone is inferred from choices, never stated by the text, and it can shift inside a single paragraph.
mood (atmosphere) — the feeling the text produces in you, rather than the attitude of the writer; keeping the two apart is what lets you write "a bureaucratic tone produces a mood of helplessness".
register — level of formality, and the vocabulary and syntax that signal it; a register break is nearly always the most productive sentence on the page.
irony — a gap between what is said and what is meant or known: verbal (saying the opposite), dramatic (the audience knows more than the character), situational (the outcome inverts the expectation). Irony always asks who is excluded from the understanding.
satire — using irony, exaggeration or parody to attack a target; it needs a victim and a norm, so "this is satirical" is worthless without naming both.
image, motif and symbol — an image is a single instance of figurative language; a motif is an image, word or object that returns; a symbol carries a meaning beyond itself that the whole text has earned.
figurative language — metaphor (one thing stated as another), simile (stated as like or as), personification (human qualities given to a thing), pathetic fallacy (weather or setting mirroring feeling), hyperbole (deliberate exaggeration).
juxtaposition — placing two unlike things side by side so the reader does the comparing; it is structure doing the work of argument, and it costs you nothing to name.
paradox and oxymoron — a statement that seems self-contradictory and turns out to be true, and a compressed two-word version of the same move ("dear enemy", "honest lie").
narrative voice — whose consciousness filters the text: first person (intimate, partial), third-person limited (one character's knowledge), omniscient (more than any character could know), second person (implicates the reader).
free indirect discourse — third-person narration that borrows a character's vocabulary and rhythm without quotation marks, so you are inside a mind that is being gently mocked or quietly endorsed.
unreliable narrator — a narrator whose account is shaped by self-interest, ignorance or self-deception; the evidence of unreliability is always in the text, and citing it is the analysis.
speaker, writer and narrator — three different people in most poems, plays and novels: the voice in the text, the person who made it, and the figure who reports it. Conflating them loses marks every session.
form, structure and genre — genre is the kind of text (sonnet, tragedy, memoir, opinion column); form is the shape it takes (fourteen lines, five acts, one long paragraph); structure is the order of its parts.
the volta — the turn: the point where a sonnet, monologue or argument changes direction. Everything before it is set-up, so a reading that ignores the volta misreads the ending.
dramatic monologue — a poem spoken by one character to a silent listener, where what the speaker reveals about themselves is not what they intend to reveal.
rhetorical appeals — ethos (the authority or character of the speaker), pathos (emotion) and logos (reason and evidence); naming which one a text leans on explains how it persuades.
rhetorical devices — the persuasive machinery: rhetorical question, tricolon (three parts), antithesis (balanced opposites), anaphora (repeated openings), asyndeton (missing conjunctions), apostrophe (addressing the absent).
sound devices — alliteration, assonance, sibilance, plosives, rhyme, rhythm, caesura (a pause inside a line) and enjambment (a sentence running past the line end) — in poetry they carry argument, not decoration.
connotation and denotation — denotation is the dictionary meaning; connotation is everything the word drags in with it. Analysis begins where the two diverge.
theme, concept and global issue — theme is what the text is about (jealousy, memory); concept is the angle you cut it with (perspective, transformation); a global issue is the version of it the world is still arguing about.
literary and non-literary text — a literary text is shaped primarily for its own aesthetic ends; a non-literary text is shaped to do something in the world. Both are analysed with the same five moves, and the difference changes which questions you ask first.
body of work — a group of texts studied together because they share author, form, period or preoccupation; the oral draws on it, and comparisons across it are what Paper 2 pays for.
The distinctionTerm ATerm BWhy the difference earns marks
Tone vs moodTone belongs to the writer's attitude.Mood belongs to the reader's experience."The tone is cold and the mood is dread" is two claims, not one, and each can be evidenced separately.
Symbol vs motifA symbol carries meaning beyond itself.A motif simply recurs.A recurring image is a motif; it becomes a symbol only when the text gives it a second meaning you can name.
Form vs structureForm is the kind of container (sonnet, act, chapter).Structure is the order of what is inside it."The poem's form promises a resolution; its structure withholds one" is a comparison, and comparisons score.

Upgrade the noun and the paragraph upgrades with it

Band 3 writes "the writer uses imagery to create a sad atmosphere". Band 7 writes "a single extended metaphor of domestic delivery — signed for, set down, unopened — turns grief into an object the narrator can avoid". Same observation. The difference is that the second names the type of image, the domain it comes from, and the consequence for the reader. Build a personal list of the twenty terms above and drill one a day until the precise word arrives before the vague one does.

Three errors that look like analysis and are not

"This is ironic." Irony is not the same as coincidence, misfortune or sarcasm; say which kind it is and who is left out of the knowing. "The writer uses imagery." Every writer uses imagery. Name the image, its domain, and what it makes you believe. "The narrator is unreliable." Unreliability is not a verdict you announce; it is a conclusion you support with the specific gap between what the narrator says and what the text shows. Announced without evidence, all three lose the mark they were meant to win.

5. The close-reading toolkit

Close reading is the only skill in this subject that is examined on material you have not prepared. It is therefore the highest-value thing you can practise, and it is also the thing most students never actually practise, because they confuse reading carefully with annotating productively. The difference is that productive annotation produces clusters you can build paragraphs out of.

5.1 The procedure

How to use it — seven passes, in this order
  1. Read once for gist, without a pen. One pass, no marking. You are establishing what kind of text this is and what it wants. Students who annotate on the first read annotate the first half brilliantly and the second half not at all.
  2. Box the shifts. Draw a vertical line wherever the tone, tense, addressee, or argument changes. Shifts are the skeleton of your essay: they hand you paragraph order for free.
  3. Circle the repetitions. Any word, image or structure that returns is a deliberate motif. Two returns is a pattern; three is an argument. Repetition is the most reliable evidence you will ever get, because it cannot be accidental.
  4. Star the odd word. The register break, the unexpected adjective, the colloquialism in a formal text, the Latinate word in a plain one. Oddness is always a choice.
  5. Arrow the end back to the beginning. Does the closing image answer the opening one? Circular structure is a two-minute paragraph and most students never look for it.
  6. Write the opposites in the margin. Public/private, then/now, us/them, control/chaos, near/far. The binaries you list are the raw material of a thesis.
  7. Group into clusters and give each a name. Three isolated images are three sentences. Three images from the same domain — say, heating and cooling — are a paragraph with a claim in it. Clustering is the step that converts observation into argument.

5.2 What you are looking for

Seven features, each with a definition you could say out loud under pressure. The first four appear on every paper; the last three are the ones students forget and examiners notice.

Diction
Vocabulary choice, read for register, connotation and word-class origin. Ask: is this word Latinate or Anglo-Saxon, technical or domestic, abstract or concrete? "The committee relocated the residents; the residents were moved out." Two dictions, one event — the first bureaucratically euphemistic, the second physical and involuntary. The clash is the point.
Imagery
Language that pictures something or maps one domain onto another — metaphor, simile, personification. Always ask what domain the imagery comes from, because the domain carries the attitude. "Grief arrived like a parcel — signed for, set down in the hall, and not opened for weeks." The domestic-absurd domain makes grief manageable, and therefore more damaging.
Syntax
Sentence length, order, subordination and punctuation as meaning. Short sentences accelerate or close down; long periodic sentences qualify and defer; asyndeton accumulates; anaphora builds rhythm; the passive deletes agency. "We waited. We waited in the rain. We waited until the rain became the only thing we were doing."
Structure
The order of parts, and the way that order manages what the reader knows and when. Openings establish a contract; endings answer openings; a withheld detail released late reorganises everything before it. Structure is not a container for meaning — in this subject it is a primary carrier of it.
Register
Level of formality, and the vocabulary, syntax and address that signal it. Register is social: it tells you who the writer thinks they are and who they think you are. A register break — a colloquialism in a formal document, a legalism in a personal letter — is always the most productive sentence on the page.
Modality
The degree of certainty or obligation a text encodes: must, may, should, might, it is essential that, there is no question that. Modality is where persuasion hides, because it commits the reader to a position without asking permission. "We must act now" and "we might consider acting" describe the same action and construct entirely different readers.
Point of view
Whose consciousness filters the text, and how much they know. First person gives intimacy and bias; free indirect discourse borrows a character's voice inside third-person narration; second person implicates the reader. "You have heard this story before. You have already decided she was asking for it." The second person converts the reader from judge into accomplice.
Common trap

Do not write "the writer uses imagery to create a vivid picture." Vivid is not an effect — it is a compliment. The effect is always a response in a reader: discomfort, complicity, false security, grief deferred. Name the response, then say who benefits from it. That is evaluation, and it is criterion B.

5.3 Three worked close readings

The three passages below were written for this site, so you may annotate, print and reuse them freely. Each worked example runs the full procedure: annotate, cluster, claim, then one paragraph.

Worked example 1 Non-literary — a charity campaign (original)

Passage.

Around the corner, someone is cold tonight. Not "somewhere in the city". Around the corner. You will walk past them. You will walk past them because that is what we do, and we have become extremely good at it. £4 pays for a bed. £4 is a coffee. We are not asking you to be a better person. We are asking you to be a slightly less efficient one. Original campaign copy written for this page

Step 1 — shifts. Two. The first block is spatial and descriptive; the second, from "You will walk past them", accuses. The third, from "£4", converts accusation into a transaction.

Step 2 — repetitions. "Around the corner" three times in two sentences. "You will walk past them" twice. "£4" twice. "We are asking you" twice. Four repetitions in eight sentences is not carelessness; it is a text built on parallelism.

Step 3 — odd words. "Extremely good at it" — a phrase of praise attached to an act of cruelty. "Slightly less efficient" — an adjective from operations management attached to morality.

Step 4 — clusters. (a) Proximity: around the corner, walk past. (b) Arithmetic: £4, a bed, a coffee. (c) Efficiency: good at it, better person, less efficient.

Step 5 — claim. The advert's central strategy is to make avoidance measurable: by replacing the abstract noun homelessness with a spatial phrase and then with a price, it converts a political condition into a transaction the reader is already equipped to perform.

Step 6 — the paragraph.

The copy's decisive move is lexical rather than emotional: it refuses the available abstractions. "Someone is cold" withholds the category entirely, while the rejected phrase "somewhere in the city" is placed in quotation marks and dismissed, so that the reader is denied the comfortable distance that statistical language provides. What replaces it is proximity — "around the corner", repeated three times in as many sentences — which relocates the problem from a social stratum to a place the reader physically passes. The tactic is completed by the arithmetic: "£4 pays for a bed. £4 is a coffee." The asyndetic pairing offers no comparison the reader can refuse, since the two clauses establish equivalence without ever arguing for it. Most strategically, the closing antithesis — "not a better person", only "a slightly less efficient one" — lowers the moral price of entry to almost nothing, and in doing so removes the reader's last available excuse, which was that virtue was being demanded of them.

Why this paragraph works: every sentence names a choice (withheld noun, quoted-and-rejected abstraction, spatial repetition, asyndetic equivalence, antithesis) and every choice is attached to an effect on a reader — denial of distance, refusal of comparison, removal of an excuse. No sentence paraphrases the advert. There are four quoted fragments and not one of them exceeds six words.
Worked example 2 Literary prose — a departure (original)

Passage.

The house had been sold in the morning and by evening my mother was still wiping the same shelf. She did it twice more before my father turned off the lights. Neither of them mentioned the keys. Outside, the new owner's car was already in the drive, ticking as it cooled, a sound so like a clock that for years afterwards I would wake at night believing something had been left running. Original prose written for this page

Step 1 — shifts. One large one: "Outside." The turn from the interior to the drive is also a turn from what the parents do to what the child hears, and it is the hinge of the passage.

Step 2 — repetitions. "Still", "twice more", "the same shelf" — repetition as stasis. Later, "cooling" and "clock" and "running" repeat the idea of a mechanism that continues on its own.

Step 3 — odd words. "The same shelf" — the definite article plus same turns a cleaning action into a loop. "Ticking as it cooled" — a car does not tick; a clock does. The simile arrives before the word that licenses it, which is why it reads as memory rather than as description.

Step 4 — clusters. (a) Mechanical continuation: sold, still, twice more, turned off, ticking, left running. (b) Withheld speech: "Neither of them mentioned the keys" — the one thing the passage refuses to say out loud.

Step 5 — claim. The passage renders grief as domestic machinery: by distributing the loss across objects that continue operating — a shelf being wiped, a car cooling — the narrator makes departure legible as a process the family cannot switch off.

Step 6 — the paragraph.

The passage's most consequential choice is grammatical. "The house had been sold" is passive, and the agent is withheld: no buyer, no decision, no one to blame. What follows is a sequence of small, repeated actions performed by people who are not discussing the event — "still wiping the same shelf", "twice more" — so that the scale of the loss is registered entirely through the disproportion between the event and the response to it. The sentence "Neither of them mentioned the keys" is the structural centre: it names an absence of speech as though it were an action, and it is the only sentence in the passage that tells the reader what the family is not doing. The turn outside then transfers agency from people to objects. The new owner's car "ticking as it cooled" is a simile the narrator has not yet earned, and the paragraph's final move — "a sound so like a clock that for years afterwards I would wake" — makes the comparison retroactive, locating the adult narrator inside the child's mishearing. Grief here is not described; it is installed as a mechanism that keeps running after everyone has stopped attending to it.

Why this paragraph works: it begins with a grammatical choice (the passive with a withheld agent) and moves through structure (the central negative sentence) to a temporal effect (the retroactive simile). It explains why the reader feels something rather than asserting that they do, and it never once uses the words "sad", "moving" or "effective".
Worked example 3 Poetry — an interview (original)

Passage.

They asked what I remembered. I said: the kettle,
the blue of it, the way it held the whole kitchen
in its one dull flank. They wrote that down.
They always write down the kettle. Nobody asks
about the door. Original poem written for this page

Step 1 — shifts. The volta sits on "Nobody asks" in line 4. Everything before it is compliant; everything after is a refusal.

Step 2 — repetitions. "They" four times; "the kettle" twice; "wrote down" / "write down". The repetition of they is the argument: an institution that asks and records.

Step 3 — odd words. "Dull" — an adjective of failure attached to the object of memory. "Flank" — a word for an animal's side, or a military exposure, applied to a kettle. "Held" — the kettle contains the kitchen, rather than the reverse.

Step 4 — clusters. (a) Officialdom: asked, wrote down, always write down. (b) Reversal of scale: the kettle holds the kitchen; the small object is the container. (c) Withheld object: the door, named last, unexplained.

Step 5 — claim. The poem argues that institutional memory is built from the details a speaker will volunteer, and that the detail which matters is the one the speaker cannot say — so the poem's form, which withholds "the door" until the final unstressed line, performs the failure it describes.

Step 6 — the paragraph.

The poem stages an interview, and its first structural choice is to make authority plural and procedural: "they asked", "they wrote that down", "they always write down". The anaphora reduces the interviewers to a function, and the shift from past to present tense in "They always write down" converts a single event into a standing practice. Against this, the speaker offers an object so minor that its colour is the first thing remembered — "the blue of it" — and then grants it an absurd scale: the kettle "held the whole kitchen in its one dull flank". That reversal is the poem's argument in miniature, since it insists that memory attaches to the object rather than the event, and the adjective "dull" concedes in advance that the attachment is unwarranted. The volta on "Nobody asks" is placed after a full stop and mid-line, so the refusal arrives without the rhetorical preparation a stanza break would have given it; and "the door" is withheld to the final line, unglossed, its grammar incomplete. What the poem refuses to explain — what passed through that door, or who closed it — is precisely the material that would be legible to an institution, and the poem's silence about it is the point.

Why this paragraph works: it treats form as argument — anaphora, tense shift, line-placed volta, terminal withholding — and it ties each formal observation to a claim about power. It also does the hardest thing in poetry analysis: it says something specific about the last line without inventing a meaning for it.

6. The analytical paragraph

A paragraph is the unit the examiner reads. Not the essay, not the sentence: the paragraph. A band-7 script is five or six paragraphs that each do the same five things in the same order, and a band-3 script is five or six paragraphs that each do something different.

How to use it — claim / evidence / commentary
  1. Claim — one sentence answering part of the question. No quotation yet. It must be arguable, meaning someone could deny it.
  2. Evidence — a short quotation or precise paraphrase, embedded grammatically into a sentence of your own. Two to eight words is usually enough. A quotation of more than about eight words is normally hiding the fact that you have not decided what it means.
  3. Technique — name it exactly. Not "language" but "asyndetic list", "free indirect discourse", "the passive construction", "a shift into the historic present".
  4. Effect on the reader — what it does to a reader, and why this technique rather than another. This is where band 7 is won and where most students stop one sentence early.
  5. Link to meaning — one clause returning to the thesis or the global issue, never "in conclusion" and never a summary of what you just said.

6.1 The same paragraph, weak and strong

Below is one idea — that Othello's collapse is registered in his language — written twice. Both paragraphs are about 100 words. They are not worth the same.

Band 3 versionBand 7 version
Shakespeare uses language in Othello to show how Othello changes throughout the play. At the start Othello speaks in a grand and impressive way, but later on he speaks more like Iago does. This shows that Iago has influenced him and that Othello is losing control. Shakespeare also uses imagery to make the play more dramatic and to show the audience how Othello is feeling. This is effective because it makes the audience feel sorry for him and it makes the ending more tragic. Overall, language is very important in this play. Othello's collapse is registered first as a collapse of register. In Act 1 he addresses the Senate in controlled, periodic sentences — "Her father loved me, oft invited me" — where the subordinate clause delays the main claim and displays a man confident of being heard. By Act 4 he is producing the broken, image-choked fragments Iago has been modelling since Act 2, the nouns arriving without verbs or syntax to hold them. The shift is not decorative: because the play has established eloquence as the basis of Othello's authority in Venice, the loss of syntax is the loss of the only power he had. Shakespeare makes the audience hear a man being unmade before he has done anything irreparable, which is why the murder reads as a consequence rather than a decision.
Why the second one scores
  1. The weak version is true and empty. Every sentence in it is defensible and none of them is at risk. "Language is very important in this play" could be said of any play. Criterion B rewards a claim that someone could deny.
  2. The weak version summarises; the strong version locates. "Speaks in a grand way" is summary. "Periodic sentences where the subordinate clause delays the main claim" is a location — an examiner could open the text and check it.
  3. The weak version uses "imagery" as a compliment. "Makes the play more dramatic" describes nothing. The strong version explains what the change of syntax costs the character within the play's own value system — eloquence is authority in Venice, so losing it is losing power.
  4. The weak version credits the author with an intention; the strong version credits the author with a mechanism. "Shakespeare uses language to show" is a claim about what a dead man wanted. "The play establishes X, so the loss of X means Y" is a claim about how a text works, and it can be argued with.
  5. The strong version ends with an effect on the audience that follows from the mechanism. "The murder reads as a consequence rather than a decision" is a genuine interpretive payoff, and it is the sentence that moves the paragraph from criterion B into the top band.
Trap — the four habits that keep a paragraph at band 3
  1. "This shows that…" as the analytical verb. It asserts a link without making it. Replace it with a mechanism: "because", "so that", "which means that", "thereby".
  2. Plot before point. A paragraph that begins by telling the reader what happens in the scene has spent its first forty words on criterion A when it needed them for criterion B.
  3. A quotation that is not in a sentence. Dropping a quote in with a colon and then starting a new sentence about it is not embedding. Integrate it: "the speaker's refusal to name the 'curfew' he has just imposed".
  4. Ending on a summary. If your last sentence could have been written before you read the text, it is not doing any work.

7. Themes, global issues and how to write about them without being vague

A theme is what a text is about — memory, honour, jealousy. A global issue is what a text is about that the world is still arguing about. The distinction matters because the individual oral requires a global issue and Paper 2 questions are usually framed around concepts rather than themes, and because "memory is unreliable" is not an issue: nobody disagrees with it.

How to use it — four checks for a global issue statement
  1. Is it arguable? State the opposite. If the opposite is nonsense ("war is bad"), you have a slogan. If the opposite is a position someone actually holds ("permanent digital records protect the vulnerable by making denial impossible"), you have an issue.
  2. Is it specific? "Gender inequality" is a category. "The assumption that a woman's account requires a man's corroboration before it counts as evidence" is an issue.
  3. Does it contain a mechanism? The best statements contain a because or a when: "communities that treat honour as public property produce violence that everyone can foresee and no one stops."
  4. Do both extracts genuinely engage it? Not "could be made to". If you have to strain to attach the non-literary text, change the text or the issue.

Culture, identity and community"Identity is important.""A community that defines honour as a collective asset will kill to defend it, and will describe the killing as unavoidable." Chronicle of a Death Foretold; The World's Wife
Beliefs, values and education"Religion affects people.""Scripture used as a source of narrative authority determines whose version of an event survives as history." The World's Wife; Othello

Exam technique

A global issue statement must be arguable, not a slogan. "Honour is important" is a bumper sticker. "Communities that prize collective honour over individual life produce violence that everyone can foresee and no one stops" is an issue, because someone could disagree. Test yourself: can you state the opposite? If the opposite is absurd, you have a slogan, not an issue.

One more practical rule. Write the global issue after you have chosen your extracts, never before. Students who begin with an issue go hunting for texts that fit it and end up with a forced pairing they cannot speak about for ten minutes. Students who begin with two extracts they know well can usually state an issue in ninety seconds, and it will be an issue the texts actually support.

8. Text types and bodies of work

The course is built on a deliberate mixture: literary works studied in depth, and non-literary texts studied for how they act in the world. "Text type" is not a filing label — each type brings a different set of questions, and knowing which questions belong to which type is a large part of what Paper 1 measures.

Literary — prose fictionWho narrates and how reliable are they? How is time ordered? What is withheld and until when?What does the narration gain by telling it this way? A narrator always has an interest.
Literary — dramaWho is on stage and who is addressed? What is verse and what is prose, and why? What do objects do?Performance: a line means one thing spoken and another overheard. Stage directions are authorial choices too.
Literary — poetryWhat does the form promise? Where is the volta? What is the relationship between line and sentence?What is the silent listener doing? In a dramatic monologue the addressee is the argument.

8.1 Building a body of work

Your learner portfolio is the collection of non-literary texts you gather over the two years. It is not formally submitted or marked on its own — but it is the raw material for the individual oral, which is 30% of the SL grade. A thin portfolio means a rushed, generic oral. A rich portfolio means you walk in with texts you already know how to analyse.

How to use it

Build it like this, and stop improvising in year two:

  1. Collect one text a week from the real world: a print or online advertisement, an opinion column, a speech, a charity campaign, a blog post, a government infographic, an Instagram or TikTok campaign, a memoir extract, a satirical piece. Screenshot or print it; keep the source and date — context is examinable.
  2. File it under a global issue from the five categories above. Ten texts, five categories, two each. That way any oral brief is already covered.
  3. Annotate it immediately: purpose, audience, tone, three authorial choices, and one line on what it assumes about the reader. Annotation memory decays fast — do it the day you find the text.
  4. Pair it with a literary work in your head. Every time you file a text, write one sentence: "This would sit next to ___ because ___." That sentence is a future oral.
  5. Keep it to 20–30 texts, not 100. Depth beats volume. You need texts you could speak about for five minutes without notes.
Connection

The portfolio is also your Paper 1 insurance. Analysing non-literary texts weekly is exactly the skill Paper 1 tests on an unseen passage. See Paper 1, and the eight original portfolio texts on the Individual oral page if you want a starter set.

10. Assessment criteria decoded

Four criteria, five marks each, twenty marks, on every component. The wording in the guide is dense; the table below translates it into the behaviour that actually moves you between bands.

CriterionBand 7 behaviourBand 3 behaviourWhere the marks are really lost
A — Knowledge and understanding Precise references — act and scene, poem title, the exact moment in the novella — woven into claims. Context attached to a choice in a single clause. Long plot summary at the start of each paragraph. Vague recall ("in the play, Iago tricks Othello"). Confuses the two works' plots. Students lose A by displaying knowledge rather than using it. A quotation that is not supporting a claim earns nothing.
B — Analysis and evaluation Technique → effect → meaning, every time. At least one point evaluates why this technique rather than another. Comparisons are of choices, not themes. Feature lists. "Both texts are about revenge." Effects named as compliments ("vivid", "effective", "powerful"). This is the criterion that separates 5 from 7. The missing sentence is always the one beginning "because".
C — Focus and organisation An arguable thesis in the first hundred words; both texts in every body paragraph; paragraphs that build on each other; a conclusion that states what the comparison revealed. Two halves — paragraphs 1–4 on text X, 5–8 on text Y — with one sentence of comparison at each end. No thesis, or a thesis that restates the question. C is almost purely mechanical and is therefore the cheapest criterion to fix. Most students never fix it.
D — Language Technical vocabulary used precisely; varied sentence length; comparative words ("whereas", "by contrast", "more radically") doing structural work. "Both texts… Also… Similarly…" repeated. Clunky sentences with ideas bolted on with "and". D is capped by unclear sentences, not by simple ones. A clean, plain sentence beats an ambitious broken one.
Trap — the five habits that cap a strong student at 5 or 6
  1. Feature-spotting. Naming techniques without effects. It reads as fluent and scores as band 3 on criterion B. Fix: forbid yourself from naming a technique unless the next word is "which" or "because".
  2. Two mini-essays. The block structure with a comparison only in the introduction and conclusion caps criterion C. Fix: both texts in every body paragraph — it is a rule, not a preference.
  3. Theme comparison without technique. "Both texts present strong women" is a theme. "Where Duffy gives her speaker the demotic register of the kitchen, García Márquez withholds his women's speech entirely" is a comparison of choices.
  4. Context as a paragraph. Biography before analysis. Fix: one clause, attached to a choice, maximum.
  5. Summary conclusions. A conclusion that restates the introduction earns nothing on C and wastes the last two minutes. Fix: state what the comparison revealed — the thing you did not know before you compared them.
The 5/6 ceiling, honestly

A 5/6 script is usually a script with a real argument, decent knowledge and one structural flaw: it describes rather than evaluates, or it keeps its two texts in separate rooms. Both flaws are mechanical and both are visible in a plan you wrote in eight minutes. This is why the highest-value revision activity in this subject is not reading — it is writing a plan, showing it to someone who knows the criteria, and rewriting the plan.

12. Practice

Eight questions, graded from conceptual to hard. Answer them in writing before you open anything — the answers below are only useful as a comparison against something you actually produced.

  1. Explain the difference between Language A: Literature and Language A: Language and Literature in one paragraph, and state which single piece of classroom evidence settles which course you are on.

    Show the answer

    Literature A studies literary works only and asks what a work means and how it functions as literature; Language and Literature studies literary works and non-literary texts and asks how any text, of any kind, acts on its reader. The practical consequence: in Language and Literature the corpus includes advertisements, speeches, opinion columns and infographics as objects of study, Paper 1 offers one literary and one non-literary unseen passage, and the individual oral requires a literary extract paired with a non-literary one.

    The decisive classroom evidence is the oral requirement. If your teacher is asking you to collect advertisements or opinion pieces and pair them with a set work, you are in Language and Literature. Confirm the subject code with your coordinator rather than inferring it from your reading list.

    Why this is worth knowing: students who write about one course as if it were the other produce essays that are fluent, well-evidenced and off-target — usually because they treat a non-literary text as though it had no audience and no purpose.

  2. Read the following sentence and identify the three most analytically productive choices in it: "The committee relocated the residents; the residents were moved out." Explain what each choice does.

    Show the answer
    1. The passive with a withheld agent. "Were moved out" deletes the people who did the moving. The effect is to make the event look like a process rather than a decision, which protects whoever made it.
    2. The register clash between two dictions for one event. "Relocated" is Latinate, abstract and bureaucratic; "moved out" is Anglo-Saxon, physical and directional. Placing them in parallel clauses makes the euphemism visible — the second clause is the translation the first clause was avoiding.
    3. The shift of grammatical subject. The first clause has the committee as subject and the residents as object; the second reverses it. The reversal gives the residents one clause of grammatical agency in a sentence about their removal, which is a small, bitter effect and entirely produced by syntax.
    Why this is worth knowing: one short sentence produced three separate paragraphs' worth of material. That is the density you should expect from any sentence an examiner has chosen to put in front of you.

  3. Rewrite each of the following so that it becomes an arguable global issue rather than a slogan: (a) "Memory is unreliable." (b) "Women have been treated badly throughout history." (c) "Social media affects people."

    Show the answer
    1. (a) "Because a written record outlives the people who made it, a private misunderstanding can harden into public evidence that its author never intended to produce." — contains a mechanism, and someone could defend the opposite.
    2. (b) "A canonical story told from the minor character's perspective changes the genre, not merely the sympathy — which suggests that authority over a narrative is a form of authority over the people in it." — specific, and it makes a claim that can be denied.
    3. (c) "Permanent digital archives protect the vulnerable by making denial impossible, at the cost of removing a person's ability to revise their own past." — note that this one states a trade-off, which is the most reliable way to make a statement arguable.
    Why this is worth knowing: the oral's criterion C is largely decided by this one sentence. A slogan costs you marks in the first sixty seconds and is almost impossible to recover later.

  4. The close-reading procedure in §5 lists seven passes. Which single pass most reliably produces a thesis, and why?

    Show the answer

    Step 7 — grouping observations into named clusters. Steps 2 to 6 all produce observations; only step 7 produces an argument. A single metaphor is a sentence; four metaphors from the same semantic field are a paragraph with a claim in it, because a cluster lets you say something about the text's system rather than about one instance.

    The runner-up is step 6, the binaries in the margin, because a thesis is almost always a claim about a tension between two terms — and once you have written "public/private" or "then/now" in the margin you have the shape of one. But the binaries are only raw material until a cluster organises them.

    Why this is worth knowing: students who skip step 7 produce essays that are a sequence of true observations with no thesis, which is exactly the profile of a band-3 script on criterion C.

  5. Explain how modality works as a persuasive device, using the difference between "you must register before the deadline" and "you may wish to register before the deadline".

    Show the answer
    1. "Must" is deontic modality of obligation. It encodes a rule and removes the reader's choice, which positions the writer as an authority and the reader as a subordinate. It also transfers responsibility: if you fail to register, that is now your failure.
    2. "May wish to" stacks two hedges. Permission plus desire. It preserves the reader's autonomy, which flatters them, but it also weakens the writer's own commitment — the same sentence now permits non-compliance without contradiction.
    3. The effect is on the relationship, not the action. Both sentences describe the same act. The difference is who is constructed as being in charge, and how much blame is available afterwards.
    4. Analytically: always ask what the modal verb lets the writer avoid. High obligation modals avoid negotiation; hedged modals avoid accountability.
    Why this is worth knowing: modality is the most frequently missed feature in the whole toolkit, and it is the fastest route to an original point in a non-literary Paper 1, where half the room will be writing about imperatives and rhetorical questions.

  6. A charity advert uses the second person throughout ("You will walk past them"). Explain the effect and name the risk the writer is taking.

    Show the answer

    Second person collapses the distance between the described situation and the reader's own body. The third person ("people walk past them") would let the reader classify the behaviour as someone else's. The second person makes the prediction specific and unfalsifiable in advance: it asserts what the reader will do, which is a form of accusation delivered as a forecast.

    The risk is resentment. Second-person accusation can produce defiance rather than compliance, because the reader has been convicted before being persuaded. Skilled campaign copy manages this by pairing the accusation with a concession that lowers the moral price of entry — "we are not asking you to be a better person" — which is why that sentence appears alongside the second person rather than instead of it.

    Why this is worth knowing: naming a risk rather than only an effect is what evaluation looks like. It is the difference between "this is effective" and "this works because it manages the resistance it creates".

  7. A student's Paper 2 has an introduction, four paragraphs on Othello, four paragraphs on The Sense of an Ending, and a conclusion that says "both texts show that memory is unreliable". Identify the criteria affected and the cheapest fix.

    Show the answer
    1. Criterion C is capped. The block structure with comparison only at the two ends is the classic band-3/4 shape. The examiner's test is mechanical: open any body paragraph — if only one work is in it, C is limited regardless of the quality of the writing.
    2. Criterion B is weakened. "Both texts show that memory is unreliable" is a theme comparison with no technique in it, and it is also not arguable.
    3. Criterion A is probably fine — the knowledge is there — which is why the script feels better than it scores.
    4. Cheapest fix: keep the same four points and rewrite each paragraph so that it makes one claim about both works, using a comparative conjunction doing structural work: "where Shakespeare withholds… Barnes over-shares…". Same evidence, same knowledge, different arrangement, and C moves by two bands.
    Why this is worth knowing: most 5-to-7 gains in this subject are rearrangements of material the student already had. This one is a twenty-minute rewrite, not a re-read.

  8. Take the opening and closing lines of any text you know and test for circular structure. Explain what you would need to find for it to be worth a paragraph, and what you would conclude if you found the opposite.

    Show the answer
    1. What to look for: a repeated image, word, or grammatical construction at the start and the end, where the second occurrence has changed meaning. A pure repeat is a frame; a transformed repeat is an argument.
    2. Worth a paragraph if: the transformation encodes the text's central change. In The Sense of an Ending, Part Two's return to the Part One material is not a repeat but a reversal, and that reversal is the novel's whole claim about self-knowledge.
    3. If you find the opposite — an ending that refuses to answer the opening — that is also a paragraph. A refusal is a choice: it usually signals that the text is denying the reader the closure the opening promised, which is itself a claim about the subject.
    4. The rule: never report a structural feature without saying what the reader is made to do or feel by it. "The text is circular" is description; "the circularity forces the reader to re-read the opening as a lie" is analysis.
    Why this is worth knowing: circular structure is a two-minute observation that most candidates never make, so it is one of the cheapest sources of an original point on a set-work question.
Vocabulary
unseen text — a passage you have not studied, printed in the exam and analysed under time.
guiding question — the question printed beneath a Paper 1 passage, which your commentary must answer.
learner portfolio — your collection of non-literary texts, gathered over two years and used for the oral.
global issue — a concern that matters beyond the page and is genuinely contested in the world.
authorial choice — a deliberate decision by the writer that a reader can locate in the words.
area of exploration — one of three angles of approach: time and space; intertextuality; readers, writers and texts.
concept — one of seven abstract ideas: identity, culture, creativity, communication, perspective, transformation, representation.
weighting — the share of the final grade a component carries.
annotation — marking a text for shifts, repetitions, odd words and clusters before you write.
comparative essay — a Paper 2 essay arguing one thesis across two studied works.
diction — word choice, read for register, connotation and origin.
register — level of formality, and what it implies about writer and reader.
modality — the degree of certainty or obligation a text encodes (must, may, should, might).
syntax — sentence structure: length, order, subordination and punctuation as meaning.
imagery — language that pictures something or maps one domain onto another.
point of view — whose consciousness filters the text, and how much they know.
volta — the turn or shift in a poem, often marked by a conjunction or a stanza break.
anaphora — the repetition of the same word or phrase at the start of successive clauses.
asyndeton — the omission of conjunctions between items in a list, producing accumulation.
antithesis — two contrasted ideas balanced in parallel grammatical structure.
tricolon — a group of three parallel items, used to suggest completeness.
free indirect discourse — third-person narration that borrows a character's voice and vocabulary.
dramatic monologue — a poem spoken by one persona to a silent listener we infer from what is said.
intertextuality — the way texts quote, answer, parody or rewrite each other.
re-vision — entering an old text from a new critical direction; changing the speaker changes the story.
cluster — a group of related observations that together support one claim.
bathos — a sudden drop from the elevated to the ordinary, often for comic or deflating effect.
band — a level in the markbands; bands 1–2 are weak, bands 6–7 are the top.
feature-spotting — naming techniques without explaining effects; a band-3 habit.
thesis — one arguable sentence stating the essay's claim, normally in the first hundred words.
