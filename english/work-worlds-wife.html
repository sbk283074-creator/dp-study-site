The World's Wife

Carol Ann Duffy, 1999. A collection of about thirty dramatic monologues spoken by the wives, mothers, sisters and abandoned women of myth, scripture, history and fairy tale — Mrs Midas, Mrs Aesop, Mrs Darwin, Frau Freud, Eurydice, Medusa, Pilate's Wife, Thetis, Queen Herod, Demeter. Every poem takes a story you already know and hands the microphone to the woman who was in it. That single decision is the whole book, and it is the most concentrated example of intertextuality you will study.

1. Author and context

Carol Ann Duffy (b. 1955) is a Scottish poet who grew up in industrial Staffordshire, became a major figure in late-twentieth-century British poetry, and was Poet Laureate from 2009 to 2019 — the first woman in the post's 341-year history. Her signature is the demotic voice: colloquial, funny, plain English used with technical sophistication. The World's Wife appeared in 1999, at the end of a decade in which feminist revisionist myth-making (Angela Carter's fiction, Marina Warner's criticism) was at the centre of British literary life.

Why this text matters

Duffy's method is re-vision — a term from the poet Adrienne Rich meaning "the act of looking back, of seeing with fresh eyes, of entering an old text from a new critical direction". It is not parody and it is not complaint. It is the claim that a story told from one angle is incomplete, and that changing the speaker changes the genre. For the IB's concept of creativity and the area of exploration intertextuality, this collection is the textbook case.

2. Form and structure

The dramatic monologue

A dramatic monologue is a poem spoken by a single fictional persona to a silent listener whose presence we infer from what the speaker says. Three consequences follow, and they are the three things you analyse:

  1. The speaker reveals herself unintentionally. Because she is speaking rather than writing, and because she is addressing someone, she gives away more than she intends. This is how dramatic irony is created in a poem.
  2. The listener is you. When Mrs Midas says "Believe me, I'd not wanted gold", she presupposes a doubting audience — and that audience is the reader. The reader is cast as the person who needs convincing, which is historically exactly right.
  3. The address can be the revenge. In several poems the husband is the silent addressee: for the first time in the story, he has to listen.

The volta

The volta (Italian for "turn") is the point at which a poem changes direction — in argument, tone, or emotion. In Duffy's monologues it is usually the moment the speaker stops narrating and starts claiming. Two rules for the exam: (a) locate the volta before you analyse anything, because it tells you what the poem is actually about; (b) say what the turn does, not that it exists. In "Mrs Midas" the turn converts comedy into a generalising statement about gender; in "Medusa" it converts pathos into threat.

Free verse, and why it matters

Duffy writes mostly in free verse with irregular line lengths, frequent enjambment, and caesura that mimics speech. This is a political choice as much as an aesthetic one: refusing the elevated, formally "poetic" register is a refusal of the register in which those myths were originally told. The plainness is the argument.

The sequence is itself an argument

Read the collection as one long poem, not thirty short ones. The ordering matters:

Opening
The "Mrs" poems establish the premise: ordinary domestic women attached to famous men — Midas, Aesop, Darwin. Comedy first, so the reader is disarmed.
Middle
The mythological and classical speakers — Circe, Eurydice, Medusa, Thetis, Demeter — deepen the register. The tone darkens; the losses are permanent rather than comic.
Later
The biblical and historical women — Queen Herod, Pilate's Wife, Mrs Pilate — bring questions of belief, judgement and testimony. The stakes shift from domestic to theological.
Close
"Demeter" and the seasonal poems end on loss and partial return — Circe and Demeter close the book with transformation and cyclical grief, denying the reader a triumphant ending.
Exam technique

The highest-value sentence you can memorise: the sequence is the argument — the collection accumulates voices until the absence of women's testimony in the original stories becomes impossible to ignore. Use it whenever a question asks about the collection as a collection, which is the one thing students forget to answer.

3. Plot, characters and themes

Poetry collections have no plot, but The World's Wife has a repeated narrative shape: a famous man does a famous thing; the woman beside him pays the domestic cost; she describes it in her own voice.

Type of speakerExamplesWhat she is doing
The "Mrs" — domestic witnessMrs Midas, Mrs Aesop, Mrs Darwin, Mrs Icarus, Frau Freud, Mrs Faust, Mrs SisyphusDeflating male genius by describing its household consequences.
The mythological womanEurydice, Circe, Medusa, Thetis, Demeter, CassandraReclaiming a story in which she was an object, a monster, or a victim.
The biblical and historical womanQueen Herod, Pilate's Wife, Salome, Mrs Lazarus, DelilahTestifying — often about not being believed.
The fairy-tale revisionLittle Red-Cap, Mrs Beast, The Kray Sisters Recasting a moral tale as a story of appetite and self-possession.
Voice and silence
The collection's central theme. Duffy's speakers had no lines in the original stories; the poems are the lines. Silence is not absence but something imposed, and the poems are the record of its removal.
Creativity and re-vision
Each poem is an act of rewriting an authority text — myth, Bible, history, fable. The concept of creativity here is not invention out of nothing but transformation of what already exists.
Power and the domestic
Grandeur is repeatedly brought into the kitchen. Bathos is the collection's signature weapon: the golden touch ruins the cooking; the theory of evolution is lived as a wife's loneliness.
Gender and representation
How women have been pictured — as monster, muse, witch, victim — and who did the picturing. Medusa, Circe and Salome are all cases of a woman demonised by a story told about her.
Knowledge and who holds it
Mrs Darwin knows what her husband's theory will cost; Pilate's Wife knows what her husband's judgement will cost. Women in these poems frequently know first and are ignored.

4. Language and style in detail

Bathos

A sudden drop from the elevated to the ordinary. The mythic golden touch becomes a ruined kitchen; the Trojan hero becomes a boy whose mother cannot bear to look. Effect: deflation of male grandeur, and a claim that the domestic is where consequences actually land.

Colloquialism and demotic diction

Contractions, everyday idiom, blunt monosyllables, and modern brand names placed against ancient material. Effect: the ancient is made present, and the reader cannot keep myth at a safe distance.

Enjambment and caesura

Lines run on so that speech feels spontaneous; mid-line pauses stage hesitation or a change of mind. Effect: the voice sounds spoken rather than composed, which is essential to the monologue's illusion of a real person talking.

Imagery of the body

Hair, hands, bellies, teeth, skin. Duffy repeatedly relocates myth in the physical: gold on skin, a snake's nest for hair, the weight of a child on a mother's arm. Effect: abstraction is refused, so the cost is always measurable.

Direct address and the imperative

"Believe me", "Look at me", "You'll see". Direct address recruits the reader as either the jury or the husband. Effect: complicity — we cannot read these poems as neutral observers.

Humour as strategy

The comic poems are the ones that hurt most, because laughter lowers the reader's guard before the turn. Never describe Duffy's humour as relief; describe it as delivery.

5. Every poem, with mini-analysis

Each entry gives you: what happens, the key technique, the effect, and how to use the poem. Learn four of these in real depth rather than skimming the rest — but know the shape of all of them.

PoemMini-analysis
Mrs Midas The wife of the golden-touch king narrates the domestic catastrophe: the pear tree, the cooking, the bathroom, and finally the banishment of her husband upstairs. Technique: bathos — the myth reduced to household inconvenience — plus the volta into a generalising statement about "a woman's lot". Effect: the myth is shown to be a husband's story with a wife's cost omitted. Use it for: gender, voice, bathos, the volta, domestic imagery.
Mrs Aesop The fabulist's wife is bored: he turns everything into animals and moralises, and she is left with the household. Technique: comic deflation and the domestic counter-example — the fables are exposed as a way of not dealing with an actual wife. Effect: wisdom literature is reframed as avoidance. Use it for: intertextuality, creativity, the authority to moralise.
Mrs Darwin The naturalist's wife endures the cold, the isolation and her husband's obsessive work; she is present at the moment of the theory and absent from its history. Technique: imagery of cold and distance, and the placement of domestic detail beside epochal discovery. Effect: the theory of evolution is shown to have a household cost borne by a woman whose name we do not know. Use it for: science and environment global issues, knowledge, silence.
Mrs Icarus A very short poem in which the wife watches the flight with flat common sense rather than awe; she knew it would end this way. Technique: monosyllabic diction and understatement; no elevated register at all. Effect: the heroic myth is punctured by the voice of the person who had to watch. Use it for: perspective, voice, the deflation of ambition.
Frau Freud The psychoanalyst's wife send-ups the theory from inside the marriage, turning interpretation against the interpreter. Technique: parody of a professional register and the domestic comic list. Effect: a theory that claimed to explain women is shown to have been built by a man his wife can read easily. Use it for: representation, power, comedy as critique.
Salome The dancer speaks with cool, professional appetite about the head on the platter, and she is entirely unrepentant. Technique: first-person confessional with deliberately matter-of-fact diction; the volta relocates the horror in the men who requested it. Effect: the femme fatale is re-authored as a working performer and a woman with agency. Use it for: representation, gender, the gaze.
Eurydice The dead wife speaks from the underworld and says, in effect, that she did not want to be rescued — Orpheus's love was another form of possession. Technique: dramatic monologue with a silent addressee she is refusing; negation and repetition ("the worst moment…"). Effect: one of literature's great love stories is exposed as a rescue the woman never asked for. Use it for: voice, silence, perspective, love and possession.
Circe The enchantress describes turning men into animals with a housekeeper's efficiency — it is work, not magic. Technique: the domestic register applied to sorcery; the poem ends on her solitude. Effect: the "witch" becomes a woman who has learned that company is dangerous. Use it for: representation, transformation, power.
Medusa The monster tells her own story: she was a woman with boyfriends, and the snakes and the stone came afterwards. Technique: the volta from pathos to threat, and the direct address to the reader. Effect: the reader is made to see that the monster was manufactured. Use it for: representation, violence against women, the gaze.
Thetis Achilles' mother speaks of the child she held and dipped and lost; the hero's invulnerability is described in terms of a mother's hands. Technique: tactile bodily imagery and a restrained, grieving tone with no consolation. Effect: epic heroism is reframed as maternal loss. Use it for: war, motherhood, the cost of male glory.
Queen Herod Herod's wife watches the dance and the men watching it, withering about appetite and power. Technique: dramatic monologue with an implied audience of men; irony and controlled, conversational scorn. Effect: the biblical episode is re-read as a story about who is really in charge. Use it for: beliefs and values, power, the gaze.
Pilate's Wife She dreams, she warns, she is ignored — and the poem gives her the words the Gospel never did. Technique: the dream as testimony, and the direct address to a husband who will not listen. Effect: the collection's purest statement of its thesis: women knew, and were not believed. Use it for: testimony, silence, belief, politics and justice.
Little Red-Cap The girl rewrites the wolf story as an awakening of her own appetite — for knowledge, for poetry, for the world. Technique: first-person retrospective with a volta from fear to self-possession; the closing images of flowers and singing. Effect: a cautionary tale becomes a coming-of-age. Use it for: intertextuality, identity, education and values.
Demeter The goddess of the harvest mourns her abducted daughter; the seasons are a mother's grief made meteorological. Technique: extended metaphor linking landscape and emotion, with a cyclical structure. Effect: myth is explained as psychology, and grief is given a permanent, recurring shape. Use it for: environment global issues, motherhood, transformation.
Anne Hathaway Shakespeare's wife answers the famous "second best bed" of his will by turning that bed into the true stage of their love — "a spinning world / of forests, castles, torchlight" — while the "best" bed hosted guests "dribbling their prose". Technique: a sustained metaphor of the bed as world and as page, plus dense sound-play (assonance, "my body now a softer rhyme / to his, now echo, assonance") and the closing image of the "casket of my widow's head". Effect: history's casual slight is reclaimed as a love-poem; the wife seizes the imaginative, sexual centre of a marriage the record reduced to an insult. Use it for: representation, love, creativity, voice, the recovery of the silenced historical woman.
Mrs Lazarus She stages spectacular public grief, then slowly lets the dead husband fade ("he was vanishing / to the small zero held by the gold of my ring") until she begins again with a "schoolteacher" — whereupon the husband returns from the grave and she recoils from his "rotting shroud". Technique: visceral bodily and grotesque imagery (clawed stones, "white femur," "gaunt nun"), a long diminuendo of grief into a "snapshot," and a shocking volta in which resurrection meets disgust, not joy. Effect: the Lazarus miracle is retold from the wife's side as a violation; her healed moving-on, not the resurrection, is shown to be real survival. Use it for: belief and religion, love and loss, the body, perspective, the deflation of miracle.
Mrs Sisyphus In furious rhyming couplets she rails at her husband's absurd, endless labour and her own abandonment — "feeling like Noah's wife" and "Frau Johann Sebastian Bach" while he gives "one hundred per cent and more to his work". Technique: comic, bathos-laden rhyme (irk / incenses / berk / dirk; cork / park / dork) that drags myth down to marital grievance. Effect: the existential hero becomes a selfish husband; grandeur is punctured by a witty, aggrieved female voice. Use it for: representation, gender, comedy as critique, the deflation of myth, work.
The Devil's Wife A five-part monologue (DIRT, MEDUSA, BIBLE, NIGHT, A) spoken by Myra Hindley's co-accused: the seduction by "the Devil," the loss of self ("I was the Devil's wife / which made me worse"), the incantatory denials of BIBLE ("Can't remember no idea not in the room"), and a fifty-year imprisonment ending "I will finally tell". Technique: a fractured persona shifting register across the sections — colloquial seduction, gothic Medusa imagery, repetition as defence, spare confession — and an explicit intertextual link back to "Medusa". Effect: the era's most notorious "monstrous" woman is given a voice that is both seductive and self-condemning, implicating the reader in the act of staring. Use it for: representation, power and corruption, the gaze, justice, intertextuality.
Penelope Odysseus's wife fills the waiting years with embroidery — "a lifetime's industry" — and unpicks her work each night to delay the suitors; she is "most certainly not waiting" but self-contained in her own art until he returns. Technique: the extended embroidery metaphor for narrative and time, precise domestic-craft imagery (cross-stitch, a thimble "like an acorn"), and the quiet subversion of the faithful-wife myth. Effect: Penelope is reclaimed as artist and agent who controls time and her own story, not a passive waitress for a hero. Use it for: representation, gender, creativity, time, the reclamation of the silent woman.

7. Possible global issues

  • Culture, identity and community: "The authority to narrate a shared story has historically determined whose experience counts as history."
  • Art, creativity and the imagination: "Rewriting an inherited story is not vandalism but completion — the original was never the whole account."
  • Beliefs, values and education: "Cultures that treat women's testimony as unreliable lose the knowledge those women hold."
  • Politics, power and justice: "Representation — how a group is pictured — determines how that group is treated."

8. How this work behaves in the exam

Paper 2

Best on questions about intertextuality, voice, gender, representation, register, creativity and power. It pairs superbly with Othello (language as power, silence versus speech) and with Chronicle (women reported on versus women speaking). Risk: do not write about "Duffy's opinions" — she has none in these poems. Every claim must be about a speaker's choices.

Individual oral

The best oral extract in any of your four works. A single monologue is usually well under 40 lines, has one voice, one listener and one volta — which is the ideal density. Even better: you may use two short poems if together they stay within the limit, which lets you build a comparison inside your literary extract.

How to use two poems in the oral
  1. Check the line count first. Two short poems like "Mrs Icarus" and "Pilate's Wife" may fit within 40 lines; two long ones will not. Count before you plan.
  2. Choose poems that share a mechanism, not a theme. "Both are about gender" is weak. "Both use bathos to deflate male authority, but one ends in comedy and the other in threat" is an argument.
  3. Spend roughly 60% of the time on the first poem. The second exists to confirm and complicate, not to repeat.
  4. Use the same bridge as always: "What links these two speakers is ___; what separates them is ___."
  5. Pair with a non-literary text that shares the mechanism. The museum speech or the "Who gets quoted?" infographic on the oral page are both ideal, because both are about who is permitted to be a source.

9. Six practice essay questions

  1. Discuss how Duffy uses the dramatic monologue form to give authority to previously silent figures.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: The monologue is the perfect vehicle for Duffy's project because it hands a woman the two things she lacked in the original story — a listener and a turn — and the silent addressee on the page is often the man who never asked her opinion.

    Paragraphs: (1) the silent listener and reader complicity; (2) direct address as the recovery of authority; (3) the volta as a seizure of the story; (4) unintentional self-revelation in "Mrs Midas" and "Medusa".

  2. Analyse the use of humour in the collection.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Comedy in The World's Wife is never relief; it is delivery. Duffy uses bathos and demotic diction to lower the reader's guard, and the poems that make you laugh hardest are the ones with the bitterest turn.

    Paragraphs: (1) bathos in "Mrs Midas"; (2) comic deflation of male expertise in "Mrs Aesop" and "Frau Freud"; (3) the volta that converts laughter into accusation; (4) the poems where comedy is abandoned altogether, and why.

  3. Explore the presentation of power in two or three poems.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Duffy relocates power from the public achievement to the domestic witness: the golden touch, the theory of evolution and the Trojan campaign are all re-described from the kitchen, where their cost was actually paid.

    Paragraphs: (1) "Mrs Midas" and the household cost of a wish; (2) "Mrs Darwin" and the isolation of the observer; (3) "Thetis" and the mother's loss as the true price of heroism; (4) the closing poems, where power is acknowledged but not forgiven.

  4. To what extent is the collection more than the sum of its poems?

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Substantially: the sequence accumulates voices across myth, scripture, history and folklore, and the accumulation is what converts individual complaint into a structural claim about who has been permitted to testify.

    Paragraphs: (1) the range of source texts; (2) the tonal arc from comedy to grief; (3) recurring motifs — transformation, silence, the body; (4) the ending's refusal of triumph.

  5. Compare the presentation of women's voices in The World's Wife and one other studied work.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Pair with Othello: Shakespeare withholds Desdemona's account almost entirely and kills her mid-sentence, while Duffy's whole collection consists of women who were only ever described — so the same political claim is made by enforced silence in one text and by chosen speech in the other.

    Paragraphs: (1) Desdemona's shrinking share of the lines; (2) Emilia and the cost of speaking; (3) "Mrs Midas" and "Pilate's Wife" as the recovery of testimony; (4) which strategy implicates the reader more.

  6. Discuss how Duffy uses intertextuality to question who owns a story.

    Show model thesis and plan

    Thesis: Every poem assumes a source text the reader already knows, and then supplies the half that was missing — so intertextuality here is not decoration but the engine: the reader's prior knowledge is the thing being corrected.

    Paragraphs: (1) the "Mrs" poems and inherited male genius; (2) myth — Circe, Medusa, Eurydice; (3) scripture — Pilate's Wife, Queen Herod; (4) fairy tale — Little Red-Cap, and the moral that changes.

Connections — which pairings work, and why
  • → Othello (strongest pairing): both treat speech as authority. Shakespeare dramatises eloquence being stolen from a man; Duffy dramatises it being seized by women. Use for any question on language, power, silence, register or gender — and always compare form (verse drama versus free-verse monologue).
  • → Chronicle of a Death Foretold: both texts are about who is permitted to speak. Angela Vicario is reported on; Duffy's wives report. Excellent for gender and representation — but anchor every claim in technique, or the essay becomes a general opinion about patriarchy.
  • → The Sense of an Ending: the pairing for intertextuality and the authority to narrate. Barnes gives the revision to a narrator who does not realise he is revising; Duffy gives it to speakers who know exactly what they are doing. Use the contrast between dramatic irony and deliberate testimony.
  • → Paper 1: "Clearing Out" (Text B on that page) is a domestic monologue-with-a-volta in the Duffy mode. If you can analyse it, you can analyse any poem in this collection.
  • → Individual oral: pair "Mrs Midas" with the museum speech, or "Pilate's Wife" with the "Who gets quoted?" infographic. Both non-literary texts are about who counts as a source.
