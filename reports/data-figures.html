Data, tables & figures

Tables and graphs are not decoration — they are the evidence your argument rests on. A reader trusts a report whose data is presented cleanly and doubts one whose graph has no units. This page is the communication side of data: how to build tables and figures that argue for you. The measurement maths (uncertainty, propagation, best-fit lines, significant figures) is owned by the Physics IA page; here we handle how you show it.

1. Tables — units in the header, not the cells

A table's job is to let the reader find a value and trust it. Two rules dominate:

  • Put the unit in the column (or row) header, never repeated in every cell. A column headed "Time / s" with entries "0.0, 1.2, 2.4" is correct; a column of "1.2 s, 2.4 s" is noisy and error-prone.
  • Align numbers on the decimal point (or at least right-align them) so trends and magnitudes are visible at a glance. A ragged left edge hides a ten-fold jump.

Keep raw data and processed data in separate tables (or clearly labelled sections): the measurements as taken, then the calculated quantities. State the number of significant figures or decimal places consistently — a column mixing "0.90" and "1.273" looks careless even if the values are right.

2. Graphs — the axis is a sentence

A graph that cannot be read is worse than no graph. The conventions:

  • Label both axes with quantity and unit: "Period T / s", not "T" and not "seconds". The unit belongs with the quantity, with a slash.
  • Choose the right plot for the relationship. If theory predicts y = a·xⁿ, plot log y vs log x to get a straight line of slope n (log–log), or plot y vs xⁿ directly if n is known. Fitting a straight line to curved data and calling it linear is a classic error.
  • Best-fit, not connect-the-dots. Draw the line or curve that minimises scatter; the individual points stay visible. State the gradient and its units, and compare it to the theoretical value.
  • Error bars where they matter. If you treated uncertainty (and you should), show it on the graph. A best-fit line should pass through or near the error bars, not just the central points.
  • Caption below, reference in text. "Figure 1 shows the period increasing with √L" — the figure is evidence you cite, not a illustration you drop in.
Classic mistake

An axis reading "Time" with no unit, or a graph titled "Graph 1" with no caption. The reader should be able to reconstruct what was plotted from the figure alone. No unit on an axis is an automatic "imprecise communication" flag in the IB Data Analysis criterion.

3. Significant figures & rounding — a communication act

How many digits you write is itself a claim about precision. The full rules (and why they are claims) are on the Physics IA page; the writing-relevant summary:

  • Quote uncertainty to one significant figure (sometimes two if the first digit is 1). "±0.03", not "±0.0341".
  • Match the decimal places of the value to the uncertainty. If you write "9.6 ± 0.3", both stop at the tenths; "9.62 ± 0.3" over-claims precision on the value.
  • Be consistent within a column. A results table where every entry shows the same number of decimal places reads as careful, even before the reader checks a number.
  • Don't round mid-calculation. Keep guard digits; round only the reported final value. Rounding too early is a silent source of error.
Why this is writing, not arithmetic

An uncertainty of "±0.03 s" tells the reader "I trust this to the hundredth of a second." Writing "±0.0341 s" claims microsecond trust you don't have, and writing "±0.03" next to "9.623" contradicts yourself. The digit count is part of the message; getting it right is part of writing clearly.

4. Choosing the right visual

Don't reach for the first chart type. Match the visual to the job:

To show…Use…Why
A trend or relationship between two continuous variablesA scatter + best-fit lineReveals slope, scatter, whether linear.
Summarised values in rows/columnsA tablePrecise lookup; better than a bar for exact numbers.
Parts of a whole (percentages)A pie or stacked barShows proportion at a glance.
Comparing magnitudes across categoriesA bar chartEasy magnitude comparison.
A model fit or theoretical curveA line/curve on the scatterLets data and theory sit on one axes.

5. Diagrams & apparatus sketches

For a lab report, a labelled apparatus diagram often communicates the method faster than prose. Rules: original (don't scan the manual), labelled with part names and the measured quantities marked, and referenced from the Methods ("Figure 2 shows the setup; the ruler measured the drop height h"). A figure you never mention in the text is dead weight.
