Annotated model excerpts

The fastest way to internalise scientific style is to see a weak sentence next to a strong one and know why the second wins. These pairs are illustrative and transferable — they are not tied to any particular experiment or investigation, and they are not past examination responses. Each is tagged by the craft move it demonstrates, drawn from the pages on format, craft and math reports.

How to read these

For each pair, the weak version shows a common student habit; the strong version shows the fix and the craft move it demonstrates. The moves are the same ones the checklists on the toolkit page test for.

1. Methods — reproducible, not a story

Weak

"We did the experiment carefully and made sure to record all the results properly so that we could analyse them later."

Strong reproducibility

"A 0.20 m pendulum bob was released from 10° and the time for ten oscillations was measured with a stopwatch (±0.01 s); the mean of five repeats gave the period. The length was varied from 0.20 m to 1.00 m in 0.10 m steps, and the string mass was kept below 1% of the bob mass."

Why: the weak version carries zero reproducible information. The strong version names apparatus, precision, range, interval, repeats and a control — everything the reader needs.

2. Results — state the trend, don't interpret it

Weak

"As you can see from the graph, the results went up nicely and this proves the theory works perfectly with no problems at all."

Strong objective evidence

"As shown in Figure 1, the period increased monotonically with length; a plot of T against √L (Figure 2) is linear with gradient 2.01 ± 0.04 s m⁻¹/² and R² = 0.997, indicating a relationship of the form T ∝ √L."

Why: the weak version interprets inside Results ("proves… perfectly") and uses vague scale-words ("nicely", "up"). The strong version reports the trend and the fit quantitatively, saving judgement for Discussion.

3. Discussion — the "so what" with limits

Weak

"The experiment was mostly successful and the graph looked like it was supposed to. There were probably some errors but overall it worked and the answer is correct."

Strong interpret + compare + limit

"The gradient, 2.01 ± 0.04 s m⁻¹/², agrees with the theoretical 2√(L/g) prediction of 2.01 within uncertainty, supporting the simple-pendulum model. The 1% shortfall is consistent with the neglected bob-size and air-resistance terms; the largest deviation occurred at the longest length, where swing amplitude drifted above the 10° small-angle limit. Repeating with a photo-gate and a smaller release angle would tighten both uncertainties."

Why: the weak version asserts success with no evidence and no limits. The strong version states the finding, compares to theory with numbers, names a specific limitation and its effect, and proposes a tied improvement.

4. Conclusion — answer the question, not a summary

Weak (a summary)

"In conclusion, we measured the period at different lengths, made a graph, and discussed what we found. The experiment taught us about pendulums."

Strong direct answer

"Therefore, within ±0.04 s m⁻¹/², the period of a simple pendulum is proportional to the square root of its length, T = 2π√(L/g), consistent with theory; the small systematic shortfall at long lengths is explained by the breakdown of the small-angle approximation."

Why: the weak version recaps the procedure. The strong version answers the research question directly and states the degree of certainty and the one caveat — the reader leaves with the answer.

5. Math reflection — specific, not generic

Weak

"This exploration helped me learn a lot about mathematics and I now understand modelling better than before."

Strong specific reflection

"The logistic map reproduced the qualitative outbreak curve but overestimated peak size because it assumes a closed, uniformly-mixing population. An SIR compartment model would let recovery rate enter — the main limitation of my approach — and is the natural extension."

Why: the weak version could be written by anyone about any topic. The strong version names an assumption, its consequence, and a concrete alternative — that is reflection, not summary.

6. One-sentence test

If you can apply this test to any sentence, most weak habits disappear:

The "could a stranger repeat / check this?" test

For a Methods sentence: could a competent student reproduce it from your words alone? For a Results sentence: is this an observation, not a judgement? For a Discussion sentence: does it say so what, not just what? For a Conclusion sentence: does it answer the question, not recap the report? If the answer is no, rewrite.
