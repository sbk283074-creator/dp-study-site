Scientific Report Writing · Lab reports

The lab report format, section by section

The experimental lab report follows a form called IMRaD — Introduction, Methods, Results, And Discussion — usually wrapped in a Title and (sometimes) an Abstract, and closed with a Conclusion, References and an Appendix. This page walks each section: its job, what belongs in it, what a strong version looks like, and the mistake that sinks it. The physics-specific maths (uncertainty, significant figures, graph-fitting) is owned by the Physics IA page; here we handle the writing.

Two rules that hold for every section

(1) Keep evidence and judgement apart. Results is for what happened; Discussion is for what it means. The moment you interpret inside Results, the reader cannot tell observation from opinion. (2) Write for the sceptic who wasn't there. Every section should make sense to someone who has your background but none of your memories of the afternoon in the lab.

1. Title

A good title names the system and the relationship, not the procedure. "The effect of temperature on the coefficient of restitution of a tennis ball" tells the reader the independent variable, the dependent variable and the context. "Physics IA" or "Lab 4" tells them nothing. Avoid verbs in the title; a noun phrase is conventional and scannable.

2. Abstract often optional

A self-contained synopsis, written last, usually 100–300 words. It touches every section but weighs Results and Conclusion heaviest: one sentence of context, one of method, two or three of key findings (with the actual numbers), one of what it means. A reader who only reads the abstract should leave knowing the answer. If your investigation does not require an abstract (many IB write-ups don't), skip it rather than writing filler.

3. Introduction — set up the question and the physics

The introduction moves from broad context to your specific question. It should contain:

  • Context & significance — why this phenomenon is worth a study (one or two sentences of background).
  • The physics / theory you will use — the equation or principle that predicts the relationship, stated and, if needed, derived or referenced. This is where you show the reader the lens.
  • The research question — a focused question, ideally "How does X affect Y in system Z?" with X and Y named as measurable variables.
  • Hypothesis / expectation (where appropriate) — what the theory predicts will happen, so the discussion can later say whether data agreed.
Classic mistake

Writing the introduction as a personal narrative ("In this lab we learned about…"). The introduction is about the science, not your learning journey. State the principle, state the question, state the prediction.

4. Methods — make it reproducible

This is the section examiners weight most heavily for "could someone repeat this," and it is where students lose the most easy marks. Write it in the past tense and in enough detail that a competent student, given your school's equipment, could reproduce the investigation.

ElementWhat to include
Apparatus & materialsSpecific equipment, with the precision that matters (e.g. "ruler graduated to 1 mm", "stopwatch ±0.01 s"). Include manufacturer/model only if it affects the result.
ProcedureSteps in chronological order, concise but complete. State how each variable was measured and controlled, not just "we measured it."
VariablesIndependent, dependent, and the controlled variables — and how each was controlled. "Type of material" is a weak independent variable; "thickness of material in mm" is strong.
Range, interval, repeatsHow many values of the independent variable, how far apart, and how many repeats per point. This is what lets the analysis claim a trend rather than a fluke.
Safety / ethical / environmentalAny risk and how it was managed. Required by the IB Research Design criterion.
Classic mistake

"We did the experiment carefully and recorded the results." That sentence carries zero reproducible information. Name the apparatus, the ranges, the controls and the number of repeats. The method is not a story; it is a specification.

The uncertainty maths behind "±0.01 s" and how to propagate it belongs on the Physics IA page — here, just make sure you state the uncertainties you used.

5. Results — show what happened, objectively

Results presents findings with no interpretation. Use tables and figures as the evidence, then write a sentence or two pointing the reader to the key trend — without explaining why yet (that is Discussion).

  • Present raw data first (the measurements as taken), then processed data (calculated quantities).
  • Refer to every table and figure in the text: "As shown in Table 1, the period increases with length…" — never just drop a figure in with no sentence linking to it.
  • State anomalies or outliers you saw, factually, without excusing them.
  • Write in the past tense. Keep judgements ("this proves…") out.
Classic mistake

Mixing Results and Discussion: "The graph shows a clear linear relationship, which means the model is correct and the experiment worked perfectly." The first clause is Results; the second is Discussion. Split them. Results says what the graph shows; Discussion says what it means.

6. Analysis — turn data into meaning

Analysis is where raw numbers become an answer. It sits between Results (the data) and Discussion (the interpretation):

  • Calculations — show the working for at least one representative row (don't paste 40 identical sums; show the method, tabulate the rest).
  • Fitting & comparison — best-fit line or curve, slope with units, comparison of your gradient to the theoretical value.
  • Uncertainty treatment — propagate uncertainties, quote the final value as result ± uncertainty with consistent significant figures. This is the IB Data Analysis criterion made visible.

The actual propagation rules, significant-figure conventions and best-fit-line method are on the Physics IA page. In your report, the job is to present this treatment clearly so the reader can follow from raw data to your quoted result.

7. Discussion — the "so what"

The discussion is the heart of the argument. A strong discussion, in order:

1
State the main finding. One sentence: what the data showed (e.g. "The period increased with the square root of length, consistent with the simple-pendulum model").
2
Interpret it. Why does it make sense physically? Connect back to the theory from the Introduction.
3
Compare to expectation / published values. Does your gradient match the theoretical prediction within uncertainty? If not, why might that be?
4
Address limitations. Name specific weaknesses (control of X, precision of Y, range of Z) and say how each specifically affected the data — not "there were some errors."
5
Suggest realistic improvements. Tied to the weaknesses you named. Generic "use better equipment" scores nothing; "replace the manual stopwatch with a photo-gate to reduce the ±0.01 s reaction uncertainty" does.
Classic mistake

Ending the discussion at "the experiment was successful." Successful compared to what? The discussion must weigh the evidence against the prediction and against the limitations. A result that disagrees with theory is not a failure — it is a discussion waiting to be written, provided you explain why the disagreement is plausible.

8. Conclusion — answer the question, directly

The conclusion is short and answers the research question set in the Introduction. It is not a summary of the whole report. A strong conclusion:

  • Restates the answer to the research question, supported by the evidence.
  • Compares the finding to the accepted scientific context (the theoretical value or published result).
  • Acknowledges the degree of certainty (within uncertainty, the claim holds; or it does not, and here is the likely reason).
Conclusion ≠ summary

A summary says "we did X, found Y, discussed Z." A conclusion says "Therefore, within ±…, the period does depend on the square root of length as the model predicts, and the 4% shortfall is explained by…". If you can delete the paragraph and the reader loses no answer, it was a summary, not a conclusion.

9. References & Appendix

References give traceable credit for any theory, published value or method you borrowed — textbooks, notes, data sources. The IB Conclusion criterion explicitly wants comparison to an "accepted scientific context," which means you must cite where that context came from. The Appendix holds raw data sheets, long calculation tables, or code that would interrupt the flow if inline. Cite appendices from the body so the reader knows they exist.
