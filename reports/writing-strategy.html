Scientific Report Writing · Craft

The craft of scientific writing

The structure gets your report read; the style gets it believed. Scientific writing has a few hard conventions — voice, tense, hedging, precision — that separate a report that reads like science from one that reads like a diary. This page is the style manual. Most of it transfers word-for-word from a Year-12 physics write-up to a university paper.

Voice & tense Hedging Precision & banned words Paragraph logic

1. Voice — active, and first person is allowed

For decades scientific writing hid the author in the passive voice ("the sample was heated"). That habit is loosening: most guides now prefer the active voice because it is clearer and shorter, and the IB and most journals accept "we" for what you did. Use the active voice as your default:

Weaker (passive / roundabout)Stronger (active)
"The two solutions were mixed and a blue colour was observed.""The mixture turned blue."
"A needle valve was used to regulate the pressure.""A needle valve regulated the pressure."
"No evidence was found that A caused B.""A did not cause B."

Passive is still fine when the actor doesn't matter ("Mistakes were made" shifts focus to the mistake) or when your field's convention expects it. The rule is: be consistent and be clear. "We measured the period using a stopwatch" is perfectly acceptable; "The period was measured" is also fine. What reads badly is a sentence with no visible actor and three "-ing" words.

2. Tense — past for what you did, present for what is true

Tense carries meaning in a report. Get it wrong and you imply the experiment is still running, or that your result is a law of nature. The convention:

SectionTenseExample
MethodsPast (you did it)"We recorded the time for ten oscillations."
ResultsPast (what you found)"The gradient was 9.6 m s⁻²."
Introduction — established factPresent (always true)"The period of a simple pendulum is proportional to √L."
Introduction — prior studyPast (they did it)"Smith (2021) showed that…"
Discussion — interpretationPresent (what the data now means)"These results suggest the model holds."
Classic mistake

Writing the whole report in the present tense ("We measure the period and find it increases"). You didn't — you measured it. Past tense for your actions; present tense for facts and your interpretations of them.

3. Hedging — claim exactly as strongly as the evidence allows

Science rarely proves; it finds evidence consistent with a claim. Hedging is not weakness — it is intellectual honesty, and examiners reward it. The move is to scale your verb to your confidence:

shows → suggests / indicates proves → is consistent with / supports definitely → appears to / may clearly → the data indicate
The transformation

Too strong: "This experiment proves that air resistance is negligible."
Appropriately hedged: "These results are consistent with air resistance being negligible over the range tested, though the 3% deviation at high speed suggests a small residual drag."
The hedged version is more precise, not weaker: it states what was actually shown and where the limits are.

Two cautions. Don't over-hedge into mush ("it might possibly somewhat suggest") — hedging should still commit to a reading. And never use hedging to hide a bad result; if the data contradict the theory, say so plainly and explain why, rather than burying it in qualifiers.

4. Precision — specific numbers beat adjectives

Scientific writing replaces vague scale-words with measurements. "The reaction rate increased at higher temperatures" is an opinion; "the rate doubled between 40 °C and 60 °C" is a claim a reader can check. A set of words is effectively banned unless you attach a number or context:

verybig / smalllarge / little good / badbetter / worsereally / quite believe / thinkgot / gethigh / low (alone)

Other precision habits:

  • Space between number and unit: "5 m s⁻¹", "22.4 g mol⁻¹", "43 kg" — never "5m/s". IUPAC convention, and examiners notice.
  • Distinguish "significant" (statistical) from "important" (judgement). A "significant" result in science often means a statistical test; if you mean "important," say "important."
  • data is plural ("the data are"), criteria is plural ("criterion" is singular).
  • Avoid vague pronoun starters: don't open a sentence with "This…", "These…" or "It…" where the noun is unclear. Name the thing: "This deviation was caused by…".
  • Cut filler phrases: "It is shown that", "It can be noticed that", "It should be noted that", "Based on our experiments" — delete them; the sentence survives and reads better.

5. Paragraph logic — claim first, support after

Each paragraph should open with a topic sentence stating the point, then spend the rest supporting it with evidence or reasoning. The reader should get the claim in the first line, not the last. One paragraph = one claim; if you feel two ideas fighting for the opening sentence, you have two paragraphs.

The discussion paragraph shape

Topic sentence (the claim): "The measured gradient agrees with the theoretical prediction within uncertainty." → Evidence: "Our value of 9.6 ± 0.3 m s⁻² differs from 9.81 by 2%, inside the combined uncertainty." → Interpretation: "The small shortfall is consistent with air resistance and the finite pendulum-string mass, both neglected in the ideal model." Three sentences, one idea, claim front-loaded.

6. The discussion move, written out

If you only remember one thing about style, remember the discussion arc: find → interpret → compare → limit → improve. State the finding, explain why it makes physical sense, compare it to what theory predicted, name the specific limitations and their effect, then propose realistic fixes. This single shape turns a weak "the experiment worked" into a scoring discussion. (See Lab report format §7 for the step-by-step version.)

The examiner's view

Several of these pitfalls — unclear method, no uncertainty, overclaiming, generic evaluation — appear in the "examiner's most common complaints" list on the Physics IA page. Style is not separate from scoring; Communication is now embedded in every IB Physics criterion, so a clear, precise, honestly hedged report is a directly better one.
