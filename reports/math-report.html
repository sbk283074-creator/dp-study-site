The mathematical report

A mathematical report — the IB Math exploration is the canonical example — is a different animal from a lab report. There is usually no experiment to describe and no hypothesis to confirm. Instead you build an argument: you introduce a question, develop or apply mathematics, and reflect on what it showed and its limits. This page is the writing craft for that shape; the IB-specific rules (the five criteria, topic ideas, marking traps, timeline) live on the Math exploration page.

What changes versus a lab report

In a lab report the evidence is measured data and the spine is IMRaD. In a math report the "evidence" is a derived result, a worked case, or a model fit, and the spine is rationale → aim → method → development → analysis → conclusion → reflection. There is no separate "Results" you can fence off from "Discussion" — the mathematics is both. And reflection is an assessed component unique to the exploration, not an optional afterthought.

1. How a mathematical report is different

Lab report (Family A)Math report (Family B)
Evidence = measurements you took.Evidence = derivations, proofs, computations, model fits.
Method describes apparatus & procedure.Method describes which mathematics you used and why.
Uncertainty is physical (instruments).Uncertainty is about assumptions, scope, numerical error.
Discussion weighs data vs theory.Analysis is the argument; reflection weighs the math itself.
No separate "reflection" criterion.Reflection is explicitly assessed (Criterion D).

2. A workable structure

The exploration does not have a mandated section list, but a clear shape helps both you and the reader:

1
Introduction / rationale. Why this topic? What gap or curiosity motivated it? Show the reader the hook in one or two sentences — this is where personal engagement lives.
2
Aim / research question. A precise mathematical question, not "I will explore calculus." E.g. "To what extent can the logistic map model the outbreak size of a simplified epidemic?"
3
Plan / methodology. Which tools (calculus, a recurrence, software), definitions and theorems you will use. State assumptions explicitly — they become the limits you reflect on later.
4
Development (the body). Definitions, lemmas, derivations, worked examples. Build from simple to complex; each step should follow from the last.
5
Analysis. Apply the mathematics to cases, generate graphs or data, test the model against a known result or boundary condition.
6
Conclusion. What the mathematics established about the aim.
7
Reflection. What you learned, the limitations of your approach, alternative methods, extensions. This is a distinct, scored section — see §4.

3. Presenting mathematics so it communicates

The single biggest math-report failure is writing algebra with no words. Mathematics is a language; the reader needs you to translate it. Habits that mark a strong report:

  • State definitions before you use them. "Let f(x) = …" with a sentence of what it represents. Don't assume the reader infers your notation.
  • Number your equations so you can refer back ("substituting (3) into (5)…"). Unnumbered walls of working are unnavigable.
  • Explain the intuition, not just the steps. After a derivation, one sentence on what it means physically or geometrically. "This shows the fixed point is unstable when r > 3" is worth more than the algebra alone.
  • Use graphs and software purposefully. A plot that reveals a bifurcation is evidence; a plot you pasted because "the exploration needs a graph" is decoration. Say what the graph shows.
  • Don't let the computer think for you. If you used a CAS or simulation, show the setup and interpret the output. "Wolfram said 0.739" is not mathematics; "the fixed point solves x = r·x(1−x), giving x* = 1 − 1/r ≈ 0.739 for r = 3.5" is.
  • Consistent, clean notation. One symbol, one meaning. Define it once.
Classic mistake

A report that is 80% typed equations and 20% disconnected commentary. The exploration is assessed on mathematical communication (Criterion B) — the words are not filler around the math, they are the proof that you understand it. Every non-trivial step needs a sentence.

4. Reflection — the part most students underwrite

Reflection (Criterion D) is where you step back from the calculation and think about the mathematics: what worked, what was limited, what you'd do differently, what you learned. A weak reflection is generic ("I learned a lot about math"). A strong one is specific:

Weak → strong reflection

Weak: "This exploration taught me that modelling is hard and I should practise more."
Strong: "The logistic map captured the qualitative outbreak shape but overstated peak size because it assumes a closed, uniform population — relaxing that (e.g. an SIR compartment model) would let the recovery rate enter, which my model cannot represent. The assumption of discrete, non-overlapping generations is the main limit on applying this to a real epidemic."
The strong version names the assumption, the consequence, and a concrete alternative. That is reflection, not summary.

5. Common math-report pitfalls

PitfallFix
Textbook regurgitation — re-deriving standard results with no personal angle.Start from a question the textbook didn't ask; apply known math to a case of your choosing.
No reflection, or generic reflection.Dedicate a real section to specific limitations and alternatives (§4).
Weak personal engagement.Show genuine choice and initiative in the topic and method, not a borrowed RQ.
Unexplained jumps ("therefore X").One sentence of reasoning between every non-trivial step.
Over-reliance on software output.Show setup + interpretation; the machine is a tool, not the argument.
Math that is too easy or too hard for the level.Pick mathematics that stretches you but that you can fully explain.
