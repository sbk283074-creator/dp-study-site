Scientific Report Writing · Overview

Writing up science clearly, persuasively, reproducibly

This section is about writing, not about the exam. It drills the transferable skill of turning an experiment or a mathematical investigation into a report a reader can follow, trust and repeat — how to structure the standard sections, how to phrase a method so someone else could replicate it, how to discuss results without overclaiming, and how to present data as an argument rather than a dump. The training ground is the IB Physics scientific investigation and the Mathematics exploration, because that is what you actually write, but the craft transfers to any lab or modelling report in any science.

1. The two report families you will actually write

Almost everything called a "scientific report" in the Diploma is one of two shapes. Knowing which one you are writing changes what goes in each section.

Family A — the experimental lab report

You did something in the world and measured what happened. Physics, chemistry and biology practicals, the Group-4 scientific investigation (the IB Physics IA), and most science EE chapters all belong here. The spine is IMRaD: Introduction, Methods, Results, And Discussion. You state a question, collect data, then argue whether the data answers the question.

Best page for this: Lab report format (IMRaD).

Family B — the mathematical / modelling report

You investigated a question with mathematics rather than a bench experiment. The Math exploration (the IB Math IA) is the obvious example, but so are simulation studies and any "derive it and test it" write-up. There is usually no hypothesis to confirm; instead you build an argument, prove or demonstrate a result, and reflect on what the mathematics showed and its limits.

Best page for this: The mathematical report.

Which one am I writing?

Ask one question: did I generate the evidence by measuring the physical world, or by reasoning and computing? If you ran an apparatus and recorded numbers, you are in Family A and the IMRaD spine applies. If your "results" are theorems, derivations, graphs you generated from a model, or simulations, you are in Family B and the exploration spine applies. Many real investigations mix the two — a physics IA that fits a model to data, for instance — in which case use IMRaD for the experiment and borrow the reflection habit for the modelling part.

2. The shared spine — what every section must do

Both families share one job: let a sceptical reader move from what you did to what it means without having to guess. The sections below appear in nearly every scientific report, in this order. The right-hand column is the test each section must pass.

SectionIts jobThe question it must answer
IntroductionSet up the question and the physics/mathematics behind it.Why does this question matter, and what should the reader already know?
Methods (Family A)Describe exactly what was done so it can be repeated.Could a competent student reproduce this from your words alone?
ResultsShow what was found, objectively.What happened, stated in data — with no opinions yet?
AnalysisTurn raw data into meaning (calculations, fits, comparisons).What do the numbers actually say once processed?
DiscussionInterpret, compare to theory, and weigh limitations.So what? Does it agree with what we expected, and why/why not?
ConclusionAnswer the original question directly.Given the evidence, what is the answer — and how sure are we?

3. Why a fixed form exists

A lab report is not bureaucratic busywork. The form exists because science is a communal, reproducible activity. A reader — your teacher, an examiner, a future you — needs to (a) find your method to check it, (b) see your raw data to judge it, and (c) follow your reasoning from data to claim. When you bury the method in the discussion or sprinkle opinions through the results, you make the reader do detective work, and you lose marks that were yours to keep. The genre is a contract: if you put the method in Methods, the reader trusts that the results are clean observations and the discussion is where judgement lives.

The one sentence that explains everything

A scientific report is an argument built on evidence, not a story of your afternoon. Every sentence should either present evidence, explain a method, or move the argument forward. If a sentence only says "then we did the next thing," it probably belongs in a tighter methods list, not in prose.

Sources & reliability note

The structural model (IMRaD: Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, plus Title/Abstract/References) is the standard taught by university writing centres (University of Melbourne; Kennesaw State; Texas A&M Corpus Christi writing centres). The IB Physics "scientific investigation" and its four 2025 criteria (Research Design, Data Analysis, Conclusion, Evaluation; Personal Engagement and Communication no longer standalone) are taken from the IB Physics guide (first assessment 2025) as summarised by several IB-aligned sources; the detailed criterion-by-criterion breakdown lives on the Physics IA page — verify the exact wording against the guide your cohort uses. The writing-craft guidance (hedging, active voice, tense, banned vague words) reflects consensus from scientific-writing guides (Queen's University; WVU; general academic-style guides). No figures, formulas or criterion marks are invented here; where a number is the IB's, it is attributed and flagged for verification.
