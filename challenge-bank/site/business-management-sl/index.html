5.2 The output of a flow line is set by its slowest stage, so adding capacity anywhere else produces nothing, and the choice between buying capacity at the bottleneck and buying overtime at the bottleneck is a choice between a fixed cost and a variable one

The item is built on a single idea that is easy to state and easy to forget while computing: the output of a line of dependent stages is set by the slowest stage, so capacity added anywhere else changes nothing. The arithmetic is simple enough that a candidate can compute the capacity of all four stages, identify the welding stage as the bottleneck and still answer the third part by adding the second machine at the assembly stage and reporting a rise in output, because the candidate has computed the new capacity of assembly and looked at that number rather than at the line. The third and fourth parts are the same calculation applied to two different stages and they have opposite answers, which is the whole of the item. The final part then asks the candidate to compare a fixed cost with a variable one, where the weekly figures are close enough that the answer depends on the horizon and on how much flexibility the manufacturer values.

3.8 Unequal asset lives, a replacement chain, and a common cost that cancels only in part

5.5 Break-even with a stepped fixed cost: two break-even points and a region of loss above the first

Break-even is taught as one number, and the formula is applied without asking whether the fixed cost is actually fixed over the range being examined. Here it is not: above $8{,}000$ units a second shift is needed and fixed costs step up by $\pounds 25{,}000$. The single figure the formula gives, $7{,}500$ units, is a valid break-even point and a dangerously incomplete answer, because profit is positive at $8{,}000$ units, negative at $8{,}001$, and does not recover until $10{,}625$. There are two break-even points and a loss-making region between them.

3.5 Two transactions that move the liquidity ratios in opposite directions, and the cancellation inside the acid test

A ratio is not a quantity, and reasoning about it by reasoning about the numbers inside it gives the wrong direction of change. Whether a transaction that reduces both current assets and current liabilities raises or lowers the current ratio depends on whether the ratio was above or below one, which is a property of the ratio rather than of the transaction. The acid test adds a second trap: buying inventory on credit raises current assets and current liabilities by the same amount, so the equal-amounts rule applies to the current ratio, but the acid test subtracts inventory out of the numerator, so the addition and the subtraction cancel and the numerator does not move at all.

5.5 A break-even output above the capacity ceiling: a negative margin of safety and the three single levers that would close the gap

2.1 The cost of labour turnover against the cost of a retention pay rise, where the wrong comparison is total recruitment spend against total salary increase rather than the saving the proposal actually buys

It does not. The three hundred thousand pounds is already being spent and does not change unless turnover changes; what the proposal buys is the sixty leavers it prevents, worth one hundred and fifty thousand. 4.1 Market share rising from 18 to 21 per cent while unit sales, total contribution and operating profit all fall, because the market the share is measured against contracted faster than the firm did

Market share is a ratio, and a ratio can rise while both of its components fall. The market contracted by twenty per cent while Harrowgate's own sales fell by six point seven per cent, so the share rises from eighteen per cent to twenty-one per cent even though the firm sold twelve thousand fewer pots. The share is governed by the size of the market as much as by the firm's own performance, and the marketing director has quoted the one measure in the case that improves while contribution, profit and volume all deteriorate. The lever is a single one: the candidate has to notice that the denominator moved, and that a relative gain is not an absolute one.
