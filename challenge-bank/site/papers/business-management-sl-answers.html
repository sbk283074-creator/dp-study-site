1. BM-6.6-001 16 marks

(a) The twelve monthly figures are 40, 45, 38, 42, 120, 130, 125, 44, 41, 118, 135 and 46, giving a total of 924 customers a day across the months. (M1)

Mean = 924/12 = 77 customers a day. (A1)

Ordered, the two middle values are 45 and 46, so the median = (45 + 46)/2 = 45.5 customers a day. (A1)

Range = 135 - 38 = 97 customers a day. (A1)

The mean is a poor summary of a typical month. It is 31.5 customers a day above the median, and no month was close to it: five months clustered near 42 and five near 125, with the remaining two between. The distribution is bimodal, not symmetrical, so the mean describes a month that never happened. (A1)

(b) High-demand months are those above 80 customers a day: months 5, 6, 7, 10 and 11, so five of twelve. The trial estimate of the probability of high demand is 5/12 = 0.417. (M1)(A1)

Using the finance director's 0.6: kiosk = 0.6 x 40,000 + 0.4 x 10,000 = EUR 28,000; cafe = 0.6 x 120,000 + 0.4 x (-60,000) = EUR 48,000. (M1)(A1)

Interest: kiosk loan 40,000 x 6% = EUR 2,400; cafe loan 120,000 x 6% = EUR 7,200. Net expected profit: kiosk EUR 25,600; cafe EUR 40,800. (A1)

Using the trial estimate of 5/12: kiosk = (5/12) x 40,000 + (7/12) x 10,000 = EUR 22,500; cafe = (5/12) x 120,000 + (7/12) x (-60,000) = EUR 15,000. After interest: kiosk EUR 20,100; cafe EUR 7,800. (A1)

The two probabilities therefore rank the options differently.

(c) Indicative evaluation. On the finance director's probability the decision is straightforward. The cafe's expected profit of EUR 40,800 is more than half as much again as the kiosk's EUR 25,600, and the upside, EUR 120,000 in a good year, is the kind of return that would justify the larger loan. A decision tree drawn on a 0.6 chance of high demand points clearly to the cafe.

The descriptive statistics undermine the probability the tree uses. The mean of 77 customers a day is not a typical month: it sits between two clusters, and the median of 45.5 is the better guide to what most months look like. Five of the twelve months were high-demand, so the empirical probability is 0.417, not 0.6. Rerun with 5/12, the ranking reverses: the kiosk earns EUR 20,100 against the cafe's EUR 7,800. The kiosk is also the more robust choice, because its worst case is a profit of EUR 10,000 while the cafe's is a loss of EUR 60,000, half the size of the whole investment.

The evaluation therefore turns on the probability, and the probability is the one thing the directors cannot observe. If the town's economy really does deliver high demand six years in ten, the cafe is right; if the trial stall is representative, the cafe destroys value in most years and the interest payment makes the bad case worse. The strongest answers note that a decision tree is only as good as its probabilities, that the trial was one stall in one year, and that opening the kiosk first keeps the option open. Credit any supported conclusion. (A1)(A1)(A1)(A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1)(A1) for the mean, (A1) for the median and (A1) for the range. The comment mark requires the candidate to notice that the mean is well above the median and that the data are bimodal; a candidate who says only that the mean is 77 forfeits it. Accept 45.5 for the median.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for identifying the five high-demand months and the probability of 5/12; accept 0.42 or 41.7%. Award (M1)(A1) for the two expected profits on the 0.6 probability and (A1) for the interest and net profits; then (A1) for the two expected profits on 5/12 and their net values. Follow through on arithmetic slips, but a candidate who omits the interest in one option and not the other cannot reach the evaluation marks.

(c) The discriminating marks are for rerunning the expected values with the empirical probability and for noticing that the ranking reverses. A candidate who reports the mean of 77 and recommends the cafe cannot earn more than two marks. Credit a conclusion that chooses the cafe only if it is justified by evidence about the local economy rather than by the finance director's assumption. Do not credit answers about the cafe's menu or staffing.

3. BM-3.8-002 16 marks

(a) Machine P returns $\pounds 90{,}000$ a year against an outlay of $\pounds 120{,}000$, so

$$\text{payback}_{P} = \frac{120{,}000}{90{,}000} = 1.33 \text{ years}$$

which is about one year and four months (A1). Machine Q recovers $\pounds 230{,}000$ at the same
rate, so

$$\text{payback}_{Q} = \frac{230{,}000}{90{,}000} = 2.56 \text{ years}$$

or roughly two years and seven months (A1). On payback, P is the clear winner, recovering its cost
more than a year sooner (A1). Note that payback ignores the second purchase P must make at the end
of year 2, which is one reason the ranking it produces should not be trusted on its own.

(b) The four-year annuity factor at $10\%$ is

$$\frac{1}{1.1} + \frac{1}{1.1^{2}} + \frac{1}{1.1^{3}} + \frac{1}{1.1^{4}} = 3.1699$$

so the present value of the inflows, identical for both machines, is

$$90{,}000 \times 3.1699 = \pounds 285{,}288 \tag{A1}$$

For P the outflows are the purchase at $t = 0$, the replacement at $t = 2$, and decommissioning at
$t = 2$ and $t = 4$ (M1):

$$120{,}000 + \frac{120{,}000}{1.1^{2}} + \frac{20{,}000}{1.1^{2}} + \frac{20{,}000}{1.1^{4}}
= 120{,}000 + 99{,}174 + 16{,}529 + 13{,}660 = \pounds 249{,}363 \tag{A1}$$

For Q the outflows are the single purchase at $t = 0$ and decommissioning at $t = 4$ (M1):

$$230{,}000 + \frac{20{,}000}{1.1^{4}} = 230{,}000 + 13{,}660 = \pounds 243{,}660 \tag{A1}$$

Hence

$$\text{NPV}_{P} = 285{,}288 - 249{,}363 = \pounds 35{,}925, \qquad
\text{NPV}_{Q} = 285{,}288 - 243{,}660 = \pounds 41{,}628 \tag{A1}$$

Both are positive, so both are worth undertaking; on net present value Q should be chosen, by
$\pounds 5{,}702$ (A1). This reverses the payback ranking in (a), and it also reverses the average
rate of return, which favours P at $16.7\%$ against $12.0\%$ for Q precisely because P is measured
against a much smaller capital base.

(c) The director is right about one of the two payments and wrong about the other. Both machines
pay $D$ at the end of year 4, and that payment is multiplied by the same discount factor
$\frac{1}{1.1^{4}}$ in both columns, so it is common and does cancel (A1). Machine P, however, also
pays $D$ at the end of year 2, and Q pays nothing at that date; this payment has no counterpart to
cancel against (A1). Its present value is

$$\frac{20{,}000}{1.1^{2}} = \pounds 16{,}529 \tag{A1}$$

so the decommissioning charge contributes $\pounds 16{,}529$ to the difference between the two
machines — not zero, and not the $\pounds 33{,}058$ that a candidate who doubled it would report. The
claim is therefore only partly correct: the charge is irrelevant to the comparison in year 4 and
decisive in year 2 (A1).

The practical consequence is that the charge is not a detail. Without it the two machines would
differ by

$$(120{,}000 + 99{,}174) - 230{,}000 = -\pounds 10{,}826$$

in P's favour, and P would be chosen. It is the year-2 payment alone, worth $\pounds 16{,}529$, that
overturns a gap of $\pounds 10{,}826$ and turns the decision round.

(d) Let the decommissioning charge be $D$. The year-4 payments cancel as before, so the difference
between the present values of the two cost streams is

$$\left(120{,}000 + \frac{120{,}000}{1.1^{2}} + \frac{D}{1.1^{2}} + \frac{D}{1.1^{4}}\right)
- \left(230{,}000 + \frac{D}{1.1^{4}}\right) = -10{,}826 + \frac{D}{1.1^{2}} \tag{M1}$$

where the two $\frac{D}{1.1^{4}}$ terms have been cancelled (A1). Q is preferred while this is
positive and P while it is negative, so the recommendation changes where

$$-10{,}826 + \frac{D}{1.21} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad D = 10{,}826 \times 1.21
= \pounds 13{,}100 \tag{A1}$$

At $D = 13{,}100$ the two machines are exactly equivalent, and the firm is indifferent (A1). Since
the actual charge of $\pounds 20{,}000$ exceeds $\pounds 13{,}100$, Q is confirmed as the better
choice — but the margin is $\pounds 5{,}702$, and a decommissioning quote that came in under
$\pounds 13{,}100$ would switch the decision to P.

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for each payback figure, one for the comparison. Accept 1.3 and 2.6 years or the equivalent in months. Allow follow-through to (b) from a payback error. (b) One mark for the present value of the inflows, one for identifying the four outflow terms for P, one for the P total, one for the Q total, one for the two NPVs and the recommendation. Do not award the recommendation mark without both NPVs. Condone rounding to the nearest pound throughout; the exact totals are 249,362.75 and 243,660.27. A candidate who omits the replacement purchase should lose the term mark but may still earn the method mark for discounting. (c) One mark for saying the year-4 payment cancels, one for saying the year-2 payment does not, one for the figure 16,529, one for the conclusion that the claim is partly right. Credit the figure even if the candidate describes it as 'the extra cost of P'. (d) One mark for setting up the difference with the year-4 terms cancelled, one for the equation, one for D = 13,100, one for stating which machine wins on either side of it. A candidate who cancels both D terms gets -10,826 and should be held at the first mark: the point of the part is precisely that the cancellation is only partial.

4. BM-5.5-002 16 marks

(a) Contribution per unit is

$$\pounds 14 - \pounds 6 = \pounds 8 \tag{A1}$$

and break-even output is fixed cost divided by contribution per unit (M1):

$$\frac{60{,}000}{8} = 7{,}500 \text{ units} \tag{A1}$$

This lies below $8{,}000$ units, so the $\pounds 60{,}000$ figure is the right one to use and the
answer is consistent.

(b) At $8{,}000$ units the higher fixed cost has not yet been triggered, so

$$\text{profit} = 8 \times 8{,}000 - 60{,}000 = 64{,}000 - 60{,}000 = \pounds 4{,}000 \tag{M1A1}$$

At $8{,}001$ units the step applies to the whole year's output, so fixed costs are
$\pounds 85{,}000$ and not $\pounds 60{,}000$ (M1):

$$\text{profit} = 8 \times 8{,}001 - 85{,}000 = 64{,}008 - 85{,}000 = -\pounds 20{,}992 \tag{A1}$$

The two figures differ by $\pounds 24{,}992$ even though output has risen by a single unit (A1). Of
that, $\pounds 8$ is the contribution earned by the extra unit and $\pounds 25{,}000$ is the step in
fixed cost, so almost the entire movement is the step and not the unit. A profit-and-loss account
drawn up for any output from $8{,}001$ units upwards must therefore use $\pounds 85{,}000$, and the
$7{,}500$-unit figure from (a) tells the business nothing about that region.

(c) In the upper band, fixed costs are $\pounds 85{,}000$ and contribution per unit is unchanged at
$\pounds 8$ (M1):

$$\frac{85{,}000}{8} = 10{,}625 \text{ units} \tag{A1}$$

At $10{,}625$ units contribution is $8 \times 10{,}625 = \pounds 85{,}000$, which exactly covers the
higher fixed cost, so profit is zero (A1). Because $10{,}625 > 8{,}000$ the answer lies inside the
band to which it applies and is self-consistent, exactly as in (a) (A1).

The business therefore breaks even twice, at $7{,}500$ units and again at $10{,}625$ units, and
makes a loss at every output in between, with the single worst point being $8{,}001$ units. Any
output from $10{,}626$ units upward is profitable. The margin of safety at a planned output of
$12{,}000$ units is $12{,}000 - 10{,}625 = 1{,}375$ units, or $11.5\%$ — measured against the second
break-even point, not the first, which would give a meaningless $37.5\%$.

(d) At $10{,}000$ units the business is in the upper band, so fixed costs are $\pounds 85{,}000$
(M1). Let the selling price be $p$; contribution per unit is $p - 6$ (M1). Break-even requires

$$10{,}000(p - 6) = 85{,}000 \tag{A1}$$

so

$$p = \frac{85{,}000}{10{,}000} + 6 = 8.50 + 6 = \pounds 14.50 \tag{A1}$$

The current price of $\pounds 14.00$ gives a contribution of $\pounds 80{,}000$ against fixed costs
of $\pounds 85{,}000$ and a loss of $\pounds 5{,}000$, so the required increase is $\pounds 0.50$,
or $3.6\%$. Two cautions are worth recording. The answer is the break-even price at this output, not
a recommended price: at $\pounds 14.50$ the business makes exactly nothing on $10{,}000$ units, and a
price below $\pounds 14.50$ loses money. And the answer is specific to $10{,}000$ units, because the
$\pounds 85{,}000$ is spread over whatever volume is actually produced; at $12{,}000$ units the
break-even price would be only $\pounds 13.08$.

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for the contribution, one for dividing fixed cost by contribution, one for the figure 7,500. Award the final mark only if the candidate states or implies that 7,500 is below 8,000 and therefore consistent. (b) One mark for using 60,000 at 8,000 units, one for the 4,000, one for switching to 85,000 at 8,001 units, one for the -20,992, one for noting that the whole step and not just the extra unit drives the change. A candidate who applies 85,000 to only the units above 8,000 gets 3,992 and should lose the third and fourth marks. (c) One mark for using 85,000, one for dividing by 8, one for 10,625, one for checking that the answer lies in the band to which it applies. Allow 10,625 or 10,626; do not allow 7,500 as the only answer. (d) One mark for using 85,000 rather than 60,000, one for setting up the equation, one for 14.50, one for the interpretation. Accept a correctly argued answer in pence. Follow-through applies throughout: a candidate who misreads the step but is otherwise consistent should not be penalised twice.

5. BM-2.1-002 16 marks

(a) Overall turnover of $14.4\%$ on $250$ staff gives a total of

$$0.144 \times 250 = 36 \text{ leavers} \tag{M1}$$

of which $9$ came from A and $11$ from C, so department B accounts for (M1)

$$36 - 9 - 11 = 16 \text{ leavers} \tag{A1}$$

(b) Applying the formula to each department in turn (M1):

$$\text{A}: \frac{9}{120} \times 100 = 7.5\%, \qquad
\text{B}: \frac{16}{80} \times 100 = 20.0\%, \qquad
\text{C}: \frac{11}{50} \times 100 = 22.0\% \tag{A1A1A1}$$

The overall rate is the total leaving divided by the total employed, not the average of the three
percentages:

$$\frac{9 + 16 + 11}{120 + 80 + 50} \times 100 = \frac{36}{250} \times 100 = 14.4\% \tag{M1A1}$$

which confirms the figure given.

(c) The simple average of the three departmental rates is

$$\frac{7.5 + 20.0 + 22.0}{3} = \frac{49.5}{3} = 16.5\% \tag{M1A1}$$

against a true overall rate of $14.4\%$, so the average overstates turnover by $2.1$ percentage
points (A1). The reason is that the departments are not the same size. The average gives each
department's rate a weight of one third, but department A actually employs $48\%$ of the workforce
and department C only $20\%$ (A1). The overall rate is a weighted mean in which each department's
rate is weighted by its headcount:

$$\frac{120(7.5) + 80(20.0) + 50(22.0)}{250}
= \frac{900 + 1{,}600 + 1{,}100}{250} = \frac{3{,}600}{250} = 14.4\%$$

The $7.5\%$ earned by the largest department pulls the figure down more than the $22\%$ earned by
the smallest pulls it up, which is exactly the effect the unweighted average discards.

(d) Reducing department A to $5\%$ means $0.05 \times 120 = 6$ leavers, a saving of $3$ (M1). Total
leavers fall to $33$ and the overall rate becomes

$$\frac{33}{250} \times 100 = 13.2\% \tag{A1}$$

an improvement of $1.2$ percentage points, at a saving of $3 \times 3{,}500 = \pounds 10{,}500$.

Reducing department C to $10\%$ means $0.10 \times 50 = 5$ leavers, a saving of $6$ (M1). Total
leavers fall to $30$ and the overall rate becomes

$$\frac{30}{250} \times 100 = 12.0\% \tag{A1}$$

an improvement of $2.4$ percentage points, at a saving of $6 \times 3{,}500 = \pounds 21{,}000$.

Department C should be prioritised. It delivers twice the reduction in the overall rate and twice
the cost saving, from $20\%$ of the workforce rather than $48\%$ (A1). The reason is that the
improvement depends on two things multiplied together — the size of the department and the size of
the cut — and department C's cut is $12$ percentage points against department A's $2.5$.

Two qualifications belong in a full answer. Neither intervention reaches the $10\%$ target on its
own: both together give $12.0\%$, and hitting $10\%$ would require $25$ leavers in total, so a
further five must come from somewhere. And the arithmetic is silent on why people leave. Department
C's $22\%$ may reflect working conditions, pay or management, and if the underlying cause is not
addressed the reduction may prove temporary; conversely, if department A's low turnover reflects a
genuinely well-managed operation, cutting it further may be the cheapest of all. The numbers say
where the leverage is, not what to do about it (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for converting 14.4% of 250 to 36 leavers, one for subtracting the known leavers, one for the figure 16. (b) One mark for applying the formula, one for each of the three departmental rates, one for dividing total leavers by total employed. Do not award a rate calculated as an average of the others. Accept rates to one decimal place. (c) One mark for the average of 16.5%, one for the comparison with 14.4%, one for explaining that the departments differ in size, one for the weighted-mean calculation or an equivalent explanation in words. A candidate who says only 'the average is wrong' without saying why should be held at two marks. (d) One mark for the 3 leavers saved in A, one for 13.2%, one for the 6 leavers saved in C, one for 12.0%. The evaluation mark is awarded for a supported choice of C; allow A only if a cost or feasibility argument is given. Credit the 10,500 and 21,000 figures even if the candidate backs the other option. Follow-through: a candidate who finds 16 in (a) by a different route should not be penalised again in (b), provided the method is consistent.

6. BM-4.4-001 15 marks

(a) Wave 1: $\dfrac{118}{400} \times 100 = 29.5\%$. Wave 2: $\dfrac{153}{600} \times 100 = 25.5\%$. On this comparison purchase intent appears to have fallen by $4.0$ percentage points between the two waves.

(b) Wave 1. 18–34: $\dfrac{80}{200} = 40.0\%$. 35–54: $\dfrac{30}{120} = 25.0\%$. 55+: $\dfrac{8}{80} = 10.0\%$.

Wave 2. 18–34: $\dfrac{54}{120} = 45.0\%$. 35–54: $\dfrac{54}{180} = 30.0\%$. 55+: $\dfrac{45}{300} = 15.0\%$.

Intent rose in every single band — by 5.0, 5.0 and 5.0 percentage points respectively. The overall comparison in part (a) conceals this completely, because the two waves did not interview the same mix of people: wave 2 drew 300 of its 600 respondents from the 55+ band, against 80 of 400 in wave 1, and that band has the lowest intent in both waves. The headline fall is an artefact of who was asked, not a change in what people think.

(c) Applying the target mix to the wave 1 band rates: $0.30(40.0) + 0.40(25.0) + 0.30(10.0) = 12.0 + 10.0 + 3.0 = 25.0\%$. Applying it to the wave 2 band rates: $0.30(45.0) + 0.40(30.0) + 0.30(15.0) = 13.5 + 12.0 + 4.5 = 30.0\%$. Both waves are therefore reweighted onto the same population, and on that like-for-like basis intent rose by $5.0$ percentage points.

(d) The proposal should be rejected. The wave 2 overall rate of 25.5% is computed on a sample that is not representative of the target market: it over-weights the 55+ band by a factor of roughly five relative to the target mix. Applying it gives $900\,000 \times 0.255 = 229\,500$ buyers. Applying the standardised wave 2 rate of 30.0% gives $900\,000 \times 0.30 = 270\,000$ buyers. The difference is $40\,500$ units, and at a gross margin of £1.40 that is $40\,500 \times 1.40 = £56\,700$ of margin left out of the forecast. That is large enough to change the decision if the launch has a fixed cost close to the projected margin — a project that looks marginal at 229 500 units may clear its threshold at 270 000. Against this, the standardised figure is not free of doubt: wave 2 was an online panel taken on weekdays, so it may under-represent people who shop in stores at weekends, and stated purchase intent reliably overstates actual buying. The defensible conclusion is that the director's method is wrong in a known direction and by a known amount, and that the forecast should use the standardised rate while being presented as an upper estimate.

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for each rate and one for the comparison. Accept rates given to one decimal place. Do not penalise a candidate who comments that the two rates are not comparable — that is creditworthy here and is developed in later parts.

(b) One mark for each wave's set of three band rates (two marks total), one for the observation that intent rose in every band, and one for identifying the differing age mix as the cause. A candidate who computes the band rates but does not draw the conclusion earns the first two marks only.

(c) One mark for setting up the weighted sum with the target mix, one for each standardised rate, and one for the resulting comparison. Candidates may use either the percentages or the counts; both are acceptable. Follow-through applies from part (b).

(d) This is an AO3 part: marks are for the judgement and its support, not for the arithmetic alone. Award one mark for rejecting the proposal, one for each of the two forecasts (four marks total across the set), one for the quantified difference in units and one for the money figure. A candidate who reaches the correct numbers but does not say what they mean for the decision is capped at three. Conversely, a candidate who rejects the proposal with sound reasoning but whose arithmetic slips may still earn three, provided the direction of the error is identified. Credit the counter-argument about stated intent overstating actual purchase where it is developed rather than merely listed.

  • Return on capital employed $= 288 \div 1800 = 16.0\%$
  • Current ratio $= (300 + 320) \div 310 = 620 \div 310 = 2.00$

Year 2 (A1 for the year):

  • Net profit margin $= 352 \div 3200 = 11.0\%$
  • Return on capital employed $= 352 \div 1920 = 18.3\%$

(M1) Employees who are leaving for a better career or for more varied work will continue to do so, because the factor that would change their mind is not the one that has been altered. (A1)

(d) The 4% rise costs $480\times 28\,000\times 0.04=€537\,600$ per year, and it is a permanent addition to the pay bill. (M1)

The turnover it is intended to reduce costs about $96\times 3300=€316\,800$ per year to manage. (A1) Even if the rise eliminated turnover completely, which Herzberg's theory suggests it will not, the company would be spending €537 600 to avoid at most €316 800, so the measure cannot pay for itself on these figures. In any case the company must recruit 216 people this year because 120 of them are needed for the expansion, not because of turnover, so the pay rise would not remove that cost either. A 4% rise is also a permanent, across-the-board commitment, whereas recruitment costs are incurred only when staff actually leave; if the turnover rate were to fall for other reasons, the company would be locked into the higher pay bill with no offsetting saving. The money would be better spent on the motivators identified in part (c), or on reducing the €907 200 recruitment and training bill by improving retention through job design and progression. (A1)

Part (a): accept the use of closing staff numbers instead of average staff numbers if the reason is stated, since the average is not given; the method mark should be awarded either way. Part (b): the two component marks are for the expansion requirement and for the replacement requirement; a candidate who reports only 120 has answered half the question and can earn at most two marks. Do not accept a candidate who adds the 96 leavers twice. Part (c): any theory that identifies pay as a hygiene or lower-order need should be credited, including Maslow's hierarchy with pay located among physiological and safety needs. A candidate who states the theory correctly but does not apply it to Bramble's situation earns the first method mark only. Part (d): the comparison between €537 600 and €316 800 is the discriminating mark; a candidate who evaluates the pay rise as 'expensive' without a figure does not reach it. Do not accept an evaluation that ignores the fact that the pay rise is permanent and recurring.

  • import tariff at 18% of €28: €5.04

  • expected rework cost: $0.04\times 6=€0.24$

This is €2.82 per unit below the current cost of €42.00. The €39.18 is also a best case: it excludes the cost of managing an overseas supplier — incoming quality inspection, extra safety stock for longer lead times, and currency exposure — none of which appears in the current €42.00.

(a) Award up to four marks for two business functions, each explained in the context of Nettle & Vine.

Operations (production). The operations function is the transformation process: it converts bought-in inputs that cost EUR 1.20 a litre into bottled cordial that sells for EUR 4.00 a litre. Almost all of the value the business adds is created here, because the recipe, the brewing and the bottling turn cheap ingredients into a product a customer will pay a premium for. (A1)(A1)

Also creditable: finance (arranging and servicing the EUR 200 000 loan and costing the supermarket contract), human resources (recruiting and training the eight staff the contract would need), and product development (Priya's recipes and the product range).

The mean is 2.5 times the median, so the 'average customer' is not a typical customer. The five largest accounts alone provide EUR 100 000, or 41.7% of trade revenue. The business is already more concentrated than the mean figure suggests. (A1)

Value added = revenue minus bought-in materials and services.

23. BM-3.1-001 14 marks

(a) Capital expenditure is spending on non-current assets that will be used in the business for more than a year - it buys something the business keeps. Revenue expenditure is spending on the day-to-day running of the business, which is consumed within the year. (A1)(A1)

In this case the five items in the table are all capital expenditure: the conversion of the unit, the grooming equipment, the van, the software and the signage are assets the business will use for several years. The rent, wages, utilities, insurance, dog food and marketing are revenue expenditure, because they are incurred month by month and create no lasting asset. (A1)

The distinction matters because the two types of spending have to be financed differently. Capital expenditure is a large, one-off sum that a bank will often lend against, because the asset can be secured. Revenue expenditure recurs every month, is not secured by an asset, and must be financed from cash or from working capital - so it is easy to underestimate and hard to borrow against. (A1)

(b) Capital expenditure $= EUR 52 000 + EUR 16 000 + EUR 28 000 + EUR 6 000 + EUR 8 000 = EUR 110 000$. (M1)(A1)

Monthly running costs $= EUR 3 000 + EUR 7 800 + EUR 1 200 + EUR 400 + EUR 4 600 + EUR 500 = EUR 17 500$. Three months of running costs must be financed before the business earns anything, so working capital $= 3 \times EUR 17 500 = EUR 52 500$. (M1)(A1)

Contingency $= 10\% \times (EUR 110 000 + EUR 52 500) = 10\% \times EUR 162 500 = EUR 16 250$. Total finance required $= EUR 110 000 + EUR 52 500 + EUR 16 250 = EUR 178 750$. (A1)

(c) Indicative evaluation, using the financial section of the business plan.

The plan looks balanced on its own assumptions: it claims EUR 180 000 of funding against a requirement of EUR 178 750, a margin of EUR 1 250. But two of the three funding assumptions are wrong.

The bank will lend at most 60% of the value of the capital items, which is $60\% \times EUR 110 000 = EUR 66 000$, not the EUR 115 000 requested. The grant is capped at EUR 12 000, not EUR 20 000. The realistic funding is therefore $EUR 45 000 + EUR 66 000 + EUR 12 000 = EUR 123 000$, against a requirement of EUR 178 750. The shortfall is EUR 55 750, and the plan's apparent EUR 1 250 surplus becomes a EUR 55 750 hole. (M1)(A1)

The shortfall is almost entirely a working-capital problem. The business needs EUR 52 500 of cash to survive three months with no income, and the bank will not lend against it. The founders have focused on the visible assets and underestimated the cost of simply keeping the doors open.

The lease offer helps the capital side but not enough. Leasing the grooming equipment and the van (EUR 44 000) would cut capital expenditure to EUR 66 000, but it adds EUR 980 a month to running costs, raising monthly costs to EUR 18 480 and working capital to EUR 55 440. With a 10% contingency on EUR 121 440, the total requirement becomes EUR 133 584. The bank's limit then falls to EUR 39 600, so available funding is EUR 96 600 and the shortfall is EUR 36 984. Leasing reduces the gap by about EUR 19 000 but increases the monthly cost permanently.

Rob's aunt's EUR 56 000 would close the gap, but it costs 25% of the business, which both founders have said they do not want to give up. It also transfers a quarter of any future growth to someone who is contributing money rather than work.

A supported conclusion is required. A candidate who recommends delaying the opening, phasing the spend, reducing the contingency, or accepting the aunt's money, and who justifies it with the figures, should be credited. The key judgement is that the plan is not realistic as written: it understates the finance needed by about EUR 55 750 and misunderstands how a bank lends to a start-up.

24. BM-5.1-001 14 marks

(a) Pennine Workwear operates a transformation process. The inputs are bought-in fabric, thread and components costing EUR 7 a garment, together with 2 400 hours of direct labour a month and the use of the factory and machinery. The process is cutting, sewing, finishing, inspecting and packing. The outputs are finished protective garments sold at EUR 18 each, which for many customers must also be safe, durable and compliant with industry standards.

The operations function adds value in two ways. First, it converts EUR 7 of materials into a garment that sells for EUR 18 - the transformation itself is where the added value is created. Second, it is responsible for the quality and reliability that make the garment worth buying: a protective jacket that fails in use is worthless, so the inspection and finishing stages protect the price the business can charge. Operations therefore supports the whole business strategy, not just the production line.

(b) Labour productivity = garments produced per direct labour hour.

Before the change: $12 000 / 2 400 = 5.0$ garments per labour hour. (M1)(A1)
Since the change: $15 000 / 2 500 = 6.0$ garments per labour hour. (A1)
That is a rise of $(6.0 - 5.0) / 5.0 = 20\%$, which is the figure the operations manager is reporting.

Value added = revenue minus bought-in materials minus rework cost.

Before the change: revenue $= 12 000 \times EUR 18 = EUR 216 000$; materials $= 12 000 \times EUR 7 = EUR 84 000$; defects $= 2\% \times 12 000 = 240$ garments, rework $= 240 \times EUR 4 = EUR 960$. Value added $= EUR 216 000 - EUR 84 000 - EUR 960 = EUR 131 040$, or $EUR 131 040 / 12 000 = EUR 10.92$ per garment. (M1)(A1)

Since the change: revenue $= 15 000 \times EUR 18 = EUR 270 000$, less 450 garments sold as seconds at EUR 9 instead of EUR 18, a loss of $450 \times EUR 9 = EUR 4 050$, so EUR 265 950; materials $= 15 000 \times EUR 7 = EUR 105 000$; defects $= 7\% \times 15 000 = 1 050$ garments, rework $= 1 050 \times EUR 4 = EUR 4 200$. Value added $= EUR 265 950 - EUR 105 000 - EUR 4 200 = EUR 156 750$, or $EUR 156 750 / 15 000 = EUR 10.45$ per garment. (M1)(A1)

The two measures disagree. Labour productivity is up 20%, but value added per garment has fallen from EUR 10.92 to EUR 10.45, a fall of about 4.3%. Total value added has risen, from EUR 131 040 to EUR 156 750, but only because 25% more garments are being made. Each garment now adds less value than before, because the cost of rework and of selling seconds has risen faster than the extra output is worth. Efficiency has improved; effectiveness has not.

(c) Indicative evaluation, using descriptive statistics on the twelve monthly defect rates.

The mean defect rate for the year is $(2.1 + 1.8 + 2.3 + 1.9 + 2.2 + 2.1 + 6.8 + 7.1 + 6.4 + 7.6 + 8.1 + 7.4) / 12 = 55.8 / 12 = 4.65\%$. Taken alone, that looks acceptable. The median is 4.35% and the range is $8.1 - 1.8 = 6.3$ percentage points. (M1)(A1)

The mean is misleading. Splitting the data at the change gives a mean of 2.07% for months 1 to 6 and 7.23% for months 7 to 12. The process did not drift; it stepped from about 2% to about 7% at the moment the change was introduced, and the annual mean of 4.65% describes no month that actually occurred. This is the classic trap of a summary statistic that conceals a structural break.

Two of the largest customers, together worth about 30% of sales, have complained, and one has asked for a meeting. Three experienced machinists have left in four months, which will put further pressure on quality. The operations manager's bonus depends on productivity, which explains why he is recommending a further increase in line speed, but his measure ignores the cost of defects and returns.

Conclusion: the change should not be kept in its present form. The productivity gain is real, but it has been bought by cutting the quality checks that protect the EUR 18 price. A supported conclusion is required; a candidate who recommends keeping the change but restoring the inspection stage, and justifies this from the figures, is equally creditworthy.
