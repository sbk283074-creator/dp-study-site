1. A mean that says grow and a spread that says do not

[16 marks]BM-6.6-001

Unit 6: The SL Toolkit · Unit 6: 6.6 Decision trees and 6.7 Descriptive statistics (the SL Toolkit). P2 Section B. · difficulty 5

(a) The twelve monthly figures are 40, 45, 38, 42, 120, 130, 125, 44, 41, 118, 135 and 46, giving a total of 924 customers a day across the months. (M1)

Mean = 924/12 = 77 customers a day. (A1)

Ordered, the two middle values are 45 and 46, so the median = (45 + 46)/2 = 45.5 customers a day. (A1)

Range = 135 - 38 = 97 customers a day. (A1)

The mean is a poor summary of a typical month. It is 31.5 customers a day above the median, and no month was close to it: five months clustered near 42 and five near 125, with the remaining two between. The distribution is bimodal, not symmetrical, so the mean describes a month that never happened. (A1)

(b) High-demand months are those above 80 customers a day: months 5, 6, 7, 10 and 11, so five of twelve. The trial estimate of the probability of high demand is 5/12 = 0.417. (M1)(A1)

Using the finance director's 0.6: kiosk = 0.6 x 40,000 + 0.4 x 10,000 = EUR 28,000; cafe = 0.6 x 120,000 + 0.4 x (-60,000) = EUR 48,000. (M1)(A1)

Interest: kiosk loan 40,000 x 6% = EUR 2,400; cafe loan 120,000 x 6% = EUR 7,200. Net expected profit: kiosk EUR 25,600; cafe EUR 40,800. (A1)

Using the trial estimate of 5/12: kiosk = (5/12) x 40,000 + (7/12) x 10,000 = EUR 22,500; cafe = (5/12) x 120,000 + (7/12) x (-60,000) = EUR 15,000. After interest: kiosk EUR 20,100; cafe EUR 7,800. (A1)

The two probabilities therefore rank the options differently.

(c) Indicative evaluation. On the finance director's probability the decision is straightforward. The cafe's expected profit of EUR 40,800 is more than half as much again as the kiosk's EUR 25,600, and the upside, EUR 120,000 in a good year, is the kind of return that would justify the larger loan. A decision tree drawn on a 0.6 chance of high demand points clearly to the cafe.

The descriptive statistics undermine the probability the tree uses. The mean of 77 customers a day is not a typical month: it sits between two clusters, and the median of 45.5 is the better guide to what most months look like. Five of the twelve months were high-demand, so the empirical probability is 0.417, not 0.6. Rerun with 5/12, the ranking reverses: the kiosk earns EUR 20,100 against the cafe's EUR 7,800. The kiosk is also the more robust choice, because its worst case is a profit of EUR 10,000 while the cafe's is a loss of EUR 60,000, half the size of the whole investment.

The evaluation therefore turns on the probability, and the probability is the one thing the directors cannot observe. If the town's economy really does deliver high demand six years in ten, the cafe is right; if the trial stall is representative, the cafe destroys value in most years and the interest payment makes the bad case worse. The strongest answers note that a decision tree is only as good as its probabilities, that the trial was one stall in one year, and that opening the kiosk first keeps the option open. Credit any supported conclusion. (A1)(A1)(A1)(A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1)(A1) for the mean, (A1) for the median and (A1) for the range. The comment mark requires the candidate to notice that the mean is well above the median and that the data are bimodal; a candidate who says only that the mean is 77 forfeits it. Accept 45.5 for the median.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for identifying the five high-demand months and the probability of 5/12; accept 0.42 or 41.7%. Award (M1)(A1) for the two expected profits on the 0.6 probability and (A1) for the interest and net profits; then (A1) for the two expected profits on 5/12 and their net values. Follow through on arithmetic slips, but a candidate who omits the interest in one option and not the other cannot reach the evaluation marks.

(c) The discriminating marks are for rerunning the expected values with the empirical probability and for noticing that the ranking reverses. A candidate who reports the mean of 77 and recommends the cafe cannot earn more than two marks. Credit a conclusion that chooses the cafe only if it is justified by evidence about the local economy rather than by the finance director's assumption. Do not credit answers about the cafe's menu or staffing.

2. 5.2 The output of a flow line is set by its slowest stage, so adding capacity anywhere else produces nothing, and the choice between buying capacity at the bottleneck and buying overtime at the bottleneck is a choice between a fixed cost and a variable one

[15 marks]BM-5.2-201

Unit 5: Operations management · Unit 5: 5.2 Operations methods (flow production and the capacity of a line of dependent stages), with 3.3 Costs and revenues and 5.1 The role of operations management · difficulty 5

(a) The weekly capacity of a stage is the minutes available divided by the minutes each unit takes at that stage. With 2 400 minutes available at every stage:

Cutting: 2 400 / 3.0 = 800 units a week.
Welding: 2 400 / 5.0 = 480 units a week.
Assembly: 2 400 / 4.0 = 600 units a week.
Finishing: 2 400 / 2.5 = 960 units a week.

The welding stage has the smallest capacity, at 480 units a week.

(b) A unit must pass through all four stages, so the line can produce no more units in a week than its slowest stage can pass. Welding is the slowest stage at 480 units a week, so the maximum weekly output is 480 units. Demand is 600 units a week, so the shortfall is 600 - 480 = 120 units a week, and those 120 units of sales are lost every week.

The other stages are not working at their limit. At an output of 480 units a week, cutting is using 480 x 3.0 = 1 440 of its 2 400 minutes, which is sixty per cent of its capacity; assembly is using 480 x 4.0 = 1 920 minutes, which is eighty per cent; and finishing is using 480 x 2.5 = 1 200 minutes, which is fifty per cent. Welding is using all 2 400 minutes, which is one hundred per cent.

(c) A second assembly machine halves the time assembly takes per unit, from 4.0 minutes to 2.0 minutes, so the capacity of assembly becomes 2 400 / 2.0 = 1 200 units a week. The capacities of the four stages are then 800 at cutting, 480 at welding, 1 200 at assembly and 960 at finishing.

The slowest stage is still welding, at 480 units a week, so the output of the line does not change. It remains 480 units a week and the shortfall remains 120 units a week. The money spent on the second assembly machine buys no additional output at all, because assembly was not limiting the line; the 120 extra units a week that the second machine could pass through cannot reach it, since welding cannot send them on.

(d) A second welding machine halves the time welding takes per unit, from 5.0 minutes to 2.5 minutes, so the capacity of welding becomes 2 400 / 2.5 = 960 units a week. The capacities of the four stages are then 800 at cutting, 960 at welding, 600 at assembly and 960 at finishing.

The slowest stage is now assembly, at 600 units a week, so the output of the line rises to 600 units a week, which is exactly the demand. The shortfall is closed. Welding is no longer the constraint and has spare capacity of 360 units a week; cutting is now the next tightest stage after assembly, at 800 units a week.

The two answers differ because output is governed by the slowest stage and by nothing else. In (c) the stage that was improved was not the slowest stage, so the line's output was still set by welding and did not move. In (d) the stage that was improved was the slowest stage, so the constraint moved to assembly and the output rose until it reached the new limit. Improving a stage that is not the constraint does not raise output; it only adds idle capacity to a stage that already had some. This is why the operations manager's belief that assembly was the problem was mistaken: assembly takes four minutes a unit, which is longer than cutting and longer than finishing, but welding takes five, and it is the longest time per unit that sets the pace.

(e) Either route closes the 120-unit shortfall, and the choice turns on the shape of the cost rather than on the size of it.

Overtime at the welding stage costs 36 dollars an hour. To make 120 extra units at 5.0 minutes a unit requires 120 x 5.0 = 600 minutes, which is 10 hours a week. At 36 dollars an hour that is 360 dollars a week, or 18 720 dollars a year. Overtime is a variable cost: it is paid only when the extra output is wanted, and it can be stopped in a week in which demand falls.

The welding machine costs 85 000 dollars depreciated over five years, which is 17 000 dollars a year, or about 326.92 dollars a week. It is a fixed cost: it is incurred every week whether the extra 120 units are made or not, and it commits the manufacturer for five years.

On cost alone the machine is slightly cheaper, at about 326.92 dollars a week against 360 dollars a week, which is about 2.72 dollars per extra unit against 3.00 dollars. Over five years the saving is roughly 1 720 dollars a year, about 8 600 dollars in total, which is small against an outlay of 85 000 dollars, and the payback on the machine is very long if it is justified only by the shortfall. The machine does have advantages beyond the weekly cost. It raises welding capacity to 960 units a week, so it leaves room for demand to grow to 600 units without any further spending, whereas overtime has to be paid for every extra unit and becomes more expensive if demand rises. It also removes the dependence on staff being willing to work overtime week after week, which is difficult to sustain and which raises the risk of absence and of poor quality on a tired shift.

Against that, the machine is a commitment. If demand falls, the 85 000 dollars is spent and the depreciation continues, while overtime can be cancelled. The manufacturer's position is not stated in the case, and the recommendation has to turn on it.

On the information given, the manufacturer should pay the overtime in the first instance, and revisit the machine if demand rises or proves to be permanent. The shortfall is 120 units a week, which is twenty per cent of demand, and the cost difference between the two routes is small enough, about 33 dollars a week, that it does not by itself justify an 85 000-dollar commitment. Overtime keeps the manufacturer flexible while it establishes whether the extra demand is durable, and it can be replaced by the machine once the demand has been shown to be permanent. The machine becomes the better choice as soon as demand is expected to rise towards the new welding capacity of 960 units, or as soon as the overtime proves difficult to staff; at that point the fixed cost is being spread over a larger output and the flexibility that overtime buys is no longer worth its price.

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for the correct method, minutes available divided by minutes per unit, one mark for the four capacities 800, 480, 600 and 960, and one mark for identifying welding as the smallest. Follow-through is allowed from one arithmetic slip if the identification is consistent with the candidate's own figures.

(b) One mark for the output of 480 units a week, one mark for the shortfall of 120, and one mark for the reason, that output is limited by the slowest stage. A candidate who adds the stage capacities or takes the average has not understood the line and scores zero for this part.

(c) One mark for the new assembly capacity of 1 200 units a week, one mark for the statement that output is unchanged at 480 units a week, and one mark for the reason, that welding is still the slowest stage. An answer that reports a rise in output because assembly capacity has risen scores zero for this part, however correct the arithmetic.

(d) One mark for the new welding capacity of 960 units a week, one mark for the new output of 600 units a week with assembly as the new constraint, and one mark for the explanation that improving the constraint moves the constraint while improving any other stage changes nothing. Accept 600 units as the output because it matches demand; a candidate who states that assembly now limits the line at 600 units a week has the point even if the answer is expressed as 'up to 600'.

(e) No mark for naming a route. One mark for the correct weekly costs, overtime at 360 dollars a week or 18 720 dollars a year and the machine at about 326.92 dollars a week or 17 000 dollars a year, one mark for identifying the machine as the fixed cost and overtime as the variable one and drawing the consequence for flexibility, and one mark for a recommendation that states what would change it. A candidate who recommends the machine because it is cheaper per week takes two marks only if the flexibility argument is also addressed; the cost difference alone, at about 33 dollars a week, does not settle the question.

4. 5.5 A contract that crosses the relevant range: the range of order sizes for which a positive contribution still destroys profit

[14 marks]BM-5.5-301

Unit 5: Operations management · Unit 5: 5.5 Break-even analysis (contribution, break-even output, margin of safety, the limitations of break-even analysis and the relevant range); 3.3 Costs and revenues · difficulty 5

(a) Contribution per unit at the current price is £25 − £13 = £12 (A1). Break-even output is fixed costs divided by the contribution per unit, £48 000 ÷ £12 = 4 000 units per month (M1)(A1). Current output is exactly 4 000 units, so the current monthly profit is 4 000 × £12 − £48 000 = £0: the business is precisely at break-even.
(b) On the contract the price is £20, so the contribution per unit is £20 − £13 = £7 (A1). A contract of 2 500 units takes total output to 4 000 + 2 500 = 6 500 units, which is above the 6 000-unit ceiling of the first shift, so fixed costs rise to £48 000 + £18 000 = £66 000 (M1). Total contribution is 4 000 × £12 + 2 500 × £7 = £48 000 + £17 500 = £65 500, so the monthly profit becomes £65 500 − £66 000 = −£500 (A1). Kestrel would move from breaking even to losing £500 a month.
(c) Let q be the contract size. While 4 000 + q ≤ 6 000, that is q ≤ 2 000, no second shift is needed and profit is 7q, which is positive for every q > 0 (M1). Once q > 2 000 the extra £18 000 of fixed cost is incurred, so profit is 7q − 18 000, which is negative for q < 18 000 ÷ 7 = 2 571.43, that is up to and including 2 571 units (M1)(A1). The contract therefore leaves Kestrel worse off than it is now for every size from 2 001 to 2 571 units inclusive (A1), and the smallest size at which the step repays its own cost is 2 572 units, where profit is 7 × 2 572 − 18 000 = £4 (A1). Profit is not monotonic in the size of the contract: 2 000 units earns £14 000, 2 001 units loses £3 993, and 2 572 units earns £4.
(d) The director's claim is true of the unit and false of the decision. Each contract unit does add £7, but the relevant comparison is not between £7 and zero; it is between the £17 500 the contract contributes and the £18 000 of fixed cost that exists only because the contract was taken (M1). At 2 500 units the contract therefore reduces profit from £0 to −£500 a month, and because the contract runs for a year the loss is about −£6 000 over its life, before any allowance for the disruption of running a second shift for twelve months (A1). Two things qualify this. First, the arithmetic is knife-edge: a loss of £500 a month is 0.8% of monthly contribution, so a small change in the terms reverses the answer, and the business may rationally accept a marginal loss to secure a national listing (A1). Second, the loss is a property of the size and not of the idea: the contract is profitable below 2 001 units and above 2 571 units. The recommendation is to renegotiate one of the two (M1). At 2 500 units the contribution per unit must satisfy 2 500c ≥ 18 000, so c ≥ £7.20 and the price must be at least £20.20; alternatively the contract should be capped at 2 000 units or raised to at least 2 572 units (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for £12, (M1) for dividing fixed costs by contribution, (A1) for 4 000 units and £0. Accept break-even quoted as £100 000 of revenue if the unit figure accompanies it.
(b) (A1) for £7, (M1) for noticing that 6 500 units exceeds the 6 000-unit ceiling and adding £18 000, (A1) for −£500. A candidate who computes £17 500 of contribution and ignores the step scores at most one of the three; this is the whole item, so do not award follow-through from it.
(c) (M1) for treating the two ranges separately, (M1) for solving 7q ≥ 18 000, (A1) for the closed range 2 001 to 2 571 units, (A1) for 2 572 units. Accept 2 571.4 units quoted as the break-even size provided the candidate then rounds *up* to 2 572 for the smallest whole-unit size; a candidate who rounds down to 2 571 has not answered the question and loses the final mark.
(d) (M1) for comparing £17 500 with £18 000 rather than with zero, (A1) for the conclusion that the contract as offered reduces profit, (A1) for a developed qualitative point such as the twelve-month commitment or the strategic value of the listing, (M1)(A1) for a recommendation supported by a figure — £20.20, 2 000 units or 2 572 units. Do not credit 'accept because contribution is positive' however well argued; the question asks for an evaluation of that claim, not a restatement of it.

5. 4.4 Non-response that is not random: recovering the population figure from two segment readings instead of from the pooled sample

[14 marks]BM-4.4-301

Unit 4: Marketing · Unit 4: 4.4 Market research (sampling methods, the reliability of market research data, and the difference between primary and secondary research); 4.1 The role of marketing · difficulty 5

(a) Premium response rate: 80 ÷ 100 = 80%. Standard response rate: 90 ÷ 300 = 30%. Overall: 170 ÷ 400 = 42.5% (M1)(A1). Premium members are a quarter of the membership, 100 ÷ 400 = 25%, but they are 80 of the 170 responses, which is 47.1% of the achieved sample (A1). A sample in which one segment is represented at nearly twice its true weight is not representative, and the cause is visible in the response rates: premium members were more than two and a half times as likely to reply.
(b) Of the 170 responses, 56 + 36 = 92 said they would use the wall, so the pooled figure is 92 ÷ 170 = 54.1% (M1)(A1). The manager concludes that a majority — more than half — of members want the climbing wall, and recommends approval (A1).
(c) Weight each segment's approval rate by its share of the membership. Premium approval is 56 ÷ 80 = 70% and standard approval is 36 ÷ 90 = 40%. The weighted estimate is 0.25 × 70% + 0.75 × 40% = 17.5% + 30% = 47.5% (M1)(A1), which is 0.475 × 400 = 190 of the 400 members (A1). The pooled figure is higher because the sample contains too many premium members, and premium members are the segment that wants the wall most: 70% against 40%, a gap of thirty percentage points. The direction of the error was predictable before any arithmetic, because the segment with the higher response rate is also the segment with the higher approval rate, so self-selection must inflate the pooled answer (A1).
(d) The claim is not supported. The weighted estimate is 47.5%, which is below half, and 190 of 400 members is 10 short of a majority (M1). Two things qualify this. First, 47.5% is itself an estimate resting on the assumption that non-respondents in each segment resemble respondents in that segment — an assumption the data cannot test, and one that is least safe here, since people who did not bother to answer a questionnaire about a climbing wall may be people who do not want a climbing wall (A1). Second, the commercial case may not need a majority: if premium members generate most of the revenue and 70% of them want the wall, the board might rationally build it anyway (A1). The recommendation is to re-run the survey so that the answer can be tested rather than assumed: weight the responses, or use quota sampling with a target of 100 premium and 300 standard members, follow up non-respondents, and report the result by segment with the response rate stated alongside it (M1)(A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for a correct response rate calculation in any of the three forms, (A1) for all three rates correct, (A1) for the comparison of 25% of the membership with 47.1% of the sample. A candidate who says the sample is unrepresentative because only 42.5% replied, without comparing the two segments, has identified a real but different problem: credit it only if the segment comparison is also attempted.
(b) (M1) for 92 of 170, (A1) for 54.1%, (A1) for stating the manager's conclusion. The conclusion must be reported faithfully even though it is wrong; the evaluation comes in (d).
(c) (M1) for weighting by membership shares rather than by response counts, (A1) for 70% and 40%, (A1) for 47.5% and 190 members, (A1) for the direction argument — the segment that responds more also approves more, so the pooled figure must be too high. Credit an equivalent argument carried out in counts rather than percentages.
(d) (M1) for the conclusion that the claim is not supported, with the weighted figure used, (A1) for the point that non-respondents are unobserved and may differ, (A1) for a second developed consideration such as revenue weighting by segment, and (M1)(A1) for a recommendation that would let the claim be tested — quota sampling, follow-up of non-respondents, or reporting response rates and segment breakdowns. Do not credit 'the sample is too small' on its own: 170 responses is a large sample; the problem is which 170.

6. 6.7 An open-ended final class: the mean is not estimable and the median is, and the target has to be judged on the second

[14 marks]BM-6.7-301

Unit 6: The SL Toolkit · Unit 6: 6.7 Descriptive statistics (grouped data, the mean and median of a grouped distribution, quartiles and the interquartile range, and the choice of an appropriate average); 2.1 Introduction to human resource management · difficulty 5

(a) Using midpoints 1, 4, 7 and 10 for the first four classes and assuming the last class has the same width as the others, so a midpoint of 13 (M1), the estimated mean is (14 × 1 + 23 × 4 + 18 × 7 + 9 × 10 + 6 × 13) ÷ 70 = (14 + 92 + 126 + 90 + 78) ÷ 70 = 400 ÷ 70 = 5.71 days (A1). The assumption is that the six employees in the open class were each absent for about 13 days; nothing in the table supports it, and the class was left open precisely because those six records were not broken down (A1).
(b) With 70 employees the median is the 35th value. Cumulative frequencies are 14, 37, 55, 64 and 70, so the 35th value lies in the class 3 – 5, whose lower boundary is 2.5 and whose width is 3: median = 2.5 + ((35 − 14) ÷ 23) × 3 = 2.5 + 2.74 = 5.24 days (M1)(A1). The lower quartile is the 17.5th value, also in 3 – 5: 2.5 + ((17.5 − 14) ÷ 23) × 3 = 2.96 days. The upper quartile is the 52.5th value, in 6 – 8: 5.5 + ((52.5 − 37) ÷ 18) × 3 = 8.08 days. The interquartile range is 8.08 − 2.96 = 5.13 days (A1). No assumption is needed: these are positional measures, and the six employees in the open class affect them only by standing above them in the ordering.
(c) The six employees in the final class each have at least 12 days, but the table places no ceiling on them. If they each had 60 days, the mean would be (14 + 92 + 126 + 90 + 6 × 60) ÷ 70 = 682 ÷ 70 = 9.74 days (M1); if they each had 200 days it would be 21.74 days (A1). Since the assumed value can be increased without limit, no upper bound on the mean can be derived from the table (A1). The smallest the mean can be is when all six are at the foot of their class, 12 days: (14 + 92 + 126 + 90 + 72) ÷ 70 = 394 ÷ 70 = 5.63 days, so the table guarantees only that the mean is at least 5.63 days (A1).
(d) The claim is unsound in two ways. The figure of 5.71 days is not an estimate of the mean at all: it is the value obtained when the six longest absences are each assumed to be 13 days, and the same table is consistent with a mean of 9.74 days or of 21 days, so the mean cannot be compared with the target (M1)(A1). What the table does support is a different statement: the median is 5.24 days and therefore below the target, 8.6% of the workforce (6 of 70) were absent for at least 12 days, and the distribution is right-skewed with an interquartile range of 5.13 days — so the typical employee is comfortably inside the target and a small group is far outside it (A1). The recommendation is to report absence with positional measures and the tail: the median, the interquartile range and the percentage of employees above a threshold such as twelve days, together with the exact figures for the six long-term cases, which the HR department already holds (M1)(A1).

Markscheme notes

13. 3.5 Two transactions that move the liquidity ratios in opposite directions, and the cancellation inside the acid test

[16 marks]BM-3.5-201

Unit 3: Finance and accounts · 3.5 Profitability and liquidity ratio analysis (gross, operating and net profit margins, ROCE, current ratio, acid test); 3.4 Final accounts · difficulty 4

(a) Gross profit is revenue less cost of goods sold, so (M1)

$$\pounds 480{,}000 - \pounds 300{,}000 = \pounds 180{,}000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{180{,}000}{480{,}000} = 37.5\% \tag{A1}$$

Operating profit deducts the operating expenses: $\pounds 180{,}000 - \pounds 120{,}000 = \pounds 60{,}000$, so the operating profit margin is $60{,}000/480{,}000 = 12.5\%$ (A1).

Net profit also deducts interest and tax: $\pounds 60{,}000 - \pounds 12{,}000 - \pounds 9{,}600 = \pounds 38{,}400$, so the net profit margin is $38{,}400/480{,}000 = 8.0\%$ (A1).

The three margins fall by design: each one deducts a further layer of cost, so the sequence $37.5\%$, $12.5\%$, $8.0\%$ is a description of where the revenue goes. The largest single deduction is the $\pounds 120{,}000$ of operating expenses, which takes 25 percentage points of revenue and is the reason the gross margin is three times the operating margin.

(b) Capital employed is the long-term money in the business, which here is equity plus non-current liabilities (M1):

$$\pounds 200{,}000 + \pounds 100{,}000 = \pounds 300{,}000$$

Return on capital employed is operating profit divided by capital employed (M1):

$$\frac{60{,}000}{300{,}000} = 20.0\% \tag{A1}$$

The numerator must be operating profit, not net profit. Using net profit would give $38{,}400/300{,}000 = 12.8\%$, which understates the return on the assets because it charges the capital employed with interest that has already been deducted in arriving at net profit. Using equity alone as the denominator would give $60{,}000/200{,}000 = 30.0\%$, which overstates it by leaving the non-current liabilities out of the capital they helped to fund.

(c) Current assets are inventory, receivables and cash (M1):

$$\pounds 45{,}000 + \pounds 60{,}000 + \pounds 15{,}000 = \pounds 120{,}000$$

The current ratio is current assets over current liabilities (A1):

$$\frac{120{,}000}{60{,}000} = 2.00$$

The acid test removes inventory from the numerator, because inventory is the current asset least likely to convert to cash at its book value (A1):

$$\frac{120{,}000 - 45{,}000}{60{,}000} = \frac{75{,}000}{60{,}000} = 1.25$$

(d) Paying $\pounds 10{,}000$ in cash reduces cash to $\pounds 5{,}000$, so current assets become $\pounds 110{,}000$ and current liabilities become $\pounds 50{,}000$ (M1).

$$\text{current ratio} = \frac{110{,}000}{50{,}000} = 2.20 \qquad \text{acid test} = \frac{110{,}000 - 45{,}000}{50{,}000} = \frac{65{,}000}{50{,}000} = 1.30 \tag{A1}$$

Both ratios rise, from 2.00 to 2.20 and from 1.25 to 1.30, even though the business is now smaller by $\pounds 10{,}000$ of cash (A1).

The reason is that the same amount leaves the top and the bottom of the fraction. Writing the current ratio as $a/b$ with $a > b$, the new ratio is $(a-x)/(b-x)$, and

$$\frac{a-x}{b-x} > \frac{a}{b} \iff b(a-x) > a(b-x) \iff ab - bx > ab - ax \iff a > b$$

so the ratio rises whenever the transaction removes equal amounts from both and the ratio was above one. The size of the payment does not matter, and neither does the fact that it is a payment: any transaction that reduces current assets and current liabilities by the same amount has this effect. The same algebra run in reverse shows that adding equal amounts to both *lowers* a ratio above one, which is what part (e) does.

(e) Buying $\pounds 30{,}000$ of inventory on credit raises inventory to $\pounds 75{,}000$ and current liabilities to $\pounds 80{,}000$, so current assets become $\pounds 140{,}000$ (M1).

$$\text{current ratio} = \frac{140{,}000}{80{,}000} = 1.75 \tag{A1}$$

The acid test numerator is unchanged, because the $\pounds 30{,}000$ added to inventory is subtracted again (M1):

$$\frac{140{,}000 - 75{,}000}{80{,}000} = \frac{65{,}000}{80{,}000} = 0.8125 \approx 0.81 \tag{A1}$$

The two transactions together have moved the current ratio from 2.00 to 1.75 and the acid test from 1.25 to 0.81, so on balance the liquidity position is weaker, not stronger (A1).

The acid test is the ratio that should be read here, and the current ratio is the one that flatters. At 1.75 the current ratio still looks comfortable, and it has fallen by less than the acid test because inventory is doing the work: inventory is now $\pounds 75{,}000$ of $\pounds 140{,}000$ of current assets, so more than half of what the business counts as liquid is stock that has to be sold before it becomes cash. The acid test has crossed below 1.00, which means the business cannot settle its current liabilities from the assets it already holds in near-cash form. It would have to sell inventory, and selling inventory is what generates the receivables and cash in the first place, so the position is circular rather than merely tight.

The evaluation is not one-sided, however. The $\pounds 30{,}000$ of inventory was bought to be sold, and if it turns over as the existing stock does it will convert into receivables and then cash within the working capital cycle, restoring the acid test without any new financing. The $\pounds 10{,}000$ paid to the supplier also released credit and may have earned a settlement discount. So the transactions weaken the *reported* liquidity position while leaving the *underlying* trading position intact, and the honest conclusion is that the ratios have moved in a way the business should monitor rather than a way that shows it is in difficulty. What the figures cannot settle is how quickly the new inventory will turn over, and that is the question a lender would ask next.

14. 5.5 A break-even output above the capacity ceiling: a negative margin of safety and the three single levers that would close the gap

[16 marks]BM-5.5-201

Unit 5: Operations management · 5.5 Break-even analysis (contribution, break-even output, margin of safety, target profit); 3.3 Costs and revenues · difficulty 4

(a) Contribution per unit is the selling price less the variable cost per unit (M1):

$$\pounds 40 - \pounds 24 = \pounds 16 \tag{A1}$$

Break-even output is fixed costs divided by contribution per unit (A1):

$$\frac{200{,}000}{16} = 12{,}500 \text{ units}$$

(b) The margin of safety is the amount by which the planned output exceeds the break-even output, so with output at the capacity ceiling (M1):

$$10{,}000 - 12{,}500 = -2{,}500 \text{ units}$$

As a percentage of capacity (A1):

$$\frac{-2{,}500}{10{,}000} \times 100 = -25\%$$

A negative margin of safety means the business cannot reach break-even at any output it is able to produce (A1). Every unit it makes sells for $\pounds 16$ more than its variable cost, and that contribution is never enough to cover $\pounds 200{,}000$ of fixed costs, so the business makes a loss in every year in which it operates. The negative figure is not a shortfall to be made up by selling harder; it is a statement that the break-even point lies outside the set of outputs the business can reach.

(c) At full capacity the total contribution is (M1)

$$10{,}000 \times \pounds 16 = \pounds 160{,}000$$

and the loss is the fixed costs less that contribution (A1):

$$\pounds 160{,}000 - \pounds 200{,}000 = -\pounds 40{,}000$$

Because contribution per unit is positive, each additional unit sold reduces the loss by $\pounds 16$, so profit increases with output throughout the feasible range (M1). The largest output the line can produce is therefore the least unprofitable one, and $\pounds 40{,}000$ is a loss at the best point rather than at a poor one: there is no output at which Ridgeway Ceramics makes a profit, so the maximum profit it can achieve in a year is a loss of $\pounds 40{,}000$.

(d) Each change is found by setting the total contribution at capacity equal to the fixed costs.

For the selling price, let it be $p$ (M1):

$$10{,}000(p - 24) = 200{,}000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p - 24 = 20 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = \pounds 44.00 \tag{A1}$$

For the annual fixed costs, with contribution per unit still $\pounds 16$:

$$10{,}000 \times 16 = \pounds 160{,}000 \tag{A1}$$

For the variable cost per unit, let it be $v$:

$$10{,}000(40 - v) = 200{,}000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 40 - v = 20 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \pounds 20.00 \tag{A1}$$

So the three thresholds are a price of $\pounds 44.00$ (a rise of $\pounds 4.00$, or 10%), fixed costs of $\pounds 160{,}000$ (a cut of $\pounds 40{,}000$, or 20%) and a variable cost of $\pounds 20.00$ (a cut of $\pounds 4.00$, or 16.7%). Each figure is a threshold and not a target: at exactly that value the business breaks even at full capacity and makes nothing, so any of the three has to be bettered before a profit appears.

(e) The three changes are not equally available, and the comparison has to be made on two axes: how large the change is, and whether the business controls it.

The price rise is the smallest proportional change and it requires no investment, no renegotiation and no new equipment, which makes it attractive on paper. It is also the least likely to hold. Ridgeway sells a single product into a market it does not control, and a 10% rise has to be accepted by every customer for the arithmetic to work; if it is accepted by fewer, the contribution gain on the remaining units does not compensate for the volume lost, and at a price of $\pounds 44$ the business needs *all* 10 000 units to break even, leaving no room for any loss of demand at all. The remedy that looks cleanest is the one with the narrowest margin for error.

The fixed-cost cut is the largest change in absolute terms, $\pounds 40{,}000$, and it is the one the business controls most directly, since fixed costs are largely internal commitments. But the kiln line is the fixed cost that matters, and it cannot be halved; a 20% reduction in a single-line ceramics business means cutting maintenance, staffing or the second shift, and cutting the second shift is precisely what would reduce capacity and make the position worse. A fixed-cost cut that lowers the ceiling lowers the output the business can sell, and the break-even output would have to be recalculated against the smaller capacity rather than assumed unchanged.

The variable-cost cut is the one most likely to be achievable. $\pounds 4.00$ per unit is a 16.7% reduction in the cost of materials and power, and variable costs are the category a manufacturer can most often attack: renegotiating a clay or glaze contract, reducing kiln waste, buying in larger quantities. It requires no change to the price the customer sees and no reduction in capacity, so it does not trade one problem for another. The difficulty is that a 16.7% cut is substantial for a business whose inputs are commodity-like, and any saving is likely to arrive in stages rather than at once.

Taken together, the most likely single change is the variable-cost reduction, and the strongest reason is not that it is the largest or the smallest but that it is the only one that leaves the capacity ceiling and the selling price untouched. The most reliable answer, however, is not to rely on any single lever: a smaller price rise combined with a smaller variable-cost reduction reaches the same threshold while exposing the business to less risk on either one, and a management that pursues only one of the three is betting the whole recovery on the assumption that is hardest to verify. What the figures cannot show is how the market would respond to a price rise or how far a supplier would move, and those are the questions that decide between the options.

Contingency $= 10\% \times (EUR 110 000 + EUR 52 500) = 10\% \times EUR 162 500 = EUR 16 250$. Total finance required $= EUR 110 000 + EUR 52 500 + EUR 16 250 = EUR 178 750$. (A1)

(c) Indicative evaluation, using the financial section of the business plan.

The plan looks balanced on its own assumptions: it claims EUR 180 000 of funding against a requirement of EUR 178 750, a margin of EUR 1 250. But two of the three funding assumptions are wrong.

The bank will lend at most 60% of the value of the capital items, which is $60\% \times EUR 110 000 = EUR 66 000$, not the EUR 115 000 requested. The grant is capped at EUR 12 000, not EUR 20 000. The realistic funding is therefore $EUR 45 000 + EUR 66 000 + EUR 12 000 = EUR 123 000$, against a requirement of EUR 178 750. The shortfall is EUR 55 750, and the plan's apparent EUR 1 250 surplus becomes a EUR 55 750 hole. (M1)(A1)

The shortfall is almost entirely a working-capital problem. The business needs EUR 52 500 of cash to survive three months with no income, and the bank will not lend against it. The founders have focused on the visible assets and underestimated the cost of simply keeping the doors open.

The lease offer helps the capital side but not enough. Leasing the grooming equipment and the van (EUR 44 000) would cut capital expenditure to EUR 66 000, but it adds EUR 980 a month to running costs, raising monthly costs to EUR 18 480 and working capital to EUR 55 440. With a 10% contingency on EUR 121 440, the total requirement becomes EUR 133 584. The bank's limit then falls to EUR 39 600, so available funding is EUR 96 600 and the shortfall is EUR 36 984. Leasing reduces the gap by about EUR 19 000 but increases the monthly cost permanently.

Rob's aunt's EUR 56 000 would close the gap, but it costs 25% of the business, which both founders have said they do not want to give up. It also transfers a quarter of any future growth to someone who is contributing money rather than work.

A supported conclusion is required. A candidate who recommends delaying the opening, phasing the spend, reducing the contingency, or accepting the aunt's money, and who justifies it with the figures, should be credited. The key judgement is that the plan is not realistic as written: it understates the finance needed by about EUR 55 750 and misunderstands how a bank lends to a start-up.

(a) Two marks for the distinction, one for applying it correctly to the case, one for explaining why it affects the finance decision. A candidate who says only 'capital is long term, revenue is short term' earns one mark. Do not credit an answer that classifies the monthly rent as capital because it is paid under a lease.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for the capital total, (M1)(A1) for the monthly running cost and the three-month working capital, and (A1) for the contingency and the total. Follow through on arithmetic errors. Do not penalise a candidate who presents the contingency as 10% of capital plus working capital in a different order. (c) The two discriminating marks are for the bank's 60% limit and the grant cap. 36. A twenty per cent productivity gain that makes each garment worth less

[14 marks]BM-5.1-001

Unit 5: Operations management · Unit 5: 5.1 The role of operations management (the transformation process; adding value through operations; efficiency and effectiveness). Toolkit: descriptive statistics. · difficulty 4

(a) Pennine Workwear operates a transformation process. The inputs are bought-in fabric, thread and components costing EUR 7 a garment, together with 2 400 hours of direct labour a month and the use of the factory and machinery. The process is cutting, sewing, finishing, inspecting and packing. The outputs are finished protective garments sold at EUR 18 each, which for many customers must also be safe, durable and compliant with industry standards.

The operations function adds value in two ways. First, it converts EUR 7 of materials into a garment that sells for EUR 18 - the transformation itself is where the added value is created. Second, it is responsible for the quality and reliability that make the garment worth buying: a protective jacket that fails in use is worthless, so the inspection and finishing stages protect the price the business can charge. Operations therefore supports the whole business strategy, not just the production line.

(b) Labour productivity = garments produced per direct labour hour.

Before the change: $12 000 / 2 400 = 5.0$ garments per labour hour. (M1)(A1)
Since the change: $15 000 / 2 500 = 6.0$ garments per labour hour. (A1)
That is a rise of $(6.0 - 5.0) / 5.0 = 20\%$, which is the figure the operations manager is reporting.

Value added = revenue minus bought-in materials minus rework cost.

Before the change: revenue $= 12 000 \times EUR 18 = EUR 216 000$; materials $= 12 000 \times EUR 7 = EUR 84 000$; defects $= 2\% \times 12 000 = 240$ garments, rework $= 240 \times EUR 4 = EUR 960$. Value added $= EUR 216 000 - EUR 84 000 - EUR 960 = EUR 131 040$, or $EUR 131 040 / 12 000 = EUR 10.92$ per garment. (M1)(A1)

Since the change: revenue $= 15 000 \times EUR 18 = EUR 270 000$, less 450 garments sold as seconds at EUR 9 instead of EUR 18, a loss of $450 \times EUR 9 = EUR 4 050$, so EUR 265 950; materials $= 15 000 \times EUR 7 = EUR 105 000$; defects $= 7\% \times 15 000 = 1 050$ garments, rework $= 1 050 \times EUR 4 = EUR 4 200$. Value added $= EUR 265 950 - EUR 105 000 - EUR 4 200 = EUR 156 750$, or $EUR 156 750 / 15 000 = EUR 10.45$ per garment. (M1)(A1)

The two measures disagree. Labour productivity is up 20%, but value added per garment has fallen from EUR 10.92 to EUR 10.45, a fall of about 4.3%. Total value added has risen, from EUR 131 040 to EUR 156 750, but only because 25% more garments are being made. Each garment now adds less value than before, because the cost of rework and of selling seconds has risen faster than the extra output is worth. Efficiency has improved; effectiveness has not.

(c) Indicative evaluation, using descriptive statistics on the twelve monthly defect rates.

The mean defect rate for the year is $(2.1 + 1.8 + 2.3 + 1.9 + 2.2 + 2.1 + 6.8 + 7.1 + 6.4 + 7.6 + 8.1 + 7.4) / 12 = 55.8 / 12 = 4.65\%$. Taken alone, that looks acceptable. The median is 4.35% and the range is $8.1 - 1.8 = 6.3$ percentage points. (M1)(A1)

The mean is misleading. Splitting the data at the change gives a mean of 2.07% for months 1 to 6 and 7.23% for months 7 to 12. The process did not drift; it stepped from about 2% to about 7% at the moment the change was introduced, and the annual mean of 4.65% describes no month that actually occurred. This is the classic trap of a summary statistic that conceals a structural break.

Two of the largest customers, together worth about 30% of sales, have complained, and one has asked for a meeting. Three experienced machinists have left in four months, which will put further pressure on quality. The operations manager's bonus depends on productivity, which explains why he is recommending a further increase in line speed, but his measure ignores the cost of defects and returns.

Conclusion: the change should not be kept in its present form. The productivity gain is real, but it has been bought by cutting the quality checks that protect the EUR 18 price. A supported conclusion is required; a candidate who recommends keeping the change but restoring the inspection stage, and justifies this from the figures, is equally creditworthy.

Markscheme notes

(a) Two marks for a correct description of inputs, process and outputs, and two for explaining how operations adds value. A candidate who describes the process without linking it to value added earns at most two. Do not credit a list of departments with no explanation.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for the two productivity figures and (A1)(A1) for the two value added per garment figures. The comment mark is the discriminating one and requires the candidate to notice that productivity and value added per unit move in opposite directions. A candidate who reports only the productivity improvement, or who says 'both improved because total value added rose', forfeits the comment mark. Follow through on arithmetic slips. Accept value added calculated without the rework cost only if the same treatment is applied to both periods; a candidate who includes rework in one period and not the other cannot reach full marks.

(c) The descriptive statistics marks are for computing the mean, the median and the range, and for noticing that the annual mean conceals the step change. A candidate who reports only the mean of 4.65% and concludes that quality is acceptable has not used the data. The evaluation must end in a decision; credit either conclusion provided it is supported. Do not credit an answer that attributes the defects to staff attitudes without evidence.

37. 3.7 A six-month cash-flow forecast against an existing overdraft limit, and why re-timing receipts inside the forecast period cannot close a deficit

[12 marks]BM-3.7-201

Unit 3: Finance and accounts · Unit 3: 3.7 Cash flow (cash-flow forecasts, the difference between profit and cash, and the management of working capital); 3.4 Final accounts · difficulty 4

(a) Each month's closing balance is its opening balance plus the receipts of that month less the payments of that month, and the opening balance of each month is the closing balance of the month before (M1). January opens at 12 000 and closes at $12 + 20 - 24 = 8\,000$; February opens at 8 000 and closes at $8 + 18 - 23 = 3\,000$; March opens at 3 000 and closes at $3 + 14 - 26 = -9\,000$; April opens at minus 9 000 and closes at $-9 + 16 - 13 = -6\,000$; May opens at minus 6 000 and closes at $-6 + 22 - 13 = 3\,000$; and June opens at 3 000 and closes at $3 + 26 - 15 = 14\,000$ (A1). The closing balances are therefore 8, 3, minus 9, minus 6, 3 and 14 thousand, and the lowest of them is minus 9 000 in March (A1). It is worth noting that the business recovers by May with no action at all, which is why this is a liquidity problem rather than a solvency one: the cash is arriving late rather than not arriving, and the question is only whether the business can survive the gap.

(b) The existing limit is minus 5 000, so the March closing balance of minus 9 000 breaches it by $9\,000 - 5\,000 = 4\,000$ (M1)(A1). March is the month in which the breach is deepest, but it is not the only one: the April balance of minus 6 000 breaches the same limit by 1 000, so the facility is exceeded in two consecutive months.

(c) Bringing 8 000 of April's receipts into March raises March's receipts from 14 000 to 22 000 and reduces April's from 16 000 to 8 000, leaving the total for the two months unchanged at 30 000 (M1). Re-chaining the balances gives 8, 3, minus 1, minus 6, 3 and 14 thousand (A1). The lowest balance is now minus 6 000, in April, so the breach has been reduced from 4 000 to 1 000 but has not been removed (A1). The proposal therefore fails, and the reason is structural rather than arithmetic. The closing balance at the end of April is the opening balance of 12 000 plus the receipts of the first four months less the payments of the first four months, that is $12 + 68 - 86 = -6$, and that expression contains no reference to which month any receipt falls in, so moving a receipt from April to March cannot change it (A1). The discount also carries a cost the director has not counted: paying 8 000 early in return for a discount of, say, two per cent reduces the cash actually received by about 160, so the position after the proposal is slightly worse than the figures above show.

(d) Any re-timing of receipts or payments inside January to April changes when cash arrives but not how much cash the window contains, and the closing balance at the end of April is fixed by that total (M1). Delaying a March payment into April has exactly the same effect as bringing an April receipt into March: the March balance improves and the April balance gives the improvement back, so the deficit moves rather than closes, and the best that re-timing can achieve is a lowest balance of minus 6 000, which is still a breach of 1 000 (A1). What would work is permanent cash arriving before the trough. A loan or a capital injection of 4 000 received in January raises every later closing balance by 4 000 and gives 12, 7, minus 5, minus 2, 7 and 18 thousand, whose lowest balance of minus 5 000 sits exactly on the limit (A1). Extending the overdraft facility to 9 000 would achieve the same result by changing the constraint rather than the cash, and a permanent reduction of 4 000 in payments, for example by renegotiating the March payment run, would achieve it by raising the cumulative total. The one option that is not available is to reschedule inside the window, because the total the window contains is already fixed.

(a) (M1) for the method of chaining opening balance, receipts and payments, (A1)(A1) for the six closing balances and the identification of March. Accept a candidate who shows the balances as a running total rather than in a table. A candidate who computes receipts minus payments without carrying the opening balance forward obtains 4, 5, 12, 3, 9 and 11 and scores no marks: the whole point of the part is that the opening balance of each month is the previous closing balance.
(b) (M1) for comparing the lowest balance with the limit, (A1) for 4 000 and March. Accept 'exceeds the limit by 4 000' without the month, provided the month is named somewhere in the part. Follow-through applies from (a).
(c) (M1) for the revised receipts, (A1) for the revised balances, (A1) for the conclusion that the breach is not removed, (A1) for the reason. The reason must be about the invariance of the cumulative total, not merely an assertion that the deficit has moved: a candidate who says only that 'the problem now happens in April' scores (A1) for the conclusion and no mark for the reason. Accept a candidate who reaches minus 6 000 by any correct route.
(d) (M1) for the statement that re-timing cannot change the total cash in the window, (A1) for the numerical consequence, that the best achievable balance is minus 6 000, (A1) for a workable recommendation supported by figures. Accept any of the three routes named in the answer, and accept a candidate who recommends deferring a payment beyond April provided the figures are given. A recommendation with no figures scores (M1) at most.

38. 4.1 Market share rising in both regions while the overall share falls

[11 marks]BM-4.1-401

Unit 4: Marketing · Unit 4: 4.1 The role of marketing (market share and market growth); 4.2 Marketing planning; 6.7 Descriptive statistics (weighted averages and the comparison of groups) · difficulty 4

(a) In year 1 the firm sells 80 + 210 = 290 units out of a market of 1 000, so its overall share is 290 / 1 000 = 29.0 per cent. In year 2 it sells 147 + 111 = 258 units, so its overall share is 258 / 1 000 = 25.8 per cent (M1)(A1). The North market grew from 400 to 700 units, a growth of (700 - 400) / 400 = 75 per cent. The South market fell from 600 to 300 units, a decline of (600 - 300) / 600 = 50 per cent (A1).

(b) An overall market share is not an average of the two regional shares taken equally; it is a weighted average in which the weight of each region is the size of that region's market. In year 1 the weights are 400 and 600, so the overall share is (400 x 0.20 + 600 x 0.35) / 1 000 = (80 + 210) / 1 000 = 0.290. In year 2 the weights are 700 and 300, giving (700 x 0.21 + 300 x 0.37) / 1 000 = (147 + 111) / 1 000 = 0.258 (M1)(A1). Both regional shares did rise, from 20.0 to 21.0 per cent in the North and from 35.0 to 37.0 per cent in the South, so the firm is right about its own performance in each region (A1). But the weight moved at the same time and moved against the firm: the North, where it holds only about a fifth of the market, grew by 75 per cent and now carries 70 per cent of the overall market, while the South, where it holds more than a third, halved. The gain from performing better in each region is more than cancelled by the loss from being under-represented in the region that grew, and the two effects together leave the overall share 3.2 percentage points lower (A1).

(c) The claim is half right, and the half that is wrong matters. It is wrong to say the firm performed worse: its share rose in both regions, from 20.0 to 21.0 per cent and from 35.0 to 37.0 per cent, and a rising share in a market is the clearest available evidence of competitive performance (A1). But it is true that the year was a setback, and the reason is not only the change in the mix. The firm's own volume fell from 290 units to 258, a decline of 11 per cent, in a market whose total size did not change at all; the mixture effect explains why the share fell faster than the volume did, but it does not explain the fall in volume, and a firm that sells fewer units in a static market has lost ground by any commercial measure (A1). The mixture effect also understates the danger, because the region that shrank is the region where the firm is strong: if the South keeps contracting at 50 per cent a year, the firm's strongest position disappears on its own, whatever it does about its share (M1). The marketing plan should therefore concentrate on the North. At 700 units and growing at 75 per cent, the North is where the market is, and the firm holds only 21 per cent of it. Lifting the North share to the level already achieved in the South, 37 per cent, would mean selling 259 units there instead of 147, an extra 112 units, and would raise the overall share to (259 + 111) / 1 000 = 37.0 per cent, well above the 29.0 per cent of year 1 (A1). Chasing share in the South would be harder work for less: the market there is halving, so gaining share in it means winning customers in a shrinking pool while the firm's absolute volume still falls. The recommendation is a North-led plan with a target share stated as a number, and the South treated as a market to defend rather than to grow.

(a) (M1) for summing the firm's units in each year; (A1) for 29.0 and 25.8 per cent; (A1) for +75 per cent in the North and -50 per cent in the South. Accept the shares given as 290/1 000 and 258/1 000 without conversion to percentages.

(b) (M1) for identifying the overall share as a weighted average with the regional market sizes as weights; (A1) for the two weighted calculations producing 0.290 and 0.258; (A1) for stating the two regional shares as rising, 20.0 to 21.0 and 35.0 to 37.0; (A1) for the explanation that the weight moved towards the region where the firm is weak, with the 400-to-700 and 600-to-300 change cited. A candidate who calls the result impossible, or who says one of the figures must be wrong, scores nothing: the part is testing whether the arithmetic of a changing mixture has been understood. Do not accept a purely verbal answer that does not produce the two weighted sums.

(c) (A1) for rejecting the claim that performance fell, with the two regional increases cited; (A1) for the separate observation that the firm's own volume fell from 290 to 258 in an unchanged market, which is a real loss and not a mixing effect; (M1) for the strategic risk that the strong region is the shrinking one; (A1) for a quantified recommendation. Accept any quantified target in the North, such as matching the South's 37 per cent share, or restoring the 29 per cent overall share, provided the calculation is shown. Do not accept 'increase advertising' or 'improve the product' without a figure and a region.

39. 4.1 Market share rising from 18 to 21 per cent while unit sales, total contribution and operating profit all fall, because the market the share is measured against contracted faster than the firm did

[16 marks]BM-4.1-201

Unit 4: Marketing · Unit 4: 4.1 The role of marketing (market share and market growth); Unit 3: 3.3 Costs and revenues (contribution). Toolkit: descriptive statistics. · difficulty 3

(a) Market share is the firm's own unit sales expressed as a percentage of the total market. For Year 1,
$$\frac{180\,000}{1\,000\,000} \times 100 = 18.0\ \%,$$
and for Year 2,
$$\frac{168\,000}{800\,000} \times 100 = 21.0\ \% \;(M1)(A1).$$
The change is therefore $21.0 - 18.0 = +3.0$ percentage points (A1). It is worth noting at once that the share has risen by three points while the numerator has fallen, which means the denominator must have fallen faster; part (b) confirms that it did.

(b) Harrowgate's own unit sales fell from 180 000 to 168 000, a change of
$$\frac{168\,000 - 180\,000}{180\,000} \times 100 = -6.67\ \% \;(M1)(A1).$$
The market fell from 1 000 000 units to 800 000 units, a change of
$$\frac{800\,000 - 1\,000\,000}{1\,000\,000} \times 100 = -20.0\ \% \;(A1).$$
The difference is $-6.67 - (-20.0) = +13.33$ percentage points. Harrowgate therefore outperformed the market by a wide margin, losing volume more slowly than the market as a whole did. That is a real achievement and it is what the share figure is measuring, but it is a statement about relative performance and says nothing about whether the firm is bigger, more profitable or better off than it was.

(c) Unit contribution is the selling price less the variable cost per unit, $24.00 - 15.00 = 9.00$ dollars (M1). Total contribution is that figure multiplied by the units sold:

$$\text{Year 1}: 180\,000 \times 9.00 = \$1\,620\,000,$$
$$\text{Year 2}: 168\,000 \times 9.00 = \$1\,512\,000 \;(A1),$$
a fall of $\$108\,000$. Operating profit is contribution less fixed costs less the marketing budget:

$$\text{Year 1}: 1\,620\,000 - 1\,140\,000 - 420\,000 = \$60\,000,$$
$$\text{Year 2}: 1\,512\,000 - 1\,140\,000 - 510\,000 = -\$138\,000 \;(A1)(A1).$$
So the change in operating profit is $-138\,000 - 60\,000 = -\$198\,000$, and it splits into two parts: $\$108\,000$ lost because twelve thousand fewer pots were sold at nine dollars of contribution each, and $\$90\,000$ spent on the additional marketing budget. The firm moved from a small profit into a loss in the year in which its market share rose.

(d) Market share is a ratio of two quantities and it is sensitive to both. When the market contracts faster than the firm does, the share rises even though the firm's own sales are falling (M1). A share is therefore a statement about the firm's position relative to its competitors, not about the size of the business, and it cannot be read as a measure of the marketing function's own effectiveness without knowing what happened to the denominator (A1). In this case the marketing budget rose by 21.4 per cent while unit sales fell by 6.67 per cent, contribution fell by $108 000 and the firm moved into loss, so the one measure that improved is the one measure that ignores the market. A share gain earned by standing still in a collapsing market is a different achievement from a share gain earned by growing, and the figures here are of the first kind.

(e) The case for approving a further increase is that the share rose by three points and the firm beat the market by 13.33 percentage points while spending an extra $90 000. If the market were stable, that would be a strong result. But the market is not stable: it contracted by twenty per cent in a single year, and the firm's own volume, contribution and profit all fell, with operating profit turning from a $60 000 profit into a $138 000 loss. The extra spend therefore bought relative position in a category that is shrinking, and there is no evidence in the figures that the extra $90 000 reversed the decline: it coincided with a year in which the firm lost more contribution than it spent. The judgement the board has to make is whether the decline in the market is temporary or structural. If it is temporary, spending to hold share is defensible as defensive investment, because the firm will be well placed when the market recovers, and the 13.33 point outperformance suggests the marketing is working. If it is structural, the same spending is throwing money at a shrinking category, and the board should either cut the budget and manage for cash or diversify out of garden pots. On the figures alone the second reading is better supported: the firm is now making a loss, the market has fallen by a fifth, and a further increase would raise the break-even volume at exactly the moment when volume is falling. A defensible answer must therefore recommend that the budget increase be refused or made conditional on evidence that the market has stopped contracting, and must say what evidence would change that judgement.
