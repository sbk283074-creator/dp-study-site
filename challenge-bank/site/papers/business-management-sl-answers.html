1. BM-6.6-001 16 marks

(a) The twelve monthly figures are 40, 45, 38, 42, 120, 130, 125, 44, 41, 118, 135 and 46, giving a total of 924 customers a day across the months. (M1)

Mean = 924/12 = 77 customers a day. (A1)

Ordered, the two middle values are 45 and 46, so the median = (45 + 46)/2 = 45.5 customers a day. (A1)

Range = 135 - 38 = 97 customers a day. (A1)

The mean is a poor summary of a typical month. It is 31.5 customers a day above the median, and no month was close to it: five months clustered near 42 and five near 125, with the remaining two between. The distribution is bimodal, not symmetrical, so the mean describes a month that never happened. (A1)

(b) High-demand months are those above 80 customers a day: months 5, 6, 7, 10 and 11, so five of twelve. The trial estimate of the probability of high demand is 5/12 = 0.417. (M1)(A1)

Using the finance director's 0.6: kiosk = 0.6 x 40,000 + 0.4 x 10,000 = EUR 28,000; cafe = 0.6 x 120,000 + 0.4 x (-60,000) = EUR 48,000. (M1)(A1)

Interest: kiosk loan 40,000 x 6% = EUR 2,400; cafe loan 120,000 x 6% = EUR 7,200. Net expected profit: kiosk EUR 25,600; cafe EUR 40,800. (A1)

Using the trial estimate of 5/12: kiosk = (5/12) x 40,000 + (7/12) x 10,000 = EUR 22,500; cafe = (5/12) x 120,000 + (7/12) x (-60,000) = EUR 15,000. After interest: kiosk EUR 20,100; cafe EUR 7,800. (A1)

The two probabilities therefore rank the options differently.

(c) Indicative evaluation. On the finance director's probability the decision is straightforward. The cafe's expected profit of EUR 40,800 is more than half as much again as the kiosk's EUR 25,600, and the upside, EUR 120,000 in a good year, is the kind of return that would justify the larger loan. A decision tree drawn on a 0.6 chance of high demand points clearly to the cafe.

The descriptive statistics undermine the probability the tree uses. The mean of 77 customers a day is not a typical month: it sits between two clusters, and the median of 45.5 is the better guide to what most months look like. Five of the twelve months were high-demand, so the empirical probability is 0.417, not 0.6. Rerun with 5/12, the ranking reverses: the kiosk earns EUR 20,100 against the cafe's EUR 7,800. The kiosk is also the more robust choice, because its worst case is a profit of EUR 10,000 while the cafe's is a loss of EUR 60,000, half the size of the whole investment.

The evaluation therefore turns on the probability, and the probability is the one thing the directors cannot observe. If the town's economy really does deliver high demand six years in ten, the cafe is right; if the trial stall is representative, the cafe destroys value in most years and the interest payment makes the bad case worse. The strongest answers note that a decision tree is only as good as its probabilities, that the trial was one stall in one year, and that opening the kiosk first keeps the option open. Credit any supported conclusion. (A1)(A1)(A1)(A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1)(A1) for the mean, (A1) for the median and (A1) for the range. The comment mark requires the candidate to notice that the mean is well above the median and that the data are bimodal; a candidate who says only that the mean is 77 forfeits it. Accept 45.5 for the median.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for identifying the five high-demand months and the probability of 5/12; accept 0.42 or 41.7%. Award (M1)(A1) for the two expected profits on the 0.6 probability and (A1) for the interest and net profits; then (A1) for the two expected profits on 5/12 and their net values. Follow through on arithmetic slips, but a candidate who omits the interest in one option and not the other cannot reach the evaluation marks.

(c) The discriminating marks are for rerunning the expected values with the empirical probability and for noticing that the ranking reverses. A candidate who reports the mean of 77 and recommends the cafe cannot earn more than two marks. Credit a conclusion that chooses the cafe only if it is justified by evidence about the local economy rather than by the finance director's assumption. Do not credit answers about the cafe's menu or staffing.

2. BM-3.8-001 15 marks

(a) Project A. Cumulative cash flow: after Year 1, €120 000; after Year 2, €280 000; after Year 3, €460 000. The investment of €400 000 is recovered during Year 3, when €120 000 of the €180 000 inflow is still needed. (M1)

Payback $=2+\dfrac{120\,000}{180\,000}=2.67$ years. (A1)

Project B. Cumulative cash flow: after Year 1, €60 000; after Year 2, €160 000; after Year 3, €320 000; after Year 4, €560 000. The investment is recovered during Year 4, when €80 000 of the €240 000 inflow is still needed. (M1)

Payback $=3+\dfrac{80\,000}{240\,000}=3.33$ years. (A1)

(b) Project A: total net cash flow is $120\,000+160\,000+180\,000+80\,000=540\,000$, so total profit is $540\,000-400\,000=140\,000$ and average annual profit is $\dfrac{140\,000}{4}=35\,000$. (M1)

ARR $=\dfrac{35\,000}{400\,000}\times 100=8.75\%$. (A1)

Project B: total net cash flow is €560 000, so total profit is €160 000 and average annual profit is $\dfrac{160\,000}{4}=40\,000$. ARR $=\dfrac{40\,000}{400\,000}\times 100=10.00\%$. (A1)

(c) Project A: the present values of the inflows are $120\,000(0.909)=109\,080$; $160\,000(0.826)=132\,160$; $180\,000(0.751)=135\,180$; and $80\,000(0.683)=54\,640$. The total present value is €431 060. (M1)(A1)

NPV of A $=431\,060-400\,000=+€31\,060$. (A1)

Project B: the present values are $60\,000(0.909)=54\,540$; $100\,000(0.826)=82\,600$; $160\,000(0.751)=120\,160$; and $240\,000(0.683)=163\,920$, totalling €421 220. NPV of B $=421\,220-400\,000=+€21\,220$. (A1)

(d) Project B also requires €50 000 of working capital at the start, an immediate outflow that must be discounted at the Year 0 factor of 1.000, and it recovers €50 000 at the end of Year 4, whose present value is $50\,000(0.683)=34\,150$. (M1)

Revised NPV of B $=421\,220-400\,000-50\,000+34\,150=+€5\,370$. (A1)

Recommendation: accept Project A. (A1)

Justification: Project A is superior on two of the three methods. It pays back in 2.67 years against 3.33, which matters because the loan is being serviced at 10% and a shorter payback shortens the period during which the company carries the risk of the cash flows not materialising. Its NPV is €31 060 against €5 370 for Project B once the working capital is included, and since the money is borrowed at exactly the 10% used to discount, the NPV is the method that measures the effect on shareholder wealth directly. Project B is only superior on ARR, at 10.00% against 8.75%, and that measure is misleading here because it ignores the timing of the cash flows and, as presented, ignores the working capital entirely. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Parts (a) to (c): award the method marks for a correct cumulative total and the accuracy marks for the final figure; accept answers given in years and months if the conversion is correct. Part (b): accept an alternative ARR that uses average annual profit as a percentage of average investment, provided the formula is stated, but do not allow a candidate to mix the two formulae across the two projects. Part (c): candidates who discount the €400 000 investment should be corrected but not penalised twice, since the investment is at Year 0 where the discount factor is 1.000 anyway; credit their NPV if the arithmetic is otherwise correct. Part (d): the working-capital treatment is the discriminating mark. A candidate who subtracts €50 000 but forgets to add back the discounted recovery will obtain €21 220 − €50 000 = −€28 780 and conclude that B should be rejected; award the method mark and the recommendation mark, but not the accuracy mark. The justification marks require reference to at least two of the three methods and to the 10% cost of borrowing; a recommendation that rests on payback alone is incomplete. Follow-through applies throughout: a candidate whose NPVs are both wrong but who reasons consistently from their own figures should still earn the justification mark.

3. BM-5.4-001 15 marks

(a) Site X: total cost $=280\,000+1.80(220\,000)=280\,000+396\,000=€676\,000$. (M1)(A1)

Site Y: total cost $=140\,000+2.60(220\,000)=140\,000+572\,000=€712\,000$. (A1)

Site X is cheaper by €36 000 per year at this level of demand. (A1)

(b) Setting the two total costs equal: $280\,000+1.80q=140\,000+2.60q$. (M1)

So $140\,000=0.80q$ and $q=175\,000$ units. (M1)(A1)

Site Y is cheaper below this output and Site X above it, because Site X trades higher fixed costs for a lower variable cost per unit.

(c) Site X: $0.6(676\,000)+0.4(496\,000)=405\,600+198\,400=€604\,000$. (M1)(A1)

Site Y: $0.6(712\,000)+0.4(452\,000)=427\,200+180\,800=€608\,000$. (A1)

The expected costs differ by only €4000 per year on a commitment of more than half a million euros per year, which is well under 1% of the total. (A1) The expected-value calculation therefore does not settle the question: whichever site is chosen, the company is making almost the same bet on cost, and the decision will be decided by which risk the management team is more willing to carry rather than by the arithmetic. It is also worth noting that the expected value conceals the shape of the risk: Site Y is cheaper in the low-demand outcome and more expensive in the high-demand outcome, so choosing Y is choosing to be relatively safe if demand disappoints, while Site X is choosing to be better off if demand materialises.

(d) Recommendation: choose Site X, the city centre. (A1)

The quantitative case is narrow but consistent: Site X is €36 000 per year cheaper at the higher demand level, it has the lower expected cost at €604 000 against €608 000, and its crossover point of 175 000 units sits well below the 220 000 units that marketing considers more likely than not. Since the lease runs for ten years and the higher-demand outcome is the one the company's growth plan depends on, the cheaper variable cost is the more valuable characteristic to lock in. (A1)

The decisive qualitative factor is space. Site X has no room to expand at any price, so if demand exceeds 220 000 units the company would have to acquire a second site and duplicate management, which would destroy most of the cost advantage it has just calculated. Site Y has a large plot available now, which gives the business the option to grow without a second location. (A1)

The counter-argument is labour. Site X has a large local pool of skilled production staff, while Site Y would require training, which adds cost and time and creates a dependency on retaining the staff it trains. A preserves manufacturer depends on consistent product quality, so the availability of experienced staff reduces operational risk. On balance the expected cost difference is too small to decide the matter, and the choice between the two risks comes down to which the company fears more: a permanent capacity ceiling on a growing product, or an operating cost it cannot control. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a): award the method mark for the linear cost structure and the accuracy marks for the two totals; a candidate who omits the fixed cost entirely should be corrected, because the whole point of the comparison is the trade-off between fixed and variable cost. Part (b): accept the answer obtained by any correct method, including a graphical one, provided the crossover output is identified. Part (c): accept an answer rounded to the nearest thousand, but require the two expected costs to be compared rather than merely stated. The comment mark depends on the candidate noticing how small the difference is relative to the size of the commitment; a candidate who simply says 'Site X is cheaper' has not commented. Part (d): the recommendation mark is independent of which site is chosen, provided the reasoning is internally consistent. The two justification marks require one qualitative factor handled well rather than two factors listed, and a candidate who merely quotes from the table without explaining the consequence for the business does not reach them. Do not accept a recommendation that ignores the expansion constraint, since it is the factor that makes the ten-year lease relevant.

4. BM-5.2-001 15 marks

(a) The consultant's case for flow production rests on volume and cost. The bench is a single, standardised product made in large numbers - 48 000 units a year - and flow production is designed for exactly this: a continuous sequence of operations with little or no changeover, which spreads a high fixed cost over a large output and gives a low variable cost. Second, demand for the bench is predictable in total, and the consultant argues that a dedicated line would remove the batch changeovers that currently interrupt production. (A1)(A1)

The eleven other products are the problem. Flow production is only economic when a line can run for long periods without being stopped and altered. Hartswood makes twelve variants in total, and a flow line for the bench cannot make tables, chairs or the other bench designs. Those products must stay on batch production, so the investment would improve the cost of one product out of twelve while leaving the rest of the factory unchanged. (A1)(A1)

(b) Mean monthly demand $= 48 000 / 12 = 4 000$ benches. (A1)

Peak demand: 80% of 48 000 $= 38 400$ benches are sold in three months, so peak monthly demand $= 38 400 / 3 = 12 800$ benches. (M1)(A1)

Unit cost at 48 000 units:

Batch: fixed cost per unit $= EUR 180 000 / 48 000 = EUR 3.75$; plus variable cost EUR 18.25, giving EUR 22.00 per bench. (M1)(A1)
Flow: fixed cost per unit $= EUR 480 000 / 48 000 = EUR 10.00$; plus variable cost EUR 10.00, giving EUR 20.00 per bench. (A1)

Comment: at 48 000 units flow production is EUR 2.00 a bench cheaper, a saving of EUR 96 000 a year, and the crossover point at which flow becomes cheaper is $(EUR 480 000 - EUR 180 000) / (EUR 18.25 - EUR 10.00) = 36 364$ benches. Since annual demand is 48 000, the arithmetic appears to favour flow - but this comparison ignores the constraint that the flow line's capacity of 8 000 benches a month imposes on a seasonal product.

(c) Indicative evaluation, using descriptive statistics and a decision tree.

The descriptive statistics show why the annual average is the wrong basis for the decision. Mean monthly demand is 4 000 benches, but peak monthly demand is 12 800 and off-season demand is about 1 067. The range is therefore about 11 733 benches a month. The flow line can make only 8 000 a month, so in each of the three peak months it is short by $12 800 - 8 000 = 4 800$ benches. Over the three months the shortfall is 14 400 benches, all of which must be made in advance and stored. (M1)(A1)

Storage costs EUR 3 a bench a month, and the consultant expects an average holding period of four months: $14 400 \times EUR 3 \times 4 = EUR 172 800$ a year. The EUR 96 000 saving from flow production is therefore more than wiped out, leaving flow production EUR 76 800 a year more expensive than batch at the higher demand level. (A1)

The decision tree makes the same point under uncertainty. Marketing estimates a 0.6 probability that demand is 48 000 and a 0.4 probability that it is 30 000.

Batch production: at 48 000, cost $= EUR 180 000 + EUR 18.25 \times 48 000 = EUR 1 056 000$; at 30 000, cost $= EUR 180 000 + EUR 547 500 = EUR 727 500$. Expected cost $= 0.6 \times EUR 1 056 000 + 0.4 \times EUR 727 500 = EUR 924 600$. (M1)(A1)

Flow production: at 48 000, cost $= EUR 480 000 + EUR 10 \times 48 000 = EUR 960 000$, plus storage of EUR 172 800, giving EUR 1 132 800. At 30 000, peak monthly demand is $0.8 \times 30 000 / 3 = 8 000$, which exactly equals the line's capacity, so no storage is needed and cost $= EUR 480 000 + EUR 300 000 = EUR 780 000$. Expected cost $= 0.6 \times EUR 1 132 800 + 0.4 \times EUR 780 000 = EUR 991 680$. (M1)(A1)

Batch production has the lower expected cost, by EUR 67 080, and the conclusion does not depend on the probabilities: flow is more expensive in the high-demand state and also more expensive in the low-demand state, so no plausible weighting reverses it.

The flow line would also commit EUR 600 000, would be difficult to change over, and could not make the other eleven products. Conclusion: Hartswood should not invest in the flow line. A stronger answer will note that the same analysis would justify revisiting flow production if the company could smooth demand, reduce the product range, or add a second line to raise peak capacity.

Markscheme notes

(a) Two marks for the consultant's reasoning (high volume of a standardised product; low unit cost) and two for the constraint created by the other eleven products. A candidate who says only 'flow production is cheaper' has not explained why it would be suitable and earns one mark.

(b) Award (A1) for the mean and (M1)(A1) for the peak monthly demand. The peak is the discriminating figure: a candidate who divides 48 000 by 12 and uses 4 000 as the monthly requirement has not understood the seasonality. Award (M1)(A1) for the batch unit cost and (A1) for the flow unit cost. The comment must note that flow is cheaper on unit cost alone and identify the crossover, but that the comparison ignores capacity.

(c) Award (M1)(A1) for the pre-build quantity of 14 400 benches and the storage cost of EUR 172 800; a candidate who omits the storage cost cannot earn the evaluation marks, because that omission is exactly the error the question is testing. Award the decision-tree marks for the two expected costs. A candidate who computes the expected costs without the storage cost will obtain EUR 888 000 for flow against EUR 924 600 for batch and conclude, wrongly, that flow is cheaper; award the method marks but not the evaluation marks, and credit the candidate if they notice the capacity constraint in words even without the full calculation. Credit any supported conclusion.

5. BM-6.1-001 15 marks

(a) SWOT is an audit of the firm's own position. It sorts factors into strengths and weaknesses, which are internal and controllable, and opportunities and threats, which are external, and it is used to match the firm's resources to its environment. STEEPLE examines the external environment alone, across social, technological, economic, environmental, political, legal and ethical factors, and is used to identify change that the firm does not control. (A1)(A1)

The difference matters here. The case example for SWOT is the supermarket leaflet contract, which the directors call a strength because it is long-standing and gives a predictable order book. The case example for STEEPLE is the social and technological shift away from printed leaflets towards supermarket apps, which is outside the firm's control and is shrinking the market for the product it prints. (A1)(A1)

(b) Total revenue: 2023 = 900 + 250 + 150 = EUR 1,300k; 2025 = 590 + 610 + 170 = EUR 1,370k. (M1)(A1)

Shares of total: leaflet printing falls from 900/1,300 = 69.2% to 590/1,370 = 43.1%; packaging rises from 250/1,300 = 19.2% to 610/1,370 = 44.5%; signage rises from 150/1,300 = 11.5% to 170/1,370 = 12.4%. (A1)

Total revenue rose by EUR 70k, or 5.4%, so the business looks as though it is growing. Revenue per employee: 2023 = 1,300,000/40 = EUR 32,500; 2025 = 1,370,000/40 = EUR 34,250. (M1)(A1)

The comment mark is for seeing that the headline is misleading. The total is up only because packaging has grown by 144%, from EUR 250k to EUR 610k, while the line the directors are defending has fallen by EUR 310k, or 34.4%, and is now smaller than packaging. The firm's future is already being decided by the line its SWOT calls a weakness. (A1)

(c) Indicative evaluation. The SWOT points one way. The leaflet contract is the firm's oldest relationship and its largest single order, and losing it would remove about 43% of revenue at a stroke. Spending EUR 60,000 on a faster press lowers the unit cost of the work the contract needs and may make Calderbank the cheapest bidder when the contract is next renewed. On this reading the EUR 60,000 protects the core of the business.

The STEEPLE reading contradicts it. The fall in leaflet revenue is not caused by anything inside the firm: readers are moving to apps, the supermarket has begun cutting print volumes, and the tax on virgin paper and extended producer responsibility raise the cost of the very product the press would print. A faster press makes Calderbank better at producing something its customer is buying less of. The weakness the SWOT admits, no packaging design experience, sits in the one line that is growing, and packaging faces rising demand because of the same environmental and legal pressures that are hurting leaflets. (A1)(A1)

A defensible answer can still support the press, because it buys time and cash while packaging capability is built. But the strongest answers notice that the two tools classify the same contract differently, as a strength internally and a threat externally, and recommend using the leaflet cash flow to fund packaging rather than to defend the declining line. Credit any supported conclusion. (A1)(A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for each of the two tool descriptions and one for each correctly applied example. A candidate who describes SWOT and STEEPLE accurately but uses no case example earns at most two. Do not credit a SWOT that lists only internal factors or a STEEPLE that includes the firm's own capabilities.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for the two totals and (A1) for the shares; accept shares to one decimal place. Award (M1)(A1) for revenue per employee. The comment mark requires the candidate to contrast the falling leaflet line with the rising packaging line; a candidate who reports only that total revenue grew forfeits it. Follow through on arithmetic slips.

(c) The two discriminating marks are for recognising that the decline is externally driven and that the admitted weakness sits in the growing line. A candidate who simply repeats the SWOT and recommends the press cannot earn more than two marks, however well written. Credit a conclusion that keeps the press only if it is justified by timing or cash flow, not by the claim that leaflets will recover. Do not credit generic statements about the internet that are not tied to the case.

6. BM-6.2-001 15 marks

(a) Relative market share = own share divided by the largest rival's share. Puppy Complete 22/20 = 1.10; Adult Complete 34/18 = 1.89; Senior Care 7/26 = 0.27; Grain-Free Fresh 9/24 = 0.38. (M1)(A1)(A1)

Growth: Puppy Complete 14% (high), Adult Complete 3% (low), Senior Care 2% (low), Grain-Free Fresh 16% (high).

Classification: Puppy Complete is a star, with high growth and high relative share. Adult Complete is a cash cow, with low growth and high relative share. Senior Care is a dog, with low growth and low relative share. Grain-Free Fresh is a question mark, with high growth and low relative share. (A1)(A1)

(b) Ansoff classifies growth by whether the product and the market are existing or new. Market development means an existing product sold into a new market: Adult Complete sold through the supermarket value channel, which Brackenwood does not currently supply. The product already exists; only the market is new. (A1)(A1)

Market penetration means more of an existing product sold into the existing market: winning more shelf space for Adult Complete in the pet shops and garden centres it already uses. (A1)

Also creditable: product development, such as new Grain-Free recipes for existing premium customers, and diversification, a new product for a new market, which is the riskiest quadrant. (A1)

(c) Indicative evaluation. The BCG reading is clear. Adult Complete has a relative market share of 1.89 and market growth of only 3%, so it is a classic cash cow: the matrix says take its cash and invest it in the star, Puppy Complete. On the premium-segment numbers this is textbook, and harvesting means withdrawing promotion and product development from Adult Complete rather than reinvesting in it.

Ansoff contradicts that instruction. The lowest-risk growth available to Brackenwood is market development: taking the existing Adult Complete product into the supermarket value segment. That is a new market for an existing product, the second-least-risky quadrant, and it needs investment, which is exactly what harvesting removes. So the BCG instruction to take cash out of Adult Complete would close the one low-risk growth option the firm has.

The data make the clash worse. The classification depends on how the market is defined. Measured against the premium segment, Adult Complete has a relative share of 1.89; measured against the whole dog-food market, its share is 11% against the value leader's 13%, a relative share of 0.85, which is a dog, not a cash cow. The same product carries opposite BCG labels. The tool cannot settle the question until the directors decide which market they intend to compete in, and that is a strategic choice, not a calculation.

A defensible answer can still harvest: the firm is small, Puppy Complete is the star, and cash is scarce. The strongest answers notice that harvesting Adult Complete would destroy the Ansoff option the same product makes possible, and recommend funding market development of the cash cow instead. Credit any supported conclusion. (A1)(A1)(A1)(A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for the relative-share method and (A1)(A1) for the four correct figures. Award (A1)(A1) for the four correct classifications; a candidate who misclassifies one product but applies the 10% and 1.0 rules consistently earns one of the two. Accept 0.38 or 0.375 for Grain-Free Fresh and 0.27 for Senior Care. A candidate who computes relative share as the largest rival divided by the firm's own share reverses the matrix and cannot earn the classification marks.

(b) Two marks for market development correctly identified and applied to the supermarket channel; one mark for a correctly applied market penetration example; one further mark for either product development or diversification correctly applied. Do not credit new product, new market as market development.

(c) The discriminating marks are for seeing that the Ansoff market-development option requires the very investment that BCG harvesting removes, and for noticing that the cash-cow label depends on the premium-segment definition. A candidate who simply repeats the BCG classification and recommends harvesting cannot earn more than two marks. Credit a conclusion that harvests only if it is justified by the firm's lack of funds, not by the claim that Adult Complete has no growth left.

7. BM-6.5-001 15 marks

(a) A circular business model keeps materials in use rather than disposing of them after one cycle. Two ways it could cut Dale & Vale's costs. First, a glass bottle that is reused replaces many single-use bottles, so the dairy stops buying a new EUR 0.12 plastic bottle for every delivery. Second, it avoids the waste-disposal and packaging charges attached to single-use plastic and reduces exposure to future levies on single-use packaging. (A1)(A1)

One way it increases costs: the model needs reverse logistics. Bottles must be collected from households, returned to the depot, washed and refilled, which adds handling cost and requires the EUR 180,000 washer and store. The dairy also bears the cost of bottles that are broken or never returned. (A1)(A1)

(b) Annual deliveries = 3,000 households x 5 bottles x 52 weeks = 780,000 deliveries. (M1)(A1)

Plastic: cost per delivery EUR 0.12; annual cost = 780,000 x 0.12 = EUR 93,600. (A1)

Plan (20 uses): cost per delivery = 0.60/20 + 0.03 = EUR 0.03 + EUR 0.03 = EUR 0.06; annual cost = 780,000 x 0.06 = EUR 46,800. (M1)(A1)

Trial (6 uses): cost per delivery = 0.60/6 + 0.03 = EUR 0.10 + EUR 0.03 = EUR 0.13; annual cost = 780,000 x 0.13 = EUR 101,400. (A1)

Break-even uses: 0.60/u + 0.03 = 0.12 gives u = 0.60/0.09 = 6.67, so a glass bottle must be used at least seven times to be cheaper than plastic. (A1)

Payback on the plan's figures: annual saving = 93,600 - 46,800 = EUR 46,800; payback = 180,000/46,800 = 3.8 years. (A1)

(c) Indicative evaluation. The plan is internally consistent and looks attractive on its own assumptions. At twenty uses a bottle, glass costs EUR 0.06 a delivery against EUR 0.12 for plastic, a saving of EUR 46,800 a year, and the EUR 180,000 is repaid in 3.8 years, inside the bank's four-year rule. The bank's condition is therefore satisfied on the plan's numbers, and the circular model also delivers the founders' environmental objective.

The trial contradicts the assumption on which the whole plan rests. At six uses, glass costs EUR 0.13 a delivery, more than the plastic it replaces, and the annual cost rises to EUR 101,400, about EUR 7,800 a year worse than doing nothing. The break-even is seven uses, so the trial is not close: it falls below the point at which the investment stops destroying value. On the trial evidence the payback never arrives, and the bank's four-year rule is not met.

The evaluation therefore turns on a judgement about the trial. Ravi's argument has some force, because the trial lasted only three months and customers were told about the scheme a week before it began, so six uses may understate a mature scheme. Meg's argument is that the dairy cannot afford to be wrong, and the shortfall is a recurring EUR 7,800 a year, not a one-off. The strongest answers note that the plan should be rewritten with a range of reuse rates, that the scheme should be re-trialled with proper notice, and that the founders' zero-waste objective could be pursued at a smaller scale before EUR 180,000 is committed. Credit any supported conclusion. (A1)(A1)(A1)(A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for each of the two cost reductions and one for the cost increase, each correctly explained; naming reuse without explaining the saving earns at most one. Credit avoided bottle purchases, disposal charges and future levies. Do not credit the claim that it is good for the environment as a cost reduction.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for the 780,000 deliveries, (A1) for the plastic cost, (M1)(A1) for the plan's cost per delivery and annual cost, (A1) for the trial cost, (A1) for the break-even of seven uses and (A1) for the 3.8-year payback. Follow through on arithmetic slips. A candidate who uses 0.60/20 without adding the EUR 0.03 washing cost cannot reach the evaluation marks, because the omission is the error the question tests.

(c) The discriminating marks are for the trial cost of EUR 0.13 and the recognition that six uses is below the break-even of seven. Part (c): the method mark is for applying the new tariff to the ex-works value, not to the landed cost; a candidate who applies 25% to €39.18 obtains €48.98 and should be corrected sharply, as this is the most common conceptual error in the question. Part (d): the recommendation mark is independent of the direction chosen, provided the reasoning is consistent with the candidate's own figures. The non-financial mark requires a factor with a stated consequence for Halden, such as reputational exposure, political risk, loss of control over quality or loss of the home workforce's skills; a list of generic MNC advantages earns nothing.

15. BM-1.1-001 14 marks

(a) Award up to four marks for two business functions, each explained in the context of Nettle & Vine.

Operations (production). The operations function is the transformation process: it converts bought-in inputs that cost EUR 1.20 a litre into bottled cordial that sells for EUR 4.00 a litre. Almost all of the value the business adds is created here, because the recipe, the brewing and the bottling turn cheap ingredients into a product a customer will pay a premium for. (A1)(A1)

Marketing. The marketing function is what allows the business to charge EUR 4.00 rather than a commodity price. The Nettle & Vine name, the founders' signatures, the story of the recipe and the refusal to use imported fruit are all marketing decisions, and they are the reason 40 independent retailers buy the cordial instead of a cheaper own-label alternative. Marketing therefore captures the value that operations creates. (A1)(A1)

Also creditable: finance (arranging and servicing the EUR 200 000 loan and costing the supermarket contract), human resources (recruiting and training the eight staff the contract would need), and product development (Priya's recipes and the product range).

(b) Customer data. Total annual spend of the 40 accounts is EUR 240 000, so the mean is $EUR 240 000 / 40 = EUR 6 000$. The median is EUR 2 400, because 25 of the 40 accounts spend only EUR 2 400 and the five largest spend EUR 20 000 each. (M1)(A1)

The mean is 2.5 times the median, so the 'average customer' is not a typical customer. The five largest accounts alone provide EUR 100 000, or 41.7% of trade revenue. The business is already more concentrated than the mean figure suggests. (A1)

Value added = revenue minus bought-in materials and services.

Option A: revenue $= 60 000 \times EUR 4.00 = EUR 240 000$; bought-in $= 60 000 \times EUR 1.20 = EUR 72 000$; value added $= EUR 168 000$. Value added per litre $= EUR 168 000 / 60 000 = EUR 2.80$. Value added per employee $= EUR 168 000 / 6 = EUR 28 000$. (M1)(A1)

Supermarket contract: revenue $= 180 000 \times EUR 1.55 = EUR 279 000$; bought-in $= 180 000 \times EUR 0.40 = EUR 72 000$; value added $= EUR 207 000$. Value added per litre $= EUR 207 000 / 180 000 = EUR 1.15$. Value added per employee $= EUR 207 000 / 8 = EUR 25 875$. (M1)(A1)

The two measures disagree. The contract adds more value in total (EUR 207 000 against EUR 168 000) and produces three times the volume, but it adds only EUR 1.15 a litre against EUR 2.80, a fall of 59%, and less value per employee. The contract buys volume with a much thinner margin.

(c) Indicative evaluation. A SWOT built from the case:

Strengths: a distinctive own brand; Priya's recipe; 40 loyal trade accounts; a premium price of EUR 4.00 a litre; a site that is full and profitable.
Weaknesses: no space to grow; no financial analysis; 42% of trade revenue from five accounts; only six staff; the founders' pay is low.
Opportunities: the supermarket contract would give three years of guaranteed volume; the craft drinks market is growing; a rival's takeover shows that small producers are attractive to larger buyers.
Threats: the contract is exclusive and would end the trade business; the brand would disappear from the bottles; the supermarket sets and reviews the price annually; a rival has cut its trade prices.

The quantitative case is closer than it first appears. The contract adds EUR 39 000 more value in total, but the EUR 200 000 loan costs EUR 16 000 a year in interest, so the gain falls to EUR 23 000, about 14% of Option A's value added. The supermarket may review the price after one year, and a cut of even 10% (to EUR 1.40) would reduce the contract's value added by EUR 18 000 and leave the business barely ahead of doing nothing while having given up its brand, its recipe and its independence. The loan also requires personal guarantees from both founders.

The SWOT shows that the contract removes the business's main weakness (no capacity to grow) by destroying its main strength (the brand). The descriptive statistics make the same point from the other direction: the business already depends on five large accounts, and the contract would replace that with dependence on a single customer that sets the price. Priya's objective is to protect the recipe and the identity of the business; Tom's is security and a proper income. The contract delivers Tom's objective only while the supermarket chooses to keep the price.

A supported conclusion is required. Rejecting the contract, or negotiating a non-exclusive, shorter or higher-priced deal, is creditworthy if the reasoning is consistent with the candidate's own figures. A conclusion that rests only on 'growth is good', or only on 'the brand matters', is not.

(a) Two functions, two marks each: one mark for naming and describing the function correctly, one for explaining how it adds value in this business. 'Marketing' with no link to the EUR 4.00 price or the brand earns one mark only. Do not credit 'management' or 'administration' as a business function.

(b) Award (M1) for the mean and (A1) for the median; a candidate who computes the mean but not the median cannot reach the comment mark. The comment must identify that the mean is pulled up by the five large accounts - saying only 'the numbers are different' forfeits it. For value added, award (M1) for revenue minus bought-in costs and (A1) for each correct set of three figures; follow through on arithmetic slips. Accept value added per employee rounded to EUR 25 900 or EUR 26 000. Do not accept a candidate who puts the loan interest inside the value added calculation - interest is a financing cost, not a bought-in input - although such a candidate may still earn the evaluation marks in (c) if they use the figure there.

(c) The SWOT marks are for use, not for listing: a candidate who writes four headings and reaches no judgement earns at most two. The discriminating move is comparing the EUR 23 000 net gain with the scale of what is given up. Credit any supported conclusion. Do not credit generic statements about supermarkets or 'big business' that are not tied to the case.

16. BM-3.1-001 14 marks

(a) Capital expenditure is spending on non-current assets that will be used in the business for more than a year - it buys something the business keeps. Revenue expenditure is spending on the day-to-day running of the business, which is consumed within the year. (A1)(A1)

In this case the five items in the table are all capital expenditure: the conversion of the unit, the grooming equipment, the van, the software and the signage are assets the business will use for several years. The rent, wages, utilities, insurance, dog food and marketing are revenue expenditure, because they are incurred month by month and create no lasting asset. (A1)

The distinction matters because the two types of spending have to be financed differently. Capital expenditure is a large, one-off sum that a bank will often lend against, because the asset can be secured. Revenue expenditure recurs every month, is not secured by an asset, and must be financed from cash or from working capital - so it is easy to underestimate and hard to borrow against. (A1)

(b) Capital expenditure $= EUR 52 000 + EUR 16 000 + EUR 28 000 + EUR 6 000 + EUR 8 000 = EUR 110 000$. (M1)(A1)

Monthly running costs $= EUR 3 000 + EUR 7 800 + EUR 1 200 + EUR 400 + EUR 4 600 + EUR 500 = EUR 17 500$. Three months of running costs must be financed before the business earns anything, so working capital $= 3 \times EUR 17 500 = EUR 52 500$. (M1)(A1)

Contingency $= 10\% \times (EUR 110 000 + EUR 52 500) = 10\% \times EUR 162 500 = EUR 16 250$. Total finance required $= EUR 110 000 + EUR 52 500 + EUR 16 250 = EUR 178 750$. (A1)

(c) Indicative evaluation, using the financial section of the business plan.

The plan looks balanced on its own assumptions: it claims EUR 180 000 of funding against a requirement of EUR 178 750, a margin of EUR 1 250. But two of the three funding assumptions are wrong.

The bank will lend at most 60% of the value of the capital items, which is $60\% \times EUR 110 000 = EUR 66 000$, not the EUR 115 000 requested. The grant is capped at EUR 12 000, not EUR 20 000. The realistic funding is therefore $EUR 45 000 + EUR 66 000 + EUR 12 000 = EUR 123 000$, against a requirement of EUR 178 750. The shortfall is EUR 55 750, and the plan's apparent EUR 1 250 surplus becomes a EUR 55 750 hole. (M1)(A1)

The shortfall is almost entirely a working-capital problem. The business needs EUR 52 500 of cash to survive three months with no income, and the bank will not lend against it. The founders have focused on the visible assets and underestimated the cost of simply keeping the doors open.

The lease offer helps the capital side but not enough. Leasing the grooming equipment and the van (EUR 44 000) would cut capital expenditure to EUR 66 000, but it adds EUR 980 a month to running costs, raising monthly costs to EUR 18 480 and working capital to EUR 55 440. With a 10% contingency on EUR 121 440, the total requirement becomes EUR 133 584. The bank's limit then falls to EUR 39 600, so available funding is EUR 96 600 and the shortfall is EUR 36 984. Leasing reduces the gap by about EUR 19 000 but increases the monthly cost permanently.

Rob's aunt's EUR 56 000 would close the gap, but it costs 25% of the business, which both founders have said they do not want to give up. It also transfers a quarter of any future growth to someone who is contributing money rather than work.

A supported conclusion is required. A candidate who recommends delaying the opening, phasing the spend, reducing the contingency, or accepting the aunt's money, and who justifies it with the figures, should be credited. The key judgement is that the plan is not realistic as written: it understates the finance needed by about EUR 55 750 and misunderstands how a bank lends to a start-up.

Markscheme notes

(a) Two marks for the distinction, one for applying it correctly to the case, one for explaining why it affects the finance decision. A candidate who says only 'capital is long term, revenue is short term' earns one mark. Do not credit an answer that classifies the monthly rent as capital because it is paid under a lease.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for the capital total, (M1)(A1) for the monthly running cost and the three-month working capital, and (A1) for the contingency and the total. Follow through on arithmetic errors. Do not penalise a candidate who presents the contingency as 10% of capital plus working capital in a different order. Do not accept a total that omits the working capital: that omission is the whole point of the question.

(c) The two discriminating marks are for the bank's 60% limit and the grant cap. A candidate who accepts the plan's EUR 180 000 at face value and concludes that it is adequate cannot earn more than one mark, however well written. The lease marks require the candidate to see that leasing reduces the upfront requirement but raises the recurring one - a candidate who says only 'leasing is cheaper' forfeits them. Credit any supported conclusion, including one that keeps the plan but changes its funding. Do not credit answers that drift into evaluating the business idea itself rather than the finance plan.

17. BM-5.1-001 14 marks

(a) Pennine Workwear operates a transformation process. The inputs are bought-in fabric, thread and components costing EUR 7 a garment, together with 2 400 hours of direct labour a month and the use of the factory and machinery. The process is cutting, sewing, finishing, inspecting and packing. The outputs are finished protective garments sold at EUR 18 each, which for many customers must also be safe, durable and compliant with industry standards.

The operations function adds value in two ways. First, it converts EUR 7 of materials into a garment that sells for EUR 18 - the transformation itself is where the added value is created. Second, it is responsible for the quality and reliability that make the garment worth buying: a protective jacket that fails in use is worthless, so the inspection and finishing stages protect the price the business can charge. Operations therefore supports the whole business strategy, not just the production line.

(b) Labour productivity = garments produced per direct labour hour.

Before the change: $12 000 / 2 400 = 5.0$ garments per labour hour. (M1)(A1)
Since the change: $15 000 / 2 500 = 6.0$ garments per labour hour. (A1)
That is a rise of $(6.0 - 5.0) / 5.0 = 20\%$, which is the figure the operations manager is reporting.

Value added = revenue minus bought-in materials minus rework cost.

Before the change: revenue $= 12 000 \times EUR 18 = EUR 216 000$; materials $= 12 000 \times EUR 7 = EUR 84 000$; defects $= 2\% \times 12 000 = 240$ garments, rework $= 240 \times EUR 4 = EUR 960$. Value added $= EUR 216 000 - EUR 84 000 - EUR 960 = EUR 131 040$, or $EUR 131 040 / 12 000 = EUR 10.92$ per garment. (M1)(A1)

Since the change: revenue $= 15 000 \times EUR 18 = EUR 270 000$, less 450 garments sold as seconds at EUR 9 instead of EUR 18, a loss of $450 \times EUR 9 = EUR 4 050$, so EUR 265 950; materials $= 15 000 \times EUR 7 = EUR 105 000$; defects $= 7\% \times 15 000 = 1 050$ garments, rework $= 1 050 \times EUR 4 = EUR 4 200$. Value added $= EUR 265 950 - EUR 105 000 - EUR 4 200 = EUR 156 750$, or $EUR 156 750 / 15 000 = EUR 10.45$ per garment. (M1)(A1)

The two measures disagree. Labour productivity is up 20%, but value added per garment has fallen from EUR 10.92 to EUR 10.45, a fall of about 4.3%. Total value added has risen, from EUR 131 040 to EUR 156 750, but only because 25% more garments are being made. Each garment now adds less value than before, because the cost of rework and of selling seconds has risen faster than the extra output is worth. Efficiency has improved; effectiveness has not.

(c) Indicative evaluation, using descriptive statistics on the twelve monthly defect rates.

The mean defect rate for the year is $(2.1 + 1.8 + 2.3 + 1.9 + 2.2 + 2.1 + 6.8 + 7.1 + 6.4 + 7.6 + 8.1 + 7.4) / 12 = 55.8 / 12 = 4.65\%$. Taken alone, that looks acceptable. The median is 4.35% and the range is $8.1 - 1.8 = 6.3$ percentage points. (M1)(A1)

The mean is misleading. Splitting the data at the change gives a mean of 2.07% for months 1 to 6 and 7.23% for months 7 to 12. The process did not drift; it stepped from about 2% to about 7% at the moment the change was introduced, and the annual mean of 4.65% describes no month that actually occurred. This is the classic trap of a summary statistic that conceals a structural break.

Two of the largest customers, together worth about 30% of sales, have complained, and one has asked for a meeting. Three experienced machinists have left in four months, which will put further pressure on quality. The operations manager's bonus depends on productivity, which explains why he is recommending a further increase in line speed, but his measure ignores the cost of defects and returns.

Conclusion: the change should not be kept in its present form. The productivity gain is real, but it has been bought by cutting the quality checks that protect the EUR 18 price. A supported conclusion is required; a candidate who recommends keeping the change but restoring the inspection stage, and justifies this from the figures, is equally creditworthy.

Markscheme notes

(a) Two marks for a correct description of inputs, process and outputs, and two for explaining how operations adds value. A candidate who describes the process without linking it to value added earns at most two. Do not credit a list of departments with no explanation.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for the two productivity figures and (A1)(A1) for the two value added per garment figures. The comment mark is the discriminating one and requires the candidate to notice that productivity and value added per unit move in opposite directions. A candidate who reports only the productivity improvement, or who says 'both improved because total value added rose', forfeits the comment mark. Follow through on arithmetic slips. Accept value added calculated without the rework cost only if the same treatment is applied to both periods; a candidate who includes rework in one period and not the other cannot reach full marks.

(c) The descriptive statistics marks are for computing the mean, the median and the range, and for noticing that the annual mean conceals the step change. A candidate who reports only the mean of 4.65% and concludes that quality is acceptable has not used the data. The evaluation must end in a decision; credit either conclusion provided it is supported. Do not credit an answer that attributes the defects to staff attitudes without evidence.
