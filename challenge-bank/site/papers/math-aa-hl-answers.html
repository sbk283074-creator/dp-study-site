1. MATH-AHL1.10-001 16 marks

(a) Take any set of four of the points. The four points form a cyclic quadrilateral, and the two diagonals of that quadrilateral are chords which cross at a point strictly inside the circle. (M1) So each $4$-element subset of the points produces at least one interior intersection point.

Conversely, take any interior intersection point. It is the meeting of two chords, and those two chords have four distinct endpoints among the given points, so they determine a unique $4$-element subset. (M1) Within that subset the two crossing chords must be the diagonals rather than the sides, so the intersection produced by the subset is exactly the one we started with.

The correspondence between $4$-element subsets and interior intersection points is therefore a bijection. (A1) Hence the number of interior intersection points equals the number of ways of choosing four points from $n$, namely $\dbinom{n}{4}$. (A1) *(AG)*

(b) We need the least integer $n$ with $\dbinom{n}{4}>1000$. (M1) Now $\dbinom{13}{4}=715$ and $\dbinom{14}{4}=1001$. (A1) Since $\dbinom{n}{4}$ increases with $n$ for $n\geq4$, the least such value is $n=14$. (A1)

(c) Label the vertices $V_0,V_1,\dots,V_5$ in order. The three chords $V_0V_3$, $V_1V_4$ and $V_2V_5$ join opposite vertices, so each is a diameter of the circumcircle and all three pass through the centre $O$. (M1) The general-position formula counts each *pair* of crossing chords separately: these three chords contribute the three crossing pairs $(V_0V_3,V_1V_4)$, $(V_0V_3,V_2V_5)$ and $(V_1V_4,V_2V_5)$ to $\dbinom{6}{4}$. (A1) But all three pairs meet at the single point $O$, so the formula counts $O$ three times when it should count it once, an overcount of $2$. (A1) Checking the remaining crossing pairs shows that no two of them meet at the same interior point, so this is the only coincidence. (A1)

(d) Start from the $\dbinom{6}{4}=15$ crossing pairs. The three crossing pairs of diameters do not give three points but one, so replacing those three points by the single point $O$ reduces the total by $3-1=2$. (M1)(A1) Therefore the number of distinct interior intersection points is $15-2=13$, that is $15-3+1=13$. (M1)(A1)(A1) The twelve remaining crossing pairs each give a distinct point, and none of them lies at $O$, so the correction is complete.

Markscheme notes

(a) The whole part is the bijection. Award (M1) for the forward direction (four points give a crossing) and (M1) for the reverse (a crossing gives four endpoints); the two (A1) marks are for stating that the correspondence is one-to-one and concluding $\dbinom{n}{4}$. A candidate who writes $\dbinom{\binom{n}{2}}{2}$ has counted pairs of chords that share an endpoint and pairs that meet outside the circle; that earns nothing in (a). An argument phrased as 'each interior intersection needs four points and any four points give one intersection' should be fully credited even if the word bijection is absent.

(b) The (M1) is for setting up $\dbinom{n}{4}>1000$. A candidate who finds $n=14$ by trial but does not exhibit $\dbinom{13}{4}=715$ and $\dbinom{14}{4}=1001$ forfeits one (A1). Follow-through is allowed if the formula from (a) is wrong: credit the method of solving their inequality.

(c) The essential marks are (i) naming the three diameters and (ii) explaining that the formula counts crossing *pairs*, not distinct points. A candidate who says only 'some chords meet at the centre' without identifying the three chords loses one (A1). The final (A1) requires the statement that no other coincidence occurs; inspection, symmetry or computation are all acceptable.

(d) Award (M1) for the correction strategy and (A1) for the reduction of $2$; the remaining marks are for the arithmetic $15-3+1=13$. Follow-through from (c) is allowed. The answer $12$, obtained by subtracting $3$ instead of $2$, is the commonest error and loses only the final (A1).

2. MATH-AHL1.12-001 16 marks

(a) Put $z=a+bi$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$. Then $z^{2}=(a^{2}-b^{2})+2abi$ and $\bar z=a-bi$. (M1) Equating real and imaginary parts,

$$a^{2}-b^{2}=a,\qquad 2ab=-b.$$ (A1)

The second equation gives $b(2a+1)=0$, so either $b=0$ or $a=-\tfrac12$.

If $b=0$, then $a^{2}=a$, so $a=0$ or $a=1$, giving $z=0$ and $z=1$. (A1)

If $a=-\tfrac12$, then $a^{2}-b^{2}=a$ gives $\tfrac14-b^{2}=-\tfrac12$, so $b^{2}=\tfrac34$ and $b=\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, giving $z=-\tfrac12\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$. (A1)

The solutions are $z=0$, $z=1$, $z=-\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$ and $z=-\tfrac12-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$.

(b) Let $z\neq0$ satisfy $z^{2}=\bar z$. Taking moduli, $|z^{2}|=|\bar z|$, so $|z|^{2}=|z|$. Since $|z|\neq0$ we may divide, giving $|z|=1$. (M1)(A1) Then

$$z^{3}=z\cdot z^{2}=z\bar z=|z|^{2}=1.$$ (A1)

So every non-zero solution satisfies $z^{3}=1$, that is, is a cube root of unity. Indeed the three non-zero solutions found in (a) are $1$, $e^{2\pi i/3}$ and $e^{4\pi i/3}$.

(c) Write $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $r\geq0$. Then $z^{n}=r^{n}e^{in\theta}$ and $\bar z=re^{-i\theta}$. (M1) Equating moduli gives $r^{n}=r$, so $r=0$ or $r=1$. (A1)

If $r=0$ then $z=0$, which satisfies the equation. If $r=1$ then $e^{in\theta}=e^{-i\theta}$, so $e^{i(n+1)\theta}=1$, giving $\theta=\dfrac{2\pi k}{n+1}$ for $k=0,1,\dots,n$. (A1) Hence the non-zero solutions are the $n+1$ roots of $z^{n+1}=1$, and together with $z=0$ the equation has $n+2$ solutions. (A1)

(d) A solution with real part $\tfrac12$ cannot be $z=0$, so by the argument in (b) it has $|z|=1$. (M1) Writing $z=e^{i\theta}$ with $\cos\theta=\tfrac12$ gives $\theta=\pm\tfrac{\pi}{3}+2\pi m$, so $z=e^{\pm i\pi/3}$, a primitive sixth root of unity. (A1)

From (c), the solutions of $z^{n}=\bar z$ are $0$ together with the $(n+1)$th roots of unity. So $e^{i\pi/3}$ is a solution exactly when it is an $(n+1)$th root of unity, that is when $\left(e^{i\pi/3}\right)^{n+1}=1$, which is $e^{i\pi(n+1)/3}=1$, so $\dfrac{n+1}{3}$ must be an even integer. (M1)

Hence $n+1$ is a multiple of $6$, that is $n\equiv5\pmod6$. (A1) The smallest such integer is $n=5$; indeed $z=e^{i\pi/3}$ satisfies $z^{5}=e^{i5\pi/3}=e^{-i\pi/3}=\bar z$. All integers $n=5,11,17,23,\dots$ work. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Equating real and imaginary parts must be explicit for the first (M1). The case split $b=0$ or $a=-\tfrac12$ is essential: a candidate who divides the imaginary equation by $b$ without considering $b=0$ loses the solutions $z=0$ and $z=1$ and forfeits the corresponding (A1). Accept the solutions in any order.

(b) The modulus argument requires dividing by $|z|$, justified by $z\neq0$; a candidate who writes $|z|^{2}=|z|$ and concludes $|z|=1$ without noting $|z|\neq0$ loses the second (A1). The deduction $z^{3}=1$ must use $z\bar z=|z|^{2}$; a candidate who multiplies $z^{2}=\bar z$ by $z$ and writes $z^{3}=1$ directly has skipped the key step and should be asked to justify it.

(c) Award (M1) for the polar substitution, (A1) for $r=0$ or $r=1$, (A1) for the arguments, and (A1) for the count. Accept 'the $(n+1)$th roots of unity together with zero'. A candidate who forgets $z=0$ loses the final (A1).

(d) The critical step is realising that a non-zero solution has modulus $1$, so $\mathrm{Re}(z)=\tfrac12$ forces $z=e^{\pm i\pi/3}$. Award (M1) for this, (A1) for identifying a primitive sixth root, (M1) for the divisibility condition, and (A1)(A1) for $n\equiv5\pmod6$ and the smallest value. Follow-through from a wrong count in (c) is allowed, but a candidate who gives only $n=5$ and misses the infinite family loses the last (A1).

3. MATH-AHL3.12-001 16 marks

(a) The displacement from $A$ to $B(k)$ is

$$\overrightarrow{AB(k)}=\mathbf{b}(k)-\mathbf{a}=\begin{pmatrix}k\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}k\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}.$$ (M1)

The vector product of the two direction vectors is

$$\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}(k)=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}2k\\k\\k-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1(k-1)-0(k)\\0(2k)-2(k-1)\\2(k)-1(2k)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}k-1\\-2(k-1)\\0\end{pmatrix}=(k-1)\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\0\end{pmatrix}.$$ (M1)

Hence

$$(\mathbf{b}(k)-\mathbf{a})\cdot(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}(k))=\begin{pmatrix}k\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}\cdot(k-1)\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\0\end{pmatrix}=(k-1)(k)=k(k-1).$$ (A1) *(AG)*

(b) The two lines are coplanar exactly when this scalar triple product vanishes, so $k(k-1)=0$, giving $k=0$ or $k=1$. (A1) The triple product measures whether the displacement $\overrightarrow{AB(k)}$ lies in the plane spanned by the two direction vectors. (R1) When it is zero the three vectors are linearly dependent and the lines lie in one common plane, but inside a common plane two lines either meet at a single point or are parallel (possibly coincident). Both alternatives make the triple product zero, so its value cannot separate them, and an independent test on the direction vectors is required. (A1)

(c) Setting $k=1$ gives $\mathbf{v}(1)=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{u}$, so the direction vectors are parallel and therefore the lines are parallel. (A1) This is consistent with the triple product vanishing at $k=1$, and it shows that the zero located in part (b) does not mean that the lines meet. (R1) To decide between distinct and coincident lines, test whether $B(1)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}$ lies on $L_{1}$. (M1) Every point of $L_{1}$ has the form $\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}+s\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}$, so its $z$-coordinate is always $1$, whereas $B(1)$ has $z$-coordinate $0$; equivalently, $\overrightarrow{AB(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}$ is not a multiple of $\mathbf{u}$, since $\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\1\end{pmatrix}\neq\mathbf{0}$. (A1) Hence for $k=1$ the lines are parallel and distinct; they do not coincide.

(d) When $k=0$ we have $B(0)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}$, which is not a multiple of $\mathbf{u}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}$, so the lines are not parallel. (M1) Since part (b) shows that they are coplanar at $k=0$, they must meet at a single point. Writing

$$\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}+s\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix},$$

the $x$-component gives $2s=0$, so $s=0$; the $y$-component $s=0$ is then satisfied automatically; and the $z$-component gives $1=-t$, so $t=-1$. (M1) The unique intersection point is $\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$, which is the point $A$ itself. (A1)

(e) For every $k\notin\{0,1\}$ the triple product equals $k(k-1)\neq0$, so the lines are not coplanar and are therefore skew. (A1) At $k=1$ the lines are parallel and distinct, and at $k=0$ they intersect at $A$. (A1) Parallelism occurs only at $k=1$, and there the lines do not share a point, so no value of $k$ makes the lines coincide. (A1)

Summary: $L_{1}$ and $L_{2}$ are skew for all $k\neq0,1$; parallel and distinct for $k=1$; intersecting at $\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$ for $k=0$; and never coincident.

Markscheme notes

(a) The two (M1) marks are for the displacement $\overrightarrow{AB(k)}$ and for the vector product $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}(k)$; a candidate who evaluates the triple product as a single $3\times3$ determinant still earns both provided the intermediate vectors are visible. The (A1) is for the simplified form $k(k-1)$, and the *(AG)* requires the result to be stated, so a candidate who leaves it as $(k-1)k$ still earns it. Watch for sign slips in the vector product: $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}(k)$ must have zero $z$-component, and a candidate who obtains $(k-1)(1,2,0)$ will report $T(k)=-k(k-1)$; that is wrong, but follow-through should be given in the later parts.

(b) Award (A1) for the values $k=0$ and $k=1$; the (R1) is for recognising that a zero triple product means coplanarity, and the final (A1) for the explicit statement that coplanarity cannot separate the parallel and intersecting cases. A candidate who claims that $T=0$ proves the lines intersect has made exactly the error the question is designed to expose and should not receive the last (A1).

(c) The (A1) is for $\mathbf{v}(1)=\mathbf{u}$; the (R1) for noting that this does not imply intersection; the (M1) for the point test; and the final (A1) for the conclusion. Any valid point test is acceptable, including solving the two equations for $s$ and $t$ and finding them inconsistent. A candidate who concludes 'coincident' merely because the direction vectors are equal forfeits the final (A1), since equality of direction vectors never establishes coincidence.

(d) Award (M1) for noting that $\mathbf{v}(0)$ is not parallel to $\mathbf{u}$, (M1) for solving the two-parameter equations, and (A1) for $\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$. Follow-through from a wrong triple product is allowed: a candidate who correctly solves their own system keeps both method marks.

(e) Full marks require all three classifications together with the observation that no value of $k$ gives coincident lines. A candidate who omits the coincidence argument loses the third (A1). Follow-through from earlier parts is permitted, but the statement that the lines are skew for all other $k$ must be justified by the non-vanishing of the triple product, not merely asserted.

4. MATH-AHL1.15-001 15 marks

(a) For $k\ge1$,
$$\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k+1}=\frac{(k+1)-k}{k(k+1)}=\frac{1}{k(k+1)}.$$ (M1) Now $(k+1)^{2}+1=k^{2}+2k+2$ and $k(k+1)=k^{2}+k$, so $$(k+1)^{2}+1-k(k+1)=k+2>0,$$ hence $(k+1)^{2}+1>k(k+1)$ and therefore $$\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}<\frac{1}{k(k+1)}=\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}.$$ (A1)(A1) *(AG)*

(b) With the hypothesis $S_k<\frac32$ the step gives $$S_{k+1}=S_k+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}<\frac32+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}.$$ (M1)(A1) The right-hand side is strictly greater than $\frac32$ because $\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}>0$, so this does not establish $S_{k+1}<\frac32$. (R1) No rearrangement of a hypothesis of the form $S_k<\frac32$ can remove a positive increment, so the hypothesis has to be strengthened to one that leaves a gap larger than the increment. (A1)

(c) Claim: $S_n\le\frac32-\frac1n$ for all $n\ge1$. Base case $n=1$: $S_1=\frac12$ and $\frac32-1=\frac12$, so the claim holds, with equality. (M1) Inductive step: assume $S_k\le\frac32-\frac1k$. Then $$S_{k+1}=S_k+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}\le\frac32-\frac1k+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}.$$ (M1) By part (a), $\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}\le\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}$, so $$S_{k+1}\le\frac32-\frac1k+\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}=\frac32-\frac1{k+1}.$$ (A1) This is the claim with $n=k+1$, so by induction the claim holds for every $n\ge1$. (A1) *(AG)* Finally $\frac1n>0$, so $S_n\le\frac32-\frac1n<\frac32$ for every $n\ge1$. (A1)

(d) We want the largest $c$ with $S_n\le\frac32-\frac{c}{n}$ for all $n\ge1$. (M1) The case $n=1$ reads $\frac12\le\frac32-c$, that is $c\le1$, so no $c>1$ is admissible; part (c) shows that $c=1$ is admissible. (A1) Therefore the greatest such constant is $c=1$. (A1) For example, $c=\frac{3}{2}$ would demand $S_1\le0$, which is false because $S_1=\frac12$, so values above $1$ genuinely fail and the answer is not merely the largest value tested.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for combining the right-hand side into the single fraction $\frac{1}{k(k+1)}$; the two (A1) marks are for the comparison $(k+1)^{2}+1>k(k+1)$ and for concluding the inequality. A candidate who checks several values of $k$ without proving the general comparison earns at most (M1): the point is the identity $(k+1)^{2}+1-k(k+1)=k+2>0$. Accept the equivalent form $\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}\le\frac{1}{k(k+1)}$ with no further work required.

(b) The step must be written out with the positive increment visible. Award (M1) for the decomposition $S_{k+1}=S_k+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}$, (A1) for substituting the hypothesis, (R1) for observing that the resulting bound exceeds $\frac32$, and the final (A1) for the structural remark that a strictly positive increment cannot be absorbed by a bound with no slack. A candidate who writes only 'it does not work' with no inequality earns just the first two marks.

(c) The strengthened statement must be stated, not assumed. The base case is checked with equality, which is acceptable. Award (M1) for the base case, (M1) for the inductive hypothesis and the decomposition, (A1) for applying the inequality from (a), (A1) for the algebra reaching $\frac32-\frac1{k+1}$, and (A1) for the concluding deduction $S_n<\frac32$. Follow-through is allowed: a candidate who carries an incorrect constant from (a) but applies it consistently keeps the method marks.

(d) The base case gives $c\le1$ and part (c) gives admissibility at $c=1$. A candidate who simply states $c=1$ without the inequality $\frac12\le\frac32-c$ loses the (M1). Condone a brief note that smaller values of $c$ give a weaker, hence also true, inequality; the question asks for the greatest constant.

5. MATH-AHL2.12-001 15 marks

(a) Put $g(t)=f(2+t)-2$. Then $$g(t)+g(-t)=\bigl(f(2+t)-2\bigr)+\bigl(f(2-t)-2\bigr)=4-4=0,$$ (M1) using the given condition with the same $t$. Hence $g(-t)=-g(t)$ for all real $t$, so $g$ is odd. (A1)

Since $f$ is a cubic, $g$ is a cubic in $t$. An odd polynomial contains only odd powers of $t$, so $g(t)=at^{3}+bt$ for constants $a$ and $b$. (R1) Writing $t=x-2$ gives $f(x)-2=a(x-2)^{3}+b(x-2)$, that is $$f(x)=2+a(x-2)^{3}+b(x-2).$$ (A1) *(AG)*

(b) The leading coefficient of $f$ is $1$, so $a=1$. (A1) With $y=x-2$, the equation $f(x)=0$ becomes $$y^{3}+by+2=0.$$ (M1) A cubic $y^{3}+py+q$ has a repeated root exactly when its discriminant $-4p^{3}-27q^{2}$ is zero. (M1) Here $p=b$ and $q=2$, so $-4b^{3}-108=0$, giving $b^{3}=-27$ and hence $b=-3$. (A1) Therefore $$f(x)=(x-2)^{3}-3(x-2)+2.$$ (A1) *(AG)*

(c) Expanding, $(x-2)^{3}=x^{3}-6x^{2}+12x-8$ and $-3(x-2)=-3x+6$, so $$f(x)=x^{3}-6x^{2}+9x=x\left(x^{2}-6x+9\right)=x(x-3)^{2}.$$ (M1)(A1) The roots are $x=0$ and the repeated root $x=3$. (A1) The point of inflection is the centre of symmetry $(2,2)$, since $f(2)=2$; equivalently $f''(x)=6x-12$ vanishes at $x=2$. (A1) As a check on the symmetry, the half-turn about $(2,2)$ carries the point $(0,0)$ to $(4,4)$, and indeed $f(4)=4$.

(d) Differentiating, $f'(x)=3x^{2}-12x+9=3(x-1)(x-3)$. (M1) Hence $f$ has a local maximum at $x=1$ with $f(1)=4$ and a local minimum at $x=3$ with $f(3)=0$. (A1) These are reflections of each other in the point of symmetry $(2,2)$, since $2\cdot2-1=3$ and $2\cdot2-4=0$. (A1) Reading the graph: for $k<0$ there is one solution; for $k=0$ there are two distinct solutions, $x=0$ and the repeated root $x=3$; for $0<k<4$ there are three solutions; for $k=4$ there are two distinct solutions; and for $k>4$ there is one solution. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for the translation $g(t)=f(2+t)-2$ and the addition of the two shifted equations; the (A1) is for recognising that $g(t)+g(-t)=0$ is precisely the definition of an odd function. The (R1) is for the statement that an odd polynomial has only odd powers, which is what converts oddness into the shape of $f$. A candidate who writes $f(x)=2+a(x-2)^3+b(x-2)^2+c(x-2)$ without eliminating the even powers has not used oddness and forfeits the (R1). Accept $b$ in place of the linear coefficient; the names are not important.

(b) The (A1) for $a=1$ follows from the stated leading coefficient. The repeated-root condition must be converted into a condition on $b$: award (M1) for the substitution $y=x-2$ and (M1) for invoking the discriminant, then (A1) for $b=-3$ and (A1) for the assembled cubic. A candidate who instead sets $f(x)=(x-r)^{2}(x-s)$ and matches coefficients should be fully credited, since it reaches the same unique answer. Condone an unsimplified form of $f$.

(c) Accept any correct factorisation route, including division by $x$ after noticing the zero constant term. The point of inflection may be found from the symmetry given in the stem or from $f''(x)=0$; either is acceptable.

(d) The (M1) is for locating the turning points, the (A1) for their values, and the (A1) for linking them by the central symmetry. The final (A1) requires all five cases, including the degenerate values $k=0$ and $k=4$ where a double root reduces the number of distinct solutions; a candidate who gives three solutions at $k=0$ or $k=4$ loses the final mark.

6. MATH-AHL3.10-001 15 marks

(a) By the compound-angle formula, $$\sqrt{2}\cos\left(x+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\sqrt2\left(\cos x\cos\frac{\pi}{4}-\sin x\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\sqrt2\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}(\cos x-\sin x)=\cos x-\sin x.$$ (M1)(A1) Also, by the double-angle formula and the difference of two squares, $$\cos 2x=\cos^{2}x-\sin^{2}x=(\cos x-\sin x)(\cos x+\sin x).$$ (A1) *(AG)*

(b) Substituting the results of (a), $$(\cos x-\sin x)(\cos x+\sin x)=\cos x-\sin x,$$ so $$(\cos x-\sin x)\bigl(\cos x+\sin x-1\bigr)=0.$$ (M1)

Case 1: $\cos x=\sin x$, that is $\tan x=1$, giving $x=\frac{\pi}{4}$ or $x=\frac{5\pi}{4}$ in $[0,2\pi)$. (A1)

Case 2: $\cos x+\sin x=1$. Writing the left side as $\sqrt2\cos\left(x-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$ gives $\cos\left(x-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, so $x-\frac{\pi}{4}=\pm\frac{\pi}{4}+2k\pi$. (M1) Hence $x=0$ or $x=\frac{\pi}{2}$ in $[0,2\pi)$. (A1)

The solutions are $x=0$, $x=\frac{\pi}{4}$, $x=\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $x=\frac{5\pi}{4}$.

(c) Squaring both sides gives $\cos^{2}2x=2\cos^{2}\left(x+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$. (M1) Using $\cos^{2}2x=1-\sin^{2}2x$ and $2\cos^{2}\theta=1+\cos2\theta$ with $\theta=x+\frac{\pi}{4}$, the right-hand side is $$1+\cos\left(2x+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=1-\sin2x.$$ (A1) Hence $1-\sin^{2}2x=1-\sin2x$, that is $\sin^{2}2x=\sin2x$, or $$\sin2x\,(\sin2x-1)=0.$$ (A1)

The squared equation therefore admits $x=0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2}$ from $\sin2x=0$ and $x=\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{5\pi}{4}$ from $\sin2x=1$. (A1) Of these, $x=\pi$ and $x=\frac{3\pi}{2}$ do not solve the original equation: at $x=\pi$ the left side is $1$ and the right side is $-1$, while at $x=\frac{3\pi}{2}$ the left side is $-1$ and the right side is $1$. They are extraneous and must be rejected.

(d) From (b), $\cos x=\sin x$ gives $x=\frac{\pi}{4}+k\pi$ for $k\in\mathbb{Z}$. (M1) Also $\cos x+\sin x=1$ gives $x=2k\pi$ or $x=\frac{\pi}{2}+2k\pi$ for $k\in\mathbb{Z}$. (A1)

In $[-2\pi,2\pi]$ the family $\frac{\pi}{4}+k\pi$ contributes $k=-2,-1,0,1$, that is $-\frac{7\pi}{4},-\frac{3\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{5\pi}{4}$, four values; the family $2k\pi$ contributes $-2\pi,0,2\pi$, three values; and the family $\frac{\pi}{2}+2k\pi$ contributes $-\frac{3\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}$, two values. (M1) These nine values are all distinct, so there are $9$ solutions. (A1)

$$\frac{1}{n(n+1)}-\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac{(n+2)-n}{n(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac{2}{n(n+1)(n+2)}.$$ (M1)

Therefore

$$u_n=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{n(n+1)}-\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}\right),$$ (A1)

a difference of two consecutive values of $w_n=\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)}$. Summing,

$$\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_n=\frac12\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(w_n-w_{n+1}\right)=\frac12\left(w_1-w_{N+1}\right)$$ (M1)

$$=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{1\cdot2}-\frac{1}{(N+1)(N+2)}\right)=\frac14-\frac{1}{2(N+1)(N+2)}.$$ (A1) *(AG)*

(c) As $N\to\infty$ the fraction $\dfrac{1}{2(N+1)(N+2)}$ tends to $0$, so the tail disappears. (A1) Hence $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_n=\frac14$. (A1)

(d) Let $v_n=\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}$. Use the same device one order higher, taking the difference of two consecutive blocks of three factors:

$$\frac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}-\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}=\frac{(n+3)-n}{n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}=\frac{3}{n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}.$$ (M1)

Hence $v_n=\dfrac13\left(\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}-\dfrac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}\right)$. (A1) Summing from $n=1$ to $N$ telescopes to

$$\sum_{n=1}^{N}v_n=\frac13\left(\frac{1}{1\cdot2\cdot3}-\frac{1}{(N+1)(N+2)(N+3)}\right).$$ (M1)

As $N\to\infty$ the second term vanishes, so $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}v_n=\frac13\cdot\frac16=\frac{1}{18}$. (A1)(A1)

Edge-case checks. In (b), at $N=1$ the formula gives $\tfrac14-\tfrac1{12}=\tfrac16$, matching the single term $u_{1}=\tfrac16$. In (d), at $N=1$ it gives $\tfrac13\!\left(\tfrac16-\tfrac{1}{24}\right)=\tfrac1{24}=v_{1}$; only this first term survives as $N\to\infty$.

Markscheme notes

(a) The cover-up (substitution) method is intended, but credit any correct route, including solving the resulting $3\times3$ system. The *(AG)* requires the three coefficients to be exhibited and the identity stated; a candidate who gives $A=\tfrac12$, $B=-1$, $C=\tfrac12$ without restating the identity still earns the accuracy marks.

(b) This is where the marks are won or lost. The three-term form from (a) does telescope, but only with careful bookkeeping of the $\tfrac12$ coefficients; a candidate who telescopes it directly and correctly should receive full credit. The cleaner route is the regrouping displayed above, in which $u_n$ is the difference of two consecutive values of $w_n=\frac{1}{n(n+1)}$. Award (M1) for the regrouping, (A1) for the constant $\tfrac12$, (M1) for recognising the telescoping, and (A1) for the closed form. Condone $N\geq1$ written as $N>0$.

(c) The two (A1) marks are for taking the limit and for the value $\frac14$. A candidate who substitutes $N=\infty$ without comment keeps both marks.

(d) The same construction one order higher: the difference of two consecutive *triple* blocks has numerator $3$, giving the factor $\tfrac13$. Follow-through applies from (b): a candidate whose method is structurally correct but whose constant is wrong keeps the two method marks. The common errors are using $\tfrac12$ instead of $\tfrac13$, and telescoping over the wrong block, which leaves the wrong first term. The final answer $\frac{1}{18}$ follows from $\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac16$.
