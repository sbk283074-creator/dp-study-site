1. PHYS-C.3-001 16 marks

(a) $s=\dfrac{\lambda D}{d}=\dfrac{589\times 10^{-9}\times 2.00}{0.400\times 10^{-3}}$ (M1) $=2.94\times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m}=2.94\ \mathrm{mm}$. (A1)

So adjacent bright fringes are $2.94\ \mathrm{mm}$ apart. (A1)

(b) The first minimum of the single-slit envelope is at the smallest angle satisfying $b\sin\theta=\lambda$: (M1)

$\sin\theta=\dfrac{\lambda}{b}=\dfrac{589\times 10^{-9}}{0.100\times 10^{-3}}=5.89\times 10^{-3}$ (A1)

The distance from the centre is $y=D\tan\theta\approx 2.00\times 5.89\times 10^{-3}=1.18\times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m}=11.8\ \mathrm{mm}$. (A1)

(c) A double-slit maximum of order $n$ occurs where $d\sin\theta=n\lambda$; the first single-slit minimum occurs where $b\sin\theta=\lambda$. (M1)

These coincide when $\dfrac{n\lambda}{d}=\dfrac{\lambda}{b}$, that is when $n=\dfrac{d}{b}$. (M1)

Here $\dfrac{d}{b}=\dfrac{0.400}{0.100}=4.0$ (A1), so the third-order condition places the minimum at $\theta$ with $\sin\theta=\dfrac{4\lambda}{d}=\dfrac{4\times 589\times 10^{-9}}{0.400\times 10^{-3}}=5.89\times 10^{-3}$, exactly the angle found in part (b). The fourth-order maximum therefore falls on a minimum of the envelope and is not visible. (A1)

More generally the missing orders are multiples of $d/b$, so orders $4,\ 8,\ 12,\ \ldots$ are missing. (A1) Order 8 would require $\sin\theta=\dfrac{8\lambda}{d}=1.18\times 10^{-2}$, which is still less than $1$, so order 8 is geometrically accessible and would also be missing. Orders beyond the first few visible ones fall outside the envelope's central region anyway.

(d) Within the central maximum of the envelope, $\left|\sin\theta\right|<\dfrac{\lambda}{b}$, so the allowed orders satisfy $\left|n\right|<\dfrac{d}{b}=4$. (M1)

So $n=-3,\,-2,\,-1,\,0,\,1,\,2,\,3$ are present (A1), giving seven bright fringes. (A1) Orders $n=\pm 4$ coincide exactly with the envelope minima from part (c) and are not visible.

(e) Each order becomes a continuous spectrum with the same angular spread, with red deviated most because $\theta$ increases with $\lambda$; the central maximum remains white since all wavelengths coincide there and it has no order number. (A1)(A1) Higher-order spectra overlap, so beyond the first order the colours are no longer separated cleanly.

Markscheme notes

Parts (a) and (b) are single-formula calculations; accept full marks for any self-consistent use of the small-angle approximation. In (a), the (A1) for the unit conversion is separate and should be awarded for 2.94 mm, 2.9 mm or 2.95 mm, but not for 2.94 without a unit. Part (c): the essential reasoning is the equality of the two conditions, not the arithmetic; a candidate who argues from the ratio $d/b$ alone earns (M1)(M1)(A1) and the final (A1) if the identification of order 4 is complete. Stating that 'all multiples of 4 are missing' without recognising that order 8 is accessible loses the last (A1). Part (d): the mark scheme does not require the exclusion of $n=\pm 4$ to be argued twice, provided the reasoning in (c) is present; a candidate who counts nine fringes has included the two missing orders and can earn at most the first (M1). Part (e): award one mark for a correct change and one for the reason; 'the fringes become coloured' with no reference to wavelength-dependent deviation earns only the first.

2. PHYS-A.3-001 16 marks

(a) In a time interval $\Delta t$ a mass $\Delta m=\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}\Delta t$ of gravel is brought from rest to the belt's speed $v$. Its horizontal momentum therefore changes by $\Delta m\,v$, so the required horizontal force is

$$F=\frac{\Delta(mv)}{\Delta t}=v\frac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}$$ (M1)(AG)

Numerically,

$$F=2.0\times 12=24\ \mathrm{N}$$ (A1)(A1)

(b) Each second a mass of $12\ \mathrm{kg}$ is accelerated from rest to $2.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, so the rate at which kinetic energy is supplied is

$$\frac{\Delta E_{k}}{\Delta t}=\tfrac12\frac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}v^{2}=\tfrac12(12)(2.0)^{2}$$ (M1)(A1)

$$=24\ \mathrm{W}$$ (A1)(A1)

(c) The belt moves at constant speed, so the motor's power equals the force it exerts multiplied by the belt's speed:

$$P=Fv=(24)(2.0)=48\ \mathrm{W}$$ (M1)(A1)

The ratio is

$$\frac{P}{\Delta E_{k}/\Delta t}=\frac{48}{24}=2$$ (A1)

so the motor supplies twice the rate at which the gravel gains kinetic energy. The remaining $24\ \mathrm{W}$ is dissipated as heat where the gravel slides against the belt while it is being accelerated: the friction force acts over the belt's displacement, which is larger than the gravel's displacement, and the difference appears as thermal energy at the sliding contact. (A1) The efficiency is therefore

$$\eta=\frac{24}{48}=0.50=50\%.$$ (A1)

(d) Since $F=v\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}$, doubling $v$ doubles the force to $48\ \mathrm{N}$. The power is $P=Fv$, and both the force and the belt speed have doubled, so

$$P=48\times 4.0=192\ \mathrm{W},$$ (M1)(A1)

which is four times the original $48\ \mathrm{W}$. Equivalently $P=\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}v^{2}\propto v^{2}$, because the belt must accelerate twice as much momentum each second and must do so through twice the distance per second. The mass flow rate has not changed, so the factor of four comes entirely from the doubling of both quantities in $P=Fv$, not from any change in the amount of gravel handled. (A1)

The efficiency is unchanged at $50\%$ when the speed doubles, and it is in fact fixed at $50\%$ for any belt speed and any mass flow rate, because the kinetic-energy rate and the motor power are both proportional to $\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}v^{2}$ and their ratio is therefore constant. It would rise above $50\%$ only if the gravel arrived already moving with the belt, so that less sliding were needed to bring it up to speed; the loss is a property of picking the mass up from rest, not of how fast the belt runs.

Markscheme notes

(a) The force must come from the rate of change of momentum, not from an energy argument. A candidate who writes $F=\tfrac12\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}v=12\ \mathrm{N}$ has used the kinetic-energy rate and has made the very error the question is designed to expose; award no method mark. Accept $F=v\Delta m/\Delta t$ with $\Delta m/\Delta t=12\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) Accept $24\ \mathrm{W}$, $24\ \mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}$ or $24\ \mathrm{N\,m\,s^{-1}}$. A candidate who uses $mv^{2}$ instead of $\tfrac12 mv^{2}$ obtains $48\ \mathrm{W}$ and will then report a 100% efficiency; that contradiction should prompt a re-check but earns nothing here.

(c) The essential reasoning is that the belt's force acts through the belt's displacement, not the gravel's. Simply stating 'half the energy is lost as heat' without identifying the sliding contact earns at most one mark. Award the efficiency mark for $0.50$ or $50\%$; a ratio written the other way round ($2$) with a clear statement is acceptable. Follow-through: if (a) or (b) is wrong but internally consistent, the ratio and efficiency marks remain available.

(d) The explanation must mention both that $F\propto v$ and that the belt speed is $v$, so that $P\propto v^{2}$. A candidate who says only 'because power depends on $v$ squared' earns the value mark but not the explanation mark. Accept $192\ \mathrm{W}$ and accept $190\ \mathrm{W}$.

3. PHYS-C.1-001 16 marks

(a) In this motion the two gliders have equal displacements in the same direction at every instant, so $x_{1}=x_{2}$ and their separation never changes; the coupling spring therefore remains at its natural length and exerts no force on either glider. (M1) Each glider is then acted on only by its own wall spring, so Newton's second law for either glider gives

$$m\ddot{x}=-kx\quad\Rightarrow\quad\ddot{x}=-\frac{k}{m}x.$$ (A1)

Comparing with the defining equation of simple harmonic motion, $a=-\omega^{2}x$, shows that $\omega_{s}=\sqrt{k/m}$ and hence

$$T_{s}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.020}{2.0\times10^{4}}}=2\pi\sqrt{1.0\times10^{-6}}$$ (A1)

$$T_{s}=6.28\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s}=6.28\ \mathrm{ms}.$$ (A1)

The coupling stiffness $k_{c}$ does not appear: in this mode the coupling spring is inert.

(b) In the antisymmetric motion $x_{1}=-x_{2}$, so the separation of the gliders changes by $2x_{1}$ and the coupling spring is stretched or compressed by twice the displacement of either glider. (M1) Its force on a glider is therefore $-k_{c}(2x_{1})$, and combining this with the wall spring gives

$$m\ddot{x}_{1}=-kx_{1}-2k_{c}x_{1}=-(k+2k_{c})x_{1},$$ (A1)

so $\omega_{a}=\sqrt{(k+2k_{c})/m}$ and

$$T_{a}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k+2k_{c}}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.020}{2.0\times10^{4}+2(3.0\times10^{4})}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.020}{8.0\times10^{4}}}=2\pi\sqrt{2.5\times10^{-7}}$$

$$T_{a}=3.14\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s}=3.14\ \mathrm{ms}.$$ (A1)

The ratio of the two periods is

$$\frac{T_{s}}{T_{a}}=\sqrt{\frac{k+2k_{c}}{k}}=\sqrt{\frac{8.0\times10^{4}}{2.0\times10^{4}}}=2.00.$$ (A1)

The antisymmetric mode is therefore the faster of the two.

(c) The piston is driven by the glider that moves in the antisymmetric mode, so the frequency of the wave in the tube is fixed by $T_{a}$:

$$f=\frac{1}{T_{a}}=\frac{1}{3.1416\times10^{-3}}=318\ \mathrm{Hz}.$$ (M1)(A1)

Using the wave equation $v=f\lambda$,

$$\lambda=\frac{v}{f}=\frac{340}{318.3}=1.07\ \mathrm{m}.$$ (A1)

The phase difference between two points separated by $\Delta x=0.534\ \mathrm{m}$ is

$$\Delta\varphi=\frac{2\pi\,\Delta x}{\lambda}=\frac{2\pi(0.534)}{1.068}=3.14\ \mathrm{rad}=\pi.$$ (A1)

The two points are in antiphase. The point nearer the piston is ahead in phase: the disturbance reaches it first, and the farther point lags by the propagation delay $\Delta x/v=1.57\ \mathrm{ms}$, which is exactly half a period.

(d) Dividing the two periods,

$$\frac{T_{s}}{T_{a}}=\sqrt{\frac{k+2k_{c}}{k}}=\sqrt{1+\frac{2k_{c}}{k}}.$$ (M1)

As $k_{c}\to0$ the ratio tends to $1$, so $T_{a}\to T_{s}$. The coupling spring has gone, the two gliders are independent identical oscillators, and every possible motion is a superposition of one single frequency; the distinction between the symmetric and the antisymmetric mode therefore disappears. (A1)

As $k_{c}\to\infty$ the ratio diverges and $T_{a}\to0$. An infinitely stiff coupling spring holds the gliders a fixed distance apart, so they can no longer move in opposite directions and the antisymmetric mode is suppressed. (A1)

For any finite $k_{c}$ the ratio is greater than one, so the antisymmetric mode is always the faster of the two, while the symmetric period is completely independent of $k_{c}$. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) requires the explicit statement that equal displacements in the same direction leave the coupling spring at its natural length. A candidate who writes $T=2\pi\sqrt{m/(k+k_{c})}$ or $T=2\pi\sqrt{m/(k+2k_{c})}$ has assumed that the coupling spring acts and forfeits the mark; this is the error the question is built to expose. The two (A1)s are for obtaining $\omega_{s}=\sqrt{k/m}$ by comparison with $a=-\omega^{2}x$, and for the numerical period. Accept $6.3\ \mathrm{ms}$, $2\pi\ \mathrm{ms}$ or $6.28\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s}$.

(b) The (M1) is for recognising that the coupling spring's extension is twice the individual displacement; a candidate who uses $k_{c}x_{1}$ instead of $2k_{c}x_{1}$ obtains $\omega_{a}=\sqrt{(k+k_{c})/m}=1.6\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ and a ratio of $1.58$, which forfeits the last two marks but should still be followed through in (c) and (d). Accept the ratio as $2$, $2.0$ or $2.00$.

(c) Follow-through from (b) is expected: if the candidate's $T_{a}$ differs, the frequency, the wavelength and the phase difference must be recomputed consistently with it and are then credited. The phase-difference mark requires the substitution $\Delta\varphi=2\pi\Delta x/\lambda$, not a quoted $\pi$; accept $3.1\ \mathrm{rad}$, $180^{\circ}$ or $\pi$. The statement of which point leads carries no separate mark, but a candidate who says the farther point leads has reversed the propagation direction and should be corrected.

(d) The (M1) requires the ratio to be written as $\sqrt{1+2k_{c}/k}$; a candidate who states the two limits without deriving the ratio earns at most one mark. Both limits and both physical explanations are needed. Do not accept 'the ratio becomes zero' for $k_{c}\to0$; it becomes one.

4. PHYS-C.4-001 16 marks

(a) For a stretched string the wave speed is given by $v=\sqrt{T/\mu}$: (M1)

$$v=\sqrt{\frac{24}{0.015}}=\sqrt{1600}=40\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.$$ (A1)

The standing-wave condition for a string fixed at both ends is $L=n\lambda/2$, so $f_{n}=nv/(2L)$: (M1)

$$f_{n}=\frac{n(40)}{2(0.80)}=25n\ \mathrm{Hz},$$

giving $f_{1}=25\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $f_{2}=50\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $f_{3}=75\ \mathrm{Hz}$. (A1)

(b) A standing wave on the string has zero displacement at every node, and the nodes of the $n$th mode lie at distances $x_{j}=jL/n$ from the left support, with $j=0,1,2,\dots,n$. (M1) The touched point is held fixed, so it must be a node, and therefore $d=jL/n$ for some integer $j$. (A1) Substituting $d=0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ and $L=0.80\ \mathrm{m}$,

$$n=\frac{jL}{d}=\frac{j(0.80)}{0.30}=\frac{8j}{3}.$$ (A1)

For $n$ to be an integer, $j$ must be a multiple of $3$, so $j=3,6,9,\dots$ and the mode number must be $n=8,16,24,\dots$ — a multiple of $8$. (A1) The lowest allowed mode is therefore $n=8$, with frequency

$$f=\frac{8v}{2L}=\frac{8(40)}{1.60}=200\ \mathrm{Hz}.$$ (A1)

(c) The next two allowed modes are $n=16$ and $n=24$: (M1)

$$f_{16}=16\times25=400\ \mathrm{Hz},\qquad f_{24}=24\times25=600\ \mathrm{Hz}.$$ (A1)

In the $n=8$ mode the nodes lie at $x_{j}=j(0.80)/8=0.10j\ \mathrm{m}$ for $j=0,1,\dots,8$, that is at $0.00,0.10,0.20,\dots,0.80\ \mathrm{m}$. Counting the two fixed ends, there are $9$ nodes in all. (A1) The untouched harmonics below $200\ \mathrm{Hz}$ are $25,50,75,100,125,150$ and $175\ \mathrm{Hz}$ — seven of them — and all seven are absent. (A1)

(d) Touching at the midpoint sets $d=L/2$, so $n=jL/d=2j$ and only even mode numbers survive: the allowed frequencies are $50,100,150,\dots\ \mathrm{Hz}$. (M1) The new lowest frequency is therefore the second harmonic,

$$f=\frac{2v}{2L}=\frac{2(40)}{1.60}=50\ \mathrm{Hz}.$$ (A1)

Every odd harmonic has an antinode at the midpoint, so holding the midpoint fixed suppresses all of them; only the even harmonics, which already have a node there, are unaffected. (A1) In general a touch at a distance $L/p$ from one end selects the harmonics whose numbers are multiples of $p$: the midpoint touch ($p=2$) keeps the even harmonics, and the one-third touch ($p=3$) keeps every third.

Markscheme notes

(a) The first (M1) is for $v=\sqrt{T/\mu}$; a candidate who writes $v=T/\mu$ or $v=\sqrt{\mu/T}$ forfeits it. Accept $40\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and the set $25\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $50\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $75\ \mathrm{Hz}$. Follow-through applies: a wrong $v$ propagates consistently through (b) to (d) and those marks remain available.

(b) The essential step is that the touched point is held at zero displacement and is therefore a node. A candidate who instead treats the touch as cutting the string into two independent segments and looks for a frequency common to both will usually reach the same lowest answer here, but that is not the intended reasoning; award the method mark only if the node condition is stated. The (A1) for $n=8j/3$ must come from substituting $d=0.30$ and $L=0.80$. A candidate who assumes that a touch at three-eighths of the length gives the eighth harmonic by analogy with the familiar one-half and one-third cases earns nothing unless the condition is derived.

(c) Accept $400\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $600\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The node count must include the two fixed ends; a candidate who reports $8$ nodes has counted only the interior nodes and loses the mark. Accept the number seven or the explicit list $25$ to $175\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

(d) The (M1) is for $n=2j$, and the new lowest frequency is $50\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The final mark requires the reason to be stated in terms of the midpoint: odd harmonics have an antinode at the centre, while even harmonics already have a node there. 'Because the string is halved' is not sufficient, since the two halves are not independent in this situation.

5. PHYS-E.1-001 16 marks

(a) The emitted photoelectrons have a range of kinetic energies up to a maximum $K_{\max}$. (A1) The stopping voltage is the reverse potential difference that just reduces the photocurrent to zero, so it removes the kinetic energy of the fastest electrons: $eV_{s}=K_{\max}$. (A1) Energy conservation for the absorption of a single photon gives $hf=\phi+K_{\max}$, and therefore

$$eV_{s}=hf-\phi$$ (A1)

(b) The energy of the photon emitted in a transition equals the difference between the two levels involved. (M1) For $n=4\to2$,

$$hf_{1}=E_{4}-E_{2}=-\frac{A}{16}-\left(-\frac{A}{4}\right)=\frac{A}{4}-\frac{A}{16}=\frac{3A}{16}$$ (A1)

and for $n=2\to1$,

$$hf_{2}=E_{2}-E_{1}=-\frac{A}{4}-(-A)=A-\frac{A}{4}=\frac{3A}{4}$$ (A1)

Since $\dfrac{3A}{4}>\dfrac{3A}{16}$, the $n=2\to1$ line delivers the more energetic photons and must give the larger stopping voltage, $36.3\ \mathrm{V}$; the $n=4\to2$ line corresponds to $5.7\ \mathrm{V}$. (A1)

(c) Applying $eV_{s}=E_{\text{photon}}-\phi$ to each line, and working in electronvolts so that the stopping voltage in volts is numerically equal to the kinetic energy in electronvolts:

$$e(5.7)=\frac{3A}{16}-\phi \qquad\text{and}\qquad e(36.3)=\frac{3A}{4}-\phi$$ (M1)

Subtracting the first equation from the second eliminates $\phi$:

$$e(36.3-5.7)=\frac{3A}{4}-\frac{3A}{16}=\frac{12A-3A}{16}=\frac{9A}{16}$$ (M1)(A1)

$$A=\frac{16\times 30.6}{9}=54.4\ \mathrm{eV}$$ (A1)

Substituting this back into the first equation gives the work function:

$$\phi=\frac{3A}{16}-e(5.7)=\frac{3(54.4)}{16}-5.7=10.2-5.7=4.5\ \mathrm{eV}$$ (A1)

Each stopping voltage is the difference between a photon energy and the work function. A single reading is therefore one equation in two unknowns, $A$ and $\phi$, and cannot be solved; only the difference of the two readings removes $\phi$ and fixes $A$, after which $\phi$ follows from either line. (A1)

(d) For a hydrogen-like ion the energy-level constant is

$$A=13.6Z^{2}\ \mathrm{eV}$$ (M1)

so

$$Z^{2}=\frac{A}{13.6}=\frac{54.4}{13.6}=4.0\quad\Rightarrow\quad Z=2$$ (A1)

The ion is therefore $\mathrm{He}^{+}$, and its ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove the electron from $n=1$ to $E=0$, namely $A=54.4\ \mathrm{eV}$. (A1) Because $A\propto Z^{2}$, a larger $Z$ increases every transition energy. Since $E=hc/\lambda$, each wavelength would be shorter, so both lines would shift to shorter wavelengths, giving more energetic photons and larger stopping voltages. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The first (A1) is for the statement that the photoelectrons have a maximum kinetic energy; the second for $eV_{s}=K_{\max}$; the third for the full energy balance $eV_{s}=hf-\phi$. A candidate who writes $eV_{s}=hf+\phi$ has the sign of the work function wrong and loses the final mark. Accept 'stopping potential' for 'stopping voltage'.

(b) The (M1) is for the principle that a photon's energy equals the difference between the two levels involved. The two (A1)s are for the algebraic results $3A/16$ and $3A/4$. The final (A1) requires both the identification and a reason: because $3A/4>3A/16$, the $n=2\to1$ line gives the larger stopping voltage, $36.3\ \mathrm{V}$. A candidate who reverses the correspondence has made a physics error rather than an arithmetic slip and loses that mark.

(c) The first (M1) is for writing the two photoelectric equations, one for each line. The second (M1) is for subtracting them so that $\phi$ cancels; a candidate who substitutes one equation into the other will still reach the answer but should be given the method mark only if the algebra is shown. Accept $A$ between $54$ and $54.5\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $\phi$ between $4.4$ and $4.6\ \mathrm{eV}$. Follow-through applies: a candidate whose $A$ is slightly off but whose $\phi$ is consistent with it keeps the final mark.

(d) The (M1) is for quoting or deriving $A=13.6Z^{2}\ \mathrm{eV}$. Accept $Z=2$ with the identification of the ion as $\mathrm{He}^{+}$. The final mark requires the direction of the shift: shorter wavelengths, larger photon energies. 'The lines would move' with no direction earns nothing.

6. PHYS-E.4-001 16 marks

(a) The energy released is the mass defect of the reaction multiplied by $c^{2}$; the incident neutron is balanced by one of the three emitted neutrons, so
$$\Delta m = m_{\mathrm{U}} - m_{\mathrm{Ba}} - m_{\mathrm{Kr}} - 2m_{\mathrm{n}}$$ (M1)
$$= 235.0439 - 140.9144 - 91.9262 - 2(1.008665) = 0.18597\ \mathrm{u}$$ (A1)
$$E = \Delta m\,c^{2} = 0.18597 \times 931.5 = 173.2\ \mathrm{MeV}$$ (A1)
$$E = 173.2 \times 1.602\times10^{-13} = 2.78\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J}$$ (A1)(AG)

(b) The thermal power equals the energy per fission multiplied by the fission rate, $P = R\,E$, so
$$R = \frac{P}{E} = \frac{3.0\times10^{9}}{2.775\times10^{-11}} = 1.08\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$$ (M1)(A1)
Each fission removes one uranium-235 atom of mass
$$m_{1} = 235.0439 \times 1.661\times10^{-27} = 3.904\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$$ (M1)
so the mass consumed each second is
$$R\,m_{1} = (1.081\times10^{20})(3.904\times10^{-25}) = 4.22\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$$ (A1)

(c) In one day the fuel consumed is
$$4.22\times10^{-5} \times 86400 = 3.65\ \mathrm{kg}$$ (A1)
while the mass converted to energy follows from $E = mc^{2}$:
$$m = \frac{P\,t}{c^{2}} = \frac{(3.0\times10^{9})(86400)}{(2.998\times10^{8})^{2}} = 2.88\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg}$$ (M1)(A1)
The ratio is $3.65/(2.88\times10^{-3}) = 1.3\times10^{3}$, close to $235/0.186$: almost all of the fuel mass is carried by nucleons that survive the reaction, and only the mass defect becomes energy. (A1)

(d) The core holds $0.030 \times 1.0\times10^{5} = 3.0\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{kg}$ of uranium-235, or $N = 3.0\times10^{3}/(3.904\times10^{-25}) = 7.7\times10^{27}$ nuclei, so
$$E_{\mathrm{tot}} = N\,E = (7.7\times10^{27})(2.775\times10^{-11}) = 2.1\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{J}$$ (M1)(A1)
At $3.0\ \mathrm{GW}$ the operating lifetime is
$$t = \frac{2.1\times10^{17}}{3.0\times10^{9}} = 7.1\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s} \approx 820\ \mathrm{days}$$ (A1)
Raising the enrichment to $4.5\%$ leaves the energy per fission unchanged, since it is fixed by the mass defect, and leaves the fission rate needed for $3.0\ \mathrm{GW}$ unchanged; it multiplies the number of fissionable nuclei by $1.5$, so the total energy and the lifetime rise by that factor. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) requires the mass defect as reactants minus products, with the three emitted neutrons counted. A candidate who omits them from the products, or counts only two, obtains the wrong $\Delta m$ and forfeits the value marks but may still earn the conversion mark by follow-through. Accept $173\ \mathrm{MeV}$ or $2.8\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J}$; using $1\ \mathrm{MeV} = 1.60\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{J}$ gives $2.77\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J}$, also accepted.

(b) The (M1) is for $P = R\,E$; a candidate who divides $E$ by $P$ has inverted it and loses the value mark. Accept $1.1\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

(c) Follow-through from (b) applies throughout. The final mark is for the explanation, not the number: the fuel consumed is the mass of the uranium nuclei, whereas only the mass defect, roughly $0.08\%$ of it, becomes energy.

(d) The (M1) is for multiplying by the enrichment fraction. Accept lifetimes between $7\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s}$ and $850\ \mathrm{days}$. The final mark needs both halves: the energy per fission is unchanged, and the total energy scales with the number of nuclei. Claiming the reactor becomes more powerful at a fixed control-rod setting confuses the rate with the energy per fission.

7. PHYS-E.5-001 16 marks

(a) The energy released is the increase in the total binding energy of the nuclei: (M1)
$$Q = B(^{12}\mathrm{C}) - 3B(^{4}\mathrm{He})$$
Each binding energy per nucleon must first be multiplied by its mass number, because the two nuclei do not contain the same number of nucleons: (M1)
$$B(^{4}\mathrm{He}) = 4(7.074) = 28.296\ \mathrm{MeV}, \qquad B(^{12}\mathrm{C}) = 12(7.680) = 92.16\ \mathrm{MeV}$$ (A1)
$$Q = 92.16 - 3(28.296) = 92.16 - 84.888 = 7.27\ \mathrm{MeV}$$ (A1)
Subtracting the per-nucleon figures directly would give $7.680 - 7.074 = 0.606\ \mathrm{MeV}$, which is the release per nucleon, not per reaction; the reaction binds twelve nucleons, so that answer is twelve times too small.

(b) The rest energy of the three helium nuclei consumed is (M1)
$$3m_{\mathrm{He}}c^{2} = 3(4.0026)(931.5) = 1.119\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{MeV}$$ (A1)
so the fraction of the fuel rest energy that appears as released energy is

(a) A. Resolve the launch velocity: the horizontal component is $30\cos 40^{\circ}=22.98$ and the vertical component is $30\sin 40^{\circ}=19.28$. The horizontal component is constant throughout, and at the highest point the vertical component has been reduced to zero by gravity, so the speed there is simply $22.98$, i.e. $23.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. (A1) The near miss is C, which reports the component that has vanished rather than the one that remains.

(b) A. The range is $v^{2}\sin 2\theta/g$, and $\sin\bigl(2(90^{\circ}-\theta)\bigr)=\sin(180^{\circ}-2\theta)=\sin 2\theta$, so the two ranges are equal. The flight time is $2v\sin\theta/g$, which is larger for the larger angle. (A1) Distractor D inverts both conclusions and C gets only the time right.

(c) A. With drag $\mathbf{F}=-k\mathbf{v}$ the component equations are separate: vertically, $m\dot v_{y}=-kv_{y}-mg$ for both balls, with the same initial condition $v_{y}=0$. The horizontal motion therefore cannot influence the descent, and both land together. (A1) This is a genuinely counter-intuitive result and B, C and D are all attempts to make the longer or faster trajectory cost time.

(d) A. Distance $=8.0(3.0)+4.0(2.0)=24+8=32\ \mathrm{m}$; displacement $=24-8=16\ \mathrm{m}$. Dividing by $5.0\ \mathrm{s}$ gives an average speed of $6.4$ and an average velocity of $3.2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. (A1) The pair is what is being tested: many candidates compute one correctly and then quote it twice.

(e) A. The crossing time is the width divided by the component of the boat's velocity perpendicular to the bank. That component is greatest, at the full $4.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, when the boat points straight across, so $t=120/4.0=30\ \mathrm{s}$ and the drift is $3.0\times30=90\ \mathrm{m}$. (A1) D is the trap: $45\ \mathrm{s}$ and landing opposite is a correct calculation of the *shortest path*, since the resultant speed is then $\sqrt{4.0^{2}-3.0^{2}}=2.65\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $120/2.65=45\ \mathrm{s}$, but the question asks for the shortest time.

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking. (a) Accept $23.0$ or $23.1\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. (b) The reasoning mark is implicit in the key: a candidate who states equal ranges but equal times has not earned it. (c) This is the hardest item in the cluster and the one most likely to be guessed wrong; accept any answer that identifies the independence of the components, and note that the result would be different for drag proportional to the square of the speed, where the components no longer separate and the fired ball would in fact land later. (d) Accept fractions; the unit mark is not separate here. (e) Award no follow-through from a mistaken minimum-time aim: the $45\ \mathrm{s}$ figure belongs to a different optimisation and using it for the drift gives $135\ \mathrm{m}$, which is not offered. Candidates who eliminate C and D first, on the grounds that cancelling the current must cost time, are using the strongest route to the key.

43. PHYS-B.4-101 5 marks

(a) A. Adiabatic means $Q=0$, so the first law $\Delta U = Q - W_{\text{by}}$ becomes $\Delta U = -W_{\text{by}} = W_{\text{on}}$. Compressing the gas means work is done *on* it, so $\Delta U>0$ and the temperature rises. (A1) B confuses adiabatic with isothermal and D then inherits that confusion.

(b) A. With $W_{\text{by}}=+200\ \mathrm{J}$ and $Q=+500\ \mathrm{J}$, $\Delta U = 500-200=+300\ \mathrm{J}$. (A1) The sign convention is the whole question: B adds, C keeps the magnitude but flips the sign.

(c) A. The achieved efficiency is $\eta = W/Q_{\text{in}} = 300/900 = 0.333$. The Carnot limit depends only on the reservoir temperatures: $\eta_{C}=1-T_{C}/T_{H}=1-300/600=0.500$. (A1) The two numbers answer different questions and D, at $67\%$, comes from subtracting the achieved efficiency from one and so reporting the rejected fraction of the input as if it were the useful output.

(d) A. For an isothermal change $pV=nRT$ is constant, so $W=\int p\,dV = nRT\ln(V_{2}/V_{1}) = 8.31\times300\times\ln 2 = 1.73\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{J}$. (A1) B is $nRT$ with the logarithm omitted, and C treats the pressure as falling linearly so that a mean pressure could be used.

(e) A. Free expansion into a vacuum does no work and involves no heat transfer, so $\Delta U=0$ for an ideal gas, but the entropy increases because the gas occupies a larger volume. The second law forbids the other three: B is the Clausius statement, C is the Kelvin statement, and D contradicts the entropy form directly. (A1)

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking. (a) Accept any answer that identifies the internal energy as increasing by the work done on the gas. (b) Candidates using the opposite sign convention consistently, so that work done on the gas is positive, should still be credited if their arithmetic and conclusion match their convention. (c) Accept $33$ or $33.3\%$ and $50\%$; a candidate who quotes the Carnot value as the achieved efficiency has not distinguished the two ideas and earns nothing. (d) Accept $1.7$ to $1.73\ \mathrm{kJ}$; note that $\ln 2$ is the only place a logarithm appears in this subtopic and it is frequently forgotten. (e) Accept any correct statement of why free expansion is permitted; a candidate who says it is permitted because no heat flows has the right answer for the wrong reason, since the decisive point is that the entropy increases. Note that B and C are both statements of the second law rather than the first, so a candidate who justifies rejecting them by saying energy would not be conserved has used the wrong law and should not be credited. Candidates often find (e) the hardest item despite it requiring no calculation, because it asks which process is permitted rather than which is forbidden; three of the four options are familiar prohibitions, so the correct answer looks wrong at first reading.

44. PHYS-C.2-101 5 marks

(a) A. A clamped end is a fixed boundary: the displacement there must be zero at every instant, so the reflected pulse must cancel the incident one at that point, which means it is inverted. A free end gives an uninverted reflection instead. (A1) The distinction is the boundary condition, not the size of the pulse.

(b) A. Frequency is set by the source and is unchanged on crossing the boundary. With $v=f\lambda$, the deep-water wavelength is $0.80/2.0=0.40\ \mathrm{m}$ and the shallow-water wavelength is $0.60/2.0=0.30\ \mathrm{m}$. (A1) C is the common error: it is the frequency that is invariant, not the wavelength.

(c) A. A path difference of $1.5\lambda$ equals three half-wavelengths, an odd number, so the phase difference is $3\pi$ radians, equivalent to $\pi$, and the superposing arrivals cancel. (A1) C is the trap worth noting: it names the correct phase difference and then draws the opposite conclusion.

(d) A. Amplitudes add before squaring: the resultant is $3A+A=4A$, so the resultant intensity is proportional to $16A^{2}$ while the larger wave alone is proportional to $9A^{2}$, giving $16/9=1.78$. (A1) B omits the squaring and C treats the waves as incoherent, which would give $(9+1)/9=1.11$ and would be right only if the phase relationship were random.

(e) A. The first polariser transmits half the unpolarised intensity, giving $I_{0}/2$ of plane-polarised light. Malus' law then multiplies by $\cos^{2}60^{\circ}=0.25$, so the final intensity is $I_{0}/2\times0.25=0.125\,I_{0}$. (A1) B, at $0.25\,I_{0}$, is Malus' law applied without the initial halving.

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking. (a) Accept any statement that the pulse is reflected and inverted; a candidate who adds that the reflection at a free end is not inverted shows the contrasting case and should not be penalised for the extra material. (b) Accept $0.40$ and $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$; the mark is for holding the frequency constant, so a candidate who changes the frequency and gets both wavelengths wrong earns nothing. (c) Accept any justification in terms of an odd number of half-wavelengths; note that $1.5\lambda$ and $2.5\lambda$ give the same verdict, so the answer does not depend on which odd multiple was chosen. (d) Accept $1.7$ to $1.8$, or the exact fraction $16/9$; a candidate who writes $16$ has done the hard part and then omitted the division, and should be given the benefit of the doubt only if the ratio was clearly intended. (e) Accept $0.125$ or $I_{0}/8$; the two-stage structure is the point, so a single-stage answer earns nothing.

45. PHYS-A.5-101 5 marks

(a) A. The second postulate fixes the speed of light at $c$ for every inertial observer, so the source's motion is irrelevant and no transformation is needed. (A1) B and C are the two Galilean habits, and they are listed because $1.60c$ and $0.40c$ are the numbers a student actually produces rather than numbers chosen at random.

(b) A. The lifetime quoted for the muon at rest is the proper time, so it is the shortest of all measured lifetimes and the laboratory value is larger: $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.98^{2}} = 5.03$, giving $5.03 \times 2.2 = 11\ \mathrm{\mu s}$. (A1) This is the effect that lets muons created in the upper atmosphere reach sea level at all.

(c) A. The proper length is measured in the rod's own frame, and the moving observer measures $L = L_{0}/\gamma = 100/1.67 = 60\ \mathrm{m}$. (A1) The identification of which length is proper is the whole question; the division is trivial once that is settled.

(d) A. Neither spacecraft's frame is the Earth frame, so the speeds must be combined with $u' = (u+v)/(1+uv/c^{2}) = 1.50/1.5625 = 0.96c$. (A1) The denominator is what keeps the result below $c$.

(e) A. $\gamma = E/E_{0} = 1.533/0.511 = 3.00$, and inverting $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}$ gives $\beta = \sqrt{1-1/9} = 0.943$. (A1) The three distractors are all the same omission at different depths.

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking. (a) Accept $3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ written in full; no credit for any option that depends on the source speed. (b) Accept $11$ to $11.1\ \mathrm{\mu s}$; award nothing if the candidate quotes $\gamma$ correctly and then divides, since the direction of the effect is the point being tested. (c) Accept $60\ \mathrm{m}$ or $60.0\ \mathrm{m}$; a candidate who states that the transverse dimensions are unchanged shows useful extra knowledge and should not be penalised. (d) Accept $0.96c$ or $2.88 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; any answer above $c$ is wrong on principle and should be treated as evidence the transformation was not used, whichever sign error produced it. (e) Accept $0.94c$; the exact surd $\sqrt{8/9}$ is preferable but not required. Watch for candidates who convert $1.533\ \mathrm{MeV}$ to joules and then lose a factor of $10^{6}$; the ratio method avoids this entirely and is worth encouraging in feedback.

46. PHYS-C.5-101 5 marks

(a) A. A moving source compresses the wavefronts ahead of it, so the wavelength in the medium is shortened while the observer samples at the usual rate: $f' = fv/(v-u_{s}) = 500 \times 340/310 = 548\ \mathrm{Hz}$. (A1) Note that the denominator contains the source speed, because it is the spacing of the crests that has changed.

(b) A. A moving observer meets unchanged wavefronts more often, so it is the numerator that changes: $f' = f(v+u_{o})/v = 500 \times 370/340 = 544\ \mathrm{Hz}$. (A1) The two answers differ by about $4\ \mathrm{Hz}$ for identical speeds, and that difference is the whole point of asking both questions: the situations are physically different even though the relative speed is the same.

(c) A. The wavelength is longer than the laboratory value, so the galaxy is receding. The redshift is $z = (689-656)/656 = 0.0503$, and since $z \ll 1$ the recession speed is $zc = 1.51 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. (A1) Direction first, magnitude second: the sign of the wavelength change carries the physics and is worth stating before any arithmetic.

(d) A. Reflection imposes the shift twice and only the along-beam component contributes, so $\Delta f = 2fv\cos 60^{\circ}/c = 2 \times 5.0\times10^{6} \times 0.40 \times 0.500/1540 = 1.30\ \mathrm{kHz}$. (A1) The factor of two is not a correction: the blood is first a moving observer and then a moving source.

(e) A. The wavelets emitted along the flight path superpose on a cone whose half-angle satisfies $\sin\theta = v_{\text{sound}}/v_{\text{source}} = 340/510 = 0.667$, giving $\theta = 41.8^{\circ}$. (A1) A faster aircraft makes the cone narrower, which is the check that rules out the cosine.

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking. (a) Accept $548$ to $549\ \mathrm{Hz}$. (b) Accept $544\ \mathrm{Hz}$; if a candidate gives $548\ \mathrm{Hz}$ here, note that they have used the source formula and consider whether part (a) should also be reviewed, since the same error produces both. (c) Accept $1.5 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and require the direction; a magnitude alone is not the answer to 'what is the motion'. Accept the relativistic expression if offered, but note that it changes the third figure only. (d) Accept $1.29$ to $1.30\ \mathrm{kHz}$, and accept $1299\ \mathrm{Hz}$. Award nothing if the factor of two is missing, even if the cosine is handled correctly, because the reflection is the part of the physics that is specific to this technique. (e) Accept $42^{\circ}$ or the exact statement $\sin\theta = 2/3$; a candidate who writes $\sin\theta = 3/2$ and then notes the impossibility has understood the geometry better than one who produces $41.8^{\circ}$ by rote, and should be credited.
