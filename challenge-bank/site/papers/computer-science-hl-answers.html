1. CS-P2-003 22 marks

(a) The utility adds $90\,000\times24\times365=7.884\times10^{8}$ rows per year. (M1)(A1) At 12 bytes each that is $7.884\times10^8\times12=9.46\times10^{9}$ bytes, so about $9.46$ GB per year and roughly $57$ GB over the six-year history. (A1) An operational row-oriented table is the wrong home for it because the workload is not transactional: nobody retrieves one reading, and the queries the utility needs are aggregations over a meter, a street or a month. A column-oriented warehouse with a meter dimension and a date dimension lets those aggregations read only the columns they need, and keeps the operational billing system — which does need single-row access — free of a table two orders of magnitude larger than anything else in it. (R1)

(b) First, missing and impossible readings must be handled. Smart meters lose connectivity and report gaps, and some report zero for a period when the meter has failed rather than when no water was used; training on those rows teaches the model that a fault looks like normal consumption. The step is to flag gaps explicitly, distinguish a transmitted zero from a missing value, and either interpolate short gaps or mark them as a feature. (M1)(A1) Second, the readings must be aggregated into a fixed-shape window before training, because the model is asked about a meter-week and the raw data is hourly. Features such as the mean night-time flow, the variance of hourly flow, and the ratio of night flow to day flow have to be computed over each window; otherwise the model is given 168 loosely comparable numbers per row and no way to learn what a burst precursor looks like. (M1)(A1)

(c) Over six years the utility has $90\,000\times52\times6=2.808\times10^{7}$ meter-weeks and only 1240 positive cases, so a model that predicts 'no burst' every single time scores $1-\dfrac{1240}{2.808\times10^{7}}=99.996\%$ accuracy while doing nothing at all. (M1)(A1) The two measures to report are recall, the fraction of real bursts the model catches, and precision, the fraction of flagged cases that turn out to be real. (A1) Recall tells the utility how much of the damage it avoids; precision tells it how much of its field capacity it spends on nothing. Neither is visible in the accuracy figure, and at this base rate accuracy cannot distinguish a useful model from a useless one. (R1)

(d) The model flags $0.001\times2.808\times10^{7}=28\,080$ meter-weeks. At 70% recall it catches $0.70\times1240=868$ real bursts, so the number of false alarms is $28\,080-868=27\,212$. (M1)(A1)(A1) Precision is therefore $\dfrac{868}{28\,080}=3.1\%$: roughly thirty false alarms for every real burst found. (A1) With 40 crews the utility can investigate 40 sites a day, so clearing the flagged list occupies about $\dfrac{28\,080}{40}=702$ crew-days, and the list regenerates continuously. Against that, an uncaught burst costs an emergency repair, road closure and water loss that is far more expensive than a planned visit. The model is therefore not useless, but it is not deployable at this flag rate: the correct evaluation is that the utility should either raise the threshold until precision reaches a level its crews can absorb, or use the score to rank rather than to flag, sending out only as many visits per week as capacity allows and accepting lower recall. (R1)

(e) *Risk 1: unequal service by area.* Meter age, pipe material and consumption patterns all correlate with the age and income of the neighbourhood, so a model trained on historical bursts will systematically score poorer areas as riskier, and those areas will receive a disproportionate share of the investigative visits and of the blame. The governance measure is to audit the flag rate and the precision separately by deprivation decile before deployment and at fixed intervals afterwards, and to withhold deployment if the false-alarm rate in any decile is materially worse than the fleet average. (M1)(A1)(A1) *Risk 2: adverse action against customers identified as meter faults.* The same model that finds faulty meters produces a list of households whose consumption looks anomalous, and a faulty meter under-reads as often as it over-reads, so a customer may be presented with a back-bill generated by a statistical score they cannot see or contest. The governance measure is to require a physical meter test before any billing adjustment is made, to prohibit the score from being used as evidence on its own, and to publish the model's error rate on the customer-facing page. (M1)(A1)

In (a) allow 9.4 to 9.5 GB and credit the warehouse argument only if the candidate contrasts the workload rather than merely asserting that the table is big. In (b) credit any two well-chosen steps, but require the problem as well as the step; 'clean the data' earns nothing. In (c) the accuracy figure must be computed or at least quantified, not asserted. In (d) allow 27 000 to 27 500 false alarms and 3.0% to 3.2% precision, and award the highest mark only where the candidate converts the alarm count into crew-days and reaches a conclusion about deployability rather than restating the numbers. In (e) award up to three marks per risk, but note that the governance measure must be specific and testable; 'be transparent' or 'monitor the model' is not creditworthy. In (e) a candidate may raise environmental risk, the security of a customer-consumption database, or the use of the score in insurance or credit decisions. All are creditworthy provided a specific control is attached. What should not be credited is a list of harms with no control, or a control that cannot be checked, such as 'use the model fairly'.

2. CS-P2-002 19 marks

(a) *Unsorted array.* Adding a patient is $O(1)$, simply appending. (A1) Producing the most urgent patient requires scanning all $n$ entries, so it is $O(n)$; at $n=18$ that is 18 comparisons every time a doctor becomes free. Escalation is $O(n)$ to find the patient and then $O(1)$ to change the category. Removal of a departed patient is $O(n)$ to find and $O(n)$ to close the gap. (A1)

*Sorted array.* Producing the most urgent patient is $O(1)$, taking the head. (A1) Insertion is $O(n)$ because on average $n/2$ entries must be shifted to make room, and escalation is worse still: the patient must be found, removed and reinserted at a new position. The structure optimises exactly the operation that is already cheap and pays for it on the two that are not. (A1)

*Binary max-heap.* Producing the most urgent patient is $O(1)$ at the root and $O(\log n)$ to restore the heap after removal; insertion is $O(\log n)$. (A1) Escalation is $O(\log n)$ if the position of the patient is known, but $O(n)$ if it is not, because a heap offers no search. Removal of a departed patient has the same difficulty. The heap is therefore the best of the three for the two operations that dominate, and its weakness is that it cannot find an arbitrary entry quickly. (R1)

```
3. CS-P2-001 18 marks

(a) Each probe is sampled $\dfrac{10\times3600}{30}=1200$ times per day, so each vehicle produces $4\times1200=4800$ readings per day. (M1)(A1) At 7 bytes each, the fleet produces

$$4800\times7\times140\times300=1.4112\times10^{9}\text{ bytes}$$

per year, which is $\dfrac{1.4112\times10^9}{2^{30}}=1.31$ GiB. (A1) Stored as 80-byte text the same readings occupy

$$4800\times80\times140\times300\div 2^{30}=15.0\text{ GiB},$$

roughly eleven times as much. (A1)

(b) The aggregate payload is only $140\times\dfrac{7}{30}\approx33$ bytes per second, so bandwidth is not the objection. (A1) The objection is per-request cost: each reading would carry its own transport handshake and encryption overhead, so a 7-byte reading could easily be carried inside a request of 800 bytes or more, multiplying the traffic by a factor well over a hundred and adding a fresh handshake every 30 seconds on every vehicle. (M1)(A1) Mobile coverage in a refrigerated vehicle is intermittent, and a per-reading push has no way to buffer across a dropout, so readings would simply be lost when a vehicle passes through a tunnel. (A1) An edge device in the vehicle changes the trade-off decisively: it can buffer readings locally through a coverage gap, batch them into one request every few minutes, evaluate the excursion rule locally so that an alert can be raised within seconds even with no network at all, and forward only alerts plus batched history to the centre. (R1)

(c) Four tables are needed:

VEHICLE(vehicleID PK, registration, depotID FK, commissionedDate)

CONSIGNMENT(consignmentID PK, vehicleID FK, departedAt, returnedAt, productCode)

PROBE(probeID PK, vehicleID FK, positionLabel, calibratedOn)

EXCURSION_ALERT(alertID PK, probeID FK, consignmentID FK, raisedAt, peakTemperature, durationSeconds, acknowledgedBy)

(M1)(A1)(A1) The schema is in third normal form because every non-key attribute depends on the whole of its table's key and on nothing else: probe calibration belongs to the probe, not to the alert, and the depot belongs to the vehicle, not to the consignment. (A1) The relationship between consignments and alerts is one-to-many: one consignment may generate any number of alerts, including none, and each alert belongs to exactly one consignment. (A1)

(d) The depot-upload proposal is cheap and simple, and for the storage requirement alone it would be adequate: the files arrive intact once a day and the six-year archive is straightforward to keep. (A1) It fails the alert deadline outright. A reading taken at the start of a shift would not reach the centre for up to ten hours, against a requirement of 60 seconds, and a spoiled consignment would be delivered before anyone knew. (M1)(A1) It also weakens the evidence trail: a vehicle involved in an accident, or one whose local storage fails, takes the whole day's record with it, and there is no independent copy against which a disputed reading could be checked. (A1) The correct evaluation is therefore that the proposal is acceptable only for the archive tier, not for the alerting tier. A hybrid is justified: the edge device raises alerts immediately over the mobile network, while the full-resolution history is batched opportunistically during the shift and reconciled at the depot. Alerts are small and time-critical; history is large and not. Splitting the two by their deadlines rather than by their size is the whole design. (R1) One further consideration belongs in any real design and is worth raising in an answer: the clock. Timestamps are generated on the vehicle, and if a vehicle's clock drifts then an alert can appear to precede the reading that caused it, which would undermine the evidence trail completely. Synchronising the edge device periodically and recording both the device time and the server receipt time on every alert removes that risk at negligible cost, and a candidate who spots it unprompted is thinking about the system rather than about the question.

4. CS-P2-005 17 marks

(a) The attacker positions the receiver within range and captures one legitimate frame, which contains the literal text OPEN followed by the 32-bit secret $S$ in hexadecimal. (A1) Because the frame is not encrypted, the attacker reads $S$ directly from the captured bytes; because nothing in the frame varies, no computation is needed at all. (A1)

To unlock later, the attacker retransmits exactly the captured bytes. The lock compares them with its stored $S$, finds them identical, and releases the bolt. (A1)

The attack remains available indefinitely, or until $S$ is changed, because the accepted value never varies and there is no timestamp, counter or expiry. Capturing one frame grants permanent access. (A1)

(b) Protocol:

1. The phone sends a request frame containing HELLO and its device identifier. (A1)
2. The lock generates a fresh 64-bit random value $N$ (a nonce) using its hardware random source, stores it with a short expiry, and transmits CHALLENGE followed by $N$. (A1)(A1)
3. The phone computes $R = H(S \mathbin\| N)$, where $H$ is a keyed hash and $\|$ denotes concatenation, and returns RESPONSE followed by $R$. (A1)
4. The lock computes the same value from its stored $S$ and the $N$ it issued, compares with $R$ using a constant-time comparison, and releases the bolt only if they match and $N$ has not expired or been used before. (A1)

(c) The captured bytes are now useless because each exchange uses a different $N$. A response $R$ valid for one nonce produces a completely different hash for the next, and the phone never transmits $S$ itself, so the attacker who captures a million exchanges has a million $(N_i, R_i)$ pairs but no way to recover $S$ or to compute the response to a nonce they have not seen. (M1)(A1)

It does not defeat an active man-in-the-middle who relays a challenge to the genuine phone and returns the genuine response, because the protocol authenticates the phone to the lock but not the lock to the phone, and it does not defeat physical attacks on the lock or compromise of the phone itself. (A1)

If the attacker instead tries to guess the next nonce, the chance that a 64-bit value repeats one seen before, by the birthday bound after $10^{6}$ exchanges, is approximately (M1)

$$\frac{N^{2}}{2^{65}} = \frac{10^{12}}{3.69\times10^{19}} \approx 2.7\times10^{-8},$$

that is, about one chance in forty million. (A1)

(d) The additional cost is negligible and the protocol is clearly acceptable. (A1) The extra energy per operation is $0.90 - 0.15 = 0.75\ \mathrm{mJ}$ for the hash plus a second transmission of about $0.15\ \mathrm{mJ}$, so about $0.90\ \mathrm{mJ}$ in total. At 25 operations per day that is (M1)

$$0.90\times10^{-3} \times 25 \times 365 = 8.2\ \mathrm{J}\ \text{per year}.$$

The battery stores $2.4\ \mathrm{Ah} \times 3600\ \mathrm{s} \times 3.0\ \mathrm{V} = 25\,920\ \mathrm{J}$, so the extra drain is (A1)

$$\frac{8.2}{25\,920} \times 100\% \approx 0.032\%\ \text{of capacity per year}.$$

On latency, the protocol adds one round trip of $40\ \mathrm{ms}$ to the $250\ \mathrm{ms}$ budget, leaving $210\ \mathrm{ms}$ for the hash and the bolt mechanism against the $4\ \mathrm{ms}$ the hash actually needs. (A1) The conclusion is that the security improvement is effectively free, so there is no engineering justification for shipping the static-token version.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award the marks for capturing the frame, retransmitting it unchanged, the lock's equality check succeeding, and the indefinite lifetime of the attack. The last is the mark most often missed and is the reason the attack is severe rather than merely possible. (b) Accept any challenge-response design in which the lock supplies a fresh unpredictable value and the response depends on both that value and the shared secret. Credit equivalent constructions such as a counter-based scheme, but note that a counter requires synchronised state and ask what happens when it desynchronises. Do not credit a design in which the phone chooses the nonce unless it also signs it, since then the nonce provides no freshness guarantee to the lock. (c) The 'does not defeat' mark requires a specific named attack, not a vague statement that no system is perfectly secure; man-in-the-middle, physical tampering and phone compromise are all acceptable. For the probability, accept the birthday form $N^2/2^{65}$ or a simpler $10^6/2^{64}$ estimate, and accept any value of the order of $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-7}$. (d) The calculation must appear; a qualitative assertion that the cost is small earns nothing. Accept any reasonable energy model that uses the figures given, and credit a candidate who also notes that the hardware random source and the constant-time comparison are implementation requirements rather than automatic consequences of the design.

(b) The claim is unsound because the number of steps the inner loop performs is not a constant — it is the length of the steady run, which is a property of the *data*, not of the code. (M1) The claim assumes that consecutive readings almost always differ by more than the tolerance, so that the loop exits after one or two steps. (A1) That assumption is false for this application: heart rate sampled once a second during steady exercise changes by far less than 5 beats per minute between consecutive samples, so long runs are the normal case and the loop walks back a long way. (A1) The greatest number of steps occurs when every consecutive difference is within tolerance — a constant series, or a slow monotonic drift. For each $i$ the loop then runs all the way back to index 0, giving $0+1+2+\dots+(n-1) = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ steps, which is quadratic rather than linear. (A1)

(c)

def steady_runs(readings, tol=5):
    n = len(readings)
    runs = [0] * n
    for i in range(1, n):
        step = readings[i] - readings[i - 1]
        if -tol <= step <= tol:
            runs[i] = runs[i - 1] + 1
    return runs

(M1) for a single pass that keeps no inner loop. (A1) for the observation that makes it work: the steady run ending at i is either empty or it is the run ending at i - 1 extended by exactly one reading, so the answer for i can be read off the answer for i - 1. (A1) for runs[i] = runs[i - 1] + 1 and (A1) for initialising every entry to 0, which is what makes the run reset after a step larger than the tolerance. (A1) for using no library module and no abs, expressing the tolerance as the two-sided test -tol <= step <= tol.

The work done is $n-1$ comparisons and one assignment per reading, so the time is $O(n)$ and the extra memory is $O(n)$ for the output. A Java version of this paper asks for the same algorithm; the translation is a for loop over the array indices and the identical recurrence, so the language is irrelevant to the reasoning.

(d) The proposal should be rejected, for three reasons.

First, it does not change the complexity. The inner loop can still run up to 1,000 steps for every reading, so the worst case is still proportional to $1000n$; capping the loop divides the running time by a constant, and a constant factor is not a change of order. (M1) With 86,400 readings a day the uncapped worst case is $86{,}400 \times 86{,}399 / 2 = 3{,}732{,}436{,}800$ inner steps, the capped version still performs up to $86{,}400 \times 1000 = 86{,}400{,}000$, and the linear version performs 86,399. (A1)

Second, and more seriously, the cap makes the result *wrong* precisely where the app matters. A steady run of 1,500 readings would be reported as 1,000, and a run of 4,000 as 1,000; the error is silent, it is always an under-report, and it is concentrated on the longest runs — which are exactly the steady states the app exists to find. (A1)

Third, the trade is unnecessary, because part (c) already gives an exact linear algorithm. Capping the loop trades a correctness guarantee for a constant factor when a change of order was available at the same cost. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Accept any trace, as a table or as prose. The essential marks are the mechanism (walk backwards while *each consecutive pair* is within tolerance) and the result. A candidate who compares every earlier reading against the current reading rather than against its own predecessor has misread the function and forfeits the trace marks — this is the single most common error.

(b) The two discriminating marks are the unstated assumption (that consecutive readings usually differ by more than the tolerance) and the worst-case count $n(n-1)/2$. A candidate who writes "it is actually O(n squared)" without naming the assumption earns one mark only. Accept a description of the worst case as "a constant series", "a steady recording" or "a slow drift"; require the recognition that the loop runs to index 0 each time.

(c) Accept any single-pass algorithm that returns the same list, including one that builds the list by appending rather than pre-allocating it. Do not award the linear mark to a candidate who keeps a nested loop, even a faster one — the question is about the order of growth. A candidate who uses abs despite the prohibition should be told the constraint was stated; the reasoning marks may still be awarded. A candidate who uses the csv-style two-pointer scan but does not derive the recurrence should be capped at two marks.

(d) The evaluation must distinguish a constant-factor improvement from a change of order. A candidate who says "1,000 is small enough" has missed that the cap silently corrupts the longest runs, and that those runs are the app's output. Award the final mark only when the answer connects the cap to a correctness loss rather than to performance alone.

6. CS-B3.2-001 16 marks

(a) For SeatPlan(20, 12) the attribute annual_fee holds 108. (A1) It is not 2160 because of the order in which the two constructors run. The first statement of SeatPlan.__init__ is super().__init__(months), and inside Plan.__init__ the assignment self.annual_fee = self.monthly_fee() * months calls self.monthly_fee(). (M1) Because monthly_fee is overridden, that call dispatches to SeatPlan.monthly_fee, which reads self.seats. At that moment the instance has only months in its attribute dictionary - the statement self.seats = seats has not run yet - so the lookup falls back to the class attribute seats = 1. The fee is therefore computed for a one-seat plan: $1 \times 9 = 9$ per month and $9 \times 12 = 108$ per year. (A1) Only after the superclass constructor returns is the instance attribute set to 20, and by then annual_fee has already been stored and is never recomputed; a later call to monthly_fee() correctly returns $20 \times 9 = 180$, so the live query and the cached value disagree.

(b) The defect is not that a constructor calls a method. It is that a constructor calls a method on an object that is only partly built, so the method can observe a field that does not exist yet. Composition removes the half-built object by making the pricing rule a separate, fully constructed collaborator:

class SeatPricing:
    PER_SEAT = 9
    def __init__(self, seats):
        self.seats = seats
    def monthly_fee(self):
        return self.seats * self.PER_SEAT

class Plan:
def __init__(self, months, pricing):
self.months = months
self.pricing = pricing
self.annual_fee = pricing.monthly_fee() * months

p = Plan(12, SeatPricing(20))
print(p.annual_fee) # 2160
```

(M1) for giving Plan a pricing collaborator instead of a subclass that overrides a price method. (A1) for constructing that collaborator completely before the plan uses it, so no method ever reads a field that has not been set. (A1) for computing the annual fee from the collaborator's result. (A1) for the correct value, $180 \times 12 = 2160$. (A1) for noting that Plan.__init__ still calls a method, but on a different object that is already finished, which is why the same line is now safe.

Two inheritance-preserving fixes also work and are acceptable. Assigning the subclass field before the superclass constructor runs:

class SeatPlan(Plan):

        self.seats = seats        # set first
        super().__init__(months)  # monthly_fee() now sees 20

and passing the already-computed fee up to the base constructor, so the base never calls an overridable method at all:

class Plan:
    def __init__(self, months, monthly_fee):
        self.months = months
        self.annual_fee = monthly_fee * months

All three produce annual_fee == 2160. (A1)

(a) An in-order traversal visits the left subtree, then the node, then the right subtree, and applied to tree A it yields 20, 30, 35, 45, 50. (A1) The traversal is ascending because of the ordering property of a binary search tree: every key in the left subtree of a node is smaller than the node's key, and every key in the right subtree is larger. (M1) Visiting left, then node, then right therefore emits each key only after every smaller key in its subtree, and before every larger one, so the output is sorted whatever the shape of the tree.

(b) Insertion compares the new key with each node from the root downwards and moves left when the key is smaller and right when it is larger. Starting at 30, the key 40 is larger, so it goes right; at 45 it is smaller, so it goes left; at 35 it is larger, so it goes right, and the position is empty. (M1)(A1) The key 40 therefore becomes the right child of 35. (A1) That path visits four nodes, 30, 45, 35 and 40, so the height of the tree rises from 3 to 4.

(c) A node with two children cannot simply be removed, because the two subtrees would have to be reattached. The standard method is to replace the key being deleted with its in-order successor, which is the smallest key in its right subtree, and then delete that successor from the right subtree, where it is guaranteed to have at most one child. (M1)(A1) For tree A, the in-order successor of 45 is 50, since 50 is the only key in 45's right subtree and is therefore its smallest. (A1) The key at the node is overwritten with 50, and the original node holding 50, which is a leaf, is then removed. The resulting tree has root 30, left child 20, and right child 50 with left child 35, so its height is 3. (A1)

(d) In a tree shaped like A the height grows with the logarithm of the number of keys, because each comparison halves the range still to be searched. (M1) For 200 000 keys the height is about log2(200000), which is 17.6, so a search visits at most 18 nodes and needs at most 18 comparisons. (A1) In a tree shaped like B, each node has only one child, so the tree is a chain and a search may have to visit every node. (M1) For 200 000 keys that is 200 000 comparisons, which is roughly 11 000 times as many as the balanced shape needs. The practical significance is that the degenerate shape offers no improvement over scanning a list, so the choice of a tree delivers nothing unless the shape is controlled. (A1)

(e) A hash table computes the position of a key from the key itself, so lookup, insertion and deletion are all constant-time on average, and the three operations would be fast. (M1) However, the keys in a hash table are stored in an order determined by the hash function, not by the keys, so the third operation, printing the catalogue in ascending order of accession number, cannot be done without retrieving every key and sorting it, which costs O(n log n) each time. (A1) The hash table is therefore the better structure only if the ordered listing is not required, or if it is produced once and cached; as specified, it does not satisfy the third requirement. (A1)
