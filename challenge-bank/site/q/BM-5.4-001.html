Two sites, one crossover point, and a forecast that will not commit

Business management SL · BM-5.4-001

Tarn Valley Preserves

Tarn Valley Preserves makes jams and chutneys and has outgrown its current premises. Two sites are available and the company must choose one of them; it cannot afford both.

Marketing estimates a 0.6 probability that demand for the company's products will be 220 000 units per year and a 0.4 probability that it will be 120 000 units per year. The production cost structure differs between the two sites, and the qualitative differences are set out in the table.

Question

Tarn Valley Preserves must commit to one site, and the choice will be difficult to reverse because the lease runs for ten years.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the total annual cost at each site if demand is 220 000 units, and state which site is cheaper.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the output at which the total annual costs of the two sites are equal.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Using the probabilities given, calculate the expected annual cost at each site and comment on what your answer means for the decision.

    (Comment)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Recommend which site Tarn Valley Preserves should choose, justifying your answer with reference to your calculations and to two qualitative factors.

    (Recommend)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Site X: total cost $=280\,000+1.80(220\,000)=280\,000+396\,000=€676\,000$. (M1)(A1)

Site Y: total cost $=140\,000+2.60(220\,000)=140\,000+572\,000=€712\,000$. (A1)

Site X is cheaper by €36 000 per year at this level of demand. (A1)

(b) Setting the two total costs equal: $280\,000+1.80q=140\,000+2.60q$. (M1)

So $140\,000=0.80q$ and $q=175\,000$ units. (M1)(A1)

Site Y is cheaper below this output and Site X above it, because Site X trades higher fixed costs for a lower variable cost per unit.

(c) Site X: $0.6(676\,000)+0.4(496\,000)=405\,600+198\,400=€604\,000$. (M1)(A1)

Site Y: $0.6(712\,000)+0.4(452\,000)=427\,200+180\,800=€608\,000$. (A1)

The expected costs differ by only €4000 per year on a commitment of more than half a million euros per year, which is well under 1% of the total. (A1) The expected-value calculation therefore does not settle the question: whichever site is chosen, the company is making almost the same bet on cost, and the decision will be decided by which risk the management team is more willing to carry rather than by the arithmetic. It is also worth noting that the expected value conceals the shape of the risk: Site Y is cheaper in the low-demand outcome and more expensive in the high-demand outcome, so choosing Y is choosing to be relatively safe if demand disappoints, while Site X is choosing to be better off if demand materialises.

(d) Recommendation: choose Site X, the city centre. (A1)

The quantitative case is narrow but consistent: Site X is €36 000 per year cheaper at the higher demand level, it has the lower expected cost at €604 000 against €608 000, and its crossover point of 175 000 units sits well below the 220 000 units that marketing considers more likely than not. Since the lease runs for ten years and the higher-demand outcome is the one the company's growth plan depends on, the cheaper variable cost is the more valuable characteristic to lock in. (A1)

The decisive qualitative factor is space. Site X has no room to expand at any price, so if demand exceeds 220 000 units the company would have to acquire a second site and duplicate management, which would destroy most of the cost advantage it has just calculated. Site Y has a large plot available now, which gives the business the option to grow without a second location. (A1)

The counter-argument is labour. Site X has a large local pool of skilled production staff, while Site Y would require training, which adds cost and time and creates a dependency on retaining the staff it trains. A preserves manufacturer depends on consistent product quality, so the availability of experienced staff reduces operational risk. On balance the expected cost difference is too small to decide the matter, and the choice between the two risks comes down to which the company fears more: a permanent capacity ceiling on a growing product, or an operating cost it cannot control. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a): award the method mark for the linear cost structure and the accuracy marks for the two totals; a candidate who omits the fixed cost entirely should be corrected, because the whole point of the comparison is the trade-off between fixed and variable cost. Part (b): accept the answer obtained by any correct method, including a graphical one, provided the crossover output is identified. Part (c): accept an answer rounded to the nearest thousand, but require the two expected costs to be compared rather than merely stated. The comment mark depends on the candidate noticing how small the difference is relative to the size of the commitment; a candidate who simply says 'Site X is cheaper' has not commented. Part (d): the recommendation mark is independent of which site is chosen, provided the reasoning is internally consistent. The two justification marks require one qualitative factor handled well rather than two factors listed, and a candidate who merely quotes from the table without explaining the consequence for the business does not reach them. Do not accept a recommendation that ignores the expansion constraint, since it is the factor that makes the ten-year lease relevant.

Why this question is hard

The crossover calculation is the centre of the question and it is easy to perform mechanically without noticing what it does. There is a level of output at which the two sites cost the same, and below it the site with the lower fixed cost wins while above it the site with the lower variable cost wins. Writing that conclusion explicitly is what turns a calculation into analysis, and it is what most candidates omit.

The more interesting feature is the expected value. It is a legitimate technique and the numbers are easy, but the answer it gives is a €4000 difference on annual costs of more than €600 000, which is less than the uncertainty in any of the underlying estimates. A number that precise, resting on probabilities that are themselves a judgement, cannot bear the weight of a ten-year decision. Recognising that a calculation can be correct and still not be decisive is the analytical step the question is testing, and it is the difference between a candidate who uses a tool and one who is used by it.

That is why the qualitative factors do the real work. The decisive one is the absence of expansion space at Site X. Ten years is a long commitment for a business whose management believes demand is more likely to rise than to fall, and a site with a hard capacity ceiling turns success into a problem. The labour factor pulls the other way and a strong answer will acknowledge it rather than ignore it. The judgement is not about which factor is objectively more important but about which risk the business is better placed to absorb: a company that is already short of space is likely to value the option to expand more than a modest reduction in the risk of training costs.
