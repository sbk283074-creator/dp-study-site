D.4 Switching on a series circuit containing an inductor: the initial rate of rise of the current is set by the e.m.f. and the inductance alone, the final current and the time constant are set by the total resistance including the resistance of the coil itself, and the energy stored rises as the square of the current

Theme D: FieldsD.4 Induction (AHL): Faraday's law applied to the coil itself, so that a changing current induces a back e.m.f. in the coil, the time constant of a series circuit containing an inductor, and the energy stored in the magnetic field of the coilP213 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedCalculateDetermineDetermineExplain

Question

A coil of inductance $0.250\ \mathrm{H}$ has a resistance of $8.0\ \Omega$. It is connected in series with a resistor of resistance $40.0\ \Omega$ and a battery of negligible internal resistance and e.m.f. $12.0\ \mathrm{V}$. A switch in the circuit is closed at time $t = 0$, and the current $i$ then rises from zero towards its steady value. Because the current is changing, the flux through the coil is changing, and by Faraday's law a back e.m.f. $L\,\dfrac{di}{dt}$ opposes the change, so the current cannot jump to its final value at the instant the switch is closed. The figure shows the rise and the tangent to the curve at the origin.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the rate of change of the current at the instant the switch is closed, and state which of the circuit's parameters that rate does not depend on.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the steady current reached after a long time, and the time constant of the circuit, explaining why the resistance of the coil must be included in both.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine the energy stored in the magnetic field at the steady state, and the fraction of that energy that is stored at $t$ equal to one time constant.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain why the tangent drawn at the origin meets the horizontal asymptote at $t$ equal to exactly one time constant, and what the e.m.f. across the coil is at that instant.

    (Explain)

Reveal the answer

(a) At $t = 0$ the current is zero, so there is no potential difference across either resistance. The whole of the battery's e.m.f. therefore appears across the coil, and the back e.m.f. equals the applied e.m.f. (M1):
$$L\frac{di}{dt} = 12.0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{di}{dt} = \frac{12.0}{0.250} = 48.0\ \mathrm{A\,s^{-1}} \;(A1).$$
This rate depends only on the e.m.f. and the inductance. It does not depend on the $40.0\ \Omega$ resistor, nor on the coil's own $8.0\ \Omega$ resistance, because at that instant neither resistance carries any current and neither therefore produces any potential difference (A1).
(b) After a long time the current is steady, so $\dfrac{di}{dt} = 0$ and the back e.m.f. is zero. The circuit is then simply two resistances in series with the battery, and the coil's resistance is one of them (M1):
$$I = \frac{\varepsilon}{R + r} = \frac{12.0}{40.0 + 8.0} = \frac{12.0}{48.0} = 0.250\ \mathrm{A} \;(A1).$$
The time constant is $\tau = \dfrac{L}{R + r} = \dfrac{0.250}{48.0} = 5.21 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s} = 5.21\ \mathrm{ms}$ (M1)(A1). The coil's resistance must appear in both because it is in the same series loop as the resistor: charge passing through the coil passes through the coil's own resistance too, and there is no way to separate the two contributions to the total opposition in the loop. Using $R = 40.0\ \Omega$ alone gives $0.300\ \mathrm{A}$ and $6.25\ \mathrm{ms}$, both of which are wrong.
(c) The energy stored in the field of an inductor is $U = \tfrac{1}{2}Li^{2}$, so at the steady state
$$U = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.250)(0.250)^{2} = 7.81 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{J} = 7.81\ \mathrm{mJ} \;(M1)(A1).$$
At $t = \tau$ the current is $i = I(1 - e^{-1}) = 0.250 \times 0.6321 = 0.158\ \mathrm{A}$ (M1), so
$$U_{\tau} = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.250)(0.158)^{2} = 3.12 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{J},$$
which is a fraction
$$\frac{U_{\tau}}{U} = (1 - e^{-1})^{2} = 0.400$$
of the final energy (A1). The current has reached $63\%$ of its final value but the energy only $40\%$, because the energy is proportional to the square of the current.
(d) The initial slope of the curve is $\dfrac{di}{dt} = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{L}$, and the asymptote is at $i = I = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{R + r}$. The tangent line is therefore $i = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{L}t$, and it meets the asymptote where
$$\frac{\varepsilon}{L}t = \frac{\varepsilon}{R + r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = \frac{L}{R + r} = \tau \;(A1).$$
The result is not a coincidence of the numbers: the e.m.f. cancels from both sides, so the tangent at the origin of any such rise meets its own asymptote at one time constant (A1). It is a useful way to read $\tau$ off a graph, since the intersection of the tangent with the asymptote is easier to judge than the point at which the curve reaches $63\%$ of its final value.
At $t = \tau$ the current is $0.158\ \mathrm{A}$, so the potential difference across the coil's resistance is $0.158 \times 8.0 = 1.27\ \mathrm{V}$ and across the $40.0\ \Omega$ resistor is $0.158 \times 40.0 = 6.32\ \mathrm{V}$, a total of $7.59\ \mathrm{V}$. The remaining $12.0 - 7.59 = 4.41\ \mathrm{V}$ is the back e.m.f. of the coil (A1). It is still substantial, which is why the current has not yet reached its final value; at the steady state this back e.m.f. has fallen to zero and the whole $12.0\ \mathrm{V}$ is shared between the two resistances as $10.0\ \mathrm{V}$ and $2.0\ \mathrm{V}$.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for recognising that the whole e.m.f. appears across the coil at $t = 0$, (A1) for $48.0\ \mathrm{A\,s^{-1}}$, (A1) for the statement that the rate is independent of both resistances. A candidate who answers $12.0/40.0 = 0.30$ has divided by a resistance to obtain a rate and scores nothing; a candidate who writes $48\ \mathrm{A\,s^{-1}}$ but attributes the independence to the coil's resistance only, overlooking the external resistor, loses the third mark.
(b) (M1) for adding the coil's resistance to the external resistance, (A1) for $0.250\ \mathrm{A}$, (M1) for the time constant with the same total resistance, (A1) for $5.21\ \mathrm{ms}$. Condonation: $5.2\ \mathrm{ms}$ is accepted. A candidate who uses $R = 40.0\ \Omega$ throughout scores (M1) for the structure of both calculations and nothing else, because both numerical answers are wrong. The explanation must connect the coil's resistance to the series loop; a bare assertion that the coil has resistance scores the numerical marks but not the explanation.
(c) (M1) for $\tfrac{1}{2}Li^{2}$ with the steady current, (A1) for $7.81\ \mathrm{mJ}$, (M1) for the current at one time constant, (A1) for the fraction $0.400$ or the value $3.12\ \mathrm{mJ}$. A candidate who reports the fraction as $0.632$ has used the fraction of current rather than the fraction of energy and loses the last mark; the square is the whole point of the part.
(d) (A1) for forming the tangent line and the asymptote and solving for their intersection, (A1) for noting that the e.m.f. cancels so the result is general, (A1) for the back e.m.f. of about $4.4\ \mathrm{V}$ at $t = \tau$. Accept any answer between $4.3\ \mathrm{V}$ and $4.5\ \mathrm{V}$, and accept a candidate who obtains it as $12.0 - 0.158 \times 48.0$ or by any equivalent route. A candidate who says the back e.m.f. is zero at $t = \tau$ has confused the transient with the steady state and scores the first two marks only.
Follow-through: a candidate whose total resistance is wrong in (b) but who then uses the same total consistently keeps the method marks in (c) and (d). Forfeits: treating the coil as having no resistance; using $i = I(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau = L/R$; and asserting that the current cannot change at all while the switch is closed.

Why this question is hard

The item is built around a single question: which circuit parameters govern which quantity. There are three quantities and they do not share the same governing set, and the whole difficulty is in keeping them apart.

The initial rate of rise is the cleanest case. At the instant the switch closes the current is still zero, so there is no potential difference across either resistance, and the entire e.m.f. of the battery is dropped across the coil. The back e.m.f. is $L\,di/dt$, so $L\,di/dt = \varepsilon$ and the rate is $\varepsilon/L = 48\ \mathrm{A\,s^{-1}}$. Neither $40.0\ \Omega$ nor $8.0\ \Omega$ appears. This is the part of the item that earns the non-governing-variable label: the resistances are present in the circuit diagram and are genuinely irrelevant to the quantity asked for.

The steady current is the opposite case. Once the current stops changing, the back e.m.f. is zero and the coil is just a resistance of eight ohms. The total resistance of the loop is $48.0\ \Omega$, not $40.0\ \Omega$, and both the final current and the time constant depend on that total. A candidate who treats the coil as ideal obtains $0.300\ \mathrm{A}$ and $6.25\ \mathrm{ms}$; both numbers are wrong by twenty per cent, and they are wrong in a way that a sketch of the graph would not reveal, because the shape of the rise is the same.

The third quantity, the stored energy, is where the square matters. At one time constant the current has completed sixty-three per cent of its rise, which is the number most candidates remember. The energy has completed only forty per cent, because $U = \tfrac{1}{2}Li^{2}$ and $0.632^{2} = 0.400$. An inductor that is sixty-three per cent charged in current is only forty per cent charged in energy, and a candidate who reports the energy fraction as sixty-three per cent has used the wrong measure.

Part (d) ties the two together with a piece of geometry that is worth knowing. The tangent at the origin is the line $i = (\varepsilon/L)t$ and the asymptote is $i = \varepsilon/(R + r)$; they meet at $t = L/(R + r) = \tau$, and the e.m.f. cancels from the comparison, so this is true of every exponential rise of this kind and not a feature of these particular numbers. The figure is drawn so that the intersection is visible: the dashed tangent leaves the curve immediately and reaches the asymptote exactly one time constant along the axis.

Challenge lever
Three quantities are wanted from one transient and each is governed by a different combination of the circuit parameters. The initial rate of rise is the e.m.f. divided by the inductance, so it contains neither resistance at all, and a candidate who reaches for the steady-state current as a rate, or who divides the e.m.f. by the resistance and calls the result a rate, is using the wrong pair of quantities. The final current and the time constant are both governed by the total resistance, and the coil's own eight ohms is in series with the forty-ohm resistor, so neither may be computed from the resistor alone. The last part asks for the energy at one time constant, where the current has reached sixty-three per cent of its final value and the energy only forty per cent, because the energy goes as the square of the current. The geometric fact that the tangent at the origin meets the asymptote at exactly one time constant ties the two together and is not obvious from either formula.

Adaptation
The A-level treatment usually gives an ideal inductor and asks for the time constant and the final current. Here the coil carries its own resistance, so the total resistance is not the value printed on the resistor and both the final current and the time constant change; the initial rate of rise is asked for separately because it is governed by a different pair of quantities; and the final part asks for the back e.m.f. at one time constant, which the source material does not ask for. The item is an extension of the IB D.4 treatment of Faraday's law rather than a syllabus item, and the circuit is solved with exponential decay, which IB students meet in E.3.
Verification
Every value was computed from the circuit equations with the coil resistance included in the series loop. The initial rate of rise is the e.m.f. divided by the inductance and is independent of both resistances, which was checked symbolically; the steady current and the time constant were computed from the total resistance of 48.0 ohms; the current and the energy at one time constant were evaluated as I(1 - 1/e) and one half L I squared and the energy fraction confirmed to equal (1 - 1/e)^2. The claim that the tangent meets the asymptote at the time constant was verified algebraically, since the e.m.f. cancels.
