E.2 Stopping potential against frequency, with one anomalous reading

Physics HL · PHYS-E.2-201

Theme E: Nuclear and quantum physicsE.2 Quantum physics (photoelectric effect; stopping potential; Einstein's photoelectric equation) / experimental skillsP1 · Section B12 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedPlotDetermineCalculateIdentify

Student investigation of the photoelectric effect

A student uses a set of monochromatic light sources of known frequency to illuminate a clean metal cathode in an evacuated photocell. For each source the stopping potential is found by adjusting the reverse potential difference until the photocurrent just falls to zero. The stopping potential is measured with a voltmeter of resolution 0.01 V, and each reading is taken three times and averaged; the spread of the three readings is never more than 0.02 V. The work function of the cathode is not known in advance. The frequencies are quoted to three significant figures.

Question

The results are shown in the table. Einstein's photoelectric equation may be written $eV_{s} = hf - \phi$, where $V_{s}$ is the stopping potential and $\phi$ is the work function of the cathode.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    State the quantity that should be plotted on the vertical axis so that the data give a straight line, and state what the gradient of that line represents.

    (Plot)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the gradient of the best-fit straight line through the reliable points, and hence determine a value for the Planck constant.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Calculate the threshold frequency and the work function of the cathode in electronvolts.

    (Calculate)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    Identify the reading that is inconsistent with the rest, and justify your choice using a residual rather than by eye.

    (Identify)
  5. [2 marks](e)

    Suggest one experimental cause for that reading, and state what a repeat measurement would need to show for the reading to be accepted.

    (Suggest)

Total: 12 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Plot $V_{s}$ on the vertical axis against $f$ on the horizontal axis. Because $eV_{s} = hf - \phi$, this is linear, and comparing with $y = mx + c$ shows that the gradient is $h/e$, the Planck constant divided by the elementary charge. The intercept on the $V_{s}$ axis is $-\phi/e$.

(b) The five reliable points are at $f = 5.00, 6.00, 8.00, 9.00$ and $10.0$ in units of $10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$, with $V_{s} = 0.57, 0.98, 1.81, 2.22$ and $2.64\ \mathrm{V}$.

For these five points $\sum f = 38.0$, $\sum V_{s} = 8.22$, $\sum f^{2} = 306$ and $\sum f V_{s} = 69.59$ (M1)(A1). Hence
$$m = \frac{5(69.59) - (38.0)(8.22)}{5(306) - (38.0)^{2}} = \frac{347.95 - 312.36}{1530 - 1444} = \frac{35.59}{86.0} = 0.414\ \mathrm{V\,per\,}10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$$

The gradient is $h/e$, so (M1)
$$h = \frac{m e}{10^{14}} = \frac{0.414 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}}{10^{14}} = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$$

which is within $0.1\ \%$ of the accepted value $6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$ (A1).

(c) The intercept is
$$c = \frac{\sum V_{s} - m\sum f}{5} = \frac{8.22 - 0.4138 \times 38.0}{5} = -1.50\ \mathrm{V}$$

The threshold frequency is where $V_{s} = 0$ (M1):
$$f_{0} = -\frac{c}{m} = \frac{1.50}{0.4138} = 3.63 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz} \tag{A1}$$

The work function is the energy of a photon at the threshold (M1):
$$\phi = hf_{0} = (6.63 \times 10^{-34})(3.63 \times 10^{14}) = 2.41 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J} = \frac{2.41 \times 10^{-19}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}} = 1.50\ \mathrm{eV} \tag{A1}$$

(d) The reading at $f = 7.00 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is inconsistent. Using $V_{s} = 0.4138f - 1.50$ in units of $10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$, the residuals are $+0.002, -0.002, +0.354, +0.000, -0.003$ and $+0.003\ \mathrm{V}$ for the six points in order (M1). The residual at $7.00$ is $+0.354\ \mathrm{V}$, more than a hundred times the largest of the others and some seventeen times the $0.02\ \mathrm{V}$ repeatability of the measurements, so it cannot be explained by the scatter of the apparatus (A1).

(e) A likely cause is that the violet or blue source used for that point was not clean monochromatic light, or that the cathode surface had been contaminated or oxidised since the previous reading, which would raise the apparent work function locally and hence the stopping potential. Another possibility is that the reverse potential was adjusted too quickly and the photocurrent had not fallen fully to zero when the reading was taken.

A repeat measurement would need to reproduce the raised value with the same source and a freshly cleaned cathode, and the residual would need to fall within the same few millivolts as the other points, before the reading could be accepted as evidence of a real departure from the linear relation.

Markscheme notes

(a) A1 for $V_{s}$ against $f$ (either axis assignment stated explicitly), A1 for the gradient being $h/e$. Accept "Planck constant divided by electronic charge" or "$h/e$" without a numerical value.

(b) M1 for a least-squares or gradient calculation from widely separated points, A1 for the sums, M1 for using gradient $= h/e$, A1 for $6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$. A candidate who takes the gradient from adjacent table rows will be pulled by the anomalous point and should not receive the final A1. Accept a gradient in the range $0.410$ to $0.418$ and $h$ in the range $6.57$ to $6.70 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$.

(c) M1 for $f_{0} = -c/m$ or for reading the intercept from the graph, A1 for $3.6 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$, M1 for $\phi = hf_{0}$, A1 for $1.5\ \mathrm{eV}$. Follow-through applies from the gradient and intercept in (b). Accept $1.49$ to $1.51\ \mathrm{eV}$.

(d) A1 for naming $f = 7.00 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ (or the $1.75\ \mathrm{V}$ reading), A1 for justifying it with a residual or with the deviation from the line. Naming the point by eye with no numerical justification earns only the first mark: the part exists to test whether the student can quantify rather than eyeball.

(e) A1 for a physically sensible cause that would raise the stopping potential, A1 for a statement of what the repeat must show. A candidate who suggests "human error" with no mechanism earns 0.

Forfeits: fitting all six points, which lowers the gradient to about $0.404$ and gives $h = 6.47 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, a $2.4\ \%$ error; using $\mathrm{Hz}$ without the $10^{14}$ factor, which changes the gradient by a factor of $10^{14}$ and must be penalised as a unit error. Condonation: a candidate who works in SI frequencies throughout and obtains the same $h$ should be given full credit.

Why this question is hard

This item tests the whole experimental loop rather than a single formula: choose the linearising variable, fit the reliable data, extract two physical constants, and then account for the point that does not fit. The last of those is the discriminating step, because the natural instinct is to average all six points and report whatever comes out, which is exactly what makes the anomaly dangerous.

The anomalous reading at $7.00 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is raised by $0.35\ \mathrm{V}$ above the line defined by the other five. That is large: the stated repeatability is $0.02\ \mathrm{V}$, so the deviation is roughly seventeen times the measurement scatter, and it cannot be argued away as noise. Because it sits near the middle of the frequency range, it has real leverage on the fitted gradient, and including it lowers $h$ from $6.63$ to $6.47 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, an error of $2.4\ \%$ that is well outside the precision the other points support. The item is therefore arranged so that the careless route produces a visibly wrong constant, which is the strongest possible argument for looking at residuals.

Part (e) is deliberately open, but it is not free: the mark is for a cause with a mechanism and for a stated criterion for accepting the reading. The physically motivated answers all involve something that would raise the stopping potential for that one source, such as contamination of the cathode or a source that is not monochromatic. The part is there because a data-based question that only asks students to find the outlier teaches them to delete data; asking what would have to be true for the reading to be kept teaches them to test it.
