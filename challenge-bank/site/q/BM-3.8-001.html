Two projects, three appraisal methods, three different answers

Business management SL · BM-3.8-001

Unit 3: 3.8 Investment appraisal (payback period, average rate of return, net present value); 3.2 Sources of finance. Toolkit: decision trees are not required here.P2 · Section A15 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateRecommendJustify

Kestrel Leisure plc

Kestrel Leisure plc operates forty indoor climbing centres and is considering two mutually exclusive investment projects. Each requires an immediate investment of €400 000 and each has an expected life of four years, after which it will have no residual value.

The company will finance whichever project it chooses with a bank loan at an interest rate of 10% per year. Project B would also require €50 000 of working capital at the start of Year 1, which the company expects to recover in full at the end of Year 4. This amount is not included in the cash flows shown below.

Question

The two projects are mutually exclusive, so Kestrel Leisure can accept at most one of them.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the payback period of each project, giving your answers in years to two decimal places.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the average rate of return of each project, using average annual profit as a percentage of the initial investment.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Using the discount factors provided, calculate the net present value of each project.

    (Calculate)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Recalculate the net present value of Project B to include the working capital requirement, and then recommend which project Kestrel Leisure should accept. Justify your recommendation using all of your calculations.

    (Recommend)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Project A. Cumulative cash flow: after Year 1, €120 000; after Year 2, €280 000; after Year 3, €460 000. The investment of €400 000 is recovered during Year 3, when €120 000 of the €180 000 inflow is still needed. (M1)

Payback $=2+\dfrac{120\,000}{180\,000}=2.67$ years. (A1)

Project B. Cumulative cash flow: after Year 1, €60 000; after Year 2, €160 000; after Year 3, €320 000; after Year 4, €560 000. The investment is recovered during Year 4, when €80 000 of the €240 000 inflow is still needed. (M1)

Payback $=3+\dfrac{80\,000}{240\,000}=3.33$ years. (A1)

(b) Project A: total net cash flow is $120\,000+160\,000+180\,000+80\,000=540\,000$, so total profit is $540\,000-400\,000=140\,000$ and average annual profit is $\dfrac{140\,000}{4}=35\,000$. (M1)

ARR $=\dfrac{35\,000}{400\,000}\times 100=8.75\%$. (A1)

Project B: total net cash flow is €560 000, so total profit is €160 000 and average annual profit is $\dfrac{160\,000}{4}=40\,000$. ARR $=\dfrac{40\,000}{400\,000}\times 100=10.00\%$. (A1)

(c) Project A: the present values of the inflows are $120\,000(0.909)=109\,080$; $160\,000(0.826)=132\,160$; $180\,000(0.751)=135\,180$; and $80\,000(0.683)=54\,640$. The total present value is €431 060. (M1)(A1)

NPV of A $=431\,060-400\,000=+€31\,060$. (A1)

Project B: the present values are $60\,000(0.909)=54\,540$; $100\,000(0.826)=82\,600$; $160\,000(0.751)=120\,160$; and $240\,000(0.683)=163\,920$, totalling €421 220. NPV of B $=421\,220-400\,000=+€21\,220$. (A1)

(d) Project B also requires €50 000 of working capital at the start, an immediate outflow that must be discounted at the Year 0 factor of 1.000, and it recovers €50 000 at the end of Year 4, whose present value is $50\,000(0.683)=34\,150$. (M1)

Revised NPV of B $=421\,220-400\,000-50\,000+34\,150=+€5\,370$. (A1)

Recommendation: accept Project A. (A1)

Justification: Project A is superior on two of the three methods. It pays back in 2.67 years against 3.33, which matters because the loan is being serviced at 10% and a shorter payback shortens the period during which the company carries the risk of the cash flows not materialising. Its NPV is €31 060 against €5 370 for Project B once the working capital is included, and since the money is borrowed at exactly the 10% used to discount, the NPV is the method that measures the effect on shareholder wealth directly. Project B is only superior on ARR, at 10.00% against 8.75%, and that measure is misleading here because it ignores the timing of the cash flows and, as presented, ignores the working capital entirely. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Parts (a) to (c): award the method marks for a correct cumulative total and the accuracy marks for the final figure; accept answers given in years and months if the conversion is correct. Part (b): accept an alternative ARR that uses average annual profit as a percentage of average investment, provided the formula is stated, but do not allow a candidate to mix the two formulae across the two projects. Part (c): candidates who discount the €400 000 investment should be corrected but not penalised twice, since the investment is at Year 0 where the discount factor is 1.000 anyway; credit their NPV if the arithmetic is otherwise correct. Part (d): the working-capital treatment is the discriminating mark. A candidate who subtracts €50 000 but forgets to add back the discounted recovery will obtain €21 220 − €50 000 = −€28 780 and conclude that B should be rejected; award the method mark and the recommendation mark, but not the accuracy mark. The justification marks require reference to at least two of the three methods and to the 10% cost of borrowing; a recommendation that rests on payback alone is incomplete. Follow-through applies throughout: a candidate whose NPVs are both wrong but who reasons consistently from their own figures should still earn the justification mark.

Why this question is hard

The three methods produce a genuine disagreement, and the disagreement is the question. Project A is better on payback and, once the working capital is included, overwhelmingly better on NPV; Project B is better on ARR. A student who treats the three methods as interchangeable will not be able to recommend anything, because no single method ranks the projects in isolation from the company's circumstances.

The way through is to ask what the money costs. Kestrel Leisure is borrowing at 10%, and that is exactly the rate used to discount, so the NPV is measuring the effect of the investment on the value of the company after the lender has been paid. A positive NPV means the project earns more than the loan costs; a NPV of €5 370 on a €450 000 commitment is barely positive, which means Project B is close to being value-destroying once the working capital is properly accounted for. The payback figure tells the same story from the risk side: a company with debt wants cash back quickly, and Project A returns the investment almost two-thirds of a year sooner.

ARR is the odd one out, and it is worth being able to say why. It is an accounting measure that averages the profits over the life of the project, so it treats €240 000 received in Year 4 as though it were as valuable as €240 000 received in Year 1. It also ignores the working capital unless the candidate chooses to include it, and it takes no account of the size or timing of the cash flows. Here it flatters Project B precisely because B's cash flows come late and are large, which is the opposite of what a company servicing a loan should want.

The working-capital adjustment is where the question stops being routine. Working capital is not an expense: it is cash paid out that comes back. Discounting the recovery is what makes the arithmetic honest, because €50 000 returned in four years is worth €34 150 today. Students who treat the whole €50 000 as a cost will reject Project B for the wrong reason, and students who ignore it entirely will recommend it for the wrong reason.

Metadata

Challenge lever
The three appraisal methods rank the two projects differently and none of them is wrong, so the student has to decide which measure is decision-relevant for a company that is financing the investment with borrowed money; the working-capital adjustment then moves one project from comfortably acceptable to marginal.
