4.1 Market share rising from 18 to 21 per cent while unit sales, total contribution and operating profit all fall, because the market the share is measured against contracted faster than the firm did

Business management SL · BM-4.1-201

Unit 4: MarketingUnit 4: 4.1 The role of marketing (market share and market growth); Unit 3: 3.3 Costs and revenues (contribution). Toolkit: descriptive statistics.P2 · Section A16 marksdifficulty 3technology: permittedCalculateCalculateDetermineExplain

Harrowgate Ceramics: two years of the garden-pot market

Harrowgate Ceramics makes a single product, a glazed garden pot, and sells it in one national market. The marketing director has presented the board with the figures for the last two years and argues that the marketing budget should rise again, because the firm's market share increased in Year 2 while the budget was raised. Fixed costs were unchanged at $1,140,000 in both years. The selling price and the variable cost per unit were also unchanged. The firm has no other product and no other source of revenue.

Question

The table gives the size of the national garden-pot market, Harrowgate Ceramics' own unit sales, its unit price and unit variable cost, its marketing budget and its fixed costs, for each of the two years. Answer all parts using only the figures in the table.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate Harrowgate's market share in each of the two years, and state the change in percentage points.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the percentage change in Harrowgate's own unit sales and the percentage change in the size of the market over the same period, and state the difference between the two.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the total contribution and the operating profit in each year, and determine the change in operating profit between the two years.

    (Determine)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    Explain why a rise in market share is not by itself evidence that the marketing function performed well in Year 2.

    (Explain)
  5. [4 marks](e)

    Evaluate whether the board should approve a further increase in the marketing budget for Year 3, justifying your answer with reference to your calculations and to the nature of the market Harrowgate sells into.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Market share is the firm's own unit sales expressed as a percentage of the total market. For Year 1,
$$\frac{180\,000}{1\,000\,000} \times 100 = 18.0\ \%,$$
and for Year 2,
$$\frac{168\,000}{800\,000} \times 100 = 21.0\ \% \;(M1)(A1).$$
The change is therefore $21.0 - 18.0 = +3.0$ percentage points (A1). It is worth noting at once that the share has risen by three points while the numerator has fallen, which means the denominator must have fallen faster; part (b) confirms that it did.

(b) Harrowgate's own unit sales fell from 180 000 to 168 000, a change of
$$\frac{168\,000 - 180\,000}{180\,000} \times 100 = -6.67\ \% \;(M1)(A1).$$
The market fell from 1 000 000 units to 800 000 units, a change of
$$\frac{800\,000 - 1\,000\,000}{1\,000\,000} \times 100 = -20.0\ \% \;(A1).$$
The difference is $-6.67 - (-20.0) = +13.33$ percentage points. Harrowgate therefore outperformed the market by a wide margin, losing volume more slowly than the market as a whole did. That is a real achievement and it is what the share figure is measuring, but it is a statement about relative performance and says nothing about whether the firm is bigger, more profitable or better off than it was.

(c) Unit contribution is the selling price less the variable cost per unit, $24.00 - 15.00 = 9.00$ dollars (M1). Total contribution is that figure multiplied by the units sold:

$$\text{Year 1}: 180\,000 \times 9.00 = \$1\,620\,000,$$
$$\text{Year 2}: 168\,000 \times 9.00 = \$1\,512\,000 \;(A1),$$
a fall of $\$108\,000$. Operating profit is contribution less fixed costs less the marketing budget:

$$\text{Year 1}: 1\,620\,000 - 1\,140\,000 - 420\,000 = \$60\,000,$$
$$\text{Year 2}: 1\,512\,000 - 1\,140\,000 - 510\,000 = -\$138\,000 \;(A1)(A1).$$
So the change in operating profit is $-138\,000 - 60\,000 = -\$198\,000$, and it splits into two parts: $\$108\,000$ lost because twelve thousand fewer pots were sold at nine dollars of contribution each, and $\$90\,000$ spent on the additional marketing budget. The firm moved from a small profit into a loss in the year in which its market share rose.

(d) Market share is a ratio of two quantities and it is sensitive to both. When the market contracts faster than the firm does, the share rises even though the firm's own sales are falling (M1). A share is therefore a statement about the firm's position relative to its competitors, not about the size of the business, and it cannot be read as a measure of the marketing function's own effectiveness without knowing what happened to the denominator (A1). In this case the marketing budget rose by 21.4 per cent while unit sales fell by 6.67 per cent, contribution fell by $108 000 and the firm moved into loss, so the one measure that improved is the one measure that ignores the market. A share gain earned by standing still in a collapsing market is a different achievement from a share gain earned by growing, and the figures here are of the first kind.

(e) The case for approving a further increase is that the share rose by three points and the firm beat the market by 13.33 percentage points while spending an extra $90 000. If the market were stable, that would be a strong result. But the market is not stable: it contracted by twenty per cent in a single year, and the firm's own volume, contribution and profit all fell, with operating profit turning from a $60 000 profit into a $138 000 loss. The extra spend therefore bought relative position in a category that is shrinking, and there is no evidence in the figures that the extra $90 000 reversed the decline: it coincided with a year in which the firm lost more contribution than it spent. The judgement the board has to make is whether the decline in the market is temporary or structural. If it is temporary, spending to hold share is defensible as defensive investment, because the firm will be well placed when the market recovers, and the 13.33 point outperformance suggests the marketing is working. If it is structural, the same spending is throwing money at a shrinking category, and the board should either cut the budget and manage for cash or diversify out of garden pots. On the figures alone the second reading is better supported: the firm is now making a loss, the market has fallen by a fifth, and a further increase would raise the break-even volume at exactly the moment when volume is falling. A defensible answer must therefore recommend that the budget increase be refused or made conditional on evidence that the market has stopped contracting, and must say what evidence would change that judgement.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for the method of expressing the firm's sales as a percentage of the market, (A1) for both percentages, 18.0 and 21.0, (A1) for the change of +3.0 percentage points. Accept the shares written as 18 and 21 without the decimal place. Do not accept a candidate who reports the change as 3 per cent: the unit is percentage points, and the distinction matters because the share itself is already a percentage.

(b) (M1) for the percentage-change formula applied to the firm's sales, (A1) for -6.67 per cent, (A1) for -20.0 per cent for the market. The difference of 13.33 percentage points is expected but is not separately marked. Accept -6.7 per cent and -6.666 per cent. A candidate who computes the two changes in the wrong order and obtains +6.67 and +20 keeps the method mark only.

(c) (M1) for unit contribution as price less variable cost, (A1) for total contribution in both years, (A1) for operating profit in both years, (A1) for the change of -$198 000. Accept the candidate who computes the change as the sum of the volume effect and the budget effect, -$108 000 and -$90 000, provided both components are shown. A candidate who omits fixed costs and reports contribution as profit has answered a different question and loses the profit marks but keeps the contribution marks.

(d) (M1) for the observation that the share is a ratio and can rise while the firm's own sales fall, (A1) for the conclusion that the share measures relative position and not the size or the profitability of the business. An answer that says only 'the market shrank' without drawing the conclusion about what the share does and does not measure scores (M1) only. Credit an answer that uses the figures to show that the budget rose while contribution and profit fell.

(e) This is an AO3 question and the marks are for judgement, not for repetition of the calculations. Award up to 2 marks for a conclusion that is consistent with the candidate's own figures, and up to 2 marks for the reasoning, which must address the fact that the market is contracting and must say what the decision turns on. A candidate who recommends approving the increase because 'share rose' without addressing the loss scores at most 2 of 4 even if the arithmetic is perfect. A candidate who recommends refusing or deferring the increase, gives the loss and the contracting market as the reason, and states what would justify a reversal, scores 4. Credit any well-argued answer that recognises the decline may be temporary, provided the candidate also states what evidence would settle it.

Forfeits: treating the market as unchanged and reading the share gain as growth; reporting the Year 2 operating profit as +$372 000 by omitting the marketing budget; and answering part (e) with a list of the marketing mix rather than a judgement on the budget.

Why this question is hard

This item turns on a distinction that is easy to state and easy to forget: a market share is a fraction, and a fraction can move in the opposite direction to its numerator. Harrowgate sells twelve thousand fewer pots in Year 2 than in Year 1, and its share rises by three percentage points, and both statements are true. The reason is that the market fell by two hundred thousand units, which is a fifth of it, while the firm fell by twelve thousand, which is under a fourteenth. The share is not lying; it is answering a question about relative position, and the marketing director is quoting it as though it answered a question about performance.

The arithmetic is deliberately plain. Unit contribution is nine dollars, the volume fall costs $108 000 of contribution, the marketing budget rose by $90 000, and the firm's operating profit went from a $60 000 profit to a $138 000 loss. Those four numbers together are the whole of the case, and the interesting part is that the one measure that improved is the only measure in the table that is a ratio. Volume fell, contribution fell, profit fell, and share rose. A candidate who reports the share gain as the headline result has chosen the one statistic that cannot go down in a shrinking market unless the firm does worse than everyone else.

The item is careful about what it does not claim. Harrowgate did outperform the market by 13.33 percentage points, and that is genuinely a signal that something in the marketing is working. The point is not that the share gain is worthless but that it is not sufficient, and the reconciliation of the two readings is the AO3 work the question asks for. A candidate who concludes that the marketing is working and a candidate who concludes that the budget is being wasted can both score full marks, provided each confronts the fact that the firm is now making a loss in a market that has lost a fifth of its volume.

The last part is where the item earns its marks. The board is being asked to raise the budget again, and the decision turns on something the figures cannot supply: whether the contraction is cyclical or structural. Raising the budget raises the break-even volume, and the firm's volume is falling, so the same decision that looks like investment in a recovering market looks like waste in a declining one. A good answer says which reading it favours, says why, and says what evidence would change its mind, because the item has deliberately withheld the one fact that would settle it.

The item is claimed at difficulty 3 because it has a single lever. Once a candidate has seen that the denominator moved, everything else follows from four multiplications and a subtraction, and there is no second idea anywhere in it. The share-versus-size distinction is the whole of the difficulty, and the evaluation part asks for judgement rather than for a further insight.

Challenge lever
Market share is a ratio, and a ratio can rise while both of its components fall. The market contracted by twenty per cent while Harrowgate's own sales fell by six point seven per cent, so the share rises from eighteen per cent to twenty-one per cent even though the firm sold twelve thousand fewer pots. The share is governed by the size of the market as much as by the firm's own performance, and the marketing director has quoted the one measure in the case that improves while contribution, profit and volume all deteriorate. The lever is a single one: the candidate has to notice that the denominator moved, and that a relative gain is not an absolute one.
