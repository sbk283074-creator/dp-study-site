A.4 Rigid body mechanics

Physics HL · PHYS-A.4-101

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.4 (Rigid body mechanics: moment of inertia, rotational dynamics, rolling without slipping)P1 · Section A5 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedCalculateDetermineState

Question

Five independent multiple-choice questions on rigid-body mechanics. Each one stands alone, so you can attempt any of them without the others. Use $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}$ where a numerical value is needed, and give each answer to three significant figures.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    A solid cylinder and a thin-walled hollow cylinder have the same mass and the same outer radius. Both are released from rest and roll without slipping down the same incline. What is the ratio of the acceleration of the solid cylinder to the acceleration of the hollow cylinder?

    (Calculate)
  2. [1 mark](b)

    A disc of moment of inertia $0.80\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{2}}$ rotates freely about a fixed vertical axle at $12\ \mathrm{rad\ s^{-1}}$. A lump of clay of mass $0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$, initially at rest, is dropped onto the disc and sticks at a point $0.60\ \mathrm{m}$ from the axle. What is the angular speed immediately afterwards?

    (Calculate)
  3. [1 mark](c)

    A solid cylinder is released from rest on a rough incline and rolls down it without slipping, accelerating as it goes. What is the direction of the static frictional force that the surface of the incline exerts on the cylinder?

    (State)
  4. [1 mark](d)

    A light inextensible string is wound around a disc of radius $0.25\ \mathrm{m}$ whose moment of inertia about its axle is $0.30\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{2}}$. The disc turns on a fixed horizontal axle with negligible friction, and a block of mass $1.2\ \mathrm{kg}$ hangs from the free end of the string. What is the tension in the string as the block descends?

    (Calculate)
  5. [1 mark](e)

    A solid uniform sphere of moment of inertia $\frac{2}{5}MR^2$ about its centre rolls without slipping along level ground at a constant speed of $3.0\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-1}}$. What fraction of the sphere's total kinetic energy is rotational kinetic energy?

    (Determine)

Total: 5 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) For rolling without slipping the acceleration down an incline is $a = \dfrac{g\sin\theta}{1 + I/(MR^2)}$. The solid cylinder has $I = \tfrac{1}{2}MR^2$ and so accelerates at $\tfrac{2}{3}g\sin\theta$; the thin-walled hollow cylinder has $I = MR^2$ and so accelerates at $\tfrac{1}{2}g\sin\theta$. The ratio is $\tfrac{2/3}{1/2} = \tfrac{4}{3} = 1.33$. (A1) Option B inverts it, option C assumes every rolling body behaves the same, and option D takes the ratio of the moments of inertia without the denominator.

(b) Angular momentum about the axle is conserved, because neither the axle force nor the weight of the clay exerts a torque about it. Initially $L = 0.80 \times 12 = 9.6\ \mathrm{kg\ m^2\ s^{-1}}$. The clay contributes $mr^2 = 0.50 \times 0.60^2 = 0.18\ \mathrm{kg\ m^2}$, so the final moment of inertia is $0.98\ \mathrm{kg\ m^2}$ and the final angular speed is $9.6/0.98 = 9.80\ \mathrm{rad\ s^{-1}}$. (A1) Option B ignores the clay, option C reports the angular momentum as though it were a speed, and option D adds $mr$ instead of $mr^2$.

(c) The weight acts through the centre of mass and the normal force passes through the contact point, so neither produces a torque about the centre. Only friction can supply the torque that increases the angular speed, and it must act up the incline to do so. (A1) Note that static friction opposes the motion the contact point *would* have, not the motion of the centre of mass.

(d) The string does not slip, so $a = \alpha R$. Then $mg - T = ma$ for the block and $TR = I\alpha$ for the disc, giving $T = Ia/R^2$. Hence $a = \dfrac{mg}{m + I/R^2} = \dfrac{1.2 \times 9.81}{1.2 + 4.8} = 1.96\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}$, and $T = 4.8 \times 1.96 = 9.42\ \mathrm{N}$. (A1) Option B neglects the disc's inertia, option C is the resultant force rather than the tension, and option D uses $I = \tfrac{1}{2}mR^2$ with the block's mass.

(e) With $I = \tfrac{2}{5}MR^2$ and $\omega = v/R$, the rotational term is $\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2 = \tfrac{1}{5}Mv^2$ and the translational term is $\tfrac{1}{2}Mv^2$. The rotational fraction is therefore $\dfrac{1/5}{1/5 + 1/2} = \dfrac{2}{7} = 0.286$. (A1) Option B quotes the coefficient in $I$, option C splits the energy evenly, and option D is the translational fraction.

Markscheme notes

One mark for each correct option. There is no penalty for a wrong answer under the Paper 1A rules, so every question should be attempted, and a student who cannot finish a calculation should eliminate on dimensional or limiting-case grounds and choose. In (a) condone the ratio written as $4:3$ or as $1.33$. In (b) allow follow-through from an incorrect final moment of inertia provided the conservation statement itself is explicit and correct. In (c) accept 'up the slope' or 'up the plane'; the direction must be given relative to the incline, and 'opposite to the motion' is not sufficient because the phrase is ambiguous here. In (d) accept $9.4\ \mathrm{N}$ to two significant figures, and allow an answer obtained by computing the acceleration first. In (e) accept $2/7$ or $0.29$; the fact that the answer has no unit is itself part of what is being assessed. Candidates most often lose these marks not through algebra but by applying a purely translational model to a body that is also rotating; watch for a line of working in which the moment of inertia has quietly disappeared. A calculator is available throughout, so no credit is due for an unreduced surd where a decimal was requested, and values should carry three significant figures.

Why this question is hard

What makes this cluster discriminate is that every distractor is the residue of a specific physical decision rather than of arithmetic noise. In (a) and (e) the trap is to treat a rolling body as a translating point mass: the moment of inertia enters twice, once through the dynamics and once through the energy, and dropping either occurrence produces options C and B respectively. In (b) the item is really asking whether you know that angular momentum, and not angular speed or rotational kinetic energy, is the quantity conserved when the moment of inertia changes. In (c) the popular wrong answer 'down the incline' comes from a half-remembered rule that friction opposes motion, when what friction opposes here is the relative motion at the contact point. In (d) the tension is not the weight because part of the released gravitational energy is going into spinning the disc; forgetting that gives option B, which is the single most common error in this subtopic. A reliable check on any answer in this cluster is the limiting case: as the moment of inertia tends to zero the body must behave like a point mass moving freely, and every correct expression here reduces to that limit. A candidate who tests a result this way catches most of the traps the distractors set without redoing the algebra, which is why the habit is worth teaching explicitly.
