Six outcomes, three questions, and the effect of replacement

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.5-001

SL 4.5 (concepts of trial, outcome, equally likely outcomes, sample space and event)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedDetermineFind

Question

Two different numbers are chosen at random from the set $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$. In the final part the two numbers are chosen with replacement.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Determine the sample space of all pairs of different numbers, and state the number of equally likely outcomes.

    (Determine)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the probability that the larger of the two numbers is exactly $3$.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the probability that the larger number is at least $3$ and the smaller number is odd.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    The two numbers are now chosen with replacement; determine the probability that the two numbers are equal, and explain how the sample space changes.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Choosing two different numbers from four gives $\binom{4}{2}=6$ pairs, namely $\{1,2\}$, $\{1,3\}$, $\{1,4\}$, $\{2,3\}$, $\{2,4\}$ and $\{3,4\}$ (M1)(A1). Because each pair is equally likely and order does not matter, each outcome has probability $\frac{1}{6}$ (A1). The sample space therefore contains six equally likely outcomes, and every event can be counted directly from this list (A1). The unordered convention avoids counting each pair twice.

(b) The larger number is exactly $3$ in the pairs $\{1,3\}$ and $\{2,3\}$ (M1)(A1). There are two such outcomes out of six, so the probability is $\frac{2}{6}=\frac{1}{3}$ (A1). The outcome $\{3,4\}$ is excluded because its larger number is $4$, not $3$ (R1). The count is a direct enumeration, so no multiplication rule is needed.

(c) The condition requires the larger number to be at least $3$ and the smaller number to be odd. The qualifying pairs are $\{1,3\}$, $\{1,4\}$ and $\{3,4\}$ (M1)(A1), giving three outcomes out of six, so the probability is $\frac{3}{6}=\frac{1}{2}$ (A1). The pair $\{2,3\}$ fails because its smaller number, $2$, is even (R1). Checking each pair against both conditions is the only reliable way to count this event.

(d) With replacement each of the two choices is made from four numbers, giving $4\times 4=16$ equally likely ordered outcomes (M1)(A1). The two numbers are equal in the four outcomes $(1,1)$, $(2,2)$, $(3,3)$ and $(4,4)$, so the probability is $\frac{4}{16}=\frac{1}{4}$ (A1). The space now contains ordered pairs and repeated values, so it is larger and its outcomes are different from before (R1). Replacement is what makes the two choices independent.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for recognising the count $\binom{4}{2}$ or for listing pairs systematically, and (A1) for all six pairs. The second (A1) is for the probability $\frac{1}{6}$, and the final (A1) for stating that the outcomes are equally likely. A candidate who lists ordered pairs must still identify the six unordered outcomes.

(b) The (M1) is for identifying the outcomes whose larger member is $3$, and the (A1) for the pair $\{1,3\}$ or $\{2,3\}$. The final (A1) requires both pairs and the value $\frac{1}{3}$. The (R1) is for explaining the exclusion of $\{3,4\}$; a candidate who counts three favourable outcomes earns at most one mark.

(c) The (M1)(A1) pair is for the qualifying pairs $\{1,3\}$, $\{1,4\}$ and $\{3,4\}$. The (A1) is for $\frac{1}{2}$. The (R1) is for testing at least one excluded pair against both conditions, such as $\{2,3\}$ failing the odd condition.

(d) Award (M1) for the product $4\times 4$, and (A1) for the sixteen ordered outcomes. The (A1) is for the four equal pairs and the probability $\frac{1}{4}$, and the (R1) for describing how replacement changes the sample space. Follow through from part (a) is allowed.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on the concepts of trial, outcome, sample space and event, and it uses a space small enough to enumerate but phrased so that careless counting fails. Two different numbers are chosen from four, giving six unordered pairs. The sample space is fixed and finite, and each outcome is equally likely, so every probability in the first three parts is a simple ratio of counts.

The first subtlety is that the pairs are unordered. If a candidate lists ordered pairs there are twelve, and each has probability $\frac{1}{12}$; the probabilities are unchanged, but the counts double and the arithmetic must be adjusted. The convention should be stated before counting begins, because mixing the two conventions is the most common source of error.

Part (b) asks for the larger number to be exactly three, which is true of $\{1,3\}$ and $\{2,3\}$ only. Part (c) adds a second condition, that the smaller number is odd, and the qualifying pairs are $\{1,3\}$, $\{1,4\}$ and $\{3,4\}$. The pair $\{2,3\}$ looks promising because its larger number is three, but its smaller number is even, so it is excluded. This is the conditional phrasing that hides the answer: a candidate who counts only on the larger number obtains the wrong event.

Part (d) changes the trial by allowing replacement. The sample space becomes the sixteen ordered pairs, repeated values are now possible, and the event that the two numbers are equal has probability $\frac{4}{16}=\frac{1}{4}$. The numbers were chosen so that the probabilities are the simple fractions $\frac{1}{6}$, $\frac{1}{3}$, $\frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{1}{4}$, and so that the excluded pair in part (c) is genuinely tempting. Replacement is the lever that rebuilds the space.
