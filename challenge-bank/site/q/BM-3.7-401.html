3.7 A cash-flow forecast in which one outflow drives the balance negative in five of six months

Business management SL · BM-3.7-401

Unit 3: Finance and accountsUnit 3: 3.7 Cash flow (cash-flow forecasts, the difference between profit and cash, and the management of working capital); 3.4 Final accountsP2 · Section B10 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedDetermineExplainJustify

Question

The figure is a six-month cash-flow forecast prepared by the finance officer of a small engineering firm. The bars show the net cash flow for each month in pounds: January +3 000, February +2 000, March -8 000, April +4 000, May +5 000 and June +6 000. The line shows the closing balance at the end of each month: -9 000 at the end of January, -7 000 at the end of February, -15 000 at the end of March, -11 000 at the end of April, -6 000 at the end of May and 0 at the end of June. The opening balance at the start of January is not shown.

The finance officer presents the forecast to the bank with the comment that five of the six months are cash-positive and that the business is not therefore in any difficulty. The bank lends only by granting an overdraft facility, and it will grant one of at most £10 000 on the strength of this forecast.

(a) Determine the opening balance at the start of January and the net cash flow for the six months as a whole.

(b) Explain how the closing balance can be negative at the end of five of the six months when only March shows a negative net cash flow, and state the largest amount by which the business is overdrawn during the six months and the month in which this occurs.

(c) Justify, using figures, whether the business can operate within an overdraft facility of £10 000 on this forecast, and recommend one change to the timing of the flows that would bring it within the limit.

Reveal the answer

(a) The closing balance at the end of January is the opening balance plus January's net cash flow, so the opening balance is -9 000 - 3 000 = -12 000 (A1). The business therefore begins the period 12 000 pounds overdrawn, a figure the forecast does not state and which can only be found by reversing the first month. The net cash flow for the six months as a whole is the sum of the six bars: 3 000 + 2 000 - 8 000 + 4 000 + 5 000 + 6 000 = +12 000 (M1)(A1). The closing balance at the end of June confirms it, since -12 000 + 12 000 = 0.

(b) The closing balance is a running total, and the net cash flow is a monthly figure; the sign of a bar says nothing about the sign of the line above it. January's inflow of 3 000 does not make the business cash-positive, it reduces the overdraft from 12 000 to 9 000, and the balance stays negative because the business started the year owing 12 000 more than it held (M1). A surplus month therefore only moves the balance towards zero; it cannot lift it above zero while the opening position is negative. March's single outflow of 8 000 is larger than the combined surplus of January and February, so it takes the balance from -7 000 to -15 000, and the three surpluses that follow, totalling 15 000, are only just enough to bring the balance back to zero by the end of June (A1). The business is overdrawn by at most 15 000, at the end of March, which is the month after the deficit rather than the deficit month itself (A1): the peak in the balance follows the peak in the outflow, and the two are not the same thing.

(c) The deepest point of the forecast is a balance of -15 000 at the end of March, so the facility required is 15 000 and a limit of 10 000 is exceeded by 5 000 at that point (M1)(A1). The business cannot operate within a 10 000 facility on this forecast, and the failure is not marginal: the facility would be exhausted before the end of March and every payment after that point until the balance recovered would be refused, which is the month in which the surpluses of April, May and June begin to arrive (A1). A single change to the timing of the flows is enough. Deferring 5 000 of the March payment into April turns March's net cash flow into -3 000 and the end-March balance into -10 000, exactly at the limit; deferring 6 000 brings the balance to -9 000 and leaves 1 000 of headroom, and either version ends the six months with a positive closing balance of 10 000 (A1). Note that the same 5 000 could equally be found by collecting 5 000 of receivables earlier, which changes the timing without changing the total. What matters is that the change is to the timing of the March outflow and not to its size, because the business does have to make the payment eventually and the forecast is otherwise cash-generative.

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for the opening balance of -12 000 with the reversal of January stated; (M1) for summing the six bars; (A1) for +12 000. Accept a candidate who obtains -12 000 by working backward from the June balance through all six months provided the arithmetic is shown. Do not accept an opening balance given as a positive 12 000: the business is overdrawn from the start.

(b) (M1) for distinguishing the monthly flow from the running balance, with January's inflow identified as a reduction of the overdraft rather than a move into credit; (A1) for the explanation that the accumulated deficit of 12 000 keeps the balance negative while each surplus month only reduces it; (A1) for 15 000 at the end of March. Award the third mark for 15 000 with March identified; 8 000, the largest single outflow, scores nothing here. A candidate who says the business is profitable in five months and illiquid in five is making the distinction the part is testing and should be credited.

(c) (M1) for comparing the deepest balance with the facility limit; (A1) for the conclusion that 10 000 is insufficient, with the 5 000 shortfall stated; (A1) for the consequence, that payments would be refused from March onwards until the balance recovers; (A1) for a quantified timing change. Accept deferring at least 5 000 of the March outflow, collecting receivables earlier, or any other change that lifts the end-March balance to -10 000 or above, provided a figure is given. Do not accept 'increase the facility' as the recommended change, because the question fixes the facility at 10 000 and asks for a change to the timing. A candidate who notes that 5 000 lands exactly on the limit and that 6 000 leaves headroom has answered the part fully.

Why this question is hard

The chart is a standard cash-flow forecast and the only unusual thing about it is how far apart the two series have been made to sit. One bar is negative out of six and five balances are negative out of six, so the same figure supports two opposite descriptions of the year, and the description chosen determines the lending decision. This is not a trick in the sense of withholding information: both series are labelled and both are drawn, and the finance officer's comment about five cash-positive months is literally true. What is false is the inference, and the inference is the thing the bank is being asked to act on.

The opening balance is deliberately absent. Recovering it is a single subtraction, but it is a subtraction inside an operator, in the sense that the closing balance is defined in terms of the opening balance and the month's flow, so the unknown has to be produced before anything else can be said. Once it is recovered, the shape of the year becomes obvious: the business is overdrawn from day one, three of the six surplus months exist only to service a standing deficit, and the whole year is a recovery from 12 000 of accumulated arrears rather than a year of healthy trading. A candidate who reads the bars instead sees two strong months, one bad month and three strong months, which is a completely different narrative and is the one the finance officer is selling.

The distinction between profit and cash is the syllabus point underneath, and this forecast makes it concrete rather than notional. A month with positive net cash flow is not a month with a positive balance, and the business could be profitable in accounting terms in every one of the six months and still be unable to pay its bills in five of them. The reverse is also true and worth noting: a loss-making month with strong cash collection can leave the balance higher than the month before.

The final part is where the item becomes a decision rather than an exercise. A facility of 10 000 against a requirement of 15 000 is a refusal, and the refusal is informative because it locates the problem precisely: not profitability, not the size of the year's surplus, but the timing of one payment in March against the arrival of three surpluses in the three months after it. That is the classic working-capital judgement, and it is almost always cheaper to move a payment by a month than to borrow against it, which is why the recommendation the question asks for is a change of timing rather than a change of amount.
