4.4 The effect of one influential point on r and on the regression line, and why a high r does not make a prediction reliable

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.4-201

Topic 4: Statistics and probabilitySL 4.4 (linear correlation of bivariate data; Pearson's product-moment correlation coefficient r); SL 4.10 (equation of the regression line of y on x)P2 · Section B12 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedDescribeCalculateComment

A student's measurements

A student measures a quantity y for ten different settings of a control variable x. The settings are 1, 2, 3, ..., 10 and the measurements are given in the table. The student plots the ten points and asks a graphing calculator for the product-moment correlation coefficient and the regression line of y on x.

Question

The table gives the ten pairs of values of $x$ and $y$, and the diagram shows the corresponding scatter diagram with the line of best fit for all ten points. Give all numerical answers correct to three significant figures unless the question says otherwise.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Describe the relationship between $x$ and $y$ that the diagram suggests, and identify the single point that has the greatest influence on the position of the line of best fit. Justify your choice by reference to the diagram.

    (Describe)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Calculate the product-moment correlation coefficient $r$ for all ten pairs of values, and find the equation of the regression line of $y$ on $x$ in the form $y = mx + c$.

    (Calculate)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    The point $(10,\,30)$ is now removed from the data. Find the new value of $r$ and the new regression line, and hence determine the change in the value of $y$ predicted at $x = 12$. Comment on what your answers show about relying on $r$ alone to decide whether a prediction from the line is trustworthy.

    (Comment)

Reveal the answer

(a) The nine points with the smaller $x$-values lie close to a straight line of positive gradient passing near the origin, so the diagram suggests a strong positive linear relationship (A1). The point $(10,\,30)$ lies well above that line, and it is also the point furthest from the mean of the $x$-values (M1). Because a point at the edge of the range of $x$ exerts the greatest leverage on the line, and because its vertical distance from the line is the largest of the ten, that is the point with the greatest influence on the position of the line of best fit (A1).

(b) For all ten pairs, $n = 10$, $\sum x = 55$, $\sum y = 123$, $\sum x^{2} = 385$ and $\sum xy = 883$, so $S_{xx} = 385 - \tfrac{55^{2}}{10} = 82.5$, $\bar{x} = 5.5$ and $\bar{y} = 12.3$ (M1). Also $S_{xy} = 883 - \tfrac{55 \times 123}{10} = 206.5$, so the gradient is $m = \tfrac{206.5}{82.5} = 2.503$ (A1) and the intercept is $c = 12.3 - 2.503 \times 5.5 = -1.467$ (A1). The regression line is therefore $y = 2.50x - 1.47$. For the correlation coefficient, $S_{yy} = 2095 - \tfrac{123^{2}}{10} = 582.1$, so $r = \tfrac{206.5}{\sqrt{82.5 \times 582.1}} = 0.942$ (A1).

(c) For the nine remaining pairs $n = 9$, $\sum x = 45$, $\sum y = 93$, $\sum x^{2} = 285$ and $\sum xy = 583$, so $S_{xx} = 285 - \tfrac{45^{2}}{9} = 60$, $S_{xy} = 583 - \tfrac{45 \times 93}{9} = 118$ and $S_{yy} = 1195 - \tfrac{93^{2}}{9} = 234$ (M1). The new gradient is $\tfrac{118}{60} = 1.967$, the new intercept is $\tfrac{93}{9} - 1.967 \times 5 = 0.500$, so the new line is $y = 1.97x + 0.500$ (A1), and the new coefficient is $r = \tfrac{118}{\sqrt{60 \times 234}} = 0.996$ (A1). At $x = 12$ the ten-point line predicts $2.503 \times 12 - 1.467 = 28.6$, while the nine-point line predicts $1.967 \times 12 + 0.500 = 24.1$, a change of $4.5$, or about $19\%$ (A1). Removing the point therefore makes $r$ larger while making the gradient $27\%$ steeper in the other direction, so the two summaries move oppositely: $r$ describes how tightly the observed points hug their own line, and says nothing about how far that line will be trusted beyond the measured range, where one point at the edge of the range has already changed the prediction by nearly a fifth (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for a strong positive linear relationship, (M1) for recognising that the point at the largest $x$ is the one to examine, (A1) for justifying it by its position at the edge of the range or by its distance from the line. Accept either reason, but the justification must be about the point's position, not about the size of its $y$-value alone: a point with a large $y$ in the middle of the range would not have the same effect.
(b) (M1) for the correct summary statistics, (A1) for the gradient, (A1) for the intercept, (A1) for $r$. Accept values obtained from a graphing calculator without the intermediate sums, provided the line and $r$ are both given. Do not penalise a candidate who writes the line as $y = 2.50x - 1.47$ using rounded coefficients: the three-significant-figure instruction applies here.
(c) (M1) for the nine-point sums, (A1) for the new line, (A1) for the new $r$, (A1) for both predictions and their difference, (A1) for the comment. The comment must engage with the fact that $r$ rose while the prediction moved a long way; a candidate who writes only that 'the outlier affected the line' scores no mark for the comment, because the point of the part is that the two diagnostics disagree. Accept any equivalent wording of the extrapolation caution, and accept a candidate who argues that the tenth point may be genuine, provided the arithmetic is used to support the judgement.
Follow-through applies from (b) to (c): a candidate whose gradient in (b) is wrong still earns the marks in (c) for a correct method applied consistently.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on a disagreement between two numbers that students are taught to trust together. Removing the tenth point raises $r$ from $0.942$ to $0.996$, which reads as an improvement, and at the same time lowers the gradient from $2.50$ to $1.97$, which changes the prediction at $x = 12$ from $28.6$ to $24.1$. A candidate who reports that 'the correlation improved, so the model is better' has answered a question about fit and been graded on a question about prediction.

The mechanism is leverage. A least-squares line is a weighted compromise, and the weight a point carries in determining the gradient grows with the square of its distance from the mean of $x$. The point at $x = 10$ is the furthest from $\bar{x} = 5.5$ of any point in the set, so it pulls the line towards itself far more strongly than a point of the same vertical distance placed at $x = 5$ would. That is why the influential point here is not simply the point with the largest residual: leverage and residual size are different quantities, and the item rewards a candidate who can separate them.

There is a deliberate trap in the direction of the change. The usual teaching example has an outlier that wrecks a correlation, so the expected move is 'remove it and $r$ goes up, therefore the fit is now sound'. Here $r$ does go up, and the prediction moves further from the trend the other nine points describe, because the removed point was dragging the gradient upwards as well as adding scatter. A candidate who has memorised the direction rather than understood the geometry will write the correct number and the wrong conclusion.

Part (a) is where the diagram earns its place. The table alone cannot show leverage: it takes the picture to see that nine points sit in a band and one sits above the end of it, and to see that the tenth is not merely high but high *and* far to the right. A candidate who works only from the table will often nominate the point with the largest $y$, which here happens to be the same point, and will then be unable to say why.
