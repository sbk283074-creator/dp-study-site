The ball that bounces higher than it was dropped

Physics HL · PHYS-A.2-001

A.2 Forces and momentum (Newton's laws, momentum, impulse, conservation of momentum in elastic collisions)P2 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatCalculateDetermineExplain

Question

A small ball of mass $m=0.050\ \mathrm{kg}$ rests lightly on top of a large ball of mass $M=1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$. The two balls are released from rest together and both fall through a height $h=0.20\ \mathrm{m}$ before the large ball reaches a hard horizontal floor; the balls are small compared with $h$, so they stay on the same vertical line throughout. The large ball strikes the floor and rebounds; that collision is perfectly elastic, so the large ball leaves the floor moving upwards with the same speed it had just before impact. At that instant the small ball has not yet reached the floor and is still moving downwards. The two balls then collide along the vertical line, and that collision is also perfectly elastic. Air resistance is negligible.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that, just before the two balls collide, the large ball is moving upwards at speed $v$ and the small ball is moving downwards at the same speed $v$, where $v=\sqrt{2gh}$, and calculate the value of $v$.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    By conserving momentum and using the fact that, for a perfectly elastic collision, the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation, show that the velocity of the small ball immediately after the collision is $w=\dfrac{(3M-m)v}{M+m}$ upwards, and calculate the value of $w$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the maximum height above the floor reached by the small ball, express it as a multiple of $h$, and explain why it is greater than $h$.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain why the multiple of $h$ found in part (c) tends to $9$ as $M/m\to\infty$, and state the largest multiple of $h$ that any mass ratio can give.

    (Explain)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The large ball falls freely through a height $h$, so just before it reaches the floor its speed is $v=\sqrt{2gh}$. (M1)

The floor collision is perfectly elastic, so the large ball leaves the floor with speed $v$ directed upwards. The small ball has fallen through the same height and is still in free fall, so it is moving downwards at the same speed $v$. (A1)

$$v=\sqrt{2\times 9.81\times 0.20}=\sqrt{3.924}=1.98\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$$ (A1)

(b) Take upwards as positive. Immediately before the collision between the balls the large ball has velocity $+v$ and the small ball has velocity $-v$; let their velocities immediately afterwards be $V$ and $w$. (M1)

Conservation of momentum:

$$Mv+m(-v)=MV+mw\quad\Rightarrow\quad MV+mw=(M-m)v$$ (M1)

For a perfectly elastic collision the relative speed of separation equals the relative speed of approach:

$$w-V=v-(-v)=2v\quad\Rightarrow\quad V=w-2v$$ (M1)

Substituting this into the momentum equation:

$$M(w-2v)+mw=(M-m)v\quad\Rightarrow\quad (M+m)w=(3M-m)v$$

$$w=\frac{(3M-m)v}{M+m}$$ (AG)

Numerically,

$$w=\frac{3.0-0.050}{1.050}(1.981)=\frac{2.95}{1.050}(1.981)=2.810(1.981)=5.57\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$$ (A1)

directed upwards. (The large ball recoils upwards at $V=\dfrac{M-3m}{M+m}v=\dfrac{0.85}{1.05}(1.981)=1.60\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.)

(c) The small ball leaves the collision with speed $w$ upwards, so it rises to

$$H=\frac{w^{2}}{2g}=\frac{(5.565)^{2}}{2\times 9.81}=\frac{30.97}{19.62}=1.58\ \mathrm{m}$$ (M1)(A1)

As a multiple of the drop height, $H/h=1.58/0.20=7.89$. (A1)

This is far greater than $h$ because the small ball gains energy from the large ball. In the large ball's frame the small ball approaches with relative speed $2v$, and the elastic collision reverses that relative velocity, so the small ball leaves at close to $v+2v=3v$ when $M\gg m$. Its kinetic energy is therefore much larger than the $\frac12 mv^{2}$ it had on landing, and the extra energy is drawn from the much larger kinetic energy of the large ball. (A1)

(d) Dividing numerator and denominator by $M$,

$$\frac{3M-m}{M+m}=\frac{3-m/M}{1+m/M}\to 3\quad\text{as }M/m\to\infty,$$ (M1)

so $w\to 3v$ and $H\to \dfrac{(3v)^{2}}{2g}=9\dfrac{v^{2}}{2g}=9h$. (A1) The largest multiple of $h$ that any mass ratio can give is therefore $9$. (A1) For a finite ratio the multiple is smaller: here $M/m=20$ gives only $7.89$, and a real stacked-ball drop gives far less still because neither collision is perfectly elastic and air resistance removes energy.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for $v=\sqrt{2gh}$ from free fall. The (A1) requires both statements: the floor bounce is elastic, so the large ball's speed is unchanged in magnitude and reversed in direction, and the small ball is still falling at $v$. A candidate who assumes the small ball has already reached the floor or has stopped has misread the geometry and earns nothing after the first mark. Accept $v=2.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) The (AG) requires both conservation laws to be used. A candidate who simply writes down the standard elastic result $w=(3M-m)v/(M+m)$ with no justification earns at most one (M1). Accept any consistent sign convention provided the final direction is stated. A candidate who treats the collision as perfectly inelastic and uses a common velocity afterwards obtains $w=(M-m)v/(M+m)=0.905v$ and forfeits the last three marks. Follow-through applies: if the algebra is sound but the arithmetic slips, credit the numerical (A1).

(c) Follow-through from (b) is allowed. The final (A1) requires a physical reason referring either to energy transferred from the large ball or to the reversal of the relative velocity; 'because it bounces off two balls' is not sufficient.

(d) The limit must be shown, not quoted: award (M1) for dividing numerator and denominator by $M$. Accept $9h$ or $9$. Do not accept 'infinity' or 'it keeps increasing' — the ratio is bounded.

Why this question is hard

The result is genuinely surprising: a ball dropped from $0.20\ \mathrm{m}$ rises to $1.58\ \mathrm{m}$, nearly eight times its drop height, with no energy input. Students who have met a bouncing-ball problem usually expect the small ball to return to roughly $h$, or at most to $4h$ if they mis-apply a factor of two. The point is that there are two collisions, not one, and the second one is elastic.

The key insight is to work in the large ball's frame. After the floor bounce the large ball moves upwards at $v$ while the small ball is still moving downwards at $v$, so the relative approach speed is $2v$. In a perfectly elastic collision the relative speed of separation equals the relative speed of approach, so the small ball leaves with relative speed $2v$. Because the large ball is heavy its own velocity barely changes, so in the ground frame the small ball leaves at about $v+2v=3v$. Squaring gives nine times the kinetic energy, hence $9h$.

The exact calculation replaces 'about' with the mass-ratio factor $(3M-m)/(M+m)$. With $M/m=20$ this factor is $2.81$ rather than $3$, so the height is $7.89h$ rather than $9h$; the ideal figure is only approached as the large ball becomes infinitely heavy. A candidate who quotes $9h$ without doing the mass-ratio arithmetic has answered the limiting question, not the one asked, and the markscheme separates the exact value from the limit deliberately.

The classic wrong turns are: assuming the small ball has already landed when the large ball bounces; applying momentum conservation but not the relative-velocity condition, which leaves one equation for two unknowns; and treating the second collision as perfectly inelastic. There is also a real-world check worth making: an actual stacked-ball drop gives far less than $7.9h$ because the coefficient of restitution is well below one and air resistance matters, so the idealised model is an upper bound rather than a prediction.
