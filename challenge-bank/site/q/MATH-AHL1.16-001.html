Three planes that never meet in more than a line

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.16-001

AHL 1.16 (solutions of systems of linear equations: unique, no solution, infinitely many), AHL 3.18 (intersections of three planes), AHL 3.14 (vector equation of a line)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatFindExplainState

Question

Three planes are given by
$$\Pi_{1}:\ x+y+z=2,\qquad \Pi_{2}:\ 2x+3y+2z=5,\qquad \Pi_{3}:\ x+y+kz=k+2$$ where $k$ is a real constant.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $\Pi_{1}$ and $\Pi_{2}$ intersect in a line $L$, and give a vector equation for $L$.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    For $k\neq 1$, find the position vector of the single point of intersection of the three planes, in terms of $k$.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Explain why there is exactly one value of $k$ for which the three planes have no common point. State that value and describe the geometric configuration of the three planes in that case.

    (Explain)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    For the value of $k$ found in part (c), determine the distance between the two lines in which $\Pi_{2}$ cuts $\Pi_{1}$ and $\Pi_{3}$.

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Eliminating $x$: $\Pi_{2}-2\Pi_{1}$ gives $y=1$. (M1) Substituting into $\Pi_{1}$ gives $x+z=1$, so the two planes do meet in a line, with direction vector perpendicular to both normals $(1,1,1)$ and $(2,3,2)$, for example $(-1,\,0,\,1)$. (M1)

Taking the point $x=1,\ y=1,\ z=0$: $L:\ \mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$. (A1)

(b) On $L$, $y=1$ and $x=1-z$. Substituting into $\Pi_{3}$: $(1-z)+1+kz=k+2$ (M1), so $z(k-1)=k$ (M1), giving $z=\dfrac{k}{k-1}$ (A1) and $x=1-\dfrac{k}{k-1}=-\dfrac{1}{k-1}$ (A1).

The point of intersection is therefore $\left(-\dfrac{1}{k-1},\ 1,\ \dfrac{k}{k-1}\right)$. (A1)

*(Check with $k=2$: $(-1,1,2)$, and $-1+1+2=2$, $2(-1)+3+4=5$, $-1+1+4=4=k+2$. Correct.)*

(c) The equation $z(k-1)=k$ determines $z$ for every $k$ except $k=1$: at $k=1$ it becomes $0\cdot z=1$, which has no solution. (M1)

At $k=1$, $\Pi_{3}$ becomes $x+y+z=3$, which has the same normal as $\Pi_{1}$: $x+y+z=2$. The two planes are parallel and distinct, so they share no point and the three planes have no common point. (A1)

Since $z(k-1)=k$ has a solution for every other value of $k$, this is the only such value. (A1)

The configuration is a triangular prism: $\Pi_{1}$ and $\Pi_{3}$ are parallel, and $\Pi_{2}$ cuts them in two distinct parallel lines. Each pair of planes still meets in a line, so the system is not 'inconsistent because two planes are parallel to each other alone' — every plane meets every other plane. (A1)

(d) $L_{1}=\Pi_{1}\cap\Pi_{2}$: subtracting twice $\Pi_{1}$ from $\Pi_{2}$ gives $y=1$, then $x+z=1$. (M1)

$L_{2}=\Pi_{3}\cap\Pi_{2}$: subtracting twice $\Pi_{3}$ from $\Pi_{2}$ gives $y=-1$, then $x+z=4$. (M1)

Both have direction $(-1,0,1)$. With $A(1,1,0)$ on $L_{1}$ and $B(3,-1,1)$ on $L_{2}$, $\overrightarrow{AB}=(2,-2,1)$ and $\overrightarrow{AB}\times(-1,0,1)=(-2,-3,-2)$, whose magnitude is $\sqrt{17}$; dividing by $\left|(-1,0,1)\right|=\sqrt{2}$ gives a distance of $\dfrac{\sqrt{17}}{\sqrt{2}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{17}{2}}\approx 2.92$. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a): accept any correct direction vector parallel to $(-1,0,1)$ and any point on the line; the (M1) for the elimination should be given even if the candidate then uses a different method, provided the inconsistency is not hidden. Part (b): a candidate who solves by full row reduction of the augmented matrix earns (M1)(M1) and follows through for the point marks. Part (c): the essential mark is the geometric description, not the word 'prism' — accept 'two parallel planes cut by a third' or 'the planes form a prism with no common point'. A candidate who simply states $k=1$ and 'no solution' without explaining why it is unique loses the third (A1). Part (d): accept any valid method, including projecting $\overrightarrow{AB}$ perpendicular to the common direction, or using the formula for the distance between parallel lines with a normal obtained from the cross product. Follow-through: a candidate using $\sqrt{17}$ as the final answer has divided by the wrong magnitude and loses the last (A1) only. Beware of candidates who treat the two lines as skew and compute a shortest distance between them without noticing they are parallel.

Why this question is hard

The trap is structural rather than algebraic. Two of the three planes are fixed, so their line of intersection can be found once and used for everything else; the whole question is then a one-variable problem in $k$. Students who set up a $3\times3$ determinant and expand it will reach the same conclusion after considerably more work, and are more likely to make a sign error, but they should not be penalised.

Two ideas carry the marks. The first is that a system of three equations in three unknowns can be consistent for every value of a parameter except one, and that the exceptional value is found by asking when the final equation degenerates rather than by trial. The second is that 'no solution' has more than one geometric cause. Here it is not a parallel-plane stack in which one pair fails to meet — all three planes meet each other; the failure is that the three pairwise lines are parallel, which is why each pair still intersects. A student who writes 'the planes are parallel so there is no intersection' has the right answer for the wrong reason and should lose the explanation mark.

Part (d) reuses the same line direction and asks for a distance between two parallel lines, which is a cross-product computation. The classic error is to compute the distance from a point to a plane instead, or to omit the normalisation by the magnitude of the direction vector and report $\sqrt{17}$. The check is dimensional: the answer must be shorter than the separation of the two planes themselves, and $\sqrt{8.5}\approx 2.92$ is comfortably smaller than $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\approx 0.577$.

Metadata

Challenge lever
The parameter sits in exactly one plane and the second and third planes are rigged so that the system is consistent for every value except one; the student must therefore derive the general solution first and only then discover that the exceptional case is a genuine geometric configuration, not an arithmetic slip.

Adaptation
Three-plane classification with a parameter is a standard IB idea, but it is normally set so that the parameter produces a parallel-stack failure. Here the planes are arranged so that $\Pi_{3}$ passes through the line $\Pi_{1}\cap\Pi_{2}$ for one value only, inverting the usual case: the system is consistent almost everywhere, and the prism configuration arises from a genuine degeneracy. The specific planes, the parameterisation $x+y+kz=k+2$, the prism configuration and the distance part are original; the cross-check at k=2 was computed symbolically.
Verification
The general solution was verified exactly in rational arithmetic for k = 0, 2, -3, 1/2 and 5 by substituting the closed-form point into all three plane equations. The k = 1 case was checked by substituting y = -1 into Pi_3 to obtain x + z = 4, a line parallel to x + z = 1 with y = 1. The distance was computed from the cross product: |AB x d|/|d| = sqrt(17)/sqrt(2) = 2.91548.
