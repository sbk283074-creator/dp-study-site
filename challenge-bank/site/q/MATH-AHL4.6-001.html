Two events that look dependent until the union is conditioned on

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.6-001

SL 4.6 (use of Venn diagrams, tree diagrams, sample space diagrams and tables of outcomes; combined events, mutually exclusive events, conditional probability, independence)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedFindDetermine

Question

Two events $H$ and $G$ in the same sample space satisfy $P(H)=0.6$, $P(G)=0.45$ and $P(H\cap G)=0.25$. Each part below refers to these two events.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Find $P(H\cup G)$, the probability that at least one of the two events occurs.

    (Find)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find $P(H\mid G)$ and $P(G\mid H)$, giving each answer as an exact fraction in its lowest terms.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine, showing your reasoning, whether the events $H$ and $G$ are independent.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Given that at least one of $H$ and $G$ occurs, find the probability that both occur.

    (Find)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The addition rule for two events gives $P(H\cup G)=P(H)+P(G)-P(H\cap G)$ (M1) $=0.6+0.45-0.25$ (A1) $=0.8$ (A1), so the probability that at least one of the two events occurs is $0.8$ (A1). The subtraction is needed because the outcomes lying in both events would otherwise be counted twice, so the rule is valid for any two events in the same sample space, whether or not those events are independent.

(b) The conditional probability formula gives $P(H\mid G)=\frac{P(H\cap G)}{P(G)}=\frac{0.25}{0.45}$ (M1) $=\frac{25}{45}=\frac{5}{9}$ (A1). Applying the same formula the other way round, $P(G\mid H)=\frac{P(H\cap G)}{P(H)}=\frac{0.25}{0.6}$ (M1) $=\frac{25}{60}=\frac{5}{12}$ (A1). Each conditional probability restricts attention to the conditioning event, so its denominator is the probability of that event alone, which is why the two fractions differ even though both use the same joint probability.

(c) Two events are independent precisely when $P(H\cap G)=P(H)P(G)$ (M1). Here $P(H)P(G)=0.6\times 0.45=0.27$ (A1), and this is not equal to the given value $0.25$ (R1). Hence $H$ and $G$ are not independent (A1), because the product of the separate probabilities fails to reproduce the joint probability.

(d) Conditioning on the union restricts the sample space to $H\cup G$ (M1), so the required probability is $\frac{P(H\cap G)}{P(H\cup G)}$ (A1) $=\frac{0.25}{0.8}$ (A1) $=\frac{25}{80}=\frac{5}{16}=0.3125$ (A1). This conditioning event is the union rather than a single event, so the quantity is different from either answer in part (b), and the calculation is correct because the definition of conditional probability applies to any event of positive probability.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for the addition rule with the intersection subtracted; the first (A1) is for substituting the three given probabilities, the second (A1) for the arithmetic, and the third (A1) for the final value $0.8$. A candidate who adds $0.6$ and $0.45$ and stops at $1.05$ earns only the (M1).

(b) Award (M1) for the conditional probability formula in each direction and (A1) for each exact fraction; decimals such as $0.5556$ do not earn the (A1). The two (M1) marks are independent, so a candidate who reverses the denominator for $P(G\mid H)$ still keeps the first pair.

(c) The (M1) is for stating the independence test, and the (A1) is for the product $0.27$. The (R1) requires an explicit comparison of $0.27$ with $0.25$, and the final (A1) is for the conclusion. A bare assertion of dependence without the product forfeits two marks.

(d) This is the discriminating part. The (M1) is for recognising that the conditioning event is the union, the (A1) for the quotient structure, the next (A1) for substituting $0.25$ and $0.8$, and the final (A1) for the exact value $\frac{5}{16}$ or its decimal $0.3125$. Follow through from an incorrect union is allowed provided the quotient is formed correctly.

Why this question is hard

The item turns on the difference between conditioning on one event and conditioning on the union of two events. Parts (a) to (c) establish the familiar facts: the union has probability $0.8$, the two conditional probabilities are $\frac{5}{9}$ and $\frac{5}{12}$, and the events are dependent because $0.6$ times $0.45$ is $0.27$ rather than $0.25$. A candidate who has done the standard independence test will feel that part (c) has settled the matter.

Part (d) then asks for the probability of both events given that at least one occurs, which is $\frac{0.25}{0.8}=\frac{5}{16}=0.3125$. The lever is that the conditioning event is now the union, not a single event, so the earlier independence verdict gives no shortcut: a candidate who reasons that dependent events must reduce the conditional probability, or who tries to reuse $\frac{5}{9}$ or $\frac{5}{12}$, will not obtain the answer. The correct route is to apply the definition of conditional probability to the event $H\cup G$.

The common error is to treat at least one as though it were a single named event and to condition on $H$ or on $G$ instead, which produces $\frac{5}{9}$ or $\frac{5}{12}$ and answers a different question. A second error is to assume that dependence forces the conditional probability of both to be smaller than $0.25$, whereas it is in fact larger. The numbers $0.6$, $0.45$ and $0.25$ were chosen so that the union is exactly $0.8$ and the required fraction reduces to $\frac{5}{16}$, a value distinct from both conditional probabilities computed earlier.
