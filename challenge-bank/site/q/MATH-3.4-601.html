3.4 A sector whose perimeter equals the whole circumference has angle 2pi - 2, because the two radii and not only the arc contribute to its perimeter

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-3.4-601

Question

A circle has centre $O$ and radius $6$ cm. The sector $AOB$, shown shaded in the figure, has the same perimeter as the whole circle. The angle $AOB$ is $\theta$ radians, and the figure shows that this sector is larger than a semicircle.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Given that the sector and the whole circle have the same perimeter, show that $\theta = 2\pi - 2$.

    (Show that)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Find the exact area of the sector $AOB$, in cm$^2$.

    (Find)
  3. [2 marks](c)

    Find the exact value of the ratio $\dfrac{\text{area of the sector } AOB}{\text{area of the circle}}$, and explain why this ratio is the same for every circle.

    (Find)

Reveal the answer

(a) The perimeter of a sector is made up of its two radii together with its arc. With $r = 6$ and angle $\theta$, this is $2(6) + 6\theta = 12 + 6\theta$ (M1). The circumference of the whole circle is $2\pi(6) = 12\pi$. Setting the two equal gives $12 + 6\theta = 12\pi$, so $6\theta = 12\pi - 12$ and hence $\theta = 2\pi - 2$ (A1). Numerically $\theta \approx 4.28$ radians, which is about $245°$ and is therefore consistent with the figure, where the sector is larger than a semicircle. Notice that $r$ does not appear in the answer: the two radii contribute $2r$ on the left and the whole circumference contributes $2\pi r$ on the right, so dividing through by $r$ removes it, and the angle is fixed by the shape of the condition rather than by the size of the circle.

(b) The area of a sector is $\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$ (M1), so here the area is $\frac{1}{2}(36)(2\pi - 2) = 18(2\pi - 2) = 36\pi - 36 = 36(\pi - 1)$ cm$^2$ (A1). Numerically this is about $77.1$ cm$^2$.

(c) The area of the whole circle is $\pi(6)^2 = 36\pi$. The ratio is therefore $\dfrac{36(\pi - 1)}{36\pi} = \dfrac{\pi - 1}{\pi}$ (M1)(A1). It is the same for every circle because the two areas both scale as $r^2$, so $r^2$ cancels from the quotient: for any radius the ratio is $\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta \div \pi r^2 = \frac{\theta}{2\pi}$, and the value of $\theta$ was fixed at $2\pi - 2$ by the perimeter condition without any reference to $r$. Equivalently, the ratio is the fraction of the circle the sector occupies, which depends only on the angle. It is also the reason radian measure is the natural unit here: the fraction of a circle a sector occupies is exactly $\frac{\theta}{2\pi}$, and that identity holds for every radius only because the angle is measured in radians.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for a perimeter of $2r + r\theta$ with $r = 6$, or for the general $2r + r\theta = 2\pi r$; (A1) for $\theta = 2\pi - 2$. A candidate who writes $6\theta = 12\pi$ and obtains $\theta = 2\pi$ has omitted the two straight edges and should earn no marks: the answer $2\pi$ describes the whole circle, not a sector, and the figure excludes it. Press such a candidate on what the boundary of the shaded region consists of.

(b) (M1) for using $\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$; (A1) for the exact form $36(\pi - 1)$, or the equivalent $36\pi - 36$. Do not accept a rounded decimal as the final answer: the question asks for an exact area, and this is a Paper 1 question where no calculator is available. A candidate who leaves the answer as $18(2\pi - 2)$ should be credited, since it is exact and correct.

(c) (M1) for forming the quotient of the two areas, or for identifying the ratio as $\frac{\theta}{2\pi}$; (A1) for $\frac{\pi - 1}{\pi}$ together with a reason that does not depend on the numerical value of $r$. The reason must mention either that both areas are proportional to $r^2$, or that the ratio is the fraction of the circle swept out by the angle. A candidate who says only 'because $r$ cancels' without saying from where has given the mechanism but not the reason; accept it if the cancellation is shown.

Why this question is hard

The item exists because 'perimeter of a sector' is a phrase that invites a one-term answer. The arc length formula $s = r\theta$ is met first and practised most, and a sector drawn as a shaded wedge looks like a region bounded by one curved edge. Both of these pull a candidate towards $r\theta = 2\pi r$ and the answer $\theta = 2\pi$, which is not a sector at all but the whole circle. The two radii are the entire difficulty, and they are worth two marks because they are worth noticing.

The figure is load-bearing in two separate ways. It shows the sector to be larger than a semicircle, so the degenerate answer $\theta = 2\pi$ can be rejected on sight and the correct $\theta \approx 4.28$ radians is seen to be in the right ballpark. And it draws the chord $AB$ as well as the arc $AB$, which prevents the chord from being used as the arc and keeps the two distinct objects in front of the candidate. The angle at $O$ is marked as $\theta$ and deliberately not given a value, because part (a) is the derivation of that value.

Part (c) is where the item stops being an exercise in two formulas. The ratio $\frac{\pi - 1}{\pi}$ is not a number anyone recognises, and the interesting thing about it is that it contains no $r$: the answer is a property of the angle alone, and asking why forces the candidate to see that both areas scale the same way. That is the same observation that makes radian measure worth using in the first place, which is why it belongs on this node.
