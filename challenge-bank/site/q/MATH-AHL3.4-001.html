The sector whose second radius cannot exist

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL3.4-001

SL 3.4 (the circle: radian measure of angles, length of an arc and area of a sector)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatDetermineFind

Question

A sector of a circle has radius $r$ cm and angle $\theta$ radians, and its perimeter is $20$ cm. All angles in this question are to be given in radians.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that the area $A$ cm$^{2}$ of the sector is given by $A=10r-r^{2}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the value of $r$ for which $A$ is greatest, and state the greatest value of $A$.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find, for $r=5$, the value of $\theta$ in radians and the length of the arc.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the two values of $r$ for which $A=16$, and explain why only one of them is possible.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The perimeter of the sector is $2r+r\theta=20$ (M1), so $r\theta=20-2r$ (A1). The area is $A=\frac{1}{2}r^{2}\theta=\frac{1}{2}r(r\theta)$ (M1) $=\frac{1}{2}r(20-2r)=10r-r^{2}$ (A1)(AG). Writing the area this way removes $\theta$ entirely and leaves a single-variable expression. The substitution is the whole idea of the first part: once the angle has been eliminated, the problem becomes a question about a single variable, and the fixed perimeter is the constraint that makes the elimination possible.

(b) Completing the square, $A=10r-r^{2}=25-(r-5)^{2}$ (M1)(A1). Since $(r-5)^{2}\ge 0$, the area is greatest when $r=5$ (A1), and the greatest value is $A=25$ cm$^{2}$ (A1). The value $r=5$ gives a genuine maximum rather than a minimum because the coefficient of $r^{2}$ is negative, so the parabola opens downwards. Completing the square is preferred to differentiation here because it displays the maximum and the value at which it occurs at the same time.

(c) Substituting $r=5$ into $r\theta=20-2r$ gives $5\theta=10$ (M1), so $\theta=2$ radians (A1). The arc length is $r\theta=5\cdot 2=10$ cm (M1)(A1). The angle of two radians is slightly less than a third of a full turn, and the arc length of ten centimetres is exactly half the fixed perimeter.

(d) Setting $10r-r^{2}=16$ gives $r^{2}-10r+16=0$ (M1), so $(r-2)(r-8)=0$ and $r=2$ or $r=8$ (A1). For $r=2$ the angle would be $\theta=\frac{20-4}{2}=8$ radians (A1), which exceeds $2\pi\approx 6.28$, so no such sector exists. For $r=8$ the angle is $\theta=\frac{20-16}{8}=0.5$ radians, which is admissible (R1), so only $r=8$ survives. The two roots are found by factorising, which is straightforward because their sum is ten and their product is sixteen. Only the geometric constraint removes the smaller one.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for the perimeter $2r+r\theta$; the (A1) for solving it as $r\theta=20-2r$. The second (M1) is for writing the area as $\frac{1}{2}r(r\theta)$ and substituting, and the (A1) for reaching the printed form. Candidates who use $A=\frac{1}{2}r^{2}\theta$ and substitute $\theta=\frac{20-2r}{r}$ earn the same marks.

(b) Award (M1) for completing the square, or for differentiating $10-2r$ and setting the derivative to zero, and (A1) for the completed-square form. The maximum requires the value $r=5$ and the value $25$; each carries its own (A1). Accept $A\le 25$ stated as the greatest area.

(c) The (M1) is for substituting $r=5$ into the perimeter relation; the (A1) is for $\theta=2$. The arc-length marks are independent: (M1) for using $r\theta$ and (A1) for $10$ cm. Do not accept $2^{\circ}$ or any other degree measure.

(d) The (M1) is for forming the quadratic and the (A1) for both roots. The third (A1) is for computing the angle $8$ radians, and the (R1) is for rejecting it because it exceeds $2\pi$. A candidate who rejects $r=2$ without computing the angle earns at most two of the four marks. Follow through from an incorrect quadratic is allowed provided the rejection argument is sound.

Why this question is hard

The sector is a standard optimisation, but the final part turns it into a question about which of two algebraic solutions is geometrically real. The perimeter is fixed at $20$ cm, so the radius and the angle are linked by $r\theta=20-2r$; the area then becomes the one-variable quadratic $10r-r^{2}$, whose maximum is $25$ cm$^{2}$ at $r=5$. Parts (a) to (c) are there to make that reduction explicit and to give the reader the tools needed for the last part.

The twist is that $A=16$ has two algebraic solutions, $r=2$ and $r=8$, but only one of them corresponds to a sector. The angle for $r=2$ is $\theta=8$ radians, which is larger than $2\pi$, and an angle greater than a full turn does not describe a simple sector. The angle for $r=8$ is $0.5$ radians, which is perfectly possible. The condition that must be checked is therefore $\theta\le 2\pi$, or equivalently $r\ge\frac{20}{2+2\pi}$, and it is not visible in the quadratic itself.

Candidates who stop at the two roots will report both and lose the reasoning mark. The same check explains why the maximum at $r=5$ gives $\theta=2$ radians, comfortably within range. A useful sanity check is to substitute each radius back into the perimeter: $2(2)+2(8)=20$ and $2(8)+8(0.5)=20$ both hold, so the perimeter alone cannot distinguish the two cases; only the constraint on the angle can. The numbers $20$, $16$ and the radius $5$ were chosen so that the quadratic factors over the integers and the maximum is exact. The maximum area of twenty-five square centimetres occurs at a radius of five centimetres, which is exactly a quarter of the fixed perimeter. The angle constraint is easy to forget in a purely algebraic treatment.
