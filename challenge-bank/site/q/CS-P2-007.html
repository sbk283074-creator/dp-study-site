Prioritising housing repairs

A local authority receives repair requests from tenants and must decide which to inspect within 48 hours. It has deployed a model that scores each request and flags those it predicts to be urgent. Urgent means a fault that is dangerous or that will cause rapid deterioration, such as a leaking roof or a failed heating system in winter. The authority covers two areas with very different housing stock. In the North area, of 6 000 requests in the period, 1 500 were genuinely urgent; the model flagged 1 200 as urgent and 1 080 of those were genuinely urgent. In the South area, of 2 000 requests, 400 were genuinely urgent; the model flagged 500 as urgent and 320 of those were genuinely urgent. The authority reports an overall precision of 82% and states that the model is therefore performing well. A tenant in the South area has complained that inspectors call on her neighbours repeatedly for faults that turn out to be minor, while her own leaking roof waited nine days.

Question

Using the case study, answer all four parts.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain why the single figure of 82% overall precision is a misleading summary of this model's performance, and state which two quantities must be reported separately for the complaint to be assessed.

    (Explain)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Construct the confusion matrix for each area and calculate, for each area separately, the precision, the recall, and the proportion of non-urgent requests that were wrongly flagged.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Discuss what the two sets of figures show, including anything they do not establish. Refer to the tenant's complaint in your answer.

    (Discuss)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Justify one action the authority should take, given that it cannot simultaneously maximise both precision and recall and that inspection capacity is fixed.

    (Justify)

Reveal the answer

(a) An overall figure averages over a population in which the two areas contribute very unequally, 6 000 requests from the North against 2 000 from the South, so the aggregate is dominated by the area where the model performs better. (A1) It says nothing about how the errors are distributed, and it is the distribution that the complaint is about. (A1)

The two quantities that must be reported separately are precision and recall for each area, or equivalently the false-positive rate and the false-negative rate for each area, since precision and recall alone do not determine how many non-urgent requests are wrongly flagged. (A1)(A1)

(b) *North:* flagged 1 200, of which 1 080 urgent, so 120 are not urgent. There are 1 500 urgent in total, so $1500 - 1080 = 420$ urgent requests were missed. Non-urgent requests number $6000 - 1500 = 4500$, of which 120 were wrongly flagged. (M1)(A1)

| North | flagged urgent | not flagged | total |
|---|---|---|---|
| genuinely urgent | 1 080 | 420 | 1 500 |
| not urgent | 120 | 4 380 | 4 500 |
| total | 1 200 | 4 800 | 6 000 |

*South:* flagged 500, of which 320 urgent, so 180 are not urgent. There are 400 urgent in total, so $400 - 320 = 80$ were missed. Non-urgent requests number $2000 - 400 = 1600$, of which 180 were wrongly flagged. (A1)

| South | flagged urgent | not flagged | total |
|---|---|---|---|
| genuinely urgent | 320 | 80 | 400 |
| not urgent | 180 | 1 420 | 1 600 |
| total | 500 | 1 500 | 2 000 |

North: precision $= 1080/1200 = 90.0\%$, recall $= 1080/1500 = 72.0\%$, wrongly flagged $= 120/4500 = 2.67\%$. (A1)
South: precision $= 320/500 = 64.0\%$, recall $= 320/400 = 80.0\%$, wrongly flagged $= 180/1600 = 11.25\%$. (A1)

(c) The figures support the substance of the complaint. A non-urgent request in the South is (M1)
$$\frac{11.25}{2.67} \approx 4.2$$
times as likely to be flagged as one in the North, so South tenants are much more likely to be visited for a fault that turns out to be minor, which is the nuisance she describes. (A1) At the same time the model is not uniformly worse in the South: its recall there is higher, 80% against 72%, so an urgent request in the South is more likely to be caught. That is why her own roof waiting nine days is not necessarily explained by the area statistics and may be a single unfortunate case. (A1)

What the figures do not establish is why the difference exists. The areas differ in housing stock, so the base rate of urgency differs, 25% in the North against 20% in the South, and a model trained on pooled data with one threshold will not behave identically across groups with different base rates even if it is free of bias in its inputs. The data also cannot distinguish a model that is miscalibrated from one that is correctly calibrated but operating at an inappropriate threshold, and they say nothing about whether the features used are themselves proxies for something protected. (A1)

(d) A defensible action is to set the decision threshold separately for each area so that the false-positive rates are equalised, and to publish the resulting precision and recall for both areas. (A1) This directly addresses the complaint, which is about unequal burden rather than about overall accuracy, and it is achievable within fixed inspection capacity because lowering the threshold in the North and raising it in the South reallocates roughly the same number of visits. (A1)

It must be accompanied by a stated decision about which error the authority considers more serious. If a missed urgent repair is the greater harm, the threshold should be set to favour recall and the authority must accept more minor inspections in both areas; if unnecessary visits are the greater harm, the reverse. (A1) What is not defensible is to continue reporting only the aggregate figure, because that suppresses exactly the information needed to make the choice.

Markscheme notes

(a) The mark scheme looks for the idea that an aggregate conceals distribution, and for the identification of precision and recall (or the two error rates) as the disaggregated quantities. A candidate who says only that the model is biased has asserted a conclusion the data do not yet support. (b) Award (M1) for deriving at least one cell correctly from the text, since the numbers are given as counts of flagged and correctly flagged rather than as a matrix. Accept matrices with rows and columns transposed. All six rates must appear; the wrongly-flagged proportion is the one most often omitted and is the rate the complaint is actually about. (c) The comparison mark requires the ratio. Credit the observation that South recall is higher, because it prevents the easy conclusion that the model is simply worse in the South. The 'does not establish' mark is for the point that the statistics cannot separate miscalibration from threshold choice, or for noting that different base rates alone can produce this pattern. (d) Any single action is acceptable provided it is justified against the figures and acknowledges the trade-off. Equalising false-positive rates, equalising recall, or setting thresholds from an explicit cost ratio for the two error types are all creditable. An answer that proposes collecting more data or retraining without addressing the threshold question has not engaged with the fact that the trade-off is irreducible at fixed capacity.

Why this question is hard

The central move in this question is to refuse the aggregate. The authority's 82 percent overall precision is arithmetically correct and almost useless, because it averages over two areas that contribute requests in a three-to-one ratio, so it largely describes the North. Disaggregating produces a genuinely uncomfortable result: the South has better recall but far worse precision, and a non-urgent request there is more than four times as likely to trigger a visit. That is precisely the experience the tenant describes, so her complaint is not anecdote against statistics; it is the statistics, correctly computed.

The question is deliberately constructed so that the easy conclusion is wrong. It would be natural to read the figures as showing that the model discriminates against the South, but recall there is in fact higher. What differs is the threshold's effect in a population with a different base rate of urgency: 25 percent in the North against 20 percent in the South. A single threshold applied to two groups with different base rates produces different error mixes even when the underlying model is equally good in both, which is why the data cannot by themselves tell you whether the model is miscalibrated or merely being used at the wrong operating point. Recognising that distinction is the difference between a technical fix and a political one.

The final part is unavoidable because precision and recall trade off against each other and inspection capacity is fixed, so no amount of modelling removes the choice. Someone has to decide whether a missed urgent repair or an unnecessary visit is the worse failure, and that decision is a judgement about harm rather than a result about accuracy. The only clearly indefensible position is the authority's current one, of reporting a single number that conceals the fact that the choice is being made and being made unevenly. A student who reaches that conclusion has understood why the ethical content of this topic is not an addendum to the technical material but a consequence of it.
