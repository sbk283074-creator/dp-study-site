A.2 Hooke's law and A.3 the force constant from a gradient

Physics HL · PHYS-A.3-102

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.2 (Forces: Hooke's law, the force constant of a spring) and A.3 (Work, energy and power: elastic potential energy)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedDetermineEvaluateSuggest

Question

A student hangs a series of masses on a vertical spring and records the extension of the spring for each mass. The readings are plotted in the figure, in which the extension $x$ is in centimetres and the hanging mass $m$ is in grams. The straight line shown is the line of best fit, drawn through the origin because the spring was set so that its extension was zero when no mass was hung. One reading, at $m = 250$ g, is labelled X. The spring has negligible mass and the masses hang in equilibrium. Answer all parts with reference to the data in the figure, and quote numerical answers to three significant figures. Take $g = 9.81$ N kg$^{-1}$.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Determine the gradient of the line of best fit, giving the unit of your answer.

    (Determine)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Evaluate whether reading X should be included when the line of best fit is drawn, supporting your answer with a numerical comparison against the other readings.

    (Evaluate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Hence determine the force constant of the spring, in N m$^{-1}$, using the gradient of the line of best fit.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Suggest one source of uncertainty in the value of the gradient and explain how it would affect the value you obtained for the force constant.

    (Suggest)
  5. [4 marks](e)

    The experiment is repeated with a second identical spring joined in series with the first, so that the two springs hang end to end and the masses hang from the lower spring. Suggest how the gradient of the graph and the force constant you calculated in part (c) would change, and justify your answer.

    (Suggest)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The line passes through the origin and through the reading at $m = 300$ g, where the line gives $x = 12.15$ cm. (M1) The gradient is therefore $12.15/300 = 0.0405$ cm g$^{-1}$. (A1)

(b) The line predicts $x = 0.0405(250) = 10.1$ cm at $m = 250$ g, but the recorded reading is 11.4 cm, so X lies 1.3 cm above the line. (M1) The other five readings lie within about 0.05 cm of the line, which is the scale of the random scatter, so the deviation of X is roughly twenty-five times larger than the scatter of the rest of the data. (A1) A single reading that far off the pattern is an anomalous reading, most likely a misread scale or a mass recorded wrongly, and it should be excluded from the line of best fit and investigated. (A1)

(c) Converting the gradient into SI units, $0.0405$ cm g$^{-1} = 0.0405 \times 10^{-2}$ m per $10^{-3}$ kg $= 0.405$ m kg$^{-1}$. (M1) In equilibrium $mg = kx$, so $x/m = g/k$ and hence $k = g/(x/m)$. (M1) Therefore $k = 9.81/0.405 = 24.2$ N m$^{-1}$. (A1)

(d) The gradient depends on how accurately the extension was read against the scale, and the smallest scale division limits how precisely each point can be placed. (A1) Reading each extension too large by a fixed amount, for example by taking the scale from the wrong mark, makes the gradient too large. (M1) Since $k = g/(x/m)$, an over-large gradient gives a force constant that is too small, so the uncertainty in $k$ is in the opposite direction to the error in the gradient. (A1)

(e) Each spring now supports the same force $mg$, so each extends by the same amount as the single spring did on its own. (M1) The total extension is therefore twice the extension of one spring for the same hanging mass, so the gradient doubles to 0.0810 cm g$^{-1}$. (A1)(A1) Since $k = g/(x/m)$ and the extension per unit mass has doubled, the force constant of the combination is $24.2/2 = 12.1$ N m$^{-1}$, which is the expected result for two identical springs in series. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Award M1 for reading a pair of coordinates from the line, A1 for $0.0405$ cm g$^{-1}$ with the unit. Accept 0.040 to 0.041; accept the gradient quoted in m kg$^{-1}$ as 0.405 only if the unit is given. (b) M1 for comparing X with the line numerically, A1 for quantifying that comparison against the scatter of the other readings, A1 for a conclusion that X should be excluded and investigated. A candidate who simply writes that X is anomalous without any number scores M1 at most. Do not accept a conclusion that X should be kept because it is a real reading; the question asks for a judgement about the line of best fit. (c) M1 for converting the gradient to m kg$^{-1}$, M1 for using $mg = kx$ or equivalently $k = g/(x/m)$, A1 for 24.2 N m$^{-1}$. Follow through from the candidate's own gradient in (a). A candidate who omits the conversion and answers 9.81/0.0405 = 242 N m$^{-1}$ has the method right and the unit wrong, and scores 2 of the 3. (d) A1 for a source that is genuinely a source of uncertainty in the gradient, such as the resolution of the scale, the parallax in reading it, or the finite mass of the spring; M1 for connecting the sign of the effect on the gradient to the effect on $k$; A1 for the inverse relationship stated correctly. (e) M1 for stating that each spring carries the same force, A1 for the total extension doubling, A1 for the gradient doubling to 0.0810 cm g$^{-1}$, A1 for the force constant halving to 12.1 N m$^{-1}$.

Why this question is hard

The figure supplies the line of best fit already drawn, which moves the demand of this question away from plotting and onto interpretation. The line passes through the origin, because the spring was set to zero extension with no mass, and it rises to about 12.15 cm at 300 g, giving a gradient of 0.0405 cm per gram.

The interesting feature of the data is the reading at 250 g. The line predicts 10.1 cm there, and the recorded value is 11.4 cm, so the point sits 1.3 cm above the line while every other reading sits within about 0.05 cm of it. That is not scatter; it is a different kind of number. The right response is to exclude it, draw the line through the remaining five readings, and look for the cause. A candidate who keeps it will draw a steeper line and obtain a force constant that is too small.

Part (c) contains the trap that the item is really built around. The gradient is 0.0405 centimetres per gram, and a spring constant is measured in newtons per metre. Those units are three powers of ten apart in the length and three in the mass, and a candidate who divides $g$ by 0.0405 without converting gets 242 N m$^{-1}$, which is exactly ten times too large. The correct conversion gives 0.405 m kg$^{-1}$ and a force constant of 24.2 N m$^{-1}$.

Part (d) asks about uncertainty rather than asking for a calculation, and the important thing is the direction. If each extension is read slightly too large, the gradient comes out too large, and because the force constant is $g$ divided by the gradient, the force constant comes out too small. Getting the inverse relationship right is the mark that separates a candidate who understands the algebra from one who has only learned to substitute.

Part (e) changes the apparatus and leaves the data behind. Two identical springs in series each carry the full hanging weight, so each extends by the same amount the single spring did. The total extension doubles, the gradient doubles, and the force constant halves to 12.1 N m$^{-1}$. A candidate who tries to reuse the numbers from part (c) without thinking about the new arrangement will not find this.
