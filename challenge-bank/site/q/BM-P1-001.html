3.5 / 1.5 / 1.4 Ratio analysis applied to a growth decision

Business management SL · BM-P1-001

3.5 (Profitability and liquidity ratio analysis), 1.5 (Growth and evolution), 1.4 (Stakeholders)P115 marksdifficulty 4technology: not applicableAnalyseEvaluateDiscuss

Maren & Daughters

Maren & Daughters is a family-owned chain of twelve bakery cafés in a medium-sized European city. It is owned entirely by the founder's three children, who work in the business and take all major decisions between them. The chain has grown steadily by opening one new café roughly every eighteen months, each funded from retained profit and a bank loan. The bank has now indicated that it will not lend further without additional security. Two of the three owners want to grow by franchising; the third wants to continue opening company-owned cafés and is prepared to bring in an outside investor. Extracts from the most recent accounts are given below, together with the projections prepared by each camp.

Question

Using the case study and the figures provided, answer all three parts.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Analyse the recent financial performance and position of Maren & Daughters using ratio analysis. Your analysis must include calculations.

    (Analyse)
  2. [6 marks](b)

    Evaluate whether Maren & Daughters should expand by franchising or by opening company-owned cafés, using the projections in the case study.

    (Evaluate)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Discuss the likely impact of the expansion on two different stakeholder groups, and explain why their interests may conflict.

    (Discuss)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Gross profit is $8.40-3.78=\unicode{8364}4.62$ m, so the gross margin is $\dfrac{4.62}{8.40}=55.0\%$. (M1)(A1) Operating profit is $4.62-3.36=\unicode{8364}1.26$ m, an operating margin of $15.0\%$, and the net margin is $\dfrac{0.756}{8.40}=9.0\%$. (A1) Return on capital employed is $\dfrac{1.26}{6.30}=20.0\%$, which is strong for a retail food business and suggests the existing estate is being used well. (A1) Liquidity looks comfortable: the current ratio is $\dfrac{1.68}{0.84}=2.0$, well above the usual benchmark of $1.5$. (A1) Gearing is $\dfrac{2.52}{6.30}=40\%$, which is not alarming in itself but matters here because the bank has already refused further lending without security. (A1) The overall picture is a profitable, liquid business whose constraint is access to capital rather than the quality of its trading. (R1)

(b) Franchising generates $\unicode{8364}1.0$ m in upfront fees $(40\times\unicode{8364}25\,000)$ and $\unicode{8364}1.44$ m a year in royalties $(40\times6\%\times\unicode{8364}600\,000)$. (M1)(A1) It requires almost no capital, it sidesteps the bank's refusal entirely, and it transfers most of the operating risk to the franchisees. (A1) Against that, the royalty stream is only as good as the franchisees: sixty per cent of the chain's future profit would depend on people the family does not employ and cannot directly manage, and one café cutting corners on hygiene would damage a brand built over twenty years. The $\unicode{8364}1.0$ m of fees is also one-off, so the recurring benefit is the $\unicode{8364}1.44$ m royalty, not the headline $\unicode{8364}2.44$ m. (A1)

The company-owned option costs $8\times\unicode{8364}450\,000=\unicode{8364}3.6$ m and adds $8\times\unicode{8364}90\,000=\unicode{8364}0.72$ m a year before depreciation, or about $\unicode{8364}0.36$ m after charging depreciation over ten years. (M1)(A1) Payback is $\dfrac{3.6}{0.72}=5$ years, and the return on the new capital is roughly $10\%$, which is half the $20\%$ the business currently earns. (A1)

On the figures, franchising dominates: it produces more annual income, needs a fraction of the capital, and avoids diluting ownership. (R1) The qualification is that the comparison is not purely financial. Franchising converts a business the family controls into one it licenses, and the loss of control over standards is the genuine cost, not the lost margin. A balanced evaluation therefore recommends franchising but with a contractual and operational framework — approved suppliers, inspection rights, a training requirement — that protects the brand, and possibly a small number of company-owned cafés retained as a benchmark and a training base. (R1)

(c) *The three owner-directors.* Under franchising they keep full ownership and receive fees and royalties without new borrowing, so income rises and personal financial risk falls. (M1)(A1) However, the third sibling's objection is not about money but about control, and two of them will get their way over the third, which risks a family relationship as well as a business one. (A1)

*Employees.* Under franchising the existing staff keep their jobs but the new outlets' staff are employed by franchisees, probably on different terms and without the informal culture of a family firm; there is also no longer a clear promotion ladder into new cafés. Under the company-owned route about eighty new jobs are created inside the business with the same terms and progression. (M1)(A1)

The interests conflict because owners benefit from franchising's lower risk and faster income while employees benefit from the company-owned route's job creation and internal promotion. (A1) A candidate might also note that customers gain from faster expansion under either option but lose if franchise standards slip, which links the stakeholder analysis back to the brand risk identified in (b). (R1)

Markscheme notes

In (a) award one mark per correctly calculated and interpreted ratio, up to a maximum of four; candidates must calculate rather than describe. Accept other valid ratios such as the acid test or the gearing ratio expressed against equity, provided they are computed from the figures given. The ROCE figure is the one that matters most, because the whole question turns on whether new capital earns the 20% the business currently earns. In (b) the evaluation mark requires a supported judgement; a balanced list of advantages with no conclusion is capped at four. Candidates who prefer the company-owned route should not be penalised provided they engage with the 10% versus 20% return and the capital constraint imposed by the bank, since the bank's refusal is what makes the option difficult rather than impossible. In (c) any two stakeholder groups are acceptable, but the conflict must be identified explicitly rather than left implicit in two separate descriptions. Candidates who argue for a mixed strategy — a small core of company-owned cafés retained for training and benchmarking alongside a franchised estate — should be credited, and arguably deserve credit above a pure recommendation, because it addresses the control objection without abandoning the capital advantage. Where a candidate raises the outside investor as a third option, allow it on its merits; the case study does not quantify it, which is a legitimate point to make rather than an omission to be penalised.

Why this question is hard

The case study is constructed so that the financial analysis and the strategic conclusion pull in different directions, which is what makes it worth fifteen marks. Every ratio a candidate computes in (a) is healthy — 55% gross margin, 20% ROCE, a current ratio of 2.0 — and the tempting conclusion is that the business is doing well and should carry on as it is. The bank's refusal to lend is the one fact that breaks that reading, and it is buried in the stimulus rather than highlighted, so candidates have to notice it for themselves. Once noticed, it reframes the whole question: the constraint is capital, not capability, and that is precisely the situation in which franchising beats organic growth. Part (b) then adds a second wrinkle, because the recurring royalty of €1.44m is genuinely attractive while the €1.0m of upfront fees is not recurring at all, and a candidate who treats €2.44m as an annual figure has misread the model. The evaluation is not simply 'franchising wins': the real cost is loss of control over brand standards, which no ratio captures, and the strongest answers say so and propose a safeguard. A second teaching point is worth drawing out of (b) if there is time afterwards. The €1.0m of franchise fees is a one-off receipt while the €1.44m royalty recurs, and the difference between the two is exactly the difference between selling an asset and building an income stream. Candidates routinely add them together, and the habit of asking whether a figure recurs is one that transfers directly to cash-flow forecasting and to investment appraisal, where a terminal receipt and an annual saving must never be summed.
