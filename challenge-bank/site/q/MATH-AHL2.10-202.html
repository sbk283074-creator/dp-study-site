2.10 A parameter discussion for an exponential equation reduced to a quadratic, where the number of solutions is governed by the sign of the product of the roots rather than by the discriminant

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL2.10-202

Topic 2: FunctionsAHL 2.10 (solving equations analytically) with SL 2.8 (exponential equations) and AHL 2.12 (polynomial equations and their roots)P1 · Section B12 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatShow thatDetermineExplain

Question

The constant $k$ is real. Consider the equation
$$9^{x} - k\,3^{x} + (k + 1) = 0.$$
For each value of $k$ the equation has some number of real solutions $x$, and that number is what is to be determined.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the substitution $t = 3^{x}$ turns the equation into $t^{2} - kt + (k + 1) = 0$ with the restriction $t > 0$, and explain why every positive root $t$ gives exactly one real solution $x$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Show that the quadratic in $t$ has two distinct real roots if and only if $k < 2 - 2\sqrt{2}$ or $k > 2 + 2\sqrt{2}$, and state the single value of $k$ in the remaining range at which the two roots coincide.

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine all values of $k$ for which the original equation in $x$ has exactly one real solution, and give one such value of $k$ that is less than $-1$ together with its solution $x$.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain why the answer to part (c) is not simply $k = 2 + 2\sqrt{2}$, making explicit reference to the signs of the sum and the product of the two roots of the quadratic in $t$.

    (Explain)

Total: 12 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Since $9^{x} = (3^{x})^{2}$, substituting $t = 3^{x}$ gives $t^{2} - kt + (k + 1) = 0$ (M1). For real $x$ the quantity $3^{x}$ is positive for every $x$, and $3^{x}$ takes every positive value exactly once, because $3^{x}$ is continuous and strictly increasing with range $(0, \infty)$ (A1). So the restriction is $t > 0$, and the correspondence $x \mapsto t$ is a bijection from $\mathbb{R}$ onto $(0, \infty)$: each admissible $t$ corresponds to exactly one $x$, namely $x = \log_{3} t$ (A1).

(b) The discriminant is
$$\Delta = k^{2} - 4(k + 1) = k^{2} - 4k - 4 \;\; (M1).$$
The roots are real and distinct when $\Delta > 0$. Solving $k^{2} - 4k - 4 = 0$ gives
$$k = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 + 16}}{2} = 2 \pm 2\sqrt{2} \;\; (A1),$$
so $\Delta > 0$ for $k < 2 - 2\sqrt{2}$ or $k > 2 + 2\sqrt{2}$, and the two roots coincide at $k = 2 + 2\sqrt{2}$ (the root $k = 2 - 2\sqrt{2}$ gives a double root $t = \tfrac{k}{2} = 1 - \sqrt{2} < 0$ and so corresponds to no solution in $x$) (A1).

Strictly, the double root exists at both values of $k$; the two values are $2 - 2\sqrt{2} \approx -0.828$ and $2 + 2\sqrt{2} \approx 4.828$, and it is only the second that is positive.

(c) Since $t = 3^{x}$, the original equation has exactly one solution precisely when the quadratic in $t$ has exactly one positive root. Write the roots $t_{1}, t_{2}$, with
$$t_{1} + t_{2} = k, \qquad t_{1}t_{2} = k + 1 \;\; (M1).$$

Case $k < -1$. Then $k + 1 < 0$, so the product of the roots is negative and the roots have opposite signs: exactly one of them is positive, whatever the discriminant says. Since $k < -1 < 2 - 2\sqrt{2}$, the discriminant is indeed positive, so both roots are real. The original equation therefore has exactly one solution (A1).

For a concrete value take $k = -2$. Then $t^{2} + 2t - 1 = 0$ gives $t = -1 \pm \sqrt{2}$, of which only $t = \sqrt{2} - 1$ is positive, so
$$x = \log_{3}(\sqrt{2} - 1) \approx -0.802 \;\; (A1).$$

Case $k = 2 + 2\sqrt{2} \approx 4.828$. Here $\Delta = 0$ and the double root is $t = \tfrac{k}{2} = 1 + \sqrt{2} > 0$, so there is exactly one positive root and exactly one solution of the original equation, $x = \log_{3}(1 + \sqrt{2}) \approx 0.802$.

The remaining cases give no solution or two. For $2 - 2\sqrt{2} < k < 2 + 2\sqrt{2}$ we have $\Delta < 0$ and there are no real roots at all. For $-1 < k < 0$ the product $k + 1$ is positive and the sum $k$ is negative, so both roots are negative and neither is admissible. For $k > 2 + 2\sqrt{2}$ both roots are positive and there are two solutions. At $k = -1$ the roots are $0$ and $-1$, neither admissible, so there are none. At $k = 0$ the equation reads $t^{2} + 1 = 0$, with no real roots.

Hence the original equation has exactly one real solution if and only if
$$k < -1 \quad \text{or} \quad k = 2 + 2\sqrt{2}.$$

(d) The answer to (c) is not just $k = 2 + 2\sqrt{2}$ because the discriminant answers a different question. It decides whether the two roots of the quadratic in $t$ are real, not whether they are *positive*, and $t = 3^{x}$ admits only positive values (A1).

The signs are read off the sum and the product. For $k < -1$ the product $t_{1}t_{2} = k + 1$ is negative, so the two roots have opposite signs (A1); one is positive and one is negative, and the negative one is simply discarded by the restriction. The discriminant is positive there too, so a candidate who checks only $\Delta \ge 0$ and then asks for equality will find $k = 2 + 2\sqrt{2}$ and stop, having excluded an entire unbounded interval of valid answers. The discriminant is a necessary condition for the roots to be real; the product's sign is the condition that decides how many of them survive (A1).

In the middle range $-1 < k < 0$ the same reading runs the other way: the product is positive and the sum $k$ is negative, so both roots are negative and no solution survives at all. The sign pair $(\text{sum}, \text{product})$ is the complete description, and the discriminant is only one of its two ingredients.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for the substitution and the reduced quadratic, (A1) for $t > 0$, (A1) for the one-to-one correspondence between positive $t$ and real $x$. A candidate who notices $9^{x} = (3^{x})^{2}$ but writes the restriction as $t \ne 0$ scores the first two marks only.

(b) (M1) for the discriminant, (A1) for the two boundary values $2 \pm 2\sqrt{2}$, (A1) for the correct sign pattern of the quadratic inequality. The final (A1) also covers the statement that only $k = 2 + 2\sqrt{2}$ gives a positive double root; a candidate who names $2 - 2\sqrt{2}$ as a second value where one solution occurs forfeits it, because $t = 1 - \sqrt{2}$ is negative.

(c) (M1) for writing down the sum and product, (A1) for the interval $k < -1$ justified by the sign of the product, (A1) for a worked value of $k$ less than $-1$ with its solution. The value $k = -2$ giving $x = \log_{3}(\sqrt{2} - 1)$ is the expected answer; any $k < -1$ with correct work is accepted. A candidate who gives only $k = 2 + 2\sqrt{2}$ scores the first mark only.

(d) (A1) for distinguishing "real roots" from "positive roots", (A1) for the explicit use of the product $k + 1$, (A1) for the explicit use of the sum $k$ in at least one of the two cases. This is an explanation mark: the reasoning must be present, not merely the answer repeated.

Condonation: a candidate who handles the case $k = -1$ by observing that the roots are $0$ and $-1$ and neither is admissible scores full credit in (c) even if the interval is written as $-1 < k$ rather than $k \le -1$, provided the boundary behaviour is discussed. Forfeits: using $\Delta \ge 0$ as the whole condition, which produces the answer $k \ge 2 + 2\sqrt{2}$ with a spurious double count at the endpoint and no solution for $k < -1$ at all.

Why this question is hard

A hidden quadratic is a substitution and a discriminant, and that is all most candidates ever do with one. The substitution here is the easy part. The interesting question is not whether the quadratic has roots but how many of its roots are usable, and the two questions come apart completely in this item.

The mechanism is worth stating once in general terms, because it recurs. Substituting $t = a^{x}$ maps the real line onto the positive half-line. A quadratic in $t$ with two real roots may therefore contribute two solutions, one solution or none, and which it is depends on the signs of the roots. Vieta's relations give those signs immediately: the product $t_{1}t_{2}$ is the constant term, the sum is minus the coefficient of $t$. Product negative means one positive root, always, for every value of the parameter in that range. Here the product is $k + 1$, so the whole interval $k < -1$ -- an unbounded interval of parameter values -- produces exactly one solution, and a candidate who trusts the discriminant reports that this interval gives two.

The second thing the item tests is the ability to notice which of two boundary values is real. The discriminant vanishes at both $k = 2 \pm 2\sqrt{2}$, and both give a genuine double root of the quadratic. Only the larger one gives a positive double root, because the double root is always $t = \tfrac{k}{2}$ and a negative value of $t$ is inadmissible. That single observation removes the smaller boundary from the answer without any further algebra, and it is the same observation that governs the whole question: admissibility of $t$, not reality of the roots.

Part (c) asks for a concrete value of $k$ below $-1$ with its solution for a reason. In the abstract it is easy to say "one root is negative, discard it" and move on without ever computing what the remaining root is. $k = -2$ gives $t^{2} + 2t - 1 = 0$, whose roots are $-1 \pm \sqrt{2}$; the positive one is $\sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 0.414$ and $x = \log_{3}(\sqrt{2}-1) \approx -0.802$ is negative, which is itself a useful check that a solution of the exponential equation need not be positive even though $t$ is.

Challenge lever
Once $t = 3^{x}$ is substituted the equation is a quadratic and the reflex is to answer the root-count question with the discriminant. That is correct for the top of the range and wrong for the bottom: for every $k < -1$ the discriminant is positive and the two roots have opposite signs, so exactly one of them is a permissible value of $t = 3^{x}$ and the original equation has exactly one solution. The condition that actually governs the count is the sign of the product $k + 1$, which is never mentioned by the discriminant at all.
