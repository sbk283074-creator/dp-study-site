4.2 With unequal class widths the frequency is the area of a bar and not its height, so a bar's height alone does not give the count

Question

The figure shows a histogram summarising the values of a variable measured on 160 observations. The class intervals are of unequal width and the vertical axis is labelled frequency density.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Explain why the vertical axis of this histogram is frequency density rather than frequency.

  2. [2 marks](b)

  3. [2 marks](c)

(a) Explain why the vertical axis of this histogram is frequency density rather than frequency. [2 marks] (Explain) (b) Find the number of observations with a value between 5 and 25. [2 marks] (Find) (c) Estimate the median of the values recorded for the 160 observations. [2 marks] (Estimate)
Reveal the answer

(a) The three classes have widths $10$, $20$ and $30$ units, so they are not comparable by height: a bar spanning $30$ units would have to be three times as tall as a bar spanning $10$ units in order to represent the same number of observations (M1). If the heights were frequencies, the picture would misrepresent the data by making the wide classes look larger than they are. Frequency density is frequency divided by class width, so the *area* of each bar equals the frequency and bars of different widths can be compared fairly (A1).

(b) The frequency in a class is its height multiplied by its width. For $0 \leq x < 10$ this is $2 \times 10 = 20$; for $10 \leq x < 30$ it is $4 \times 20 = 80$; for $30 \leq x < 60$ it is $2 \times 30 = 60$. These sum to $160$, which agrees with the question (M1). For the range $5$ to $25$: the part of the first class above $5$ is half of that bar, giving $10$ observations, and the part of the second class below $25$ is $\frac{15}{20}$ of that bar, giving $\frac{15}{20} \times 80 = 60$ observations. The total is $10 + 60 = 70$ (A1).

(c) The cumulative frequencies are $20$ after the first class, $100$ after the second and $160$ after the third (M1). The median is the value in position $\frac{160}{2} = 80$, which lies in the class $10 \leq x < 30$ because the cumulative frequency reaches $20$ at $x = 10$ and $100$ at $x = 30$. Within that class the $80$th value is $80 - 20 = 60$ observations into a class containing $80$, so it lies $\frac{60}{80} = 0.75$ of the way across a class of width $20$, that is $15$ units beyond $10$. The median is therefore $10 + 15 = 25$ (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for referring to the unequal class widths, or for saying that area rather than height carries the frequency; (A1) for stating that frequency density is frequency per unit width and that the bar area is therefore the frequency. A candidate who says only 'because the classes are different sizes' without saying what the axis then measures has the observation but not the reason; award (M1) and press for the definition.

(b) (M1) for multiplying at least one height by its width, or for finding the total 160; (A1) for 70. A candidate who answers 8 has added the heights and should earn nothing: that is the misreading the part exists to detect. A candidate who correctly finds 20 and 80 but forgets that the range starts at 5 rather than 0 should earn (M1) only, since the partial bars are the whole point of the part.

(c) (M1) for the cumulative frequencies, or for identifying the median position 80 as lying in the second class; (A1) for 25. Accept linear interpolation within the class as shown, and accept a candidate who works in frequency density rather than in cumulative frequency provided the class is correctly identified. Do not accept 20 or 30, which are the class boundaries: the median is not a boundary here and the question asks for an estimate.

Why this question is hard

The item exists because a histogram is the one common statistical display in which the vertical axis is not the quantity of interest. In a bar chart the height is the count; in a histogram it is the count per unit of the horizontal axis, and the two agree only when every class is the same width. When the widths differ the picture is still readable, but only if the reader has understood that the bars are being compared by area, and a candidate who has not understood that will read the tallest bar as the most populous class and the numbers will look plausible while being wrong by a factor of twenty.

The figures are chosen so that the misreading is detectable rather than merely incorrect. The heights 2, 4 and 2 sum to 8, which cannot be the total for 160 observations, and the widest class has the same height as the narrowest while containing three times as many observations. A candidate who adds the heights has an answer that contradicts the question's own statement, which is the most useful kind of error because it can be noticed by the candidate and not only by the marker.

Parts (b) and (c) then require the areas to be used rather than merely described. Part (b) cuts the range at 5 and at 25, so both end classes are entered part-way and the candidate has to take a proportion of a bar rather than a whole one; part (c) asks for a median, which needs the cumulative frequencies and an interpolation inside a class. Both parts are straightforward once the vertical axis has been read correctly, and both are unanswerable if it has not, which is what makes the item a test of one idea rather than of three.
