4.4 Market research; 4.5 The marketing mix (price, product)

Business management SL · BM-4.5-001

Unit 4: 4.1 The role of marketing; 4.2 Marketing planning; 4.4 Market research; 4.5 The seven Ps of the marketing mix. Toolkit: descriptive statisticsP2 · Section A14 marksdifficulty 4CalculateExplainEvaluate

Vireo Sparkling

Vireo is launching a sparkling fruit water in a market where the leading brand sells at €2.20. Vireo's variable cost per bottle is €0.85 and its fixed costs for the launch year are €180 000.

A market-research agency surveyed consumers and produced the following estimates of weekly demand at four possible prices.

| Price per bottle (€) | Estimated weekly demand (bottles) |
|---|---|
| 1.50 | 24 000 |
| 2.00 | 19 000 |
| 2.50 | 13 500 |
| 3.00 | 7 000 |

The agency surveyed 400 shoppers outside four supermarkets in the city centre, between 09:00 and 11:00 on a Tuesday. Respondents were shown the bottle and asked how many they would buy at each price.

Question

Vireo must choose a launch price.

  1. [6 marks](a)

    (i) Calculate the weekly revenue at each of the four prices and identify the revenue-maximising price.

    (ii) Calculate the weekly contribution at each price and identify the contribution-maximising price. Comment on the difference between your two answers.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Explain the difference between primary and secondary market research, and evaluate the reliability of the agency's data.

    (Explain)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Using two elements of the marketing mix other than price, evaluate how Vireo should position the product.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) (i) Revenue $=$ price $\times$ demand:

| Price (€) | Demand | Weekly revenue (€) |
|---|---|---|
| 1.50 | 24 000 | 36 000 |
| 2.00 | 19 000 | 38 000 |
| 2.50 | 13 500 | 33 750 |
| 3.00 | 7 000 | 21 000 |

(A1) for at least three correct revenue figures, (A1) for identifying €2.00 as the revenue-maximising price.

(ii) Contribution per unit $=$ price $-\,0.85$; weekly contribution $=$ contribution per unit $\times$ demand:

| Price (€) | Contribution per unit (€) | Demand | Weekly contribution (€) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.50 | 0.65 | 24 000 | 15 600 |
| 2.00 | 1.15 | 19 000 | 21 850 |
| 2.50 | 1.65 | 13 500 | 22 275 |
| 3.00 | 2.15 | 7 000 | 15 050 |

(A1) for correct contribution per unit, (A1) for at least three correct weekly contributions, (A1) for identifying €2.50.

Comment: the two criteria disagree. Revenue is maximised at €2.00 but contribution at €2.50. (A1) Maximising revenue ignores the fact that every extra bottle sold also costs €0.85 to make — selling 5 000 more bottles at €1.50 rather than €2.50 adds €7 500 of revenue but only €3 250 of contribution. A business with fixed costs to cover should choose on contribution, not revenue.

(b) Primary research is data collected first-hand for this specific purpose (surveys, focus groups, observation, test marketing). Secondary research is data that already exists and was collected for another purpose (market reports, government statistics, competitor accounts). (A1)(A1)

Evaluation of the agency's data — award up to two:

  • Sample size and location. 400 respondents is reasonable, but all four supermarkets are in the city centre, so the sample under-represents suburban and rural shoppers whose price sensitivity may differ. (A1)
  • Timing. Tuesday 09:00–11:00 captures mainly retirees, shift workers and parents with pre-school children — not the working population who buy most sparkling water. This is a systematic sampling bias, not random error, and increasing the sample would not fix it. (A1)
  • Stated preference vs actual behaviour. Asking how many bottles someone *would* buy at a price is hypothetical; people systematically overstate their willingness to pay in surveys, so the demand at €3.00 is especially likely to be optimistic. (A1)

(c) Indicative evaluation, using two of product, promotion, place, people, process, physical evidence:

  • Product. At €2.50 Vireo is *above* the market leader's €2.20, so the product must visibly justify a premium — distinctive packaging, a genuine fruit content claim, or a flavour range the leader does not offer. Without that, the same demand curve at a higher price is unachievable. (A1)(A1)
  • Place. A premium position requires distribution consistent with it: independent cafés, delis and higher-end supermarkets rather than deep-discount chains, where appearing alongside cheaper own-label water would undermine the price. (A1)(A1)
  • Promotion. Launch communications should explain *why* the product costs more (ingredients, provenance), since the price itself cannot; social media and in-store sampling suit a new brand with a €180 000 budget better than television. (A1)(A1)

Award up to two marks per element, for a maximum of four. The evaluation must link the mix element back to the premium price established in (a) — generic descriptions of the seven Ps earn nothing.

Markscheme notes

(a) Follow through on arithmetic slips. The comment mark is the discriminating one and requires the insight that revenue ignores variable cost; a candidate who says only 'the answers are different' forfeits it. Note that maximising contribution is correct here *because fixed costs are unchanged* across the options — credit that qualification if offered.

(b) One mark each for the definitions. The evaluation marks are for *specific* criticisms drawn from the stated method (location, timing, hypothetical questioning), not for generic complaints about surveys. 'The sample is too small' is not creditworthy on its own — 400 is a reasonable size; the problem is who was in it.

(c) Two elements, two marks each. The link to the premium pricing must be explicit. Candidates who recommend a penetration price contradicting their own answer to (a) can still earn the marks if the mix recommendations are internally consistent with €1.50 — but should be pushed to reconcile the two.

SL boundary: sales forecasting (4.3) and international marketing (4.6) are HL only; do not require them, and do not credit analysis that depends on them.

Why this question is hard

The arithmetic in (a) is straightforward; the insight is that the two obvious criteria disagree, and that only one of them is the right basis for a decision.

Revenue maximisation is the instinctive choice — it is what 'selling more' looks like — but it ignores the cost of making each additional bottle. At €1.50 the company sells 24 000 bottles and takes €36 000; at €2.50 it sells 13 500 and takes only €33 750, yet earns *more* (€22 275 against €15 600) because each sale contributes €1.65 rather than €0.65. The gap between €2.00 (revenue-maximising) and €2.50 (contribution-maximising) is the whole point of the part. The qualification worth noting is that contribution is the correct criterion *only because* fixed costs do not vary with the price chosen; if they did, profit rather than contribution would be the target.

Part (b) is where students who have learned 'primary is data you collect yourself' get a rude shock. The question is not what the research type is but whether the data can be trusted, and the method description is deliberately full of holes: four city-centre supermarkets excludes most of the market, and Tuesday morning excludes anyone at work. The second of these is the more important, because it is a *systematic* bias — surveying 4 000 people at 09:00 on a Tuesday would reproduce it exactly. And the hypothetical format ('how many would you buy at €X?') reliably overstates demand at high prices, which is precisely where the demand curve matters most for a premium launch.

That leaves part (c) in an interesting position: the numbers say €2.50, the data supporting them are shaky, and €2.50 is above the market leader. So the marketing mix has to carry the premium — the product must look worth more and the distribution must not contradict the price. Students who recommend a premium price and then suggest discount-channel distribution have not seen the connection.

Classic wrong turns: confusing contribution with profit; assuming the revenue-maximising price is also the profit-maximising one; and criticising the survey's *size* while missing its timing bias.
