Fitting four departments into one /24

Computer science HL · CS-A2.2-001

A2.1 Network fundamentals, A2.2 Network architecture (A2.1.6 the role of IP and MAC addresses, A2.2.2 the structure of a network, subnetting)P115 marksdifficulty 4DesignDetermineExplainEvaluate

One /24 and four departments

A school has been allocated the block $192.168.40.0/24$ and must divide it between four departments and two router links. The required numbers of hosts are: science $60$, humanities $28$, administration $12$, and the library $6$. In addition, two point-to-point links between the department routers each need exactly two usable addresses. Addresses are assigned from the start of the block upwards, and no two subnets may overlap.

Question

There is more than enough address space for all six requirements, but only if the block is divided unevenly. Design the division, then justify it.

  1. [5 marks](a)

    Design a subnetting scheme using variable-length subnet masks. For each of the six requirements give the network address in dotted decimal, the prefix length, and the number of usable host addresses it provides.

    (Design)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine how many addresses in the /24 block remain unused by your scheme, and compare this with the number that would remain if the block had instead been divided into four equal subnets.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain how a host uses its own address and subnet mask to decide whether a destination address is on its own subnet, and what it does if it is not.

    (Explain)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate one advantage and one disadvantage of the variable-length scheme compared with using four equal subnets, in a network that may grow.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Work downwards in size so that each block starts on a boundary its own prefix length divides. A prefix that leaves $h$ host bits provides $2^{h} - 2$ usable addresses. (M1)

| requirement | network address | prefix | usable hosts |
|---|---|---|---|
| science, needs 60 | $192.168.40.0$ | $/26$ | $62$ |
| humanities, needs 28 | $192.168.40.64$ | $/27$ | $30$ |
| administration, needs 12 | $192.168.40.96$ | $/28$ | $14$ |
| library, needs 6 | $192.168.40.112$ | $/29$ | $6$ |
| WAN link 1, needs 2 | $192.168.40.120$ | $/30$ | $2$ |
| WAN link 2, needs 2 | $192.168.40.124$ | $/30$ | $2$ |

(A1) for each correct prefix, (A1) for the complete set of addresses, (A1) for the usable-host counts. The blocks occupy $0$–$63$, $64$–$95$, $96$–$111$, $112$–$119$, $120$–$123$ and $124$–$127$ with no overlap, because each block begins at an address divisible by its own size.

(b) The six blocks consume $64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 4 = 128$ addresses, so $256 - 128 = 128$ addresses remain free for growth. (M1)(A1) Four equal subnets would each be a $/26$, giving $4 \times 64 = 256$ addresses and leaving none at all; in that scheme $62 + 30 + 14 + 6 = 112$ required hosts are spread across $248$ usable addresses, so $136$ are wasted inside the subnets, and the two WAN links would not fit. (M1)(A1)

(c) The host performs a bitwise AND between its own address and its subnet mask to obtain its network address, then does the same to the destination address using its own mask. (M1) If the two network addresses are equal, the destination is local and the host sends the frame directly, using ARP to find the destination's MAC address. (A1) If they differ, the host sends the frame to its default gateway instead, addressing the frame to the gateway's MAC address while leaving the destination IP address untouched; the gateway then routes the packet onwards. (A1)

(d) The advantage is that the variable-length scheme leaves $128$ addresses free and can absorb growth in any department by allocating from that pool, or by renumbering only the department that has outgrown its block. (A1) The disadvantage is that it is harder to manage and harder to read: the six prefixes have to be recorded in the routing table individually rather than summarised, and a technician who sees a $/29$ and a $/30$ side by side has more to hold in their head than one who sees four identical entries. (A1) If the network is expected to grow unevenly, or to need small links like these, the extra complexity is worth it; if all four departments are likely to grow at the same rate, the simpler scheme is the better choice. (A1)

A useful check on any scheme is to write the blocks out in order and confirm that each one finishes exactly where the next one begins: $0$ to $63$, $64$ to $95$, $96$ to $111$, $112$ to $119$, $120$ to $123$ and $124$ to $127$. A scheme that leaves a gap or overlaps two blocks has placed a block on a boundary its own size does not divide, and the check takes seconds.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award the method mark for allocating in descending order of size. Accept any scheme that meets every requirement without overlap, provided each block starts on a boundary divisible by its own size; a scheme that places the /27 at .96 and the /28 at .64 is invalid because a /27 cannot start at .96 and should lose the address mark even though the sizes are right. Do not require the dotted-decimal subnet masks to be written out if the prefix lengths are given. (b) Accept $128$ and any figure from $120$ to $140$ for the equal-subnet comparison depending on how the candidate treats the two WAN links, which do not fit in that scheme at all; the observation that the WAN links cannot be accommodated is worth a mark on its own. (c) The AND operation must be named; 'it compares the first three octets' is not sufficient because it is false for these prefixes. (d) Credit only advantages and disadvantages that follow from the design rather than from networking in general. That adjacency check is worth teaching explicitly, because it catches the boundary error immediately and does not depend on remembering a rule.

Why this question is hard

Subnetting is usually taught as a procedure and examined as an arithmetic exercise, and this item is built to make the procedure itself the thing under examination. The constraint that does the work is that a block of size $2^{k}$ must start at an address divisible by $2^{k}$. Students who have memorised 'work out how many host bits you need' will compute the correct prefix lengths and then place the blocks in the wrong order, putting the $/27$ at $.96$ where it cannot legally start. Allocating in descending size order avoids the problem automatically, which is why part (a) awards a method mark for it.

Part (b) is the part that justifies variable-length masks at all. Four equal $/26$ subnets consume the entire $/24$, give $62$ usable addresses to a library that needs $6$, and leave nothing for the two router links. The variable-length scheme consumes $128$ addresses and leaves $128$ free. The comparison is not subtle, but it is invisible unless someone asks for it, and a student who produces a valid scheme has still not explained why the scheme is worth producing.

Part (c) addresses a different and equally common gap. Many candidates can calculate a subnet mask and cannot say what it is for, and the honest answer is that it is the operand of a single bitwise AND. Once the mask is understood as something the host applies to both addresses to answer one yes-or-no question — is the destination local — the rest of the behaviour, including why the frame goes to the gateway while the packet keeps its original destination address, follows without being memorised. That is also why the follow-up distinction matters: the MAC address changes at every hop and the IP address does not, and students who have not separated the two will say that the router rewrites the destination.

Challenge lever
The obvious answer, four equal subnets, wastes more than half the address space, and the candidate has to notice that before being asked. The design task then forces the blocks to be allocated in descending size order and to start on boundaries that their own prefix length divides, which is the constraint students break when they place a /27 at .64 and a /26 at .96.
