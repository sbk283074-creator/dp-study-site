Five thousand patients, one queue, and a question about the fifth

Computer science HL · CS-P2B-002

Theme B: Computational thinking and problem-solvingB2.2 (Data structures: arrays, lists, stacks, queues, priority queues and trees, and the operations each supports)P216 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedCalculateCompareRecommendJustify

Triage at a district hospital

A district hospital holds a waiting list of patients who have been assessed but not yet seen. Each patient has an integer acuity score, and a larger score means more urgent. Two things happen to the list.

The first is a lookup: a clinician asks for the patient who is currently the fifth most urgent, to decide whether the department can afford to take a less urgent case first. The second is an insertion: a newly assessed patient joins the list.

The list holds 4096 patients, and the department is choosing between two implementations. The first is a binary max-heap. The second keeps the patients in a single array held in descending order of acuity, so that position 0 is the most urgent patient and position k - 1 is the k-th most urgent.

For planning purposes treat a comparison or a movement of one element as one operation, take the cost of finding the largest element in a heap as one operation, and take the cost of restoring the heap after removing or adding one element as log base 2 of 4096, which is 12 operations. Inserting into the ordered array costs on average half the array, and a comparison sort of 4096 elements costs 4096 times 12 operations.

Question

The department expects two different workloads and has not yet decided which one it will have. Workload X builds the list once and then performs 4096 lookups for the fifth most urgent patient. Workload Y performs 4096 rounds, where each round is one lookup for the fifth most urgent patient followed immediately by one insertion. You should count operations for both structures under both workloads.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the cost of one lookup for the fifth most urgent patient under each structure, and explain briefly why the two costs differ.

    (Calculate)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Calculate the total number of operations each structure needs for workload X, taking account of the cost of building the structure as well as the cost of the lookups. State which structure is cheaper and by what factor, to one decimal place.

    (Compare)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Carry out the same comparison for workload Y, and state which structure is cheaper for that workload.

    (Recommend)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    A colleague concludes from the two structures' usual descriptions that the heap must be the right choice because insertion is logarithmic rather than linear. Justify whether that reasoning is sufficient, referring to your answers in parts (b) and (c).

    (Justify)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

Part (a). In the max-heap the largest patient is at the root, but the fifth most urgent is not at any fixed position. To reach it you remove the largest patient, restore the heap, and repeat until five patients have come out; then you put the four removed patients back. Each removal costs 12 operations to restore the heap, so the lookup costs 5 x 2 x 12 = 120 operations. (M1) for the 12, (A1) for 120.

In the ordered array the patients are already in descending order, so the fifth most urgent is simply at index 4. Reading it costs 1 operation. (A1)

The costs differ because the heap is arranged by a *partial* order: it guarantees only that a parent is at least as urgent as its children, which is enough to place the single most urgent patient but says nothing about where the fifth belongs. The array is arranged by a *total* order, which places every patient, and that is what makes an arbitrary rank cheap to read. (A1)

Part (b). Workload X: 4096 lookups, each 120 operations, gives 4096 x 120 = 491 520 operations. (M1) Building the heap from 4096 patients costs 4096 operations. (A1) Total for the heap: 4096 + 491 520 = 495 616 operations.

For the ordered array, building means sorting, which costs 4096 x 12 = 49 152 operations. (A1) The 4096 lookups cost 4096 x 1 = 4096 operations. Total: 49 152 + 4096 = 53 248 operations. (M1)

The ordered array is cheaper, by 495 616 / 53 248 = 9.3 times to one decimal place. (A1)

Part (c). Workload Y: each round is a lookup followed by an insertion, and there are 4096 rounds.

For the heap, a round costs 120 + 12 = 132 operations, so the total is 4096 x 132 = 540 672 operations. (M1)(A1)

For the ordered array, a round costs 1 + 2048 = 2049 operations, because inserting into a sorted array of 4096 elements shifts half of it on average. The total is 4096 x 2049 = 8 392 704 operations. (M1) The heap is cheaper for this workload, by 8 392 704 / 540 672 = 15.5 times. (A1)

So the two structures swap places: the array wins workload X by 9.3 times and the heap wins workload Y by 15.5 times.

Part (d). The colleague's reasoning is not sufficient, because it is a statement about *insertion* alone and the two workloads do not weight insertion the same way. (M1) In workload X there are no insertions after the list is built, so the heap's logarithmic insertion is worth nothing and the array's constant-time rank lookup decides the whole comparison, which is why the array wins by 9.3 times. (A1) In workload Y every lookup is followed by an insertion, and there the linear insertion cost of the array dominates everything else, which is why the heap wins by 15.5 times. (A1) The conclusion the colleague should draw is that the heap's advantage is conditional on insertions being frequent relative to rank lookups, and the department must first know which workload it has.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for identifying that a heap lookup requires repeated removals, A1 for 120. A1 for 1 operation for the array. A1 for the partial-order versus total-order contrast. Accept any wording that conveys that the heap only orders each parent against its own children, and that this is why a specific rank is not directly addressable. A candidate who claims the fifth most urgent is at a fixed heap index forfeits the final A1.

(b) M1 for 4096 x 120, A1 for the build cost of 4096, A1 for the array's sort cost of 49 152, M1 for the array's lookup total, A1 for the 9.3 factor. Follow-through is allowed throughout: a candidate who used a different but consistent heap lookup cost keeps the method marks and the comparison A1 provided the arithmetic of the ratio is right on their own numbers.

(c) M1 for the per-round heap cost of 132 and A1 for 540 672; M1 for the per-round array cost of 2049; A1 for 8 392 704 and the correct direction of the comparison. A candidate who omits the array's average shift and uses the full 4096 loses the second M1.

(d) M1 for recognising that the claim addresses only insertion. A1 for tying the array's win in (b) to the absence of insertions. A1 for tying the heap's win in (c) to the presence of them. No credit for repeating the asymptotic descriptions without referring to the computed totals.

Accept small rounding differences in the two ratios; the direction of each comparison and the existence of the reversal are what carry the marks.

Why this question is hard

The item is built so that the standard complexity comparison is not wrong, but is incomplete in a way that changes the answer.

The textbook statement is that a heap gives logarithmic insertion and constant-time access to the maximum, while a sorted array gives linear insertion. A student who has learned that sentence has learned something true, and it is enough to answer the question the course usually asks, which is about a priority queue that only ever needs the maximum. This item asks for the *fifth* largest, and that single change moves the heap's lookup from constant to linear in the rank, which is the crack the whole question turns on.

The deeper point is about what each structure orders. A heap enforces a partial order: every parent is at least as large as its own children, and nothing more. That is exactly enough to guarantee the root is the maximum, and it is why the root is cheap and everything else is not. A sorted array enforces a total order, which costs a great deal to maintain under insertion but makes every rank addressable. Students who can say this have understood the structure rather than memorised its cost table.

The two workloads are the mechanism. Under workload X the array wins by 9.3 times; under workload Y the heap wins by 15.5 times. Neither structure is simply better, and the arithmetic is deliberately close enough that neither answer can be reached by intuition. A student who answers only from the asymptotic descriptions will get part (d) wrong in a specific and diagnosable way, which is the point of asking it.

The classic wrong turn is to treat the heap as if it were sorted, and to assume that the k-th largest is somewhere convenient. It is not: the only guarantee is that it is not an ancestor of anything larger than itself. The second wrong turn is to forget the cost of building the structure in part (b) and to compare only the lookups, which happens to preserve the direction of the answer here but not the factor.
