B.1 Ice added to warm water when the ice does not all melt

Physics HL · PHYS-B.1-201

Theme B: The particulate nature of matterB.1 Thermal energy transfers (specific heat capacity; specific latent heat of fusion; thermal equilibrium)P2 · Section B11 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedCalculateCalculateDetermineDetermine

Question

A student places $m_{\mathrm{ice}} = 0.050\ \mathrm{kg}$ of ice, initially at $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$, into $m_{w} = 0.200\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water at $10.0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ in an insulated container of negligible heat capacity. Take the specific heat capacity of water as $c_{w} = 4180\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and the specific latent heat of fusion of ice as $L_{f} = 3.34 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Calculate the thermal energy released when the water cools to $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$.

    (Calculate)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Calculate the thermal energy required to melt all of the ice at $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the final temperature of the mixture, justifying the assumption you make about the ice.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine the mass of ice that remains unmelted at equilibrium, and state what has happened to the temperature of the original water.

    (Determine)

Total: 11 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Cooling the water from $10.0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ to $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (M1)(A1):
$$Q_{\mathrm{avail}} = m_{w}c_{w}\Delta T = 0.200 \times 4180 \times 10.0 = 8.36 \times 10^{3}\ \mathrm{J}$$

(b) Melting the whole $0.050\ \mathrm{kg}$ of ice at its melting point (M1)(A1):
$$Q_{\mathrm{need}} = m_{\mathrm{ice}}L_{f} = 0.050 \times 3.34 \times 10^{5} = 1.67 \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{J}$$

(c) Compare the two energies before doing anything else: $8.36 \times 10^{3}\ \mathrm{J}$ is available and $1.67 \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{J}$ is needed, so only part of the ice can melt (R1). The mixture therefore settles at the melting point of ice, and the final temperature is
$$T_{f} = 0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C} \tag{A1}$$

The justification is that the water cannot cool below $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ while ice is still present: any further energy it released would have to come out of the ice, which is already at its melting point and cannot get colder without freezing (R1)(A1).

(d) All of the energy released by the water goes into melting ice at $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$, so (M1):
$$m_{\mathrm{melted}} = \frac{Q_{\mathrm{avail}}}{L_{f}} = \frac{8.36 \times 10^{3}}{3.34 \times 10^{5}} = 0.0250\ \mathrm{kg} \tag{A1}$$

The ice remaining is
$$m_{\mathrm{left}} = 0.050 - 0.0250 = 0.0250\ \mathrm{kg} \tag{A1}$$

The original water has cooled all the way to $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and no further; it is now in equilibrium with ice at the melting point, so the mixture consists of $0.225\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water and $0.0250\ \mathrm{kg}$ of ice, both at $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$.

The wider lesson is worth stating plainly, because this is the part that generalises. A mixture of ice and water has two possible regimes, and the numbers decide which one applies rather than the student. If the warm body holds more energy than the fusion of all the ice requires, the ice melts completely and the final temperature follows from a single equation. If it holds less, the ice survives and the temperature is pinned at the melting point instead. The algebra of the first regime is the one everybody learns, and it is also the one that gives a nonsensical answer here, so the check has to come before the equation rather than after it.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for $m c \Delta T$ with $\Delta T = 10.0$, A1 for $8.36 \times 10^{3}\ \mathrm{J}$. Accept $8.4 \times 10^{3}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(b) M1 for $m L_{f}$ with $m = 0.050$, A1 for $1.67 \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(c) The mark here is for the comparison, not for the arithmetic. A candidate who writes $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ with no comparison of $Q_{\mathrm{avail}}$ against $Q_{\mathrm{need}}$ scores at most half the marks, because the temperature is only correct for the reason that the comparison establishes. Award R1 for explicitly stating that not all the ice can melt, A1 for the temperature, and R1A1 for the argument that the water cannot go below the melting point while ice remains.

A candidate who assumes all the ice melts gets a final temperature of about $-8.0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and scores 0 for (c). That negative answer is self-evidently wrong and should be treated as a signal that the assumption was not tested, not as an arithmetic slip to be condoned.

(d) M1 for using $Q_{\mathrm{avail}}/L_{f}$ rather than $Q_{\mathrm{need}}/L_{f}$, A1 for $0.0250\ \mathrm{kg}$ melted, A1 for $0.0250\ \mathrm{kg}$ remaining. Follow-through applies from an incorrect (a).

Forfeits: using the latent heat of vaporisation; treating the water as cooling to some intermediate temperature and then solving a second equation, which double-counts the energy. Condonation: a candidate who finds the final temperature by solving the general mixture equation and then observes that the answer is below zero, and corrects it, should be given full credit if the reasoning is shown.

Why this question is hard

This item tests whether a student tests an assumption or merely applies a formula. The familiar mixture problem has a fixed recipe: equate the heat lost by the warm body to the heat gained by the cold body, solve for $T_{f}$, done. That recipe contains a hidden premise, namely that the ice melts completely, and it is a premise that is usually true in textbook problems and false here.

The numbers are chosen so that the failure is unmistakable rather than marginal. Only $8.36\ \mathrm{kJ}$ is available while $16.7\ \mathrm{kJ}$ is needed, so the recipe produces a final temperature near $-8\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$: below the melting point of ice, in a container holding ice. A student who obtains that number and writes it down has produced a result that contradicts the data given, and the whole point of the question is whether the contradiction is noticed.

The correct reasoning is a two-line energy audit performed before any algebra: compare what is available with what is needed, conclude that the ice cannot all melt, and therefore conclude that the temperature is pinned at $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ by the coexistence of ice and water. The temperature is then not something to be calculated at all; it is fixed by the physics, and the actual calculation is the mass of ice consumed. Part (d) rewards the student who understands that the $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ answer is a consequence of the energy bookkeeping rather than a lucky guess, and it is marked so that guessing $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ without the comparison does not score full marks.
