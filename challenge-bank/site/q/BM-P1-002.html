5.4 / 3.8 / 6.3 Reshoring decision with landed cost and working capital effects

Business management SL · BM-P1-002

Unit 5: Operations management5.4 (Location), 3.8 (Investment appraisal), 6.3 (STEEPLE analysis)P115 marksdifficulty 5technology: not applicableCalculateEvaluateTo what extent

Vantar

Vantar makes technical outdoor clothing. It currently manufactures 180 000 units a year in a low-wage economy and sells them online and through a small number of independent retailers at a retail price of €59. The lead time from placing an order to having stock available is 62 days, which forces Vantar to commit to its whole season's production before it knows what will sell. The operations director has costed a move to a domestic factory: the unit production cost would be higher, but the lead time would fall to 9 days, which would allow the company to make small reorders during the season. The finance director has calculated that the average value of finished-goods stock would fall from €2.10m to €0.72m, and the company's annual cost of holding stock is 18% of its value. Vantar's marketing manager estimates that 4% of units are currently sold at an average markdown of 35% because the wrong sizes were committed to. The cost data are given below.

Question

Using the case study and the cost data, answer all three parts.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the total landed cost per unit for the offshore option and for the domestic option, and calculate the increase in annual production cost if Vantar reshores.

    (Calculate)
  2. [6 marks](b)

    Evaluate whether Vantar should move production to the domestic factory, taking into account the effect on stockholding and the markdowns described in the case study.

    (Evaluate)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    To what extent do factors other than cost determine the right decision for Vantar? Refer to at least two external factors in your answer.

    (To what extent)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Offshore: $\text{CIF}=14.00+1.20=\unicode{8364}15.20$; the tariff is $12\%$ of $\unicode{8364}15.20=\unicode{8364}1.82$; adding $\unicode{8364}0.30$ handling gives a landed cost of $\unicode{8364}17.32$ per unit. (M1)(A1) Domestic: $21.50+0.25=\unicode{8364}21.75$ per unit. (A1) The increase is $\unicode{8364}4.43$ per unit, so across $180\,000$ units the annual production cost rises by $4.426\times180\,000=\unicode{8364}796\,680$, about $\unicode{8364}797\,000$. (M1)(A1)

(b) On unit cost alone the move looks wrong: $\unicode{8364}4.43$ is a $25.5\%$ increase in the cost of every item. (A1) Two effects work against that conclusion. First, average stock falls from $\unicode{8364}2.10$ m to $\unicode{8364}0.72$ m, a release of $\unicode{8364}1.38$ m, and at an annual holding cost of $18\%$ that saves $1.38\times0.18=\unicode{8364}248\,400$ a year. (M1)(A1) Second, a nine-day lead time allows reorders during the season rather than a single pre-commitment, so the markdown problem largely disappears: $4\%$ of $180\,000$ units is $7\,200$ units, currently discounted by $35\%$ of $\unicode{8364}59$, which is about $\unicode{8364}148\,700$ of lost margin a year. (M1)(A1) Together these recover about $\unicode{8364}397\,000$, so the net additional cost is roughly $\unicode{8364}797\,000-\unicode{8364}397\,000=\unicode{8364}400\,000$ a year, or $\unicode{8364}2.22$ per unit — about $3.8\%$ of the $\unicode{8364}59$ retail price, not the $25\%$ headline figure. (A1)

Against that residual cost must be set a shorter cash cycle, far less risk of being left with a season's worth of unwanted stock, and the marketing value of being able to promote a domestic supply chain. (A1) A balanced evaluation is that the move is financially defensible if Vantar can recover $\unicode{8364}2.22$ per unit through a modest price rise or through the higher full-price sell-through that reordering makes possible, and it is not defensible if the market will not bear any increase at all. The decisive question is therefore not cost but price elasticity, which the data do not give. (R1)

(c) To a considerable extent, because the cost gap after the stock and markdown effects is small enough that external factors can easily dominate it. (A1) *Political:* the offshore option depends on a $12\%$ tariff that a trade dispute could raise overnight; a five-point increase would add about $\unicode{8364}0.76$ to every unit and close a third of the remaining gap by itself, and this risk is entirely outside Vantar's control. (M1)(A1) *Environmental and ethical:* the company sells technical outdoor clothing to customers who are unusually attentive to provenance, and a domestic supply chain with a much shorter transport leg is a marketing asset that does not appear in the cost comparison at all; conversely, moving production offshore to a jurisdiction with weaker labour protections would carry reputational risk that could cost more than $\unicode{8364}400\,000$. (M1)(A1) *Economic:* a domestic factory removes exposure to freight-cost volatility and to currency movement on a $\unicode{8364}2.52$ m annual import bill. (A1) The counter-argument is that $\unicode{8364}400\,000$ a year is real money and that none of these factors is certain, so the decision remains finely balanced; but where the quantified gap is under four per cent of retail price, factors that cannot be quantified will usually decide it. (R1)

Markscheme notes

In (a) allow €17.30 to €17.35 and €21.70 to €21.80, and credit the tariff calculation shown even if the final addition is wrong. The annual increase should be about €797,000; allow €795,000 to €800,000. In (b) the evaluation mark requires the candidate to bring in both the stockholding saving and the markdown saving; an evaluation based on unit cost alone is capped at three, and one based on unit cost plus stockholding but not markdowns is capped at four. Allow a range of about €390,000 to €410,000 for the net cost, since candidates may treat the markdown recovery differently. In (c) any two of the STEEPLE factors are acceptable provided each is applied to Vantar specifically rather than described in the abstract; a generic list of external factors with no application earns at most two marks. In (b) a candidate may reasonably question the 18% holding cost or the assumption that the whole stock reduction is achieved in the first year; both are fair challenges and should be credited where they are explained, since the case study presents them as estimates rather than facts. Do not, however, credit a rejection of the markdown saving on the grounds that it is uncertain: it is quantified in the stimulus and is the whole reason the lead-time reduction has value.

Why this question is hard

The item is built around a gap between the number a student computes first and the number that matters. The unit-cost comparison — €17.32 against €21.75 — is unambiguous and points one way, and a candidate who stops there concludes that reshoring is obviously wrong. The two effects that reverse the picture are both in the stimulus rather than in the cost table: €1.38m of stock is released, worth €248,400 a year at an 18% holding cost, and 53 days of lead time are removed, which is what allows the markdown problem to be solved at all. Neither appears in a unit cost, and that is the point — a landed-cost calculation systematically excludes working capital and obsolescence, both of which can exceed the cost difference it is being used to measure. Part (c) then asks the candidate to weigh factors that cannot be quantified against a residual gap of about €2.22 per unit, and the honest answer is that a gap of under four per cent of retail price is small enough that a tariff change or a reputational effect could overturn it. That is a genuinely transferable conclusion about offshoring decisions and is why the figures were calibrated to produce it. The item also rewards candidates who notice what the data do not contain. Nothing in the case study says how price-sensitive Vantar's customers are, and the entire recommendation rests on it: if the market will absorb €2.22 then the move is straightforward, and if it will not then the move destroys value. Naming the missing variable, and saying what it would take to find it out, is a stronger answer than any amount of further arithmetic on the figures already given.
