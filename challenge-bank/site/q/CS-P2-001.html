Distributed monitoring system — architecture, transmission and schema design

Computer science HL · CS-P2-001

Theme A: Concepts of computer scienceA2.2 Network architecture, A2.3 Data transmissions, A2.4 Network security, A3.2 Database designP1 · Section B18 marksdifficulty 5technology: not applicableCalculateExplainConstructEvaluate

Northreach cold chain

Northreach is a regional health authority that distributes vaccines by road. Its fleet is 140 refrigerated vehicles. Each vehicle carries four temperature probes inside the cargo compartment, and each probe is sampled every 30 seconds throughout the ten-hour delivery window. A reading consists of a probe identifier stored in one byte, a timestamp stored in four bytes, and a temperature stored in two bytes. The authority operates on 300 days each year. Regulatory rules require that any excursion above 8 degrees Celsius is detected and an alert raised within 60 seconds of the reading being taken, and that the full temperature history for any consignment can be produced as evidence for up to six years.

Question

The authority has asked your team to design the monitoring and alerting subsystem. The parameters you need are given in the case study.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the total volume of data the fleet generates in one operating year, giving your answer in gibibytes, and state what the volume would become if each reading were instead stored as an 80-byte plain-text record.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    A junior developer proposes that each probe pushes every reading to the central server the moment it is taken, using a separate secure request per reading. Explain why this is a poor design even though the total payload is small, and explain how placing an edge device in each vehicle changes the trade-off.

    (Explain)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Construct a relational schema, in third normal form, for the part of the system that records vehicles, consignments, probes and excursion alerts. Show the tables with their primary keys, foreign keys and a sensible attribute for each, and state the relationship between consignments and alerts.

    (Construct)
  4. [5 marks](d)

    A second proposal is that each vehicle stores readings locally and uploads the whole day's file once, when it returns to the depot. Evaluate this proposal against the regulatory requirements, considering the alert deadline, the reliability of the evidence trail, and the cost of the system.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) Each probe is sampled $\dfrac{10\times3600}{30}=1200$ times per day, so each vehicle produces $4\times1200=4800$ readings per day. (M1)(A1) At 7 bytes each, the fleet produces

$$4800\times7\times140\times300=1.4112\times10^{9}\text{ bytes}$$

per year, which is $\dfrac{1.4112\times10^9}{2^{30}}=1.31$ GiB. (A1) Stored as 80-byte text the same readings occupy

$$4800\times80\times140\times300\div 2^{30}=15.0\text{ GiB},$$

roughly eleven times as much. (A1)

(b) The aggregate payload is only $140\times\dfrac{7}{30}\approx33$ bytes per second, so bandwidth is not the objection. (A1) The objection is per-request cost: each reading would carry its own transport handshake and encryption overhead, so a 7-byte reading could easily be carried inside a request of 800 bytes or more, multiplying the traffic by a factor well over a hundred and adding a fresh handshake every 30 seconds on every vehicle. (M1)(A1) Mobile coverage in a refrigerated vehicle is intermittent, and a per-reading push has no way to buffer across a dropout, so readings would simply be lost when a vehicle passes through a tunnel. (A1) An edge device in the vehicle changes the trade-off decisively: it can buffer readings locally through a coverage gap, batch them into one request every few minutes, evaluate the excursion rule locally so that an alert can be raised within seconds even with no network at all, and forward only alerts plus batched history to the centre. (R1)

(c) Four tables are needed:

VEHICLE(vehicleID PK, registration, depotID FK, commissionedDate)

CONSIGNMENT(consignmentID PK, vehicleID FK, departedAt, returnedAt, productCode)

PROBE(probeID PK, vehicleID FK, positionLabel, calibratedOn)

EXCURSION_ALERT(alertID PK, probeID FK, consignmentID FK, raisedAt, peakTemperature, durationSeconds, acknowledgedBy)

(M1)(A1)(A1) The schema is in third normal form because every non-key attribute depends on the whole of its table's key and on nothing else: probe calibration belongs to the probe, not to the alert, and the depot belongs to the vehicle, not to the consignment. (A1) The relationship between consignments and alerts is one-to-many: one consignment may generate any number of alerts, including none, and each alert belongs to exactly one consignment. (A1)

(d) The depot-upload proposal is cheap and simple, and for the storage requirement alone it would be adequate: the files arrive intact once a day and the six-year archive is straightforward to keep. (A1) It fails the alert deadline outright. A reading taken at the start of a shift would not reach the centre for up to ten hours, against a requirement of 60 seconds, and a spoiled consignment would be delivered before anyone knew. (M1)(A1) It also weakens the evidence trail: a vehicle involved in an accident, or one whose local storage fails, takes the whole day's record with it, and there is no independent copy against which a disputed reading could be checked. (A1) The correct evaluation is therefore that the proposal is acceptable only for the archive tier, not for the alerting tier. A hybrid is justified: the edge device raises alerts immediately over the mobile network, while the full-resolution history is batched opportunistically during the shift and reconciled at the depot. Alerts are small and time-critical; history is large and not. Splitting the two by their deadlines rather than by their size is the whole design. (R1) One further consideration belongs in any real design and is worth raising in an answer: the clock. Timestamps are generated on the vehicle, and if a vehicle's clock drifts then an alert can appear to precede the reading that caused it, which would undermine the evidence trail completely. Synchronising the edge device periodically and recording both the device time and the server receipt time on every alert removes that risk at negligible cost, and a candidate who spots it unprompted is thinking about the system rather than about the question.

Markscheme notes

In (a) award the method mark for the chain of multiplications even if the final conversion is wrong; allow 1.3 GiB to 1.4 GiB and 14.9 to 15.1 GiB, and condone the use of 10^9 rather than 2^30 provided the unit is stated consistently. In (b) the mark is for recognising that per-request overhead, not payload size, dominates; a candidate who simply says 'too much data' should not be credited, because the data is not large. In (c) accept any schema that reaches third normal form and states the keys; do not require the exact attribute names given here, but do require that alert data is separated from probe metadata and that both foreign keys on the alert table are present. In (d) a recommendation is required, not a list of pros and cons: award the highest mark only where the candidate separates the alerting tier from the archive tier and justifies the split by deadline rather than by volume. Candidates may reasonably propose a message queue or a publish-subscribe broker between the vehicle and the centre, and this should be credited where the alert path is separated from the bulk path. Where a candidate argues that the whole system should be edge-only with no central alerting at all, press for how a regulator would inspect the evidence; that question is usually enough to expose the gap.

Why this question is hard

This item is built around a deliberate trap. Every number the student computes in (a) comes out small — 1.31 GiB a year is nothing — and the natural conclusion is that the system is easy. The constraint that actually binds is the 60-second deadline, which has nothing to do with volume, and the six-year evidence requirement, which means the data cannot be thrown away even though it is not needed in real time. The strongest candidates notice that the two requirements point in opposite directions: alerts must leave the vehicle immediately, history must not, and any design that treats them as one stream fails one requirement or the other. Part (b) reinforces the same lesson from the transmission side, where the overhead per request dwarfs the payload. Part (c) then tests whether the student can produce a schema in which metadata is stored once rather than repeated on every alert row, which is the practical consequence of normalisation rather than a definition to be recited. A candidate who finishes (d) by proposing a single tier has missed the point of the whole case study. It is worth marking this item with an eye on whether the candidate ever states which requirement is binding. The volume arithmetic is a decoy that most candidates will do correctly and then use to justify the wrong architecture, and the ones who compute 1.31 GiB and then say 'so the design barely matters' have walked into the trap. The alert deadline and the six-year retention rule are the only two constraints that shape the system, and they pull in opposite directions, which is exactly why the tiering answer in (d) exists rather than a single correct topology.
