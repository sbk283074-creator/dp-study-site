A logarithmic inequality proved from the sign of a derivative

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.6-001

Topic 5: CalculusSL 5.6 (derivative of x^n, n in Q; derivative of sin x, cos x, e^x and ln x); AHL 5.13 (evaluation of limits)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatShow thatDetermineDetermine

Question

For $x>0$, define $\displaystyle h(x)=\ln x-\frac{2(x-1)}{x+1}$. The four parts below use the derivative of $h$ to compare $\ln x$ with a rational function of $x$, and then ask whether that comparison is worth much once $x$ is large.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that $h(1)=0$, and show that $\displaystyle h'(x)=\frac{(x-1)^{2}}{x(x+1)^{2}}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that $h'(x)\ge 0$ for every $x>0$. Hence determine, giving a reason in each case, which of $\ln x$ and $\dfrac{2(x-1)}{x+1}$ is the larger when $x>1$ and when $0<x<1$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} h(x)$, and hence comment on whether the comparison established in part (b) gives a useful bound on $\ln x$ for large values of $x$.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Hence, without using a calculator, determine which of $\ln 2$ and $\dfrac{2}{3}$ is the larger, justifying your answer.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

[4 marks] (Show that) (c) Determine $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} h(x)$, and hence comment on whether the comparison established in part (b) gives a useful bound on $\ln x$ for large values of $x$. [4 marks] (Determine) (d) Hence, without using a calculator, determine which of $\ln 2$ and $\dfrac{2}{3}$ is the larger, justifying your answer. [4 marks] (Determine)
Reveal the answer

(a) $h(1)=\ln 1-\dfrac{2(1-1)}{1+1}=0-0=0$. (A1) For the derivative, differentiate the rational term by the quotient rule:
$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{2(x-1)}{x+1}\right)=\frac{2(x+1)-2(x-1)}{(x+1)^{2}}=\frac{4}{(x+1)^{2}}$$ (M1)(A1)
so, since $\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x=\dfrac{1}{x}$, (M1)
$$h'(x)=\frac{1}{x}-\frac{4}{(x+1)^{2}}=\frac{(x+1)^{2}-4x}{x(x+1)^{2}}=\frac{x^{2}-2x+1}{x(x+1)^{2}}=\frac{(x-1)^{2}}{x(x+1)^{2}}$$ (A1)(AG)
The numerator completes to a perfect square, which is the fact the rest of the question depends on.

(b) For $x>0$ we have $x>0$ and $(x+1)^{2}>0$, so the denominator $x(x+1)^{2}$ is positive, while $(x-1)^{2}\ge 0$ for every real $x$. Hence $h'(x)\ge 0$ on the whole domain, with equality only at $x=1$. (M1)(A1) Therefore $h$ is increasing on $(0,\infty)$. (R1) Since $h(1)=0$, it follows that
$$x>1 \Rightarrow h(x)>0 \Rightarrow \ln x>\frac{2(x-1)}{x+1},\qquad 0<x<1 \Rightarrow h(x)<0 \Rightarrow \ln x<\frac{2(x-1)}{x+1}.$$ (A1)(A1)
The inequality reverses at $x=1$, and that reversal is the point: the two expressions are equal at $x=1$ and the comparison changes direction there rather than holding one way throughout.

(c) As $x\to\infty$, the rational term satisfies $\dfrac{2(x-1)}{x+1}=\dfrac{2-2/x}{1+1/x}\to 2$, whereas $\ln x\to\infty$. (M1)(A1) Hence $h(x)\to\infty$. (A1) The bound is therefore true but increasingly weak: it never exceeds $2$, so for large $x$ it says only that $\ln x$ is bigger than a quantity that has stopped growing. (R1) What makes the inequality interesting is the opposite end. At $x=1$ the two sides are equal and, since $h'(1)=0$, their first derivatives agree as well, so near $x=1$ the rational expression approximates $\ln x$ to second order and the bound is excellent; the same statement degrades steadily as $x$ moves away from $1$.

(d) Put $x=2$, which is greater than $1$, so the first case of part (b) applies: (M1)
$$\ln 2>\frac{2(2-1)}{2+1}=\frac{2}{3}.$$ (A1)
Hence $\ln 2$ is the larger of the two. (A1) No decimal value is needed, and none is available without a calculator, which is exactly why the inequality is worth having; the conclusion is rigorous even though the gap is only about $0.026$. (R1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The quotient rule must be shown rather than quoted, because the simplification to a single square is where the mark is earned. Award (A1) for $4/(x+1)^{2}$ and do not accept an unsimplified $\big[(x+1)^{2}-4x\big]$ numerator as final. (b) The sign argument must name both factors: positivity of the denominator and non-negativity of the square. A candidate who says only 'the derivative is positive' has skipped the case $x=1$ and should not receive the final mark. Award the reversal in full only if both directions are given; a candidate who states the $x>1$ case alone has done half the question. (c) Accept any correct evaluation of the limit; the comment mark is for noticing that a bound that tends to $2$ cannot track a function that tends to infinity. Credit the observation about agreement of value and gradient at $x=1$ as an alternative acceptable comment. (d) The word 'hence' is load-bearing: the mark is for substituting $x=2$ into the part (b) result, not for quoting a decimal for $\ln 2$. A candidate who writes $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$ and compares has not answered the question as set and should be credited only for the conclusion.

Why this question is hard

This question is built around a move that students meet early and rarely see used seriously: if a function is increasing and you know its value at one point, you know an inequality everywhere. Nothing here needs a turning point. The derivative of $h$ happens to be a perfect square divided by a quantity that is positive on the whole domain, so its sign is settled by inspection, and because $h(1)=0$ the sign of $h$ itself follows immediately on either side of $1$. The result is a clean comparison between a logarithm and a rational function, obtained without evaluating anything.

The detail that makes the item worth sixteen marks is the reversal. Students expect an inequality proved by monotonicity to hold in one direction throughout, and here it does not: the two expressions are equal at $x=1$ and the comparison swaps over. A candidate who establishes $h'(x)\ge 0$ and then reports a single direction has done the hard part and then thrown away the most interesting consequence. This is why part (b) asks for both cases explicitly.

Part (c) is included because a proved inequality is not automatically a useful one, and judging usefulness is a separate skill from proving. The rational bound tends to $2$, so it cannot possibly track a function that grows without limit; the inequality is exact at $x=1$, excellent nearby, and steadily less informative as $x$ grows. Part (d) then puts the inequality to work in the one situation where it is at its best: comparing $\ln 2$ with $2/3$ is a question about a number close to $1$, and it can be settled rigorously with no calculator at all. The genre is a standard one in Chinese school mathematics, where inequalities of the form $\ln x \gtrless 2(x-1)/(x+1)$ are proved by exactly this square-over-positive-denominator argument, and it transfers cleanly to IB command terms because the reasoning is short enough to write out in full.

There is a broader lesson in the structure. Proving an inequality by differentiating a carefully chosen auxiliary function is a technique that appears across the syllabus, and the auxiliary function here is chosen so that its derivative factorises as a square. Recognising that a derivative *might* do that, and choosing the function accordingly, is the transferable skill; the particular inequality is not.
