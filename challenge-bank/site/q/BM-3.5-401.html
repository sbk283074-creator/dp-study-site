3.5 Both liquidity ratios rising while the firm removes two thirds of its cash

Business management SL · BM-3.5-401

Unit 3: Finance and accountsUnit 3: 3.5 Profitability and liquidity ratio analysis (the current ratio, the acid-test ratio and the management of working capital); 3.4 Final accounts; 3.7 Cash flowP2 · Section B10 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedCalculateExplainEvaluate

Question

The figure compares three measures of one firm's liquidity before and after a decision to pay £40 000 of trade payables earlier than the agreed date, using cash held in the business. Before the payment the current ratio was 2.00 and the acid-test ratio was 1.25. After the payment the current ratio was 2.33 and the acid-test ratio was 1.33. Cash held fell from £60 000 to £20 000.

The payment reduced current assets and current liabilities by the same £40 000: cash, which is a current asset, fell by £40 000, and trade payables, which are a current liability, fell by £40 000. Inventory was not affected, and neither were trade receivables. The current ratio is current assets divided by current liabilities, and the acid-test ratio is current assets less inventory divided by current liabilities.

The finance director tells the board that both liquidity ratios have improved and that the payment has therefore strengthened the firm's liquidity position, and proposes to repeat the practice with the remaining trade payables.

(a) Using the ratios before the payment, calculate the firm's current assets, its current liabilities and the value of its inventory before the payment.

(b) Explain why the current ratio and the acid-test ratio both rise when £40 000 is paid out of cash to reduce trade payables, given that the payment removes two thirds of the firm's cash.

(c) Evaluate the finance director's claim that the payment has strengthened the firm's liquidity position, and recommend whether the practice should be repeated.

Reveal the answer

(a) Write current liabilities as L. Before the payment the current ratio is current assets divided by current liabilities, so current assets are 2L. After the payment both fall by 40 000, giving (2L - 40) / (L - 40) = 2.333, that is 2L - 40 = 2.333L - 93.33, so 0.333L = 53.33 and L = 160, in thousands of pounds (M1)(A1). Current liabilities are therefore £160 000 and current assets are £320 000 before the payment. The acid-test ratio is 1.25, so current assets less inventory is 1.25 x 160 = £200 000, and inventory is 320 - 200 = £120 000 (A1).

(b) For any ratio greater than one, subtracting the same amount from the numerator and the denominator makes the ratio larger. Writing the current assets as A and the current liabilities as L, with A greater than L, the new ratio is (A - x) / (L - x) against the old A / L, and the new one is greater whenever x is positive and A exceeds L (M1). Both ratios were above one before the payment, at 2.00 and 1.25, so both rise; this is not an economic effect but an arithmetic one, and it would happen in exactly the same way if the firm had paid the payables with borrowed money or with inventory (A1). What the ratios measure is the balance between short-term assets and short-term liabilities. Paying a liability out of cash does not change that balance: working capital, the difference between the two, stays at 320 - 160 = £160 000 before and 280 - 120 = £160 000 after. The payment changes the composition of the working capital, moving £40 000 out of cash, and leaves its size untouched, which is precisely why a ratio of the two totals does not move in the direction the cash movement would suggest (A1).

(c) The claim confuses a measure with the thing being measured. The arithmetic of part (b) shows that the ratios rise mechanically whenever a liability is settled out of cash and the ratio is above one, and the working capital is unchanged at £160 000, so the movement in the ratios carries no information about the firm's position at all (A1). What does carry information is the composition of the liquid resources, and it has deteriorated sharply: cash falls from £60 000 to £20 000, a reduction of 67 per cent, and quick assets, the current assets less inventory that the acid-test ratio compares against current liabilities, fall from £200 000 to £160 000, a reduction of 20 per cent. So the acid-test ratio rose 6.7 per cent while the quick assets it compares fell 20 per cent, and the current ratio rose 16.7 per cent while the cash the firm holds fell 67 per cent (A1). The firm has not improved its ability to meet an unexpected payment; it has reduced it, and it has done so while the two indicators that a lender would look at moved in the opposite direction. The practice should not be repeated (A1). Paying a supplier before the agreed date costs the firm the use of the money for the period of the credit it had been granted, and nothing in the information given states a settlement discount in return, so the firm is giving up a benefit and receiving none. The recommendation is to pay on the agreed dates unless the supplier offers a discount that exceeds the cost of the funds, to hold cash at a level tied to the working-capital cycle rather than to a ratio target, and if the board wants to measure the position properly, to report the cash balance and the quick assets alongside the ratios rather than instead of them (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for setting up the equation in one unknown from the before and after current ratios; (A1) for £160 000 of current liabilities and £320 000 of current assets; (A1) for £120 000 of inventory from the acid-test ratio. Accept candidates who find the liabilities by trial as long as the resulting ratios are checked; a candidate who assumes current liabilities of 100 and reports a current ratio of 2.67 after the payment has not used the second ratio and scores nothing.

(b) (M1) for the algebraic argument that subtracting a constant from numerator and denominator of a fraction greater than one increases it, with A greater than L stated; (A1) for the observation that working capital is unchanged at £160 000 before and after; (A1) for the conclusion that the ratios measure the balance between the two totals rather than the size of the liquid resources. Do not accept 'the ratios are wrong' or 'the ratios are misleading in this case' unaccompanied by the reason: the ratios are computed correctly and behave exactly as ratios above one must.

(c) (A1) for identifying that the ratio movement is mechanical and therefore uninformative, with the unchanged working capital cited; (A1) for the quantitative deterioration, using at least two of the cash fall from £60 000 to £20 000, the quick-asset fall from £200 000 to £160 000, and the percentage comparison between the two; (A1) for the recommendation not to repeat the practice; (A1) for a reasoned basis, such as the loss of the credit period, the absence of any stated settlement discount, or the proposal to report cash alongside the ratios. A candidate who recommends repetition because the ratios improved has failed the part; a candidate who recommends repetition but identifies the loss of the credit period as a cost to be weighed earns the recommendation mark only if the position is judged to be adequately protected, which the 67 per cent fall in cash does not support.

Why this question is hard

This item is about a specific and very common analytical error: treating a movement in an indicator as evidence about the thing the indicator is supposed to measure. The current ratio and the acid-test ratio are both legitimate measures and both are computed correctly here, and both rise. The rise is not a mistake, and it is not a quirk of this firm; it follows from the arithmetic of any fraction greater than one. So the candidate cannot dismiss the movement as an error and has to explain it, which means working out what the ratios actually compare.

What they compare, in both cases, is a total of short-term assets against a total of short-term liabilities. Settling a payable out of cash reduces both totals by the same amount, so the comparison improves even though nothing has been created. The cleanest way to see that nothing has been created is the working capital, which is unchanged at £160 000: the firm's short-term funding position is exactly what it was, and only the labels on the assets have changed. That single number is worth deriving in part (b) precisely because it stops the discussion dead.

The judgement in part (c) is the part that cannot be reached by arithmetic alone, and it is the reason the item is claimed at difficulty 5. The firm has removed two thirds of its cash, and the acid-test ratio describes the firm as having moved from 1.25 to 1.33 — a 6.7 per cent improvement in a period in which the assets that ratio measures fell 20 per cent. A finance director reading only the ratio sees improvement; a supplier reading only the cash balance sees a firm that has gone from comfortably liquid to thin. Both readings are of the same accounts, and the professional task is to say which question is being asked. If the question is whether the firm's assets still exceed its liabilities, the ratios answer it and the answer is favourable. If the question is whether the firm can absorb a late customer payment or an unexpected bill, the ratios do not answer it and the cash balance does.

The general lesson is a caution about ratio analysis that is stated in every syllabus and rarely made concrete. Ratios are comparative, so they can improve while the business deteriorates, and the deterioration is invisible unless the absolute figures are quoted next to them. The proposed repetition of the practice is the dangerous part: one early payment of £40 000 leaves £20 000 of cash, and a second leaves nothing while both ratios rise again. It is worth noting that a firm can be made to look steadily stronger on these two measures all the way to insolvency, which is why lenders ask for the cash-flow forecast as well as the ratios, and why the answer to part (c) is a recommendation about what to report and not only about what to do.

Challenge lever
Both ratios improve and the firm is weaker, and the two facts are not in conflict because a ratio is a comparison and not a quantity. Subtracting the same amount from the numerator and the denominator of a ratio above one always raises it, so paying a liability out of cash raises both the current ratio and the acid test mechanically, and the working capital is unchanged at 160 000, which means the arithmetic has measured nothing at all about the firm's position. What has changed is the composition of the firm's liquid resources: cash falls from 60 000 to 20 000 and quick assets fall from 200 000 to 160 000, so the acid-test ratio rises 6.7 per cent while the assets it compares fall 20 per cent. The judgement the candidate has to reach is that the ratios are the wrong instrument for the question being asked, and that a firm whose ratios look better precisely as its buffer disappears is being described by its indicators as stronger at the moment it becomes more fragile.
