1.13 A finite sum of sines obtained by summing a geometric progression of complex numbers and taking imaginary parts, with the integral of k recovered as a limit

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.13-301

Topic 1: Number and algebraAHL 1.13 (modulus-argument (polar) form) with SL 1.3 (geometric sequences and series)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatDetermineDetermineDetermine

Question

Let $n$ be a positive integer and let $\theta$ be measured in radians. The sum
$$C = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \cos(k\theta) \quad\text{and}\quad S = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \sin(k\theta)$$
are to be evaluated in closed form. Note that the terms of each sum form a geometric progression when written in an appropriate form, and that $\theta$ is not assumed to be small. For parts (b) and (d) take $n = 100$ and $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Writing $z = e^{i\theta}$, show that $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} z^{k} = \frac{z(1 - z^{n})}{1 - z}$, and hence, using $1 - e^{i\phi} = -2i\,e^{i\phi/2}\sin\frac{\phi}{2}$, show that
    $$\sum_{k=1}^{n} z^{k} = e^{i(n+1)\theta/2}\,\frac{\sin\frac{n\theta}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta}{2}} .$$
    Deduce that $\displaystyle S = \frac{\sin\frac{n\theta}{2}\,\sin\frac{(n+1)\theta}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta}{2}}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the exact value of $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{100} \sin\Bigl(\frac{k\pi}{3}\Bigr)$, and verify your answer by using the fact that the terms repeat with period six.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine $\displaystyle\lim_{\theta \to 0}\ \frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{k=1}^{n} \sin(k\theta)$, and hence recover the well-known result for $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} k$. State your answer for $n = 100$.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    By taking the real part of the result in part (a), determine a closed form for $C$, and hence determine the exact value of $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{100} \cos\Bigl(\frac{k\pi}{3}\Bigr)$.

    (Determine)
  5. [2 marks](e)

    Explain why neither closed form is valid when $\theta$ is an integer multiple of $2\pi$, and state the value of each of $C$ and $S$ in that case.

    (Explain)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) With $z = e^{i\theta}$, the terms form a geometric progression with first term $z$ and common ratio $z$, so for $z \neq 1$
$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} z^{k} = \frac{z(1 - z^{n})}{1 - z} \;(M1)(A1).$$
Applying $1 - e^{i\phi} = -2i\,e^{i\phi/2}\sin\tfrac{\phi}{2}$ to the numerator with $\phi = n\theta$ and to the denominator with $\phi = \theta$,
$$\frac{1 - z^{n}}{1 - z} = \frac{-2i\,e^{in\theta/2}\sin\frac{n\theta}{2}}{-2i\,e^{i\theta/2}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}} = e^{i(n-1)\theta/2}\,\frac{\sin\frac{n\theta}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta}{2}} \;(M1),$$
and multiplying by the leading factor of $z = e^{i\theta}$,
$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} z^{k} = e^{i(n+1)\theta/2}\,\frac{\sin\frac{n\theta}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta}{2}} \;(A1),$$
as required. Writing the left-hand side as $\sum \cos(k\theta) + i\sum\sin(k\theta)$ and taking imaginary parts of the right-hand side, whose multiplier is the real number $\sin\frac{n\theta}{2}/\sin\frac{\theta}{2}$,
$$S = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\sin(k\theta) = \frac{\sin\frac{n\theta}{2}\,\sin\frac{(n+1)\theta}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta}{2}} \;(A1).$$
(b) With $n = 100$ and $\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{3}$, we have $\tfrac{n\theta}{2} = \tfrac{50\pi}{3}$ and $\tfrac{(n+1)\theta}{2} = \tfrac{101\pi}{6}$. Now $\tfrac{50\pi}{3} = 16\pi + \tfrac{2\pi}{3}$, so $\sin\tfrac{50\pi}{3} = \sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$; and $\tfrac{101\pi}{6} = 16\pi + \tfrac{5\pi}{6}$, so $\sin\tfrac{101\pi}{6} = \sin\tfrac{5\pi}{6} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ (M1). Hence
$$S = \frac{\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}}{\sin\tfrac{\pi}{6}} = \frac{\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}}{\tfrac{1}{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \;(A1).$$
Verification by periodicity: the values $\sin\tfrac{k\pi}{3}$ repeat with period six, and over one period
$$\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + 0 - \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} - \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + 0 = 0 .$$
Since $100 = 16 \times 6 + 4$, only the first four terms of a fresh period survive, namely $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + 0 - \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ (A1), which agrees.
(c) From the closed form, using $\sin u \sim u$ as $u \to 0$,
$$\frac{1}{\theta}\,S = \frac{1}{\theta}\cdot\frac{\sin\frac{n\theta}{2}\,\sin\frac{(n+1)\theta}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta}{2}} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{\theta}\cdot\frac{\tfrac{n\theta}{2}\cdot\tfrac{(n+1)\theta}{2}}{\tfrac{\theta}{2}} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} \;(M1)(A1),$$
the factors of $\theta$ cancelling exactly. Independently, since $\sin(k\theta) \sim k\theta$ as $\theta \to 0$, the same limit is by definition $\sum_{k=1}^{n} k$, so
$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} \;(A1),$$
which for $n = 100$ gives $\tfrac{100 \times 101}{2} = 5050$.
(d) Taking real parts in part (a) instead, $C = \Re\bigl(e^{i(n+1)\theta/2}\bigr)\,\frac{\sin\frac{n\theta}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta}{2}}$ with the real factor carried through unchanged, since only the exponential is complex $(M1)$. Because $\Re(e^{i\alpha}) = \cos\alpha$ with $\alpha = \tfrac{(n+1)\theta}{2}$,
$$C = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\cos(k\theta) = \frac{\sin\frac{n\theta}{2}\,\cos\frac{(n+1)\theta}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta}{2}} \;(A1).$$
With $n = 100$ and $\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{3}$ we use $\sin\tfrac{50\pi}{3} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ as before, together with $\cos\tfrac{101\pi}{6} = \cos\tfrac{5\pi}{6} = -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \;(A1)$, giving
$$C = \frac{\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot\bigl(-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\bigr)}{\tfrac{1}{2}} = \frac{-\tfrac{3}{4}}{\tfrac{1}{2}} = -\tfrac{3}{2} \;(A1).$$
(Over one period the cosines sum to $\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} - 1 - \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} + 1 = 0$, and the four surviving terms give $\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} - 1 - \tfrac{1}{2} = -\tfrac{3}{2}$, in agreement.)
(e) Both closed forms divide by $\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}$, which vanishes exactly when $\tfrac{\theta}{2}$ is an integer multiple of $\pi$, that is when $\theta$ is an integer multiple of $2\pi$ (M1). In that case every term is $\cos(2\pi m k) = 1$ and $\sin(2\pi m k) = 0$, so $C = n$ and $S = 0$; both numerators vanish too, so the forms give the indeterminate $0/0$ rather than the answers (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for quoting or deriving the finite geometric sum, (A1) for the correct expression $\frac{z(1-z^{n})}{1-z}$, (A1) for applying the half-angle identity to both numerator and denominator with the two different values of $\phi$ and deducing the closed form for $S$. Do not accept a candidate who writes $1 - e^{i\phi} = -2ie^{i\phi/2}\sin(\phi/2)$ without deriving or at least quoting its source: the identity is supplied in the stem but using it correctly, with $\phi$ taking the two different values $n\theta$ and $\theta$, is the substantive step. Follow-through applies downstream from any error confined to the exponent of the exponential factor, provided part (b) is answered consistently with it.
(b) (M1) for reducing the two angles modulo $2\pi$ correctly, (A1) for $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, (A1) for the independent periodicity check. The check is not optional decoration: a candidate whose closed form is wrong will generally find that the two methods disagree, and the disagreement is the intended diagnostic. Accept either order of working.
(c) (M1) for replacing sines of small arguments by their arguments, (A1) for $\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$, (A1) for $5050$ when $n = 100$. Accept a candidate who reaches $\sum k = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$ from the second route alone, but only the numerical value earns a mark if the limit has not been connected to either expression. The cancellation of $\theta$ must be exhibited at least once; 'the limit is obviously the sum of the first n integers' without showing the small-angle replacement scores at most one.
(d) (M1) for recognising that the real part is required and identifying the correct right-hand side, (A1) for the closed form, (A1) for evaluating $\sin\tfrac{50\pi}{3}$ and $\cos\tfrac{101\pi}{6}$, (A1) for $-\tfrac{3}{2}$. Note that the only change from part (b) is $\sin \to \cos$ on the second numerator factor; a candidate who carries the sine across unchanged has not taken the real part at all and scores nothing here.
(e) (M1) for identifying the vanishing denominator and its condition, (A1) for $C = n$ and $S = 0$. Condoned: omitting the remark about $0/0$, provided both values are stated. Do not accept 'the formulae give infinity': the numerators vanish simultaneously and the forms are undefined rather than divergent.

Why this question is hard

The idea that makes this work is that a trigonometric sum is the shadow of a geometric progression. Once $\sin(k\theta)$ is read as $\operatorname{Im} e^{ik\theta}$, the hundred terms stop being a hundred terms: they are one geometric progression of ratio $e^{i\theta}$, summable in a line by the formula the candidate has known since Topic 1.

That reduces the problem, and then raises the real one. The closed form you get from the geometric formula is a quotient of complex exponentials, which is not an answer anyone can read. Converting it into sines requires the identity $1 - e^{i\phi} = -2i\,e^{i\phi/2}\sin(\phi/2)$, and nothing about the question suggests reaching for it. It works by factoring out the half-angle exponential so that what remains is $e^{-i\phi/2} - e^{i\phi/2}$, which is $-2i\sin(\phi/2)$ by Euler's relation. The move is worth learning precisely because it is unmotivated: it is the standard way to turn differences of unit complex numbers into real quantities, and once seen it recurs.

Notice what the final answer does not contain. The magnitude factor is the ratio of two sines, one evaluated at $n\theta/2$ and one at $\theta/2$; the rapid oscillation is carried entirely by the exponential, and disappears on taking real or imaginary parts into a single sine or cosine at $(n+1)\theta/2$. So the sum of a hundred oscillating terms has been compressed into a product of three sines. Nothing in standard trigonometry does that in one move.

Part (c) is included because a closed form that cannot be checked against something known is a formula rather than knowledge. Dividing by $\theta$ and letting $\theta \to 0$ turns every sine into its argument, all factors of $\theta$ cancel, and out comes $\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$ -- the same result as taking the limit term by term. Two independent derivations of one familiar fact is real verification, and it also confirms that no factor of two has been dropped anywhere in the algebra.

Finally, part (e) matters more than its two marks suggest. A formula with $\sin(\theta/2)$ in the denominator is not a formula at $\theta = 2\pi m$; it is an indeterminate form. Recognising where a derived result stops being valid is the habit that separates someone who has used a tool from someone who has understood it.
