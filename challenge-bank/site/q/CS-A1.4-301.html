Choosing a translation strategy from a three-way cost model, where the compiler and the interpreter cross at a value of N that is never on the cheapest frontier

Computer science HL · CS-A1.4-301

Theme A: Concepts of computer scienceA1.4 Translation (HL only) (A1.4.1 evaluate the translation processes of interpreters and compilers, including bytecode interpreters, and the applicability of each approach to cross-platform development)P1 · Section A13 marksdifficulty 5technology: not applicableDetermineStateExplainEvaluate

Question

A laboratory writes a data-reduction tool that has to run on the three operating systems its collaborators use. The team can distribute the tool in any of three forms. In form A the source is compiled ahead of time into a native executable, and one executable has to be built and tested for each of the three systems. In form B the source is shipped as it stands and translated every time the tool is run. In form C the source is compiled once, on the laboratory's own machine, into bytecode, and that single bytecode build is shipped to all three systems and executed there by a bytecode interpreter.

The figure gives the total cost of each form, in arbitrary cost units, as a function of N, the number of times the tool is executed in total across all three systems. A dashed line marks the number of executions the team currently expects. The costs are the whole cost of owning each form: translation, distribution and execution.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Determine the value of N at which forms B and C have the same total cost, and state which of the two forms is cheaper immediately below that value and which is cheaper immediately above it. Show the equation you solved.

    (Determine)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the value of N at which forms C and A have the same total cost. Hence state, for each of the three forms, the range of N over which that form is the cheapest of the three. Where a boundary of your range is a tie rather than a strict win, say so.

    (Determine)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    The team's current estimate is the value of N marked on the figure. Determine the total cost of each of the three forms at that estimate, and state the saving the cheapest form makes against the next cheapest. A colleague recommends form A for every project because it has the lowest cost per execution. Explain why a comparison of forms A and B on their own would have supported that recommendation at this estimate, and state the smallest number of executions at which the recommendation would actually change. Reach a judgement on which form the team should ship.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 13 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The figure gives the three cost models directly: form A is $1200 + 4N$, form B is $20N$, and form C is $240 + 8N$, all in the same cost units. Forms B and C are equal when $20N = 240 + 8N$, so $12N = 240$ and $N = 20$ (M1). At that value both cost $400$ (A1). Immediately below 20 the coefficient of $N$ is smaller for B than for C, so B is cheaper: at $N = 19$, B costs $380$ and C costs $392$. Immediately above 20, C is cheaper: at $N = 21$, B costs $420$ and C costs $408$ (A1).

(b) Forms C and A are equal when $240 + 8N = 1200 + 4N$, so $4N = 960$ and $N = 240$ (M1). At that value both cost $2160$ (A1).

The three ranges are found by taking the lower of the three costs at each N, not by listing the pairwise crossovers.

  • Form B is the cheapest for $N \leq 19$. At $N = 20$ B and C are tied at $400$.
  • Form C is the cheapest for $21 \leq N \leq 239$. At $N = 240$ C and A are tied at $2160$.
  • Form A is the cheapest for $N \geq 241$. At $N = 241$, A costs $2164$ and C costs $2168$ (A1).

Two of the three boundaries are therefore ties rather than wins (A1).

(c) At the estimate marked on the figure, $N = 100$. Form A costs $1200 + 400 = 1600$; form B costs $2000$; form C costs $240 + 800 = 1040$ (M1). Form C is the cheapest, and the saving against the next cheapest, which is form A, is $1600 - 1040 = 560$ cost units (A1).

The colleague's recommendation is not unreasonable when only A and B are considered. Forms A and B are equal when $1200 + 4N = 20N$, so $N = 75$, and A is cheaper than B for every $N$ above 75 (M1). At an estimate of 100, which is above 75, a comparison of those two forms alone therefore recommends A, and the recommendation is supported by the arithmetic of that comparison. It is still the wrong recommendation, because the question is not which of A and B is cheaper but which of the three is cheapest. At $N = 75$ form A and form B both cost $1500$, while form C costs $840$ (A1). The crossover at 75 is a genuine crossover, and it is not on the cheapest frontier: at every value of N the frontier passes through, form C is below both of the strategies that cross there. Reporting the crossover at 75 as a boundary of the answer puts a segment into the frontier that does not exist, and it does so in the direction that looks most plausible, because A really is cheaper than B above 75 (A1).

The recommendation changes at $N = 241$, which is the smallest number of executions for which form A is the cheapest of the three (A1). Below that, form C is cheaper. So the estimate would have to be wrong by more than a factor of two, in the direction of more executions, before the colleague's recommendation became the right one.

The judgement is that the team should ship form C at the current estimate (A1). Form C costs $1040$ against $1600$ for form A, a saving of $560$ cost units, or thirty-five per cent of form A's total. It also keeps the property that made form A attractive in the first place, since a single bytecode build is produced once and then runs on all three systems, so no per-system build or test effort is needed. The judgement is not unconditional: it holds while the estimate stays below 241 executions. If the tool is later executed far more often than expected, the per-execution cost of $8$ for form C against $4$ for form A becomes decisive, and form A becomes the cheaper choice. A recommendation that is not attached to the estimate it was made for is not a recommendation, it is a preference (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) 3 marks. One mark for setting $20N = 240 + 8N$ and reaching $N = 20$. One mark for stating that B is cheaper below 20 and C is cheaper above it. One mark for a justification that uses the per-execution coefficients rather than a restatement of the figure.

(b) 4 marks. One mark for solving $240 + 8N = 1200 + 4N$ to $N = 240$. One mark for the range over which C is cheapest, which must run from 21 to 239. One mark for the range over which A is cheapest, which must begin at 241 and not at 240. One mark for recognising that $N = 20$ and $N = 240$ are ties, so both boundaries are stated as equal costs rather than as wins. A candidate who writes 'A is cheapest for $N > 240$' has the right boundary and should keep the mark, provided the ranges do not overlap.

A candidate who reports three ranges taken from the three pairwise crossovers, that is B below 20, C from 21 to 75 and A above 76, has computed every crossover correctly and has still produced a frontier that does not exist. Award at most two of the four marks and read part (c) before deciding the second.

(c) 6 marks. One mark for the three totals at $N = 100$: 1600, 2000 and 1040. One mark for the saving of 560 against the next cheapest, which is A and not B. One mark for locating the A and B crossover at 75 and showing that it is above the estimate. One mark for the observation that form C undercuts both strategies at that crossover, which is what removes the segment from the frontier. One mark for the change point at 241. One mark for a judgement on form C that is attached to the estimate, and that names the condition under which it would change. A bare 'ship form C because it is cheaper' earns the first four marks and not the last two.

Accept a candidate who notes that the figure's costs are the whole cost of owning each form, so the per-system build effort is already inside form A's fixed cost and must not be added again.

A candidate who answers part (c) with 'form A, because 100 is greater than 75' has answered the two-form question. This is the trap, and it must not be credited as an evaluation of the three-form choice.

Why this question is hard

This item is about the difference between a comparison and an optimisation. Any two linear cost models cross once, so there are three crossovers among three forms: A and B at 75, B and C at 20, and A and C at 240. Each is real arithmetic and each can be found by setting the two expressions equal. The cheapest form at a given N, however, is the minimum of the three, and a crossover between two strategies is only a boundary of that minimum if neither of them is beaten by the third at that value. At $N = 75$ the third form costs $840$ while the two that cross both cost $1500$, so the boundary at 75 is not a boundary of the minimum at all.

The A and B crossover is the trap because it is the one a candidate is most likely to compute. The colleague's claim in part (c) is written to invite exactly that computation: 'the lowest cost per execution' is true of form A, the comparison with form B is the one the claim invites, and at an estimate of 100 the comparison gives the colleague the right answer. Everything the candidate is told points at A, and the figure contradicts it.

The item also carries the two tie points, at $N = 20$ and $N = 240$, where two forms cost exactly the same. They matter because they fix the boundaries of the ranges: C is cheapest from 21 and not from 20, and A is cheapest from 241 and not from 240. A candidate who writes the boundaries as strict inequalities in the wrong direction creates a gap or an overlap at each tie, and part (b) asks for the ties to be named so that this is visible in the answer rather than hidden in it.

Finally, the item is an evaluation rather than a calculation because the arithmetic does not by itself say which form to ship. Form C is cheaper at the estimate and it preserves the single-build property that form A was chosen for; form A is cheaper per execution and wins if the estimate is badly wrong. The judgement mark is for attaching the recommendation to the estimate it was made for, which is the same discipline the guide asks for when it requires the use-cases of each translation approach to be considered.

Adaptation
Three changes. Context: a cross-platform distribution decision, in which the three forms differ in where the translation happens and how many build artefacts are produced, rather than an exercise that compares two translation strategies on a single machine. Given and asked: the three cost models are given only inside a figure, and the candidate is asked for the range over which each form is cheapest rather than for a single crossover. Reasoning chain: the final part requires the candidate to reject a pairwise comparison that the data supports, which is only possible once the three-way frontier has been separated from the three pairwise crossovers.
Verification
The three cost models are evaluated at every integer N from 0 to 400 and the argmin is taken with ties retained, so the frontier is enumerated rather than derived; the two tie points are then confirmed by solving each pair directly. The totals at the estimate are recomputed from the models and checked against the figure's coefficients.
