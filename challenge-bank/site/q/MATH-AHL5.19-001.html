A Maclaurin expansion whose cubic term vanishes

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.19-001

AHL 5.19 (Maclaurin series to obtain expansions for composite functions), AHL 5.13 (evaluation of limits of the form 0/0)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatDetermineStateHence

Question

Let $f(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\sin x}$.

  1. [5 marks](a)

    Using the Maclaurin expansions of $\mathrm{e}^{u}$ and $\sin x$, show that the Maclaurin expansion of $f$ begins $f(x)=1+x+\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}+a x^{3}-\dfrac{x^{4}}{8}+\cdots$ and state the value of $a$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the coefficient of $x^{5}$ in the Maclaurin expansion of $f$.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Hence state the values of $f'''(0)$ and $f''''(0)$, justifying briefly how the expansion gives them.

    (State)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Hence determine $\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{\sin x}-1-x}{x^{2}}$.

    (Hence)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

$\sin x=x-\dfrac{x^{3}}{6}+\dfrac{x^{5}}{120}-\cdots$ and $\mathrm{e}^{u}=1+u+\dfrac{u^{2}}{2}+\dfrac{u^{3}}{6}+\dfrac{u^{4}}{24}+\cdots$ (M1)

With $u=\sin x$: $u^{2}=x^{2}-\dfrac{x^{4}}{3}+\cdots$, $u^{3}=x^{3}+\cdots$, $u^{4}=x^{4}+\cdots$ (M1)

Substituting and collecting like powers: $1+\left(x-\dfrac{x^{3}}{6}\right)+\left(\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}-\dfrac{x^{4}}{6}\right)+\dfrac{x^{3}}{6}+\dfrac{x^{4}}{24}$ (M1)

The $x^{3}$ terms are $-\dfrac{1}{6}+\dfrac{1}{6}=0$, and the $x^{4}$ terms are $-\dfrac{1}{6}+\dfrac{1}{24}=-\dfrac{1}{8}$, so
$$f(x)=1+x+\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}-\dfrac{x^{4}}{8}+\cdots$$(A1)

hence $a=0$. (A1)

(b) Only three powers of $u$ contribute to $x^{5}$: the $x^{5}$ term of $u$ itself, of $u^{3}/6$, and of $u^{5}/120$. (M1)

$u^{3}=\left(x-\dfrac{x^{3}}{6}\right)^{3}+\cdots=x^{3}-3x^{2}\cdot\dfrac{x^{3}}{6}+\cdots=x^{3}-\dfrac{x^{5}}{2}+\cdots$, so $\dfrac{u^{3}}{6}$ contributes $-\dfrac{1}{12}$; $u^{5}=x^{5}+\cdots$ so $\dfrac{u^{5}}{120}$ contributes $+\dfrac{1}{120}$. (M1)

The $x^{5}$ term of $u$ is $+\dfrac{1}{120}$ and $u^{2}$, $u^{4}$ contribute nothing. (A1)

Coefficient $=\dfrac{1}{120}-\dfrac{1}{12}+\dfrac{1}{120}=\dfrac{2-20+2}{240}=-\dfrac{16}{240}=-\dfrac{1}{15}$ (A1)

(c) If $f(x)=\sum \dfrac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}x^{n}$ then the coefficient of $x^{n}$ equals $\dfrac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}$. (M1)

The coefficient of $x^{3}$ is $0$, so $f'''(0)=3!\times 0=0$. (A1)

The coefficient of $x^{4}$ is $-\dfrac{1}{8}$, so $f''''(0)=24\times\left(-\dfrac{1}{8}\right)=-3$. (A1)

(d) Writing $\mathrm{e}^{\sin x}-1-x=\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}-\dfrac{x^{4}}{8}+\cdots$ (M1) and dividing by $x^{2}$ gives $\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{x^{2}}{8}+\cdots$ (A1), so the limit is $\dfrac{1}{2}$. (A1)

The vanishing cubic term is what makes the limit finite and non-zero: had $a\neq 0$ the quotient would still have a term in $x$ and would diverge.

Markscheme notes

Award (M1) for each of the two series substitutions and once more for collecting terms; an answer that differentiates $f$ three or four times to reach the same coefficients is fully acceptable and should be credited with (M1)(M1)(M1) for the differentiation chain, provided the product and chain rules are handled. Condonation: a candidate who writes $u^{2}=x^{2}-x^{4}/3$ without justification keeps the (M1); the term in $x^{6}$ is not required. In (b), follow-through applies: a candidate with the wrong $u^{3}$ still earns the final (A1) for a consistent sum of three contributions. The most common error is omitting the $u^{5}/120$ contribution, which alone gives $-1/12+1/120=-9/120$; this loses the last (A1) but keeps both (M1). In (c) do not accept a bare 'because the coefficient is zero' without the $n!$ relationship. In (d), accepting $1/2$ with no working after part (a) earns the (A1) only. Two further condonations. A candidate who states $a=0$ in (a) and then uses $f'''(0)=0$ in (c) without recomputing the coefficient has still demonstrated the required link, so the (R1) is earned; the (A1) in (c) depends only on reading the two coefficients off the series correctly. And in (d) a candidate who reaches $1/2$ by substituting the truncated series rather than by L'Hôpital should be credited equally, since the expansion is the intended route.

Why this question is hard

The lever is that the expansion of a composite function is not obtained by differentiating the composite, but by substituting one series into another and multiplying series together. Students who try to differentiate $\mathrm{e}^{\sin x}$ four times meet the product rule applied to a growing product of trigonometric factors, and the work collapses. The composition route makes the essential difficulty visible instead: every power of $u$ is itself a series, so each coefficient of the final expansion receives contributions from several different powers of $u$, and the account has to be kept accurately.

The cancellation at $x^{3}$ is the reason the question is built around $\sin x$ rather than $x$. The $-x^{3}/6$ inside $u$ is exactly cancelled by the $x^{3}/6$ that arrives from $u^{3}/6$, and a student who keeps only the leading term of $u$ — writing $u\approx x$ and stopping — will report an $x^{3}$ coefficient of zero for the wrong reason, then get the $x^{5}$ coefficient wrong as well. The classic wrong turn is to treat $\mathrm{e}^{\sin x}$ as $\mathrm{e}^{x}$, giving $1+x+x^{2}/2+x^{3}/6$, which is the familiar expansion and is wrong here.

The question also tests whether the relationship between a Maclaurin coefficient and the value of a derivative is understood as a two-way street. Part (c) is trivial once that relationship is clear and impossible otherwise. Part (d) is the payoff: a limit that would need three applications of l'Hopital's rule, and that a student cannot evaluate from the derived derivatives without more differentiation, collapses in two lines once the expansion is known.

Metadata

Challenge lever
The expansion has to be built by composing two series rather than by differentiating, and the cubic coefficient cancels only if every contribution to it is tracked correctly; the last part then demands the same bookkeeping one order higher, where contributions arrive from three separate powers of u = sin x.

Adaptation
The device of a composite Maclaurin expansion with a cancelling term is standard in the STEP/Further Mathematics tradition rather than in IB papers, which usually ask only for the expansion of a single standard function. The choice of f(x) = e^(sin x), the vanishing cubic term, the derivative-recovery part and the final limit are all constructed here; the arithmetic was checked with an exact rational power-series computation.
Verification
The expansion was recomputed exactly with rational-arithmetic power-series composition: coefficients of x^0..x^7 are 1, 1, 1/2, 0, -1/8, -1/15, -1/240, 1/90. Derivatives follow as n! times coefficient: f'''(0)=0, f''''(0)=-3.
