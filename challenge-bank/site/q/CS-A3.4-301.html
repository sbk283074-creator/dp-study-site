Reading an append-only load table whose rows supersede one another, where the two natural wrong totals err in opposite directions

Computer science HL · CS-A3.4-301

Theme A: Concepts of computer scienceA3.4 Alternative databases and data warehouses (HL only) (A3.4.2 explain the primary objectives of data warehouses in data management and business intelligence, including the roles of append-only data, time-variant data and non-volatile data, and the role of data optimized for query performance)P1 · Section A13 marksdifficulty 5technology: not applicableStateDetermineExplainEvaluate

Question

A retail group loads its sales data into a warehouse every night. The warehouse never updates a row and never deletes one: each night's load appends new rows, and the table's whole history is still present. The same product can appear in more than one night's load, and more than one source system can report on the same product in the same night.

Two rules govern how the table is read. A row supersedes any earlier row that carries the same product and the same source, so only the newest row for a given product and source is current. Rows from different sources are not alternatives to one another and are added together, because they describe different parts of the same sale. The table is shown in the figure, together with the night on which each row was written.

The operations director has asked for the current total units and the current total revenue, and has been given two different answers by two analysts.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    State the number of rows the table holds, the number of distinct products it holds, and the number of distinct combinations of product and source. Hence state how many of the rows have been superseded, and explain why an append-only table holds more rows than it holds current facts.

    (State)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the current total units and the current total revenue, applying the reading rules given above. State how many of the four rows contribute to your total, and explain for each row that does not contribute why it does not.

    (Determine)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    One analyst adds units and revenue over every row in the table. The other finds the greatest load date in the table and adds only the rows that carry it. Calculate the units and revenue that each analyst reports, and state the direction and the size of the error in each against the correct total. Explain why the correct total is neither of the two answers nor the average of them. Then reach a judgement on which of the two errors the warehouse's own design makes easier to detect, and why.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) The table holds four rows. It holds two distinct products, P1 and P2, and three distinct combinations of product and source: P1 from the till, P1 from adjustments, and P2 from the till (A1). One of the four rows has therefore been superseded, since four rows describe three current facts (A1).

An append-only table holds more rows than current facts because it never overwrites. When a source restates its figures for a product, the correction is written as a new row rather than applied to the old one, so both the superseded row and the row that supersedes it remain in the table (A1). This is the property the guide calls time-variant: the table records what was believed at each point in time, not only what is believed now, and the earlier belief is preserved rather than erased.

(b) The current rows are found by taking, for each combination of product and source, the row with the greatest load date.

  • For P1 from the till there are two rows. The row written on 1 April carries 100 units and 2000 revenue, and the row written on 8 April carries 90 units and 1800 revenue. The later row is current, so the April 1 row does not contribute (M1).
  • For P1 from adjustments there is one row, written on 1 April, carrying 50 units and 1000 revenue. Nothing supersedes it, so it is current and it contributes (A1).
  • For P2 from the till there is one row, written on 8 April, carrying 40 units and 800 revenue. It is current and it contributes (A1).

The current totals are therefore $90 + 50 + 40 = 180$ units and $1800 + 1000 + 800 = 3600$ revenue (A1). Three of the four rows contribute. The row that does not is the 1 April row for P1 from the till, and it does not because the 8 April row from the same source for the same product supersedes it.

(c) Adding every row gives $100 + 50 + 90 + 40 = 280$ units and $2000 + 1000 + 1800 + 800 = 5600$ revenue (M1). The error is an over-statement of 100 units and 2000 revenue, and it is exactly the superseded row counted as though it were still current (A1).

Taking the greatest load date, which is 8 April, and adding only the rows that carry it gives $90 + 40 = 130$ units and $1800 + 800 = 2600$ revenue (M1). The error here is an under-statement of 50 units and 1000 revenue, and it is caused by dropping the P1 adjustments row, which was written on 1 April and has never been superseded (A1).

The correct total is 180 units and 3600 revenue, so it is neither 280 and 5600 nor 130 and 2600. It is also not the average of the two revenue figures, which would be $(2600 + 5600)/2 = 4100$ (A1). The two errors do not offset each other because they are not errors of the same kind. The first counts a row that should be excluded; the second excludes a row that should be counted. They happen to be different rows of different sizes, so nothing makes their effects equal and nothing makes them cancel. Treating two wrong answers as bracketing the right one is only sound when the errors are known to be symmetric, and nothing here establishes that (A1).

The judgement is that the warehouse's design makes the first error the easier of the two to detect (A1). The over-statement is caused by a row that is still physically present and still carries its original load date, so the row that should have been excluded can be found by comparing load dates within a product and source: wherever two rows share a product and a source, the older one is superseded, and the table itself supplies the evidence. The under-statement is caused by a row that has been silently omitted. Nothing in the data records the omission, because the row that was left out is indistinguishable from the rows that were correctly left out, and the query that produced the figure returns a smaller number with no indication that anything is missing (A1). An append-only design therefore makes the error of counting too much self-diagnosing and the error of counting too little invisible, which is the reason the reading rule has to be stated explicitly rather than inferred from the data.

Markscheme notes

(a) 3 marks. One mark for four rows, two products and three product-and-source combinations. One mark for one superseded row. One mark for the reason, that the table appends rather than overwrites so a restatement leaves both the old row and the new one in place.

(b) 4 marks. One mark for identifying the 8 April P1 till row as the current row for its pair and giving 90 and 1800 for it. One mark for keeping the 1 April P1 adjustments row and giving 50 and 1000 for it, with the reason that no later row from the same source supersedes it. One mark for the P2 row, 40 and 800. One mark for the totals, 180 units and 3600 revenue, together with the statement that three of the four rows contribute. A candidate who gives 130 and 2600 has applied the greatest-load-date rule instead of the per-pair rule and loses the second and fourth marks.

Accept 180 and 3600 in either order. Do not accept a total that includes the 1 April P1 till row, however it is justified, since the rule given in the figure supersedes it.

(c) 6 marks. One mark for 280 units and 5600 revenue. One mark for the direction and size of that error, an over-statement of 100 and 2000, attributed to the superseded row. One mark for 130 units and 2600 revenue. One mark for the direction and size of that error, an under-statement of 50 and 1000, attributed to the dropped adjustments row. One mark for rejecting the average of 4100 with a reason, namely that the two errors are of different kinds and are not symmetric. One mark for the judgement that the over-statement is the detectable one, because the superseded row is still present and can be identified by comparing load dates within a product and source, while the under-statement leaves no trace.

A candidate who says the two errors cancel, or who averages them without argument, loses the fifth mark. A candidate who reaches 3600 by a correct route and then states that the average is 4100 and therefore wrong earns that mark even without the detectability judgement.

Why this question is hard

An append-only table is a log of what was reported, not a picture of what is true. That is its strength and it is also the reason it cannot be summed naively. Every aggregate over such a table has to be paired with a rule that says which rows are current, and the rule has to be applied at the grain of the thing that can be restated. Here the grain is the pair of product and source, because the till and the adjustments ledger report on the same product independently and neither supersedes the other.

The two wrong answers are the two ways of getting the grain wrong. Summing every row applies no rule at all. Filtering on the greatest load date applies a rule at the wrong grain: it treats a load as the unit that supersedes, so a source that did not report on the most recent night disappears from the total. The second error is the more interesting one because the query looks principled. It contains a deliberate filter, it produces a smaller and therefore apparently safer number, and it is wrong because the thing being superseded is a row and not a night.

The two errors run in opposite directions, which is what makes the item a test of reasoning rather than of arithmetic. A candidate who computes both and finds one high and one low may be tempted to conclude that the truth lies between them, and the average of 4100 is not the truth. Bracket-and-average is only valid when the two errors are known to be of comparable size and opposite sign, and here they are 2000 and 1000 in opposite directions, which is a ratio of two to one and not a symmetry.

The final judgement is about the design rather than the query. Because the warehouse never deletes, the superseded row is still there and can be found by a rule that the table itself supports; the error of counting it is therefore a rule that can be checked against the data. The error of dropping a row leaves the data consistent with the wrong answer, so no amount of inspection of the table will reveal it. That asymmetry is the practical reason a warehouse specification states the currency rule rather than leaving it to be inferred, and it is the point the part is asking the candidate to reach.

Challenge lever
Two defensible ways of adding up an append-only table are both wrong, and they are wrong in opposite directions. Adding every row counts a row that a later row has replaced; adding only the rows from the most recent load drops a row from an earlier load that is still current. Neither error cancels the other, so the correct total is neither of the two and is not their average, and the two figures are close enough to each other that a candidate who checks one against the other will conclude they agree.

Verification
