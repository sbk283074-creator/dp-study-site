A twenty per cent productivity gain that makes each garment worth less

Business management SL · BM-5.1-001

Unit 5: 5.1 The role of operations management (the transformation process; adding value through operations; efficiency and effectiveness). Toolkit: descriptive statistics.P2 · Section A14 marksdifficulty 4ExplainCalculateEvaluate

Pennine Workwear

Pennine Workwear makes protective clothing for construction and agricultural customers at a single factory in Lancashire. The company employs 64 people, of whom 40 work on the production line. It makes about 120 000 garments a year, and its order book is full for the next eighteen months.

Six months ago the operations manager introduced a new production process. The number of quality checks was halved, the line speed was increased, and the final inspection stage was combined with packing. The operations manager's bonus depends on labour productivity, measured as garments produced per direct labour hour, and he has reported a 20% improvement since the change.

The finance director has collected the following figures for a typical month before the change and a typical month since it.

Before the change: output 12 000 garments; direct labour hours 2 400; selling price EUR 18 per garment; bought-in materials EUR 7 per garment; defect rate 2%; cost of reworking a defective garment EUR 4.

Since the change: output 15 000 garments; direct labour hours 2 500; selling price EUR 18 per garment; bought-in materials EUR 7 per garment; defect rate 7%; cost of reworking a defective garment EUR 4; and 3% of output is now sold as seconds at EUR 9 instead of EUR 18.

The quality manager has also recorded the monthly defect rate for the past twelve months. The change was introduced at the end of month 6. Those figures are in the table below.

Two of Pennine's largest customers, who together account for about 30% of sales, have written to complain about the quality of recent deliveries, and one has asked for a meeting. Three experienced machinists have left in the past four months. The operations manager has asked the board to approve a further increase in line speed, arguing that the productivity gain has already been proved.

Question

The board of Pennine Workwear must decide whether to keep the new production process.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain, with reference to the transformation process, how the operations function at Pennine Workwear adds value to the business.

    (Explain)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Calculate the labour productivity and the value added per garment in a typical month before the change and in a typical month since the change. Comment on what your figures show.

    (Calculate)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Using descriptive statistics on the defect-rate data, evaluate whether Pennine Workwear should keep the new production process.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Pennine Workwear operates a transformation process. The inputs are bought-in fabric, thread and components costing EUR 7 a garment, together with 2 400 hours of direct labour a month and the use of the factory and machinery. The process is cutting, sewing, finishing, inspecting and packing. The outputs are finished protective garments sold at EUR 18 each, which for many customers must also be safe, durable and compliant with industry standards.

The operations function adds value in two ways. First, it converts EUR 7 of materials into a garment that sells for EUR 18 - the transformation itself is where the added value is created. Second, it is responsible for the quality and reliability that make the garment worth buying: a protective jacket that fails in use is worthless, so the inspection and finishing stages protect the price the business can charge. Operations therefore supports the whole business strategy, not just the production line.

(b) Labour productivity = garments produced per direct labour hour.

Before the change: $12 000 / 2 400 = 5.0$ garments per labour hour. (M1)(A1)
Since the change: $15 000 / 2 500 = 6.0$ garments per labour hour. (A1)
That is a rise of $(6.0 - 5.0) / 5.0 = 20\%$, which is the figure the operations manager is reporting.

Value added = revenue minus bought-in materials minus rework cost.

Before the change: revenue $= 12 000 \times EUR 18 = EUR 216 000$; materials $= 12 000 \times EUR 7 = EUR 84 000$; defects $= 2\% \times 12 000 = 240$ garments, rework $= 240 \times EUR 4 = EUR 960$. Value added $= EUR 216 000 - EUR 84 000 - EUR 960 = EUR 131 040$, or $EUR 131 040 / 12 000 = EUR 10.92$ per garment. (M1)(A1)

Since the change: revenue $= 15 000 \times EUR 18 = EUR 270 000$, less 450 garments sold as seconds at EUR 9 instead of EUR 18, a loss of $450 \times EUR 9 = EUR 4 050$, so EUR 265 950; materials $= 15 000 \times EUR 7 = EUR 105 000$; defects $= 7\% \times 15 000 = 1 050$ garments, rework $= 1 050 \times EUR 4 = EUR 4 200$. Value added $= EUR 265 950 - EUR 105 000 - EUR 4 200 = EUR 156 750$, or $EUR 156 750 / 15 000 = EUR 10.45$ per garment. (M1)(A1)

The two measures disagree. Labour productivity is up 20%, but value added per garment has fallen from EUR 10.92 to EUR 10.45, a fall of about 4.3%. Total value added has risen, from EUR 131 040 to EUR 156 750, but only because 25% more garments are being made. Each garment now adds less value than before, because the cost of rework and of selling seconds has risen faster than the extra output is worth. Efficiency has improved; effectiveness has not.

(c) Indicative evaluation, using descriptive statistics on the twelve monthly defect rates.

The mean defect rate for the year is $(2.1 + 1.8 + 2.3 + 1.9 + 2.2 + 2.1 + 6.8 + 7.1 + 6.4 + 7.6 + 8.1 + 7.4) / 12 = 55.8 / 12 = 4.65\%$. Taken alone, that looks acceptable. The median is 4.35% and the range is $8.1 - 1.8 = 6.3$ percentage points. (M1)(A1)

The mean is misleading. Splitting the data at the change gives a mean of 2.07% for months 1 to 6 and 7.23% for months 7 to 12. The process did not drift; it stepped from about 2% to about 7% at the moment the change was introduced, and the annual mean of 4.65% describes no month that actually occurred. This is the classic trap of a summary statistic that conceals a structural break.

Two of the largest customers, together worth about 30% of sales, have complained, and one has asked for a meeting. Three experienced machinists have left in four months, which will put further pressure on quality. The operations manager's bonus depends on productivity, which explains why he is recommending a further increase in line speed, but his measure ignores the cost of defects and returns.

Conclusion: the change should not be kept in its present form. The productivity gain is real, but it has been bought by cutting the quality checks that protect the EUR 18 price. A supported conclusion is required; a candidate who recommends keeping the change but restoring the inspection stage, and justifies this from the figures, is equally creditworthy.

Markscheme notes

(a) Two marks for a correct description of inputs, process and outputs, and two for explaining how operations adds value. A candidate who describes the process without linking it to value added earns at most two. Do not credit a list of departments with no explanation.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for the two productivity figures and (A1)(A1) for the two value added per garment figures. The comment mark is the discriminating one and requires the candidate to notice that productivity and value added per unit move in opposite directions. A candidate who reports only the productivity improvement, or who says 'both improved because total value added rose', forfeits the comment mark. Follow through on arithmetic slips. Accept value added calculated without the rework cost only if the same treatment is applied to both periods; a candidate who includes rework in one period and not the other cannot reach full marks.

(c) The descriptive statistics marks are for computing the mean, the median and the range, and for noticing that the annual mean conceals the step change. A candidate who reports only the mean of 4.65% and concludes that quality is acceptable has not used the data. The evaluation must end in a decision; credit either conclusion provided it is supported. Do not credit an answer that attributes the defects to staff attitudes without evidence.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on the difference between efficiency and effectiveness, which is the heart of Unit 5.1. Efficiency asks whether the business is doing things right - more output per hour, lower unit cost. Effectiveness asks whether it is doing the right things - making something the customer will pay for and keep buying. The operations manager has improved the first and damaged the second, and his bonus is tied to the measure that improved.

The arithmetic is deliberately arranged so that the trap is visible but easy to miss. Labour productivity rises by exactly 20%, which is the figure the case quotes, and total value added also rises, from EUR 131 040 to EUR 156 750. A student who checks only these two numbers concludes that the change is a success. The figure that contradicts them is value added per garment, which falls from EUR 10.92 to EUR 10.45 because the cost of rework and of selling seconds rises faster than the extra output is worth. Twenty-five per cent more garments are being made, and each one is worth slightly less.

The descriptive statistics add a second, sharper point. The annual mean defect rate of 4.65% looks unremarkable, but no month had a defect rate near it: the process ran at about 2% and then stepped to about 7%. A summary statistic that averages across a structural break describes nothing real. This is why the question supplies twelve monthly figures rather than two averages, and it is what separates a candidate who can calculate a mean from one who can interpret it.

The qualitative evidence - two large customers complaining, three machinists leaving, a bonus tied to the wrong measure - is there to support the evaluation rather than to replace it. The strongest answers notice that the operations manager's incentive and the company's interest have diverged, which is why the recommendation is not simply 'work harder on quality' but 'restore the check that the change removed'.

Classic wrong turns: treating a 20% productivity gain as automatically good; using total value added instead of value added per unit; and reporting the annual mean defect rate without splitting it at the change.
