Why the tallest bar is not the most crowded class

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.2-001

SL 4.2 (presentation of data: frequency distributions, histograms, cumulative frequency, box-and-whisker plots)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedExplainShow thatEstimate

Question

One hundred observations are grouped into five classes of unequal width. The classes and their frequencies are $0\le x<2$ with frequency $12$, $2\le x<4$ with frequency $8$, $4\le x<8$ with frequency $40$, $8\le x<12$ with frequency $24$, and $12\le x<20$ with frequency $16$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain why frequency alone cannot be plotted on the vertical axis when the class widths differ, and calculate the frequency density of each of the five classes.

    (Explain)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that the median lies in the class $4\le x<8$, and estimate its value.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Estimate the interquartile range, showing clearly the positions you use.

    (Estimate)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Estimate the 90th percentile and interpret it in the context of the observations.

    (Estimate)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) With unequal widths a tall bar shows a wide class, not a crowded one, so the axis must show frequency per unit of width (M1). Frequency density is frequency divided by class width, so the five densities are $\frac{12}{2}=6$ (A1), $\frac{8}{2}=4$ and $\frac{40}{4}=10$ (A1), then $\frac{24}{4}=6$ and $\frac{16}{8}=2$ (A1). The tallest bar is the class $4\le x<8$, with density $10$, although its width is only four. Density, not frequency, is comparable across unequal widths.

(b) The total frequency is $100$, so the median is at position $50$ (M1). The cumulative frequencies are $12$, $20$, $60$, $84$ and $100$, so the fiftieth value falls in the class $4\le x<8$ (A1). Within that class, $30$ of the $40$ observations are needed (M1), so the median is $4+\frac{30}{40}\times 4=7$ (A1). This assumes an even spread in the class.

(c) The lower quartile lies at position $25$ (M1). The cumulative frequency reaches $20$ at $x=4$, so $5$ more come from the class of width $4$, giving $Q_1=4+\frac{5}{40}\times 4=4.5$ (A1). The upper quartile lies at position $75$, and the cumulative frequency reaches $60$ at $x=8$, so $Q_3=8+\frac{15}{24}\times 4=10.5$ (A1). The interquartile range is $10.5-4.5=6$ (A1). Each quartile uses the same interpolation as the median.

(d) The ninetieth percentile lies at position $90$ (M1). The cumulative frequency reaches $84$ at $x=12$, so $6$ observations come from the class $12\le x<20$ of width $8$ and frequency $16$, giving $P_{90}=12+\frac{6}{16}\times 8=15$ (A1)(A1). In context, about ninety per cent lie below $15$, so ten per cent exceed it (A1). The value is an estimate, again assuming an even spread in the top class.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for the idea that unequal widths require a density rather than a frequency. The three (A1) marks are for the five densities, grouped so that any three correct values earn two marks and all five earn three; accept the densities written as fractions. A candidate who plots frequencies forfeits the reasoning mark but may still earn the (A1) marks for the numbers.

(b) Award (M1) for locating position $50$, (A1) for identifying the class $4\le x<8$, (M1) for the interpolation fraction, and (A1) for the value $7$. A candidate who reads $7$ directly from the cumulative curve earns the same marks, provided the position is stated.

(c) The (M1) is for the position $25$; the (A1) is for $4.5$. The second (A1) is for $10.5$, and the final (A1) for the interquartile range $6$. Follow through from an incorrect $Q_1$ or $Q_3$ is allowed when the subtraction is consistent.

(d) The (M1) is for the position $90$ and (A1)(A1) for the interpolation and the value $15$. The final (A1) is for a contextual interpretation in terms of the proportion above or below $15$; a bare number without context does not earn it.

Why this question is hard

The item turns on frequency and frequency density answering different questions. The class $4\le x<8$ contains the largest frequency, $40$, but a narrower class can be equally crowded per unit of width: the class $0\le x<2$ has density $6$, against $10$ for the wide class. A candidate who simply plots the frequencies produces a histogram whose areas no longer represent the data, because a bar of height $40$ over a width of four claims an area of $160$ observations. The densities $6$, $4$, $10$, $6$ and $2$ restore the property that each area equals the frequency it represents.

The cumulative frequencies are needed for the median and the quartiles, and the key step is to convert a position into an interpolation within a class. For the median the position is $50$, which lies in the third class; the fraction is $\frac{50-20}{40}$, and multiplying by the class width of four gives an addition of three, so the median is $7$. The quartiles use positions $25$ and $75$ in the same way, and the percentile uses position $90$.

The common error is to assume that the median is the midpoint of the class containing it, which would give $6$ and is wrong because the observations are not evenly distributed within the class boundary. Another error is to use cumulative frequencies rather than positions, or to take $Q_3$ from the fourth class when the cumulative frequency of $75$ still lies in the third. The class limits were chosen so that each interpolation fraction is simple: $\frac{30}{40}$, $\frac{5}{40}$, $\frac{15}{24}$ and $\frac{6}{16}$ all reduce to tidy quarters or eighths, so a correct method produces exact values while a mistaken one usually does not.
