5.5 Break-even with a stepped fixed cost: two break-even points and a region of loss above the first

Business management SL · BM-5.5-002

Unit 5: Operations management5.5 Break-even analysis (contribution, break-even output, margin of safety, profit targets); 3.3 Costs and revenuesP2 · Section A16 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateShow thatDetermineEvaluate

Question

Kestrel Assembly makes a single product which it sells for $\pounds 14$. Variable cost is
$\pounds 6$ per unit.

Fixed costs are $\pounds 60{,}000$ a year for output up to and including $8{,}000$ units. Above
$8{,}000$ units a second shift is required, and fixed costs rise to $\pounds 85{,}000$ a year. The
step applies to the whole year's output, not only to the units above $8{,}000$, and there is no
change in selling price or variable cost.

(a) Calculate the break-even output on the assumption that fixed costs remain at
$\pounds 60{,}000$. \tag{3}

(b) Show that profit at an output of $8{,}000$ units is $\pounds 4{,}000$, while profit at an output
of $8{,}001$ units is $-\pounds 20{,}992$. \tag{5}

(c) Determine the output at which the business breaks even for the second time. \tag{4}

(d) Evaluate whether the business should plan an output of $10{,}000$ units, supporting your
answer with a calculation of the minimum selling price required. \tag{4}

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the break-even output on the assumption that fixed costs remain at $60{,}000$.

Reveal the answer

(a) Contribution per unit is

$$\pounds 14 - \pounds 6 = \pounds 8 \tag{A1}$$

and break-even output is fixed cost divided by contribution per unit (M1):

$$\frac{60{,}000}{8} = 7{,}500 \text{ units} \tag{A1}$$

This lies below $8{,}000$ units, so the $\pounds 60{,}000$ figure is the right one to use and the
answer is consistent.

(b) At $8{,}000$ units the higher fixed cost has not yet been triggered, so

$$\text{profit} = 8 \times 8{,}000 - 60{,}000 = 64{,}000 - 60{,}000 = \pounds 4{,}000 \tag{M1A1}$$

At $8{,}001$ units the step applies to the whole year's output, so fixed costs are
$\pounds 85{,}000$ and not $\pounds 60{,}000$ (M1):

$$\text{profit} = 8 \times 8{,}001 - 85{,}000 = 64{,}008 - 85{,}000 = -\pounds 20{,}992 \tag{A1}$$

The two figures differ by $\pounds 24{,}992$ even though output has risen by a single unit (A1). Of
that, $\pounds 8$ is the contribution earned by the extra unit and $\pounds 25{,}000$ is the step in
fixed cost, so almost the entire movement is the step and not the unit. A profit-and-loss account
drawn up for any output from $8{,}001$ units upwards must therefore use $\pounds 85{,}000$, and the
$7{,}500$-unit figure from (a) tells the business nothing about that region.

(c) In the upper band, fixed costs are $\pounds 85{,}000$ and contribution per unit is unchanged at
$\pounds 8$ (M1):

$$\frac{85{,}000}{8} = 10{,}625 \text{ units} \tag{A1}$$

At $10{,}625$ units contribution is $8 \times 10{,}625 = \pounds 85{,}000$, which exactly covers the
higher fixed cost, so profit is zero (A1). Because $10{,}625 > 8{,}000$ the answer lies inside the
band to which it applies and is self-consistent, exactly as in (a) (A1).

The business therefore breaks even twice, at $7{,}500$ units and again at $10{,}625$ units, and
makes a loss at every output in between, with the single worst point being $8{,}001$ units. Any
output from $10{,}626$ units upward is profitable. The margin of safety at a planned output of
$12{,}000$ units is $12{,}000 - 10{,}625 = 1{,}375$ units, or $11.5\%$ — measured against the second
break-even point, not the first, which would give a meaningless $37.5\%$.

(d) At $10{,}000$ units the business is in the upper band, so fixed costs are $\pounds 85{,}000$
(M1). Let the selling price be $p$; contribution per unit is $p - 6$ (M1). Break-even requires

$$10{,}000(p - 6) = 85{,}000 \tag{A1}$$

so

$$p = \frac{85{,}000}{10{,}000} + 6 = 8.50 + 6 = \pounds 14.50 \tag{A1}$$

The current price of $\pounds 14.00$ gives a contribution of $\pounds 80{,}000$ against fixed costs
of $\pounds 85{,}000$ and a loss of $\pounds 5{,}000$, so the required increase is $\pounds 0.50$,
or $3.6\%$. Two cautions are worth recording. The answer is the break-even price at this output, not
a recommended price: at $\pounds 14.50$ the business makes exactly nothing on $10{,}000$ units, and a
price below $\pounds 14.50$ loses money. And the answer is specific to $10{,}000$ units, because the
$\pounds 85{,}000$ is spread over whatever volume is actually produced; at $12{,}000$ units the
break-even price would be only $\pounds 13.08$.

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for the contribution, one for dividing fixed cost by contribution, one for the figure 7,500. Award the final mark only if the candidate states or implies that 7,500 is below 8,000 and therefore consistent. (b) One mark for using 60,000 at 8,000 units, one for the 4,000, one for switching to 85,000 at 8,001 units, one for the -20,992, one for noting that the whole step and not just the extra unit drives the change. A candidate who applies 85,000 to only the units above 8,000 gets 3,992 and should lose the third and fourth marks. (c) One mark for using 85,000, one for dividing by 8, one for 10,625, one for checking that the answer lies in the band to which it applies. Allow 10,625 or 10,626; do not allow 7,500 as the only answer. (d) One mark for using 85,000 rather than 60,000, one for setting up the equation, one for 14.50, one for the interpretation. Accept a correctly argued answer in pence. Follow-through applies throughout: a candidate who misreads the step but is otherwise consistent should not be penalised twice.

Why this question is hard

Break-even analysis is one of the first quantitative tools a business student meets, and
it is normally taught as the answer to a single question: how many units must we sell? The formula
is short enough to memorise, and the memorised version carries an assumption that is almost never
examined — that the fixed cost stays fixed across the range of output being considered. Here it does
not, and the item is built so that the assumption fails exactly where a manager would most want to
rely on it.

The naive path is to compute contribution, divide, and stop: $60{,}000$ over $8$ gives $7{,}500$
units, the business plans to sell well above that, and everything looks sound. That answer is not
wrong as far as it goes, and it is self-consistent, which is what makes it dangerous. It says
nothing at all about the region above $8{,}000$ units, and in that region the same formula applied to
the same cost structure gives a loss of nearly $\pounds 21{,}000$. A manager acting on the first
number would expand output into the step and be worse off than before expanding, having been told by
the arithmetic that the expansion was safe.

The discontinuity is worth dwelling on, because it is the point that separates understanding the
formula from being able to use it. Profit at $8{,}000$ units is $\pounds 4{,}000$; at $8{,}001$ it is
$-\pounds 20{,}992$. One extra unit destroys nearly $\pounds 25{,}000$ of profit, of which
$\pounds 8$ comes from the unit and the remaining $\pounds 25{,}000$ from the step. A candidate who
writes that down has understood that the step applies to the year's whole output rather than to the
marginal units, which is the single idea the item exists to test, and which the phrase "applies to
the whole year's output" in the stem is there to make decidable rather than ambiguous.

Two further moves carry the item past routine application. Part (c) asks for the second break-even
point and requires the candidate to check that the answer lies inside the band to which it applies
— the same consistency check that part (a) demanded, but now on the far side of the step, where the
obvious answer and the correct answer are different numbers. Part (d) then inverts the question,
fixing output and solving for price, which is the version a manager actually faces and which exposes
anyone who has been applying the formula mechanically: using $\pounds 60{,}000$ instead of
$\pounds 85{,}000$ here gives $\pounds 12.00$ and understates the required price by two pounds.

The item is adapted from a UK A-Level treatment of break-even that introduces a stepped fixed cost
and asks for the revised chart. Asking for the two break-even points explicitly, demanding the
profit figures on either side of the step so that the discontinuity has to be stated as a number,
and inverting to solve for price rather than for output are all new here. The source wants a
picture; this wants the candidate to locate a region of loss that a correctly drawn chart would show
but a single calculated figure would hide.
