B.3 A hot-air balloon treated as a body immersed in the surrounding air: the density of the hot air is set by the absolute temperature, the temperature needed for a stated payload follows from the required density, and the payload has an upper bound equal to the mass of air displaced because the density of the hot air cannot fall below zero

Physics HL · PHYS-B.3-202

Theme B: The particulate nature of matterB.3 Gas laws: the ideal gas equation pV = nRT applied to the air in a balloon envelope, the relation between density and absolute temperature at constant pressure, and the upward force on a body immersed in a fluidP2 · Section B13 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatDetermineShow thatComment

Question

A hot-air balloon consists of an open envelope of volume $2200\ \mathrm{m^{3}}$ and a basket, whose combined mass, including the burner and the fuel, is $180\ \mathrm{kg}$. The envelope is open at the bottom, so the pressure of the air inside is equal to the pressure of the air outside. The outside air is at $288\ \mathrm{K}$ and a pressure of $1.01 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa}$, and its molar mass is $0.0290\ \mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$. Take the gas constant as $R = 8.31\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. The air inside the envelope is heated by the burner to a temperature $T$ and behaves as an ideal gas with the same molar mass. The balloon carries a payload of mass $m$ in addition to the mass of the envelope and basket. The volume of the envelope is taken as constant; the fabric does not stretch appreciably as the air inside is heated.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the density of the outside air is $1.22\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, and state why the density of the air inside the envelope at temperature $T$ is $1.22 \times \dfrac{288}{T}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the minimum temperature $T$ of the air inside the envelope required to lift a payload of $400\ \mathrm{kg}$, giving your answer in kelvin and in degrees Celsius.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Show that the payload has an upper bound that does not depend on the temperature of the air inside the envelope, and determine that bound.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    A larger burner is fitted, doubling the temperature of the air inside the envelope above the value found in part (b). Determine the payload that this would allow, and comment on the claim that a sufficiently powerful burner could lift a payload of $3000\ \mathrm{kg}$.

    (Comment)

Reveal the answer

(a) For a gas of molar mass $M$ the density is $\rho = pM/(RT)$ (M1), so
$$\rho_{\text{out}} = \frac{1.01 \times 10^{5} \times 0.0290}{8.31 \times 288} = \frac{2929}{2393} = 1.22\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} \;(A1).$$
Inside the envelope the pressure is the same as outside, because the envelope is open at the bottom, and the molar mass is the same. So the ratio of the densities is
$$\frac{\rho_{\text{in}}}{\rho_{\text{out}}} = \frac{pM/(RT)}{pM/(R \times 288)} = \frac{288}{T} \;(A1),$$
and hence $\rho_{\text{in}} = 1.22 \times 288/T$ with $T$ in kelvin. The relation is between absolute temperatures: using degrees Celsius here would be meaningless, since the ratio $288/T$ would change with the zero of the scale.
(b) The upward force on the balloon is the weight of the air it displaces less the weight of the hot air inside, and for the balloon to lift a total mass $m_{\text{tot}}$ that force must at least balance the weight of that mass (M1):
$$(\rho_{\text{out}} - \rho_{\text{in}})Vg = m_{\text{tot}}g \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \rho_{\text{in}} = \rho_{\text{out}} - \frac{m_{\text{tot}}}{V} \;(A1).$$
With $m_{\text{tot}} = 180 + 400 = 580\ \mathrm{kg}$,
$$\rho_{\text{in}} \le 1.22 - \frac{580}{2200} = 1.22 - 0.264 = 0.960\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} \;(A1),$$
and from part (a), $T = 288 \times \dfrac{1.22}{0.960} = 367\ \mathrm{K}$, that is $367 - 273 = 94\ \mathrm{{}^{\circ}C}$ (A1). This is comfortably within the range of a propane burner.
(c) The bound follows from part (b) without any new physics. Since the density of the air inside is $\rho_{\text{in}} = 1.22 \times 288/T$ and $T$ is an absolute temperature, $T > 0$ always, so $\rho_{\text{in}} > 0$ always (M1). The lift per unit volume is $\rho_{\text{out}} - \rho_{\text{in}}$, and it is largest when $\rho_{\text{in}}$ is smallest, which happens as $T \to \infty$ and $\rho_{\text{in}} \to 0$ (A1). So the greatest lift available is $\rho_{\text{out}}Vg$, the weight of the air displaced, and the greatest total mass that can be carried is
$$m_{\text{max}} = \rho_{\text{out}}V = 1.22 \times 2200 = 2692\ \mathrm{kg} \;(A1),$$
of which $180\ \mathrm{kg}$ is the balloon itself, leaving a maximum payload of about $2510\ \mathrm{kg}$. No burner, however powerful, can exceed this, because the envelope cannot displace more air than its own volume and the hot air inside always has some mass.
(d) Doubling the inside temperature from $367\ \mathrm{K}$ gives $T = 734\ \mathrm{K}$, and the inside density becomes
$$\rho_{\text{in}} = 1.22 \times \frac{288}{734} = 0.480\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} \;(M1).$$
The lift is then $(1.22 - 0.480) \times 2200 = 1636\ \mathrm{kg}$, so the payload is $1636 - 180 = 1456\ \mathrm{kg}$ (A1). Doubling the absolute temperature from 367 K to 734 K has raised the payload from 400 kg to 1456 kg, which is more than a doubling but far from unlimited; 734 K is $461\ \mathrm{{}^{\circ}C}$, hot enough to threaten most envelope fabrics, and it has bought less than four times the original payload.
The claim that a $3000\ \mathrm{kg}$ payload could be lifted is false, and part (c) says why before any calculation is attempted: $3000\ \mathrm{kg}$ exceeds the $2510\ \mathrm{kg}$ bound. If the required density is computed anyway it comes out negative, $\rho_{\text{in}} = 1.22 - 3000/2200 = -0.14\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, and substituting that into $T = 288 \rho_{\text{out}}/\rho_{\text{in}}$ gives a negative absolute temperature, which is physically impossible (A1). A negative density is not a very hot gas; it is a sign that the equation has been asked for an answer that does not exist.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for $\rho = pM/(RT)$, (A1) for $1.22\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, (A1) for the inverse relation between density and absolute temperature with the reason that the pressure and molar mass are the same inside and out. A candidate who states the ratio but does not justify it from the shared pressure loses the third mark; the shared pressure is the physical content, since it follows from the envelope being open.
(b) (M1) for the force balance between the displaced air and the total mass, (A1) for $\rho_{\text{in}} = \rho_{\text{out}} - m_{\text{tot}}/V$, (A1) for $0.960\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, (A1) for $367\ \mathrm{K}$ or $94\ \mathrm{{}^{\circ}C}$. Accept 1.2238 and 0.9602 from unrounded working, and accept $367\ \mathrm{K}$ to three significant figures. A candidate who uses only the payload of $400\ \mathrm{kg}$ and omits the $180\ \mathrm{kg}$ of the balloon loses the fourth mark.
(c) (M1) for the observation that the inside density is positive for every positive absolute temperature, (A1) for the lift being greatest as $T \to \infty$, (A1) for $\rho_{\text{out}}V = 2692\ \mathrm{kg}$ and the payload bound of about $2510\ \mathrm{kg}$. A candidate who asserts that the payload is unlimited because the temperature can be made arbitrarily large scores nothing; a candidate who gives the bound but calls it the payload without subtracting the balloon's own mass loses the third mark.
(d) (M1) for the inside density at the doubled temperature, (A1) for the payload of about $1456\ \mathrm{kg}$, (A1) for the rejection of the $3000\ \mathrm{kg}$ claim with the negative density or the negative temperature as the reason. Condonation: a candidate who answers only that $3000\ \mathrm{kg}$ exceeds the bound from part (c) scores the third mark; the arithmetic with the negative density is an alternative route, not a requirement.
Follow-through: a candidate whose density in (a) is slightly different but who works consistently keeps the method marks in (b), (c) and (d). Forfeits: using degrees Celsius in the density ratio; forgetting that the balloon's own mass counts as part of what must be lifted; and treating the envelope as sealed, which would make the pressure inside rise with temperature and change the whole problem.

Why this question is hard

The physics here is a balance between two masses: the mass of the cold air the envelope pushes out of the way, and the mass of the hot air it contains. Everything else is bookkeeping. The balloon floats when the first exceeds the second plus the mass of everything hanging from it, and the only thing the burner does is reduce the second.

The relation that makes the problem solvable is that the envelope is open at the bottom. That single fact does two things: it fixes the pressure inside at the outside pressure, and it lets hot air escape as it expands, so the volume stays at 2200 cubic metres and the density falls. With the pressure and molar mass the same inside and out, the densities are in the inverse ratio of the absolute temperatures, and the temperature is the only variable the pilot controls.

Part (b) is the ordinary calculation and it is worth doing carefully, because the balloon itself counts. The payload is 400 kg but the mass to be lifted is 580 kg, and forgetting the 180 kg gives a required density of 1.042 rather than 0.960 and a temperature of 338 K rather than 367 K. The error is not large and it is in the direction of a balloon that will not leave the ground.

Part (c) is where the item stops being arithmetic. The density of the hot air is proportional to the reciprocal of the absolute temperature, and a reciprocal never reaches zero. So the lift saturates: it approaches the weight of the displaced air and stops. The number is 2692 kg of air displaced, of which 180 kg is the balloon, leaving about 2510 kg. That is a hard ceiling and it comes from the geometry of the envelope, not from the burner.

Part (d) makes the ceiling concrete. Doubling the absolute temperature from 367 K to 734 K is a real engineering achievement, and it raises the payload from 400 kg to about 1456 kg -- a large gain, but the last stretch towards the ceiling is enormously expensive, because the density falls only as $1/T$ while the temperature climbs. The request for 3000 kg is not difficult; it is impossible, and the negative density that comes out of the equation is the arithmetic telling the candidate so. Recognising that before computing is the point of asking part (c) first.

Challenge lever
The balloon is a buoyancy problem wearing the costume of a gas law problem, and the two have to be used together. The lift is the difference between the mass of cold air displaced and the mass of hot air inside, so the inside density is what has to be controlled, and the temperature is only the means of controlling it. Part (c) is the sharp end: because the inside pressure equals the outside pressure in an open balloon, the inside density is inversely proportional to the absolute temperature, and the density cannot fall below zero however hot the air becomes. The lift is therefore bounded by the mass of displaced air, and no burner can raise a payload beyond that bound. A candidate who treats temperature as the governing variable and substitutes freely will compute a negative required density for a sufficiently heavy payload and, if the substitution is carried through, a negative absolute temperature.

Adaptation
The hot-air balloon appears in textbooks as a straightforward buoyancy calculation with the density relation supplied. Here the balloon's own mass is separated from the payload, the temperature is asked for in kelvin and in degrees Celsius so that the absolute scale cannot be avoided, the maximum payload is asked for as a bound that does not depend on the burner, and the final part asks for the failure of an impossible request to be identified from the sign of the density rather than from a comparison of numbers. The volume, the masses, the temperature and the pressure are chosen here.
Verification
The outside density was computed from pM/RT at 288 K and 1.01e5 Pa. The required inside density for a total mass of 580 kg was found from the buoyancy balance, and the temperature obtained from it by inverting the density ratio. The bound was established from the positivity of the absolute temperature and evaluated as the mass of displaced air, with the balloon's own mass subtracted to give the payload. The doubled-temperature case was computed from the same equations, and the impossible 3000 kg case was carried through to show that it requires a negative density and a negative absolute temperature.
