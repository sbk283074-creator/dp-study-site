Cache memory and average memory access time

Computer science HL · CS-P1A-401

Theme A: Concepts of computer scienceA1.1 Computer hardware and operation (A1.1.4 the role of cache memory, A1.1.5 the machine instruction cycle, A1.1.6 the effect of clock speed and cache on performance)P1 · Section A14 marksdifficulty 5technology: not applicableCalculateDetermineExplainEvaluate

Question

A design team is comparing two memory configurations for a new single-core processor. The figure shows the proposed hierarchy. Every instruction fetch and every data read or write that the processor issues is presented to the level 1 cache first. A reference that misses in level 1 is passed to the level 2 cache, and a reference that also misses there is passed to main memory. The hit rate of each cache is the proportion of the references presented to it that it satisfies, and the time given for each level is the number of processor cycles that level adds when it is consulted. Registers are not part of the calculation. The team must decide whether the level 2 cache earns the die area it occupies, because that area could be given to other parts of the processor.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the average number of cycles the processor spends on one memory reference when both caches are present. Show clearly how the level 1 hit time, the level 2 hit time and the main memory access time each contribute to your total, and state the fraction of references that reach main memory.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the average number of cycles per memory reference if the level 2 cache is removed, so that every level 1 miss is passed directly to main memory. Give your answer to three significant figures.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain why the contribution that main memory makes to the average in part (a) is so much smaller than its access time of 150 cycles would suggest, and why the level 2 cache makes a larger contribution than its own hit rate alone implies.

    (Explain)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Evaluate the proposal to remove the level 2 cache in order to recover its die area. Refer to your results from parts (a) and (b), and consider the effect on the processor's performance rather than only on the cost of manufacturing it.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The level 1 miss rate is $1 - 0.94 = 0.06$ (M1). Of those misses only $1 - 0.80 = 0.20$ are not satisfied by level 2, so the fraction of all references that reach main memory is $0.06 \times 0.20 = 0.012$, that is 1.2 per cent (A1).

The level 1 cache is consulted by every reference and adds 3 cycles, so its contribution is 3 cycles. The level 2 cache is consulted only by the 0.06 of references that miss in level 1 and adds 14 cycles to each of them, so its contribution is $0.06 \times 14 = 0.84$ cycles. Main memory is consulted only by the 0.012 of references that miss in both caches and adds 150 cycles to each of them, so its contribution is $0.012 \times 150 = 1.8$ cycles (A1).

The average number of cycles per reference is therefore $3 + 0.84 + 1.8 = 5.64$ cycles (A1).

(b) With no level 2 cache every level 1 miss is passed straight to main memory, so main memory is consulted by the whole 0.06 of misses and adds 150 cycles to each of them. Its contribution becomes $0.06 \times 150 = 9$ cycles (M1), and the average becomes $3 + 9 = 12.0$ cycles (A1). Removing the level 2 cache therefore raises the average from 5.64 to 12.0 cycles, a factor of $12.0 \div 5.64 = 2.13$ (A1).

(c) Main memory contributes only 1.8 cycles even though a single access costs 150 cycles, because it is reached by 1.2 per cent of references and by no others. Its 150 cycles are paid once per reference that misses in both caches, so multiplying 150 by the overall miss rate of 0.012 gives the average cost per reference; multiplying it by anything closer to 1 would charge the whole of main memory's latency to references that never reach it (M1).

The level 2 cache contributes more than its own hit rate suggests because its hit rate is conditional. The figure states that 0.80 of the references presented to level 2 are satisfied there, but only 0.06 of all references are ever presented to it, so the level 2 cache removes $0.06 \times 0.80 = 0.048$ of all references from the path to main memory (A1). Those references would otherwise have cost 150 cycles instead of 14, so the cache saves $0.048 \times (150 - 14) = 6.53$ cycles per reference; equivalently, comparing parts (a) and (b), it saves $12.0 - 5.64 = 6.36$ cycles (A1). A hit rate is a property of the cache, not of the program, and the two must be multiplied to obtain the share of references the cache actually rescues.

(d) The proposal should be rejected on performance grounds. Removing the level 2 cache raises the average memory reference from 5.64 to 12.0 cycles, an increase of 6.36 cycles on every reference the processor issues, which is a slowdown of about 2.13 times on any part of the workload that is memory-bound (A1). Because the level 2 cache is consulted on only 6 per cent of references but converts each of those from a 150-cycle main memory access into a 14-cycle access, it removes roughly 6.5 cycles per reference from the average while occupying far less die area than the level 1 cache does (A1).

The evaluation is not settled by cost alone. Die area given to level 2 could raise the level 1 hit rate instead, but part (a) shows that the level 1 hit time is paid by every reference and the level 1 miss rate is already only 0.06, so a further improvement there can remove at most 0.06 of the references from the level 2 path; the level 2 cache is currently rescuing 0.048 of them (A1). A design that removed level 2 would also make the processor's performance far more sensitive to the level 1 hit rate, because every additional level 1 miss would then cost the full 150 cycles rather than 14 (A1).

A justified answer must therefore weigh the 2.13 times slowdown against the area recovered, and should note that the decision depends on how memory-bound the intended workload is: a workload that fits in level 1 would lose little, while one that streams through data would lose most of its throughput (A1). Merely asserting that caches are fast, or that removing a component reduces cost, earns no credit.

Markscheme notes

Part (a) is marked for the conditional weights, not for the final number. Award M1 for the level 1 miss rate 0.06, then A1 for the joint miss rate 0.012, A1 for the three contributions being 3, 0.84 and 1.8 cycles, and A1 for the total 5.64.

A candidate who computes $0.94 \times 3 + 0.06 \times 14 = 3.66$ has weighted level 2 by the level 1 miss rate, which is correct, but has omitted main memory entirely; this is the commonest error and should be given M1 only.

A candidate who computes $0.94 \times 3 + 0.80 \times 14 + 0.012 \times 150$ has weighted level 2 by its own unconditional hit rate. The arithmetic is right and the reasoning is wrong; award M1 for the level 1 term and M1 for the main memory term, but no A1 for the total.

Part (b) expects 12.0 cycles and the factor 2.13. Accept 2.1. A candidate who writes 9 cycles for main memory but forgets to add the 3 cycles of level 1 hit time scores M1 only.

Part (c) needs both directions: main memory is small because it is rarely reached, and level 2 is large because its hit rate is conditional on a level 1 miss. One direction only scores M1.

Part (d) is an evaluation and must engage with the numbers. A bare recommendation with no reference to 5.64, 12.0 or 2.13 scores no more than M1. Award the remaining marks for recognising that the level 2 cache is cheap in area relative to the level 1 cache, and for noting that the answer depends on the workload.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on a conditional-probability structure that is disguised as a sum. Three levels are drawn in a pyramid, each with a hit rate and a time, and the shape invites the reader to weight each time by the hit rate printed beside it and add. That is the trap. The hit rate of the level 1 cache is unconditional, because every reference is presented to it, but the hit rate of the level 2 cache is conditional on having missed in level 1, and main memory has no hit rate at all because it always answers. The three numbers are therefore not the same kind of quantity and cannot be combined by the same rule.

The consequence is a factor-of-two error in the answer that decides the question. A candidate who weights level 2 by 0.80 obtains $0.94 \times 3 + 0.80 \times 14 + 0.012 \times 150 = 15.8$ cycles, which makes the level 2 cache look as though it were adding 11.2 cycles to every reference rather than 0.84. A candidate who weights level 2 by 0.06 but forgets main memory obtains 3.66 cycles, which is too small. Both errors are arithmetically clean, so neither is caught by checking the working; only the conditional structure catches them.

Part (b) is the payoff. Computing the average again without level 2 gives 12.0 cycles, so the cache is worth 6.36 cycles per reference and a factor of 2.13 in average access time. A candidate carrying the 15.8 figure forward would instead conclude that removing the cache costs almost nothing, because 15.8 already contains the 11.2 that the cache was wrongly charged with. The evaluation in part (d) therefore cannot be answered correctly unless part (a) is right, which is what makes the arc work: the opening part carries the idea and the closing part tests whether it was understood.

The figure is load-bearing rather than decorative. The hit rates and the three times are printed only in the figure, the direction of the hand-off between levels is drawn only there, and the caption states that every reference is presented to level 1 first. Nothing in the stem repeats those numbers, so an answer cannot be produced without reading the diagram, which is the test STANDARD.md 4.3.1 sets for a figure.

Challenge lever
The three levels look additive, so the obvious method is to weight each level's own access time by its own hit rate and add the three products. That charges the level 2 hit time to every reference and main memory to almost none, because the weights are conditional rather than marginal: level 2 is consulted only on the 0.06 of references that miss in level 1, and main memory only on the 0.012 that miss in both. The error is invisible in the arithmetic, which is correct at every step, and it only surfaces when the two configurations are compared.
