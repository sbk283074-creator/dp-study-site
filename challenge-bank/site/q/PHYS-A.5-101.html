A.5 Galilean and special relativity

Physics HL · PHYS-A.5-101

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.5 (Galilean and special relativity)P1 · Section A5 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedStateCalculateDetermine

Question

Five independent multiple-choice questions on special relativity. Each stands alone, so any one of them can be attempted without the others. Unless a question says otherwise, $c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and the Lorentz factor is $\gamma = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^{2}/c^{2}}}$. All frames referred to are inertial, and every speed quoted is measured relative to the frame named in that question rather than relative to any other object.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    A spacecraft receding from Earth at $0.60c$ switches on a forward-pointing lamp. What speed does an observer on Earth measure for the light leaving the lamp?

    (State)
  2. [1 mark](b)

    A muon has a mean lifetime of $2.2\ \mathrm{\mu s}$ measured in its own rest frame. What mean lifetime does a laboratory observer measure for muons travelling at $0.98c$?

    (Calculate)
  3. [1 mark](c)

    A rod of proper length $100\ \mathrm{m}$ moves lengthwise at $0.80c$ past a stationary observer. What length does that observer measure?

    (Calculate)
  4. [1 mark](d)

    Two spacecraft approach each other head-on, each moving at $0.75c$ relative to Earth. At what speed does the pilot of one see the other approach?

    (Determine)
  5. [1 mark](e)

    An electron has total energy $1.533\ \mathrm{MeV}$. Taking its rest energy as $0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}$, what is its speed?

    (Calculate)

Total: 5 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) A. The second postulate fixes the speed of light at $c$ for every inertial observer, so the source's motion is irrelevant and no transformation is needed. (A1) B and C are the two Galilean habits, and they are listed because $1.60c$ and $0.40c$ are the numbers a student actually produces rather than numbers chosen at random.

(b) A. The lifetime quoted for the muon at rest is the proper time, so it is the shortest of all measured lifetimes and the laboratory value is larger: $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.98^{2}} = 5.03$, giving $5.03 \times 2.2 = 11\ \mathrm{\mu s}$. (A1) This is the effect that lets muons created in the upper atmosphere reach sea level at all.

(c) A. The proper length is measured in the rod's own frame, and the moving observer measures $L = L_{0}/\gamma = 100/1.67 = 60\ \mathrm{m}$. (A1) The identification of which length is proper is the whole question; the division is trivial once that is settled.

(d) A. Neither spacecraft's frame is the Earth frame, so the speeds must be combined with $u' = (u+v)/(1+uv/c^{2}) = 1.50/1.5625 = 0.96c$. (A1) The denominator is what keeps the result below $c$.

(e) A. $\gamma = E/E_{0} = 1.533/0.511 = 3.00$, and inverting $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}$ gives $\beta = \sqrt{1-1/9} = 0.943$. (A1) The three distractors are all the same omission at different depths.

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking. (a) Accept $3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ written in full; no credit for any option that depends on the source speed. (b) Accept $11$ to $11.1\ \mathrm{\mu s}$; award nothing if the candidate quotes $\gamma$ correctly and then divides, since the direction of the effect is the point being tested. (c) Accept $60\ \mathrm{m}$ or $60.0\ \mathrm{m}$; a candidate who states that the transverse dimensions are unchanged shows useful extra knowledge and should not be penalised. (d) Accept $0.96c$ or $2.88 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; any answer above $c$ is wrong on principle and should be treated as evidence the transformation was not used, whichever sign error produced it. (e) Accept $0.94c$; the exact surd $\sqrt{8/9}$ is preferable but not required. Watch for candidates who convert $1.533\ \mathrm{MeV}$ to joules and then lose a factor of $10^{6}$; the ratio method avoids this entirely and is worth encouraging in feedback.

Why this question is hard

This cluster is built on a single observation: at $0.6c$ and above, the Galilean answer and the relativistic answer differ by far more than experimental uncertainty, so a student cannot fall back on intuition. Every item has a Galilean reading that produces a clean number, and every one of those numbers is offered as a distractor. That is deliberate. A multiple-choice item on relativity is only diagnostic if the wrong options are the ones a candidate actually derives, and $1.50c$, $100\ \mathrm{m}$ and $1.60c$ are all derived, not guessed.

The two items that carry the most weight are (c) and (d), because both require a decision about frames before any formula is applied. In (c) the candidate has to identify which of the two lengths is the proper length, and the word 'proper' is doing real work: it means measured where the object is at rest, not 'the one mentioned first'. In (d) the candidate has to notice that the Earth frame is neither of the two frames being compared, so the speeds quoted cannot simply be added. Both errors are errors of setup, and both are invisible in the final arithmetic because the arithmetic is easy.

Item (e) is included because the relationship between energy and speed is where relativity stops being a set of formulae and becomes a single quantity, $\gamma$, from which everything else follows. Once the candidate sees that the total energy divided by the rest energy *is* the Lorentz factor, the rest is algebra, and the same move works for momentum and for the Doppler factor. That unification is what the syllabus is reaching for, and it is worth saying out loud when going through the item.
