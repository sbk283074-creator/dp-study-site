Two ways to add value, ranked in opposite orders

Business management SL · BM-1.1-001

Unit 1: 1.1 What is a business? (the nature and role of business; adding value; the business functions). Toolkit: SWOT analysis and descriptive statistics.P1 · Section A14 marksdifficulty 4ExplainCalculateEvaluate

Nettle & Vine

Nettle & Vine makes botanical cordial in a converted barn on the edge of the Lake District. Priya, a former food scientist, started the business nine years ago; her brother Tom, who had previously run a small group of cafes, joined two years later. The business employs six people and produces 60 000 litres a year at the single site, which now runs at full capacity on one shift. Tom handles sales and finance and Priya handles product development and production. Neither founder has a formal job title.

Almost all of the cordial is sold in 250 ml bottles to 40 independent cafes, delicatessens and farm shops within 100 miles of the barn. Every bottle carries the Nettle & Vine name, the founders' signatures and a short account of how the recipe was developed. Trade customers pay EUR 4.00 a litre. The bought-in materials and services - fruit, herbs, sugar, bottles, labels and the haulage that brings them in - cost EUR 1.20 a litre. Priya and Tom have never calculated how much value the business adds at each stage of production, and they have never worked out what a typical customer is worth to them.

Two of the six staff have been with the business since it began. The barn is leased from a local farmer on a rolling annual agreement. Priya sources most of her fruit and herbs from growers within the county and has twice refused cheaper imported alternatives, saying that the flavour and the story are the product.

The cordial market has changed since the business started. Supermarket own-label cordials now account for most of the volume sold in the country, and several independent producers have been bought by larger groups in the last three years. A rival producer 30 miles away, which also sells to independent retailers, was acquired by a national drinks company last spring and has since cut its trade prices. Tom believes Nettle & Vine will be squeezed out of its own market within five years if it stays the size it is.

A national supermarket chain has now approached Tom with an own-label contract. The supermarket would sell the cordial under its own brand, in its own bottle, at a price it would set. It wants 180 000 litres a year, delivered in weekly consignments, at EUR 1.55 a litre, for three years. The contract is exclusive: while it runs, Nettle & Vine could not sell its own branded cordial to any other retailer, so the existing trade business would end. The supermarket's own recipe would be used rather than Priya's, and the bottles would carry no Nettle & Vine branding. Deliveries would be steady, week by week, twelve months a year, and the supermarket would charge a penalty for any late delivery.

Because the site is at full capacity, the contract would need a new production line costing EUR 200 000, financed by a bank loan at 8% a year. The bought-in cost per litre would fall to EUR 0.40, because the supermarket would supply the fruit pulp in bulk at cost and would use its own low-cost packaging. Production would need eight staff rather than six. The bank has said that it will want personal guarantees from both founders before it lends.

The supermarket has told Tom that the EUR 1.55 price would be fixed for the first year and then reviewed annually. It has also asked for the right to visit the production site without notice and to change the specification of the cordial, within limits, without renegotiating the price. Other small suppliers to the same chain have complained publicly that their margins were cut at the first review.

The alternative - Option A - is to change nothing. Nettle & Vine keeps its own brand, its recipe, its 40 trade customers and its six staff. The founders know, however, that the business cannot grow on its current site, that a rival cordial maker has just been bought by a larger drinks group, and that two of their largest trade customers have asked for a lower price next year.

Priya and Tom each take a salary of EUR 26 000 a year, below what they could earn elsewhere, and the business has not paid a dividend in three years. All of the profit is reinvested. The trade customers value the cordial partly because it is not available in supermarkets; one cafe owner told Tom that the day he could buy it in a supermarket would be the day he stopped stocking it.

Priya is opposed to the contract. 'The moment our cordial goes into their bottle with their name on it, we are not a business any more, we are a factory. I did not spend nine years building a recipe for somebody else's label.' Tom disagrees. '180 000 litres at a stroke. We would be secure for three years, we would employ eight people instead of six, and I could finally pay myself properly. Sentiment does not repay a loan.' The two have not spoken to the supermarket since the first meeting, and Tom has not yet told Priya that the contract would require the trade accounts to be given up.

Question

Priya and Tom must decide whether Nettle & Vine should accept the supermarket's exclusive own-label contract.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain two business functions at Nettle & Vine and how each function contributes to the value the business adds.

    (Explain)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Using the customer table, calculate the mean and the median annual spend per account. Then calculate value added, value added per litre and value added per employee for Option A and for the supermarket contract.

    (Calculate)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Using SWOT analysis, evaluate whether Nettle & Vine should accept the supermarket contract. Refer to your calculations and to the objectives of the two founders.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Award up to four marks for two business functions, each explained in the context of Nettle & Vine.

Operations (production). The operations function is the transformation process: it converts bought-in inputs that cost EUR 1.20 a litre into bottled cordial that sells for EUR 4.00 a litre. Almost all of the value the business adds is created here, because the recipe, the brewing and the bottling turn cheap ingredients into a product a customer will pay a premium for. (A1)(A1)

Marketing. The marketing function is what allows the business to charge EUR 4.00 rather than a commodity price. The Nettle & Vine name, the founders' signatures, the story of the recipe and the refusal to use imported fruit are all marketing decisions, and they are the reason 40 independent retailers buy the cordial instead of a cheaper own-label alternative. Marketing therefore captures the value that operations creates. (A1)(A1)

Also creditable: finance (arranging and servicing the EUR 200 000 loan and costing the supermarket contract), human resources (recruiting and training the eight staff the contract would need), and product development (Priya's recipes and the product range).

(b) Customer data. Total annual spend of the 40 accounts is EUR 240 000, so the mean is $EUR 240 000 / 40 = EUR 6 000$. The median is EUR 2 400, because 25 of the 40 accounts spend only EUR 2 400 and the five largest spend EUR 20 000 each. (M1)(A1)

The mean is 2.5 times the median, so the 'average customer' is not a typical customer. The five largest accounts alone provide EUR 100 000, or 41.7% of trade revenue. The business is already more concentrated than the mean figure suggests. (A1)

Value added = revenue minus bought-in materials and services.

Option A: revenue $= 60 000 \times EUR 4.00 = EUR 240 000$; bought-in $= 60 000 \times EUR 1.20 = EUR 72 000$; value added $= EUR 168 000$. Value added per litre $= EUR 168 000 / 60 000 = EUR 2.80$. Value added per employee $= EUR 168 000 / 6 = EUR 28 000$. (M1)(A1)

Supermarket contract: revenue $= 180 000 \times EUR 1.55 = EUR 279 000$; bought-in $= 180 000 \times EUR 0.40 = EUR 72 000$; value added $= EUR 207 000$. Value added per litre $= EUR 207 000 / 180 000 = EUR 1.15$. Value added per employee $= EUR 207 000 / 8 = EUR 25 875$. (M1)(A1)

The two measures disagree. The contract adds more value in total (EUR 207 000 against EUR 168 000) and produces three times the volume, but it adds only EUR 1.15 a litre against EUR 2.80, a fall of 59%, and less value per employee. The contract buys volume with a much thinner margin.

(c) Indicative evaluation. A SWOT built from the case:

Strengths: a distinctive own brand; Priya's recipe; 40 loyal trade accounts; a premium price of EUR 4.00 a litre; a site that is full and profitable.
Weaknesses: no space to grow; no financial analysis; 42% of trade revenue from five accounts; only six staff; the founders' pay is low.
Opportunities: the supermarket contract would give three years of guaranteed volume; the craft drinks market is growing; a rival's takeover shows that small producers are attractive to larger buyers.
Threats: the contract is exclusive and would end the trade business; the brand would disappear from the bottles; the supermarket sets and reviews the price annually; a rival has cut its trade prices.

The quantitative case is closer than it first appears. The contract adds EUR 39 000 more value in total, but the EUR 200 000 loan costs EUR 16 000 a year in interest, so the gain falls to EUR 23 000, about 14% of Option A's value added. The supermarket may review the price after one year, and a cut of even 10% (to EUR 1.40) would reduce the contract's value added by EUR 18 000 and leave the business barely ahead of doing nothing while having given up its brand, its recipe and its independence. The loan also requires personal guarantees from both founders.

The SWOT shows that the contract removes the business's main weakness (no capacity to grow) by destroying its main strength (the brand). The descriptive statistics make the same point from the other direction: the business already depends on five large accounts, and the contract would replace that with dependence on a single customer that sets the price. Priya's objective is to protect the recipe and the identity of the business; Tom's is security and a proper income. The contract delivers Tom's objective only while the supermarket chooses to keep the price.

A supported conclusion is required. Rejecting the contract, or negotiating a non-exclusive, shorter or higher-priced deal, is creditworthy if the reasoning is consistent with the candidate's own figures. A conclusion that rests only on 'growth is good', or only on 'the brand matters', is not.

Markscheme notes

(a) Two functions, two marks each: one mark for naming and describing the function correctly, one for explaining how it adds value in this business. 'Marketing' with no link to the EUR 4.00 price or the brand earns one mark only. Do not credit 'management' or 'administration' as a business function.

(b) Award (M1) for the mean and (A1) for the median; a candidate who computes the mean but not the median cannot reach the comment mark. The comment must identify that the mean is pulled up by the five large accounts - saying only 'the numbers are different' forfeits it. For value added, award (M1) for revenue minus bought-in costs and (A1) for each correct set of three figures; follow through on arithmetic slips. Accept value added per employee rounded to EUR 25 900 or EUR 26 000. Do not accept a candidate who puts the loan interest inside the value added calculation - interest is a financing cost, not a bought-in input - although such a candidate may still earn the evaluation marks in (c) if they use the figure there.

(c) The SWOT marks are for use, not for listing: a candidate who writes four headings and reaches no judgement earns at most two. The discriminating move is comparing the EUR 23 000 net gain with the scale of what is given up. Credit any supported conclusion. Do not credit generic statements about supermarkets or 'big business' that are not tied to the case.

Why this question is hard

The case is built so that the obvious answer - grow - is supported by the headline figures and contradicted by the analysis. The contract triples the volume, raises revenue from EUR 240 000 to EUR 279 000, and adds more value in total than Option A. Tom's case is not stupid, and the item is designed so that a candidate who simply says 'the supermarket is exploiting them' has not done the work.

The lever is that adding value can be measured in two defensible ways, and they point in opposite directions. Per litre, Option A adds EUR 2.80 and the contract adds EUR 1.15, a collapse of 59%: the contract converts a premium brand into a low-margin contract manufacturer. In total, the contract adds more (EUR 207 000 against EUR 168 000), because three times the volume of a thinner margin can still be more value. Neither measure is wrong, and the decision cannot be made without deciding which one the founders are trying to maximise - which is a question about the purpose of the business, not about the arithmetic.

The second layer is financing. The EUR 200 000 loan costs EUR 16 000 a year, which cuts the gain from EUR 39 000 to EUR 23 000. Because the supermarket may review the price after a year, a modest cut wipes that out. The commitment - personal guarantees, a three-year exclusive contract, the loss of the brand - is irreversible in a way the benefit is not.

The descriptive statistics are not decoration. The mean customer spend of EUR 6 000 is two and a half times the median of EUR 2 400, because five accounts dominate. That tells the student the business is already concentrated, so replacing 40 accounts with one is not a reduction in risk but an increase in it.

Classic wrong turns: comparing revenue rather than value added; treating the loan interest as a bought-in cost inside the value added calculation; praising the contract for creating two jobs without noticing that value added per employee falls; and answering (c) with a general discussion of supermarkets that never uses the figures.
