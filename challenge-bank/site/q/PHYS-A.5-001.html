A.5 Galilean and special relativity

Physics HL · PHYS-A.5-001

A.5 Galilean and special relativity (AHL): time dilation, proper time, length contractionP215 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateDetermineExplain

Question

A spacecraft travels in a straight line from Earth to a star. In the Earth frame the star is $8.0$ light years away. The spacecraft travels at constant speed $v$ and the astronauts measure the journey as taking $3.0$ years.

A light year is the distance travelled by light in one year.

  1. [6 marks](a)

    Calculate the Lorentz factor $\gamma$ and hence the speed $v$, as a fraction of $c$.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the distance between Earth and the star as measured in the spacecraft frame.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    On arrival the astronauts send a radio message to Earth. Calculate the time interval, measured on Earth, between the launch of the spacecraft and the receipt of the message.

    (Calculate)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain, without contradiction, how the astronauts can cover $8.0$ light years in $3.0$ years of their own time.

    (Explain)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

Work in units of years and light years, so that $c=1\ \mathrm{ly\,yr^{-1}}$ and $v=\beta c$ with $\beta=v/c$.

(a) Earth-frame journey time: $t=\dfrac{8.0}{\beta}$ years. (A1)

The astronauts measure the proper time (both events — departure and arrival — occur at the same place in the spacecraft), so

$$\tau=\frac{t}{\gamma}=\frac{8.0}{\beta}\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}=3.0$$ (M1)(A1)

Squaring: $\dfrac{64(1-\beta^{2})}{\beta^{2}}=9 \Rightarrow 64-64\beta^{2}=9\beta^{2} \Rightarrow \beta^{2}=\dfrac{64}{73}$ (M1)

$$\beta=\frac{8}{\sqrt{73}}=0.9363,\qquad \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{64}{73}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{9/73}}=\frac{\sqrt{73}}{3}=2.85$$ (A1)(A1)

$$v=0.936c\ \ (\approx2.81\times10^{8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}).$$

Limiting-case check: for a subluminal speed the Lorentz factor must be real and greater than $1$. Since $\beta^{2}=64/73<1$, the speed satisfies $v<c$, and $\gamma=\sqrt{73}/3=2.85>1$; both conditions hold, so the algebra is self-consistent. (R1)

(b) The Earth–star distance is the proper length (the two endpoints are at rest in the Earth frame), so it is contracted in the spacecraft frame:

$$L=\frac{L_{0}}{\gamma}=\frac{8.0}{2.848}=2.81\ \mathrm{ly}$$ (M1)(A1)(A1)

(c) Earth-frame journey time: $t=\dfrac{8.0}{0.9363}=8.54\ \mathrm{yr}$. (A1)

The message travels $8.0$ ly at speed $c$, taking a further $8.0$ yr. (A1)

$$\text{Total}=8.54+8.0=16.5\ \mathrm{yr}$$ (A1)

(d) In the spacecraft frame the distance to be covered is not $8.0$ ly but the contracted distance $2.81$ ly from (b). (A1) The star approaches at $0.936c$, so the journey takes

$$\frac{2.81\ \mathrm{ly}}{0.936\ \mathrm{ly\,yr^{-1}}}=3.0\ \mathrm{yr},$$

which is exactly the astronauts' measured time. (A1)

There is no contradiction: distance and time intervals are frame-dependent, but the relative speed between spacecraft and star is $0.936c$ in *both* frames, so nothing exceeds $c$. The Earth observer says the trip took $8.54$ yr of Earth time; the astronauts say it took $3.0$ yr of their own time and that the distance was shorter. Both are correct. (A1)

A more formal statement of the same point is the invariance of the spacetime interval, $c^{2}\Delta t^{2}-\Delta x^{2}$. Evaluated in the Earth frame with the exact journey time $t=\sqrt{73}\ \mathrm{yr}$, it is $73-8.0^{2}=9$ (in $\mathrm{ly^{2}}$); in the spacecraft frame the two events occur at the same place, so it is $3.0^{2}-0=9$. The frames disagree about $\Delta t$ and $\Delta x$ separately but agree on the interval, which is the proper time, $3.0$ yr. (R1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The proper-time identification is essential — award (M1) only if $\tau=t/\gamma$ is used (i.e. the spacecraft time is the *shorter* one). Candidates who write $t=\gamma\tau$ with $t=8.0/\beta$ inverted forfeit the method. Accept $\gamma=2.85$ and $v=0.936c$; allow $0.94c$ at 2 s.f.

(b) Watch for the direction of the contraction: the proper length is the Earth-frame length, so the spacecraft length is *smaller*. Award at most one mark for $L=\gamma L_{0}=22.8$ ly.

(c) A common omission is the light-travel time of the message. Award the first two marks even if the candidate then forgets to add them.

(d) The mark scheme is looking for two ideas: the contracted distance from (b), and the fact that the relative speed is below $c$ in both frames. 'Time passes more slowly for the astronauts' on its own is not an explanation of the distance and earns nothing.

(a) Alternative method: eliminate $\gamma$ first by writing $t=\gamma\tau$ with $t=8.0/\beta$, then solve the resulting equation in $\beta$; award full marks provided the proper time is identified. Accept $\gamma$ in the range 2.83–2.87 and $\beta$ in 0.93–0.94. Carry at least three significant figures through the working; a final answer rounded to 2 s.f. ($0.94c$) is acceptable.

Why this question is hard

The awkward step is part (a): the speed is the unknown, and it appears *inside* $\gamma$, so the equation $3.0=\frac{8.0}{\beta}\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}$ has to be solved algebraically before any number appears. Students who are used to being handed $v$ and asked for $\gamma$ often freeze here, or try to guess-and-check with a calculator.

The second idea is that there are two different 'distances' and two different 'times' in the problem, and the question only makes sense once you know which is the proper one:

  • The Earth–star separation is a proper length (endpoints at rest in the Earth frame) → it is the largest value, $8.0$ ly.
  • The journey duration is a proper time (both events at the same place in the spacecraft) → it is the smallest value, $3.0$ yr.

Mixing these up is the single most common error, and it is self-consistent enough to look right: it gives $\gamma=1/2.85$ and $v$ still below $c$, so nothing 'feels' wrong.

Part (c) is a separate trap — the message takes 8 years to get home, nearly as long as the journey took in the Earth frame, and omitting it is the standard slip.

The payoff in (d) is the resolution: the astronauts are not 'travelling faster than light'; they simply have less distance to cover. Both observers agree on the relative speed and on the physical outcome; they disagree about how much distance and how much time were involved.

A third wrong turn looks like a paradox: dividing the Earth-frame distance $8.0$ ly by the spacecraft-frame time $3.0$ yr gives a quotient greater than $c$, which is then read as superluminal travel. The error is mixing frames — speed is a distance divided by a time *within a single frame*, and these two quantities belong to different frames. Computed consistently, the Earth observer divides $8.0$ ly by $8.54$ yr and the astronauts divide $2.81$ ly by $3.0$ yr, and both quotients give $0.936c$. A result above $c$ is a signal that the numerator and the denominator were taken from different reference frames.
