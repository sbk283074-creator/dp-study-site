5.11 The moment of a region found from its symmetry, without ever knowing the function

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-5.11-401

Topic 5: CalculusSL 5.11 Definite integrals, including the analytical approach; SL 5.5 Introduction to integration as anti-differentiation; 2.14 Odd and even functionsP1 · Section A6 marksdifficulty 4technology: not_allowedShow thatHenceExplain

Question

The figure shows the graph of a continuous function $f$ on the interval $0 \leq x \leq 4$. The graph is symmetrical about the line $x = 2$; that is,

$$f(2 + u) = f(2 - u) \qquad \text{for every } u \text{ with } 0 \leq u \leq 2.$$

The area of the region between the graph and the $x$-axis is $12$.

Let $I = \displaystyle\int_{0}^{4} x\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$.

(a) Use the substitution $x = 4 - u$ to show that $I = 48 - I$.

(b) Hence find the value of $I$.

(c) A second function $g$ on $0 \leq x \leq 4$ is symmetrical about the line $x = 3$ and also encloses an area of $12$. Explain why the argument used in part (a) gives no information about $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{4} x\,g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Using the substitution $x = 4 - u$, show that $I = 48 - I$.

    (Show that)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Hence find the exact value of the definite integral $I$ defined above.

    (Hence)
  3. [2 marks](c)

    Explain why the same argument gives no information about the corresponding integral for a function symmetrical about the line $x = 3$ on the same interval.

    (Explain)

Total: 6 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) With $x = 4 - u$ we have $\mathrm{d}x = -\mathrm{d}u$ and the limits swap, so $I = \displaystyle\int_{0}^{4} (4 - u)\,f(4 - u)\,\mathrm{d}u$ (M1). By the symmetry, $f(4 - u) = f(2 + (2 - u)) = f(2 - (2 - u)) = f(u)$, so

$$I = \int_{0}^{4} (4 - u)\,f(u)\,\mathrm{d}u = 4\int_{0}^{4} f(u)\,\mathrm{d}u - \int_{0}^{4} u\,f(u)\,\mathrm{d}u = 4 \times 12 - I = 48 - I \quad (A1).$$

(b) $2I = 48$, so $I = 24$ (M1) (A1). This is $2 \times 12$: the integral of $x\,f(x)$ over the region is the abscissa of the axis of symmetry multiplied by the area, which is what a centroid argument would also give, since the centroid of a region with a vertical axis of symmetry lies on that axis.

(c) The substitution $x = 4 - u$ reflects the interval $[0,\,4]$ about the line $x = 2$, and that reflection maps the interval onto itself (M1). Reflecting $[0,\,4]$ about the line $x = 3$ instead sends it to $[2,\,6]$, which is not the interval the integral runs over, so the substituted integral cannot be renamed as the original one and the symmetry of $g$ produces no relation between the integral and itself. A symmetry is only useful here if it is a symmetry of the interval as well as of the function (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for carrying out the substitution correctly, including the interchange of limits; (A1) for using $f(4 - u) = f(u)$ and separating the integral into $4\int f - I$. The separation is the mark: a candidate who writes the substituted integral and stops has not yet produced an equation in $I$.

(b) (M1) for $2I = 48$; (A1) for $I = 24$.

(c) (M1) for noticing that the reflection is of the interval and not only of the function; (A1) for the conclusion. Credit a candidate who makes the point by remarking that $x = 6 - u$ would be the substitution matching an axis at $x = 3$ and that it turns $[0,\,4]$ into $[2,\,6]$. Watch for the converse error, which is to claim the method still works; it does not, and a candidate who thinks it does has not noticed that the limits moved.

Why this question is hard

The item asks for a quantity that normally requires the function and then withholds the function, which is the signal that the function is not needed. The substitution $x = 4 - u$ is not a technique for evaluating an integral here; it is a way of saying that the region can be paired with itself, strip by strip, and that the pair of strips at $2 + u$ and $2 - u$ contributes $(2 + u)\,f + (2 - u)\,f = 4f$ rather than something that depends on the shape of $f$. Once that is seen, the answer is $2 \times 12$ by inspection.

Part (c) is the part that separates understanding from technique. A candidate who has learned 'use the symmetry' as a procedure will apply it to the axis at $x = 3$ without noticing that the axis has to bisect the interval. It does not here: an axis at $x = 3$ reflects the interval out from under itself. The general statement worth carrying away is that the integral of $x\,f(x)$ over a region is the area times the $x$-coordinate of the centroid, and the centroid sits on an axis of symmetry only when the axis is one about which the whole region -- interval included -- is symmetric.

The answer, twice the area, is then one line away.
