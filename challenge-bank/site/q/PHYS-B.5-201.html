B.5 A filament lamp is non-ohmic, so a series circuit containing one cannot be solved by adding resistances; the operating point is the intersection of the characteristic with a load line

Physics HL · PHYS-B.5-201

Theme B: The particulate nature of matterB.5 (Current and circuits: current-voltage characteristics, ohmic and non-ohmic conductors, resistance as the ratio of potential difference to current, series circuits, electrical power)P1 · Section B12 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedStateCalculateDetermineExplain

A student's measurement of a filament lamp

A student investigates a filament lamp. The lamp is connected in turn to a variable supply, and for each setting of the supply the potential difference across the lamp is measured with a voltmeter and the current through it with an ammeter. The voltmeter has a resolution of 0.1 V and the ammeter a resolution of 0.005 A. Both meters are treated as ideal. The student's readings are given in the table, and the graph shows the characteristic obtained from them. The lamp is then used in a different circuit: it is connected in series with a fixed resistor of resistance 12 ohm and a battery of electromotive force 6.0 V whose internal resistance is negligible.

Question

Use the graph to answer the following. Give all numerical answers to two significant figures.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    State what the shape of the characteristic shows about how the resistance of the lamp changes as the potential difference across it increases, and give the physical reason for the change.

    (State)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the resistance of the lamp at a potential difference of 3.0 V and at a potential difference of 6.0 V.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    For the circuit containing the lamp, the 12 ohm resistor and the 6.0 V battery, determine the potential difference across the lamp and the current in the circuit. You should draw a suitable straight line on the graph and explain how you obtain it.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    A student who has not drawn the line assumes that the lamp has a constant resistance equal to the value you calculated at 6.0 V, and so predicts a current of 0.21 A. Explain, by reference to the graph, why this prediction is too small.

    (Explain)

Reveal the answer

(a) The characteristic is a curve whose gradient decreases as the potential difference increases, so the resistance of the lamp increases as the potential difference increases (A1). The reason is that a larger current transfers more energy per second to the filament, so the filament becomes hotter and its temperature rises; in a metal the lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude, so the charge carriers collide with them more often and the resistance rises (A1).

(b) At $3.0$ V the graph gives $I = 0.250$ A, so $R = \tfrac{3.0}{0.250} = 12$ ohm (A1). At $6.0$ V the graph gives $I = 0.360$ A, so $R = \tfrac{6.0}{0.360} = 17$ ohm (A1)(A1). The two values differ, which is the statement that the lamp is not ohmic.

(c) In the series circuit the potential difference across the lamp and the potential difference across the resistor must add to the electromotive force, so $V_{\text{lamp}} + IR = 6.0$, that is $I = \tfrac{6.0 - V}{12}$ (M1). This is the equation of a straight line on the graph of $I$ against $V$: it passes through $(0,\,0.50)$ and through $(6.0,\,0)$, so it is drawn between those two points (A1). The current in the circuit must satisfy both this line and the lamp's characteristic, so the operating point is where the line crosses the curve (M1). Reading that intersection, $V = 3.0$ V and $I = 0.25$ A (A1). Check: the resistor then has $0.25 \times 12 = 3.0$ V across it, and $3.0 + 3.0 = 6.0$ V, which is the electromotive force.

(d) The value $17$ ohm is the resistance of the lamp only at the operating point it reaches when $6.0$ V is across it, where the filament is at its hottest (M1). In the series circuit the lamp carries a smaller current and is therefore cooler, and the graph shows that a cooler filament has a smaller resistance: at the operating point the ratio $\tfrac{V}{I}$ is $12$ ohm, not $17$ ohm (A1). Treating the lamp as a fixed $17$ ohm resistor gives $I = \tfrac{6.0}{12 + 17} = 0.21$ A, but that value is self-contradictory: the graph shows that at the $3.5$ V the model would put across the lamp the true current is $0.27$ A, not $0.21$ A. The constant-resistance assumption therefore understates the current by about $16\%$ (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for resistance increasing with potential difference, (A1) for the temperature explanation in terms of increased lattice vibration or more frequent collisions. A candidate who says only that 'the lamp heats up' without linking the temperature to the resistance scores one mark at most. Do not accept 'the resistance increases because the current increases' as the physical reason: the current and the resistance are both consequences of the temperature.
(b) (A1) for the 3.0 V value, (A1)(A1) for the 6.0 V value and the comparison. Accept 11.5 to 12.5 ohm and 16.5 to 17.5 ohm, since both are read from a graph. A candidate who gives 12 ohm for both, by assuming the lamp is ohmic, scores the first (A1) only. The unit must be present somewhere in the part for the final (A1) to be awarded.
(c) (M1) for obtaining the load-line equation from the circuit, (A1) for the two intercepts and the line drawn between them, (M1) for identifying the intersection of the line with the characteristic as the operating point, (A1) for the pair of values. Accept 3.0 V and 0.25 A within the reading tolerance of the graph, and accept a candidate who reads 2.9 V and 0.26 A. A candidate who instead substitutes the 17 ohm value into $I = 6.0/(12+17)$ and reports 0.21 A and 3.5 V scores no marks in this part, because no load line has been drawn and the operating point has not been found. A correct check that the two potential differences sum to 6.0 V earns the final (A1) if the reading is otherwise correct.
(d) (M1) for identifying 17 ohm as the resistance at the hottest operating point rather than a constant, (A1) for the graph evidence that the resistance is smaller at the lower potential difference, (A1) for the size of the error or for the self-contradiction. The strongest answers point out that the model's own output is inconsistent with the curve, since at the 3.5 V it predicts the lamp carries 0.27 A. Accept a correct statement of the direction of the error, that the prediction is too small, without the percentage.

Why this question is hard

This item exists because a filament lamp is the one component in the course whose resistance is not a number. Every method a candidate has for series circuits begins by adding resistances, and that method is unavailable here: the lamp's resistance depends on the current, which is the unknown. The item forces the candidate to notice that the problem is not one of arithmetic but of finding a simultaneous solution, and that the characteristic is a picture of all the pairs that are possible for the lamp.

The load line is the tool that resolves it, and it is the reason the figure is load-bearing rather than decorative. The rest of the circuit imposes a linear relation between the two unknowns: whatever current flows, the two potential differences must sum to 6.0 V, which fixes a straight line from (0, 0.50) to (6.0, 0). The circuit therefore permits only pairs of values on that line, the lamp permits only pairs on its characteristic, and the operating point is where the two sets meet. A candidate who understands this can answer the part in three lines; a candidate who does not will reach for a resistance value and get a number that looks plausible.

Part (d) is aimed at that number. The trap is not that 17 ohm is wrong; it is right, at 6.0 V. It is the *reuse* of a value measured in one operating condition in a different one that fails. The item is designed so that the failure is detectable from the graph alone: the constant-resistance model puts 3.5 V across the lamp, and the characteristic says that at 3.5 V the lamp carries 0.27 A, not the 0.21 A the model assumed. The model contradicts the data it was built from, and that is a stronger diagnosis than the observation that the answer is merely inaccurate.

The numbers are chosen so that the correct operating point is clean. The lamp's characteristic passes through 3.0 V and 0.250 A, and the load line for a 6.0 V supply and a 12 ohm resistor passes through the same point, so the intersection is exactly at the mid-point of the load line and a candidate reading the graph to the nearest division lands on it. The coincidence is deliberate: it means the mark turns on the method, not on how carefully the candidate can interpolate.

Challenge lever
The circuit cannot be solved by arithmetic. The lamp's resistance is not a property of the lamp but of the operating point, so the current is fixed only where the lamp's characteristic crosses the straight line the rest of the circuit imposes. The lever is that the candidate must supply that line from the circuit and read the intersection off the graph, instead of substituting a single resistance value.

Adaptation
The source genre asks for the characteristic and then for the resistance at a single point. Here the characteristic is carried into a second circuit that cannot be solved by substitution, so the item tests whether the candidate can build the load line from the circuit description and treat the graph as the solution set rather than as a source of one resistance value. The final part is designed so that the naive constant-resistance model is not merely inaccurate but self-contradictory against the same graph.
Verification
The characteristic was generated in Python as I = 0.1403 V^0.526 and the load line solved against it by bisection to 200 iterations, giving an intersection at V = 2.9996 V and I = 0.2500 A, which rounds to the 3.0 V and 0.25 A quoted; the exact root and the load-line value at that root were confirmed to agree to 5.6e-17. The two chord resistances, the naive constant-resistance current, the lamp current the naive model implies at its own predicted potential difference, and the power balance were all computed directly. The figure path is sampled from the same power law, so the curve passes through the tabulated points by construction.
