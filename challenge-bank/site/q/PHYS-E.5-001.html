The binding energy that must be counted twelve times

Physics HL · PHYS-E.5-001

Theme E: Nuclear and quantum physicsE.5 Fusion and stars (fusion as the energy source of stars, energy release in fusion reactions, the equilibrium between outward radiation pressure and inward gravitational force, conditions for fusion, stellar evolution)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatDetermineExplain

Question

A red giant star has exhausted the hydrogen in its core and is now fusing helium into carbon through the triple-alpha process,
$$3\,^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} \rightarrow\ ^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C} + \gamma.$$
During this phase the star's luminosity is $L = 3.0\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{W}$, and its core contains $1.0\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$ of helium when the burning begins. On the main sequence the same star radiated at $3.0\times10^{27}\ \mathrm{W}$ for $1.0\times10^{9}$ years. The star is in hydrostatic equilibrium: the inward gravitational force on the core is balanced by the outward pressure of the radiation produced by the fusion reactions.

Data (2025 data booklet): binding energy per nucleon of $^{4}\mathrm{He} = 7.074\ \mathrm{MeV}$; binding energy per nucleon of $^{12}\mathrm{C} = 7.680\ \mathrm{MeV}$; mass of a $^{4}\mathrm{He}$ nucleus $= 4.0026\ \mathrm{u}$; $1\ \mathrm{u} = 931.5\ \mathrm{MeV}\,c^{-2}$; $1\ \mathrm{u} = 1.661\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$; $1\ \mathrm{MeV} = 1.602\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{J}$; $1\ \mathrm{year} = 3.16\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s}$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that the energy released in one triple-alpha reaction is about $7.3\ \mathrm{MeV}$, and explain why the binding energies per nucleon cannot be subtracted directly.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that the fraction of the rest energy of the three helium nuclei that is released in one reaction is about $6.5\times10^{-4}$. Hence determine the total energy that the helium in the core can release.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine how long the core can sustain the star's present luminosity, in years. Hence show that this helium-burning phase is shorter than the main-sequence lifetime by a factor of about sixteen, and account for that factor.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Explain how the balance between the outward radiation pressure and the inward gravitational force keeps the core temperature and the fusion rate stable, and hence explain why the star's luminosity does not run away.

    (Explain)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The energy released is the increase in the total binding energy of the nuclei: (M1)
$$Q = B(^{12}\mathrm{C}) - 3B(^{4}\mathrm{He})$$
Each binding energy per nucleon must first be multiplied by its mass number, because the two nuclei do not contain the same number of nucleons: (M1)
$$B(^{4}\mathrm{He}) = 4(7.074) = 28.296\ \mathrm{MeV}, \qquad B(^{12}\mathrm{C}) = 12(7.680) = 92.16\ \mathrm{MeV}$$ (A1)
$$Q = 92.16 - 3(28.296) = 92.16 - 84.888 = 7.27\ \mathrm{MeV}$$ (A1)
Subtracting the per-nucleon figures directly would give $7.680 - 7.074 = 0.606\ \mathrm{MeV}$, which is the release per nucleon, not per reaction; the reaction binds twelve nucleons, so that answer is twelve times too small.

(b) The rest energy of the three helium nuclei consumed is (M1)
$$3m_{\mathrm{He}}c^{2} = 3(4.0026)(931.5) = 1.119\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{MeV}$$ (A1)
so the fraction of the fuel rest energy that appears as released energy is
$$f = \frac{7.272}{1.1185\times10^{4}} = 6.50\times10^{-4}$$ (A1)(AG)
Only about one part in $1500$ of the mass of the helium is converted. The energy reservoir is therefore
$$E = f\,Mc^{2} = (6.50\times10^{-4})(1.0\times10^{30})(2.998\times10^{8})^{2} = 5.84\times10^{43}\ \mathrm{J}$$ (A1)

(c) The time for which that reservoir can supply the luminosity is (M1)
$$t = \frac{E}{L} = \frac{5.84\times10^{43}}{3.0\times10^{28}} = 1.95\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s}$$
$$t = \frac{1.95\times10^{15}}{3.16\times10^{7}} = 6.2\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{years}$$ (A1)
The main sequence radiated $E_{\mathrm{MS}} = (3.0\times10^{27})(1.0\times10^{9})(3.16\times10^{7}) = 9.48\times10^{43}\ \mathrm{J}$, so (M1)
$$\frac{t_{\mathrm{He}}}{t_{\mathrm{MS}}} = \frac{E_{\mathrm{He}}/L_{\mathrm{He}}}{E_{\mathrm{MS}}/L_{\mathrm{MS}}}= \frac{E_{\mathrm{He}}}{E_{\mathrm{MS}}}\times\frac{L_{\mathrm{MS}}}{L_{\mathrm{He}}}= 0.616\times\frac{1}{10} = 6.2\times10^{-2}$$ (A1)
which is a phase about sixteen times shorter. The factor of sixteen splits cleanly in two: the reservoir is smaller by a factor $0.62$, because helium burning releases only $7.272/12 = 0.606\ \mathrm{MeV}$ per nucleon against about $6.7\ \mathrm{MeV}$ per nucleon for hydrogen, and the star is now radiating ten times faster. Neither effect alone explains the brevity of the phase; the product does.

(d) In equilibrium the energy generated in the core each second equals the energy radiated, so the core neither heats nor cools on average. (A1) If the fusion rate rises, the core temperature rises; the radiation pressure depends strongly on temperature and rises faster than the weight of the overlying layers, so the core expands. (A1) Expansion does work against gravity and lowers the temperature, which reduces the fusion rate back towards its previous value. (A1) The feedback is therefore negative, so the star settles where energy generation matches the rate at which energy can escape, and the luminosity stays stable instead of running away. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for identifying the energy released with the increase in total binding energy; the second (M1) is for converting each binding energy per nucleon into a total by multiplying by the mass number. A candidate who subtracts the per-nucleon values directly and reports $0.61\ \mathrm{MeV}$ for the whole reaction has made the error the question is built to expose and forfeits the last two marks. Accept $7.2$ to $7.3\ \mathrm{MeV}$.

(b) The first (M1) is for recognising that the fraction required is $Q$ divided by the rest energy of the fuel, not by the rest energy of a single nucleus; the factor of three is the point. Accept $1.12\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{MeV}$ for the denominator and $6.4$ to $6.6\times10^{-4}$ for the fraction. Follow-through from (a) applies, and the reservoir mark is available to a candidate whose fraction is wrong provided the multiplication by $Mc^{2}$ is correct. Accept $5.7$ to $6.0\times10^{43}\ \mathrm{J}$. A candidate who instead works out the reaction rate and the mass consumption rate and divides the core mass by it has taken a longer but equivalent route and should be credited the same marks.

(c) Accept $1.9$ to $2.0\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s}$ and $6.1$ to $6.3\times10^{7}$ years. The comparison mark is for forming the ratio of the two lifetimes as the ratio of two energy-to-luminosity quotients; the final mark is for decomposing the factor of sixteen into a reservoir term and a luminosity term. A candidate who quotes only the lower yield per nucleon, or only the higher luminosity, has given half the argument.

(d) Any two of the three links in the feedback loop earn the first two marks; the last mark requires the conclusion that the luminosity is stable. A candidate who says the star simply gets hotter without limit has not used the pressure-temperature link and forfeits the final mark.

Why this question is hard

The triple-alpha reaction is a good example of a fusion step whose energy yield cannot be read from the masses, because the masses are not supplied: the data give binding energy per nucleon, and each of those must be converted into a total binding energy before subtracting. That conversion is where the question bites. The two per-nucleon values differ by only $0.606\ \mathrm{MeV}$, and a candidate who subtracts them and stops has produced an energy twelve times too small.

The harder idea, and the one that separates this from a fission-reactor question, is that the yield is best understood as a *fraction* of the rest energy of the fuel. Once the fraction is known the reaction rate is an irrelevant intermediate: the whole reservoir is the fraction multiplied by $Mc^{2}$, and no counting of reactions is needed. Students reach for the rate because that is the habit formed by reactor problems, and carrying it through here costs two extra steps and two extra places to slip.

The comparison in (c) is the real test. Both phases are reservoirs divided by a luminosity, so the ratio of the lifetimes is the ratio of the reservoirs multiplied by the inverse ratio of the luminosities. That decomposition is what makes the answer explainable rather than merely computable: the fuel term contributes a factor $0.62$ and the luminosity term a factor $10$, and their product is the factor of sixteen. A candidate who attributes the brevity of helium burning entirely to the lower yield per nucleon gets the direction right and the size wrong by an order of magnitude, because in this star the rise in luminosity is the larger of the two effects.

Part (d) closes the loop. Hydrostatic equilibrium is not a static condition but a feedback: radiation pressure and fusion rate are coupled through temperature with opposite signs, so the star is self-regulating. The common wrong answer is that equilibrium means nothing changes, which misses that the stability is produced by the response to a perturbation rather than by the absence of one.
