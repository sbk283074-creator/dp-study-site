The binding energy that must be counted twelve times

Physics HL · PHYS-E.5-001

E.5 Fusion and stars (fusion as the energy source of stars, energy release in fusion reactions, the equilibrium between outward radiation pressure and inward gravitational force, conditions for fusion, stellar evolution)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatDetermineExplain

Question

A red giant star has exhausted the hydrogen in its core and is now fusing helium into carbon through the triple-alpha process,
$$3\,^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} \rightarrow\ ^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C} + \gamma.$$
During this phase the star's luminosity is $L = 3.0\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{W}$, and its core contains $1.0\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$ of helium when the burning begins. The star is in hydrostatic equilibrium: the inward gravitational force on the core is balanced by the outward pressure of the radiation produced by the fusion reactions.

Data (2025 data booklet): binding energy per nucleon of $^{4}\mathrm{He} = 7.074\ \mathrm{MeV}$; binding energy per nucleon of $^{12}\mathrm{C} = 7.680\ \mathrm{MeV}$; mass of a $^{4}\mathrm{He}$ nucleus $= 4.0026\ \mathrm{u}$; $1\ \mathrm{u} = 1.661\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$; $1\ \mathrm{MeV} = 1.602\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{J}$; $1\ \mathrm{year} = 3.16\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s}$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that the energy released in one triple-alpha reaction is about $7.3\ \mathrm{MeV}$, and explain why the binding energies per nucleon cannot be subtracted directly.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the number of triple-alpha reactions per second and the mass of helium consumed per second in this star.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine how long the helium in the core can sustain the star's luminosity, in years, and state one reason why the real helium-burning phase is shorter than this estimate.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Explain how the balance between the outward radiation pressure and the inward gravitational force keeps the core temperature and the fusion rate stable, and hence explain why the star's luminosity does not run away.

    (Explain)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The energy released is the increase in the total binding energy of the nuclei: (M1)
$$Q = B(^{12}\mathrm{C}) - 3B(^{4}\mathrm{He})$$
Each binding energy per nucleon must be multiplied by its mass number before subtracting: (M1)
$$B(^{4}\mathrm{He}) = 4(7.074) = 28.296\ \mathrm{MeV}, \qquad B(^{12}\mathrm{C}) = 12(7.680) = 92.16\ \mathrm{MeV}$$ (A1)
$$Q = 92.16 - 3(28.296) = 92.16 - 84.888 = 7.27\ \mathrm{MeV}$$ (A1)
That is $7.27/12 = 0.61\ \mathrm{MeV}$ per nucleon, about a tenth of the $6.7\ \mathrm{MeV}$ per nucleon released when hydrogen fuses to helium, so helium must be fused far faster to supply the same luminosity.

(b) The luminosity is the energy released each second, so the reaction rate is
$$R = \frac{L}{Q} = \frac{3.0\times10^{28}}{7.272 \times 1.602\times10^{-13}} = \frac{3.0\times10^{28}}{1.165\times10^{-12}} = 2.58\times10^{40}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$$ (M1)(A1)
Three helium nuclei are consumed per reaction, each of mass $4.0026\ \mathrm{u} = 6.648\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$: (M1)
$$\dot{m} = 3Rm_{\mathrm{He}} = 3(2.575\times10^{40})(6.648\times10^{-27}) = 5.14\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$$ (A1)

(c) With $1.0\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$ of helium available,
$$t = \frac{1.0\times10^{30}}{5.14\times10^{14}} = 1.9\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s} = \frac{1.9\times10^{15}}{3.16\times10^{7}} = 6.2\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{years}$$ (M1)(A1)
The estimate assumes that the whole core mass fuses and that the luminosity stays constant. (A1) Both assumptions make it an overestimate: only the helium in the convective core burns, and the luminosity rises as the core contracts, so the real phase is shorter. (A1)

(d) In equilibrium the energy generated in the core each second equals the energy radiated, so the core neither heats nor cools on average. (A1) If the fusion rate rises, the core temperature rises; the radiation pressure depends strongly on temperature and rises faster than the weight of the overlying layers, so the core expands. (A1) Expansion does work against gravity and lowers the temperature, which reduces the fusion rate back towards its previous value. (A1) The feedback is therefore negative, so the star settles where energy generation matches the rate at which energy can escape, and the luminosity stays stable instead of running away. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for identifying the energy released with the increase in total binding energy; the second (M1) is for converting each binding energy per nucleon into a total by multiplying by the mass number. A candidate who subtracts the per-nucleon values directly and reports $0.61\ \mathrm{MeV}$ for the whole reaction has made the error the question is built to expose and forfeits the last two marks. Accept $7.2$ to $7.3\ \mathrm{MeV}$.

(b) The first (M1) is for $L = R\,Q$; the second for the factor of three, since three helium nuclei are consumed in each reaction. Follow-through from (a) applies. Accept $2.6\times10^{40}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $5.1\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$.

(c) Accept $1.9\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s}$ and $6\times10^{7}$ years. The assumption mark is for any physically sensible assumption; the final mark requires the direction of the error, so the shorter phase must be stated.

(d) Any two of the three links in the feedback loop earn the first two marks; the last mark requires the conclusion that the luminosity is stable. A candidate who says the star simply gets hotter without limit has not used the pressure-temperature link.

Why this question is hard

The triple-alpha reaction is a good example of a fusion step whose energy yield cannot be read from the masses, because the masses are not supplied: the data give binding energy per nucleon, and each of those must be converted into a total binding energy before subtracting. That conversion is where the question bites. The two per-nucleon values differ by only $0.606\ \mathrm{MeV}$, and a candidate who subtracts them and stops has produced an energy twelve times too small.

The result is small in a second sense too. Hydrogen burning releases about $6.7\ \mathrm{MeV}$ for every nucleon fused, while the triple-alpha step releases only $0.61\ \mathrm{MeV}$ per nucleon, roughly a tenth as much. A star that has moved on to helium burning must therefore consume helium about eleven times faster than it consumed hydrogen to maintain the same luminosity. This is why the later burning stages of a massive star are so much briefer than the hydrogen-burning stage, even though the core holds less fuel.

Part (d) turns to the equilibrium that keeps the star steady. The fusion rate is extremely sensitive to temperature, so a naive account predicts runaway: a slightly higher temperature gives a much higher rate, which gives a still higher temperature. What prevents this is that the same temperature that raises the rate also raises the radiation pressure, which pushes the core outwards. The expansion cools the core and restores the balance. The two effects therefore partially cancel, and the star sits at the temperature at which the energy generated matches the energy escaping.

Classic wrong turns: reporting the per-nucleon difference as the energy released; forgetting that three helium nuclei are consumed in each reaction; and assuming that a higher fusion rate automatically means a brighter star.

Metadata

Challenge lever
The energy released in the triple-alpha step is a small difference between two large total binding energies, so each binding energy per nucleon has to be multiplied by its mass number before subtracting; the resulting yield is only 0.61 MeV per nucleon, about a tenth of the yield of hydrogen fusion, so the reaction rate needed to supply the star's luminosity is far larger than an estimate based on the luminosity alone would suggest.
