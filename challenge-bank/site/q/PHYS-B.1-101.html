Measuring the specific heat capacity of aluminium

A student embedded a $12.0\ \mathrm{V}$ electrical heater and a thermometer in an aluminium block of mass $0.500\ \mathrm{kg}$, surrounded the block with lagging, and switched the heater on at $t = 0$. The temperature of the room was $21.0\ \mathrm{^{\circ}C}$ and stayed constant. She recorded the temperature of the block every $30\ \mathrm{s}$ for five minutes. The thermometer could be read to the nearest $0.5\ \mathrm{^{\circ}C}$. The heater readings were $V = 12.0 \pm 0.1\ \mathrm{V}$ and $I = 4.00 \pm 0.05\ \mathrm{A}$, and the mass was $0.500 \pm 0.002\ \mathrm{kg}$. The accepted value for aluminium is $900\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

Question

The student intended to use $Pt = mc\Delta\theta$ with the whole data set. This question asks you to do the calculation, then to decide whether the data set supports doing it that way at all.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the mean rate of temperature rise over the first $60\ \mathrm{s}$, and hence determine a value for the specific heat capacity of aluminium. State clearly the assumption your calculation makes about heat loss.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the uncertainty in your value, combining the uncertainties in the power, the mass and the rate of temperature rise. Identify which contribution dominates and explain in one sentence why.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Fit a straight line to all eleven readings and tabulate the residuals. Describe the pattern in them, and state what that pattern establishes about the heat loss from the block.

    (Describe)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Identify the anomalous reading, suggest a specific cause that is consistent with the readings either side of it, and state what the student should have done when it occurred. Hence evaluate whether the value from part (a) is consistent with the accepted value.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Over the first $60\ \mathrm{s}$ the temperature rises from $21.0$ to $27.0\ \mathrm{^{\circ}C}$, so the mean rate is (M1)
$$\frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}=\frac{6.0}{60}=0.100\ \mathrm{K\,s^{-1}}$$
The input power is $P = VI = 12.0 \times 4.00 = 48.0\ \mathrm{W}$. (A1) Assuming no energy is lost to the surroundings, $P = mc\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t}$, so (M1)
$$c=\frac{P}{m(\Delta\theta/\Delta t)}=\frac{48.0}{0.500 \times 0.100}=960\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$$ (A1)
The assumption is that all $48\ \mathrm{W}$ goes into the block and none escapes through the lagging.

(b) Power: $\dfrac{\delta V}{V}=\dfrac{0.1}{12.0}=0.83\%$ and $\dfrac{\delta I}{I}=\dfrac{0.05}{4.00}=1.25\%$, so $\dfrac{\delta P}{P}=\sqrt{0.83^{2}+1.25^{2}}=1.5\%$. (A1) Mass: $\dfrac{0.002}{0.500}=0.4\%$. Rate: each reading carries $\pm 0.5\ \mathrm{K}$, so the rise carries $\sqrt{0.5^{2}+0.5^{2}}=0.71\ \mathrm{K}$ on $6.0\ \mathrm{K}$, which is $11.8\%$. (M1)(A1) Combining in quadrature,
$$\frac{\delta c}{c}=\sqrt{1.5^{2}+0.4^{2}+11.8^{2}}=11.9\%$$
so $\delta c \approx 110\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and $c = 960 \pm 110\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. (A1) The rate dominates, because a $6\ \mathrm{K}$ rise is only twelve times the resolution of the thermometer while the other two quantities are measured to better than $2\%$.

(c) The least-squares line is $\theta = 0.0817\,t + 22.0$. (A1) The residuals, in the order of the table, are
$$-1.02,\ -0.47,\ +0.08,\ +0.63,\ +0.68,\ +0.73,\ +0.78,\ +0.33,\ -1.12,\ -0.07,\ -0.52$$
(M1)(A1) Setting aside the $240\ \mathrm{s}$ point, they rise from about $-1.0$ to $+0.8$ and fall back to about $-0.5$: a smooth arch rather than scatter. That is the signature of a systematic effect. The data are concave downwards, meaning the rate of rise decreases with time, and that is exactly what a loss rate proportional to $(\theta - \theta_{\text{room}})$ produces while the input power stays constant. Random error alone would give residuals of alternating sign with no trend. (A1)

(d) The anomalous reading is at $t = 240\ \mathrm{s}$: $40.5\ \mathrm{^{\circ}C}$, which is about $1.3\ \mathrm{K}$ below the value expected from the readings at $210\ \mathrm{s}$ and $270\ \mathrm{s}$. (A1) The next reading returns to the smooth trend, so the curve was not permanently displaced; a genuine extra loss of heat would have lowered every later reading, not just one. A cause consistent with that is that the thermometer probe was disturbed or pulled slightly out of its hole at that moment, so it briefly read closer to the temperature of the air. (A1) The probe should have been clamped and its seating checked before the run, and any disturbance logged at the time; if one occurred, the run should be repeated rather than the point removed afterwards. Finally, $960 \pm 110$ contains $900$, so the result is consistent with the accepted value; the value is high, in the direction the residual analysis predicts, because heat loss reduces the measured rate of rise. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Accept $0.100\ \mathrm{K\,s^{-1}}$ and $960\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$; the assumption mark is separate and should not be given for 'heat losses are negligible' written without reference to the interval used. (b) Accept any combined uncertainty between $9\%$ and $14\%$; the mark scheme is checking that the candidate identifies the temperature difference as dominant and says why. A candidate who adds the percentage uncertainties linearly rather than in quadrature is being conservative and should not be penalised. (c) The residuals need not be tabulated to two decimals; the mark is for the shape. Accept 'the residuals are positive in the middle and negative at both ends' as the description, and require the word systematic or an equivalent. Do not credit 'the graph is curved' on its own, since that is the observation and not the inference. (d) Accept any cause that predicts a single-point dip followed by a return to trend; specifically reject friction-style answers that would displace all later points. The consistency judgement must quote the uncertainty interval, not just say 'close enough'.

Why this question is hard

This item is built around a trap that catches good students as often as weak ones. The natural calculation gives $960$ against an accepted $900$, which looks like a $7\%$ error, and the instinct is to hunt for the mistake. There is no mistake. The uncertainty on the value is about $12\%$, almost all of it from measuring a $6\ \mathrm{K}$ rise with a thermometer that reads to the nearest half degree, so $960$ and $900$ are the same number to within the resolution of the apparatus. Recognising that requires an uncertainty budget, which is why part (b) is worth four marks on its own.

The residual plot in part (c) then supplies what the uncertainty budget cannot: it says something about the *direction* of the error, not just its size. A student who is told that heat loss makes the measured rate too small will usually repeat it as a fact. A student who plots the residuals sees the arch, sees that it is systematic rather than random, and can then say which way the error pushes and why — the loss rate grows as the block gets hotter while the input power does not. That is the difference between knowing a correction and being able to tell whether it applies to a particular data set.

Part (d) closes the loop by asking a question that is really about the shape of the data rather than the size of the numbers. The $240\ \mathrm{s}$ reading is low, but the $270\ \mathrm{s}$ reading is back on trend, and that single fact eliminates most of the obvious explanations. A disturbance to the probe is consistent with it; a genuine change in the heat loss is not, because that would have moved the whole tail of the curve. The same reasoning is what makes the prescribed action a repeat rather than a deletion: the point carries information about what went wrong, and deleting it afterwards throws that away while leaving the run just as compromised as it was.
