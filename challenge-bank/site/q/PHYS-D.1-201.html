D.1 The field of a planet read from its g-against-r graph, and the radius at which the inverse-square law stops applying

Physics HL · PHYS-D.1-201

Theme D: FieldsD.1 (Gravitational fields: gravitational field strength, g = GM/r^2 outside a spherical mass, the field inside a uniform sphere, mean density, escape speed)P212 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedDetermineCalculateShow thatExplain

Question

The diagram shows how the gravitational field strength $g$ due to a planet varies with the distance $r$ from the centre of the planet. The planet is modelled as a sphere of uniform density. The graph has two distinct branches which meet at $r = R$, the radius of the planet. Use $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}$ N m$^{2}$ kg$^{-2}$. Give all numerical answers to two significant figures.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    From the graph, determine the radius $R$ of the planet and the value of $g$ at its surface, and determine the value of $g$ at a distance $1.5 \times 10^{6}$ m from the centre.

    (Determine)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Use the value of $g$ at the surface to calculate the mass of the planet, and state the unit of your answer.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Show that the mean density of the planet is $4.3 \times 10^{3}$ kg m$^{-3}$. State one assumption your calculation makes about the planet.

    (Show that)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    Explain why the expression $g = \dfrac{GM}{r^{2}}$ must not be used to find the field strength at a distance less than $R$ from the centre.

    (Explain)

Total: 12 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The two branches meet at the kink in the graph, and the kink is at $r = 3.0 \times 10^{6}$ m, so $R = 3.0 \times 10^{6}$ m (A1). The value of $g$ at the kink is the surface value, $g = 3.6$ m s$^{-2}$ (A1). The point $r = 1.5 \times 10^{6}$ m is half of $R$, so it lies on the straight branch, and the graph gives $g = 1.8$ m s$^{-2}$ (A1). Note that the straight branch is what carries the information here: at that distance the surface is still $1.5 \times 10^{6}$ m away and the inverse-square expression, applied blindly, would return $14$ m s$^{-2}$, which is eight times the value the graph shows.

(b) At the surface, $g = \dfrac{GM}{R^{2}}$, so $M = \dfrac{gR^{2}}{G} = \dfrac{3.6 \times (3.0 \times 10^{6})^{2}}{6.67 \times 10^{-11}}$ (M1)(A1). This gives $M = \dfrac{3.6 \times 9.0 \times 10^{12}}{6.67 \times 10^{-11}} = 4.9 \times 10^{23}$ kg (A1).

(c) The volume of a sphere of radius $R$ is $V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^{3} = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi (3.0 \times 10^{6})^{3} = 1.13 \times 10^{20}$ m$^{3}$ (M1)(A1). The mean density is therefore $\rho = \dfrac{M}{V} = \dfrac{4.86 \times 10^{23}}{1.13 \times 10^{20}} = 4.3 \times 10^{3}$ kg m$^{-3}$ (A1)(AG). The calculation assumes that the planet is a sphere of uniform density, which is what allows a single value of $M$ and a single value of $R$ to describe it and what makes the straight branch of the graph possible (A1).

(d) The expression $g = \dfrac{GM}{r^{2}}$ is derived for a point outside a spherically symmetric mass, and its derivation treats the whole mass $M$ as though it acted at the centre (M1). At a distance $r < R$ only the mass inside the sphere of radius $r$ contributes, and that enclosed mass is smaller than $M$, so using the total mass overstates the field. For a uniform planet the enclosed mass falls as $r^{3}$ while the $r^{2}$ in the denominator falls as $r^{2}$, and the two effects leave the field proportional to $r$: that is why the branch inside the surface is a straight line through the origin rather than a curve (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for the radius read from the kink, (A1) for the surface value, (A1) for the interior value. Accept 2.9 to 3.1 for the radius in units of 10^6 m, 3.5 to 3.7 for the surface value and 1.7 to 1.9 for the interior value, since all three are read from a graph. A candidate who reads the interior value as about 14 m s^-2 has applied the inverse-square expression inside the planet and scores no mark for that reading; the graph cannot be read that way, because the curve is not there.
(b) (M1) for rearranging the surface relation, (A1) for the substitution, (A1) for the value. Accept 4.8 to 4.9 in units of 10^23 kg, and accept a candidate who carries forward a slightly different radius from (a) consistently. The unit must be present.
(c) (M1) for the volume formula, (A1) for the volume, (A1) for the division, (AG) for reaching 4.3 x 10^3, (A1) for naming the uniform-density assumption. The assumption mark is not awarded for a statement that the planet is spherical alone, because that is given in the stem; the mark is for the uniformity, which is what the model adds. Follow-through applies from (b).
(d) (M1) for stating that only the enclosed mass contributes, (A1) for the consequence that the enclosed mass is less than the total mass, or for the r^3 against r^2 comparison that makes the interior field proportional to r. A candidate who writes only that the inverse-square law is 'only valid outside' without explaining what changes inside scores (M1) at most.

Why this question is hard

The item turns on a graph with a corner in it, and the corner is the answer. Everything the candidate needs to know about the planet is at the kink: its radius is the value of r where the two branches meet, and its surface field strength is the value of g there. Reading those two numbers is a graphical skill, and the rest of the item is ordinary arithmetic once they have been read.

The trap is what happens in part (a). The candidate has been taught that the gravitational field of a spherical mass is g = GM/r^2, and the graph invites that expression to be applied to the interior point, which is half the radius. Doing so requires a mass, which the candidate does not yet have in part (a), so the trap only springs for a candidate who has already carried out part (b) mentally and returns. When it springs, the answer is 14 m s^-2 against the graph's 1.8 m s^-2, a discrepancy of a factor of eight that is large enough to be noticed by anyone who looks at the graph and small enough to be missed by anyone who does not.

Part (c) contains a second, quieter route to the same number. The gradient of the straight branch is g/R, and for a uniform sphere that gradient is (4/3) pi G rho. A candidate who notices this can obtain the density from the gradient of the interior line without ever computing the mass, which is a genuinely different method and is accepted in full. It is worth naming because it is the clearest evidence that the two branches are not two drawings of the same law: the interior branch is a line precisely because the density is constant, and its gradient measures that density directly.

Part (d) asks for the reason rather than the recipe. The shell theorem is what makes the exterior expression work, and the same theorem is what makes the interior expression different: at an interior point the material outside the point contributes nothing, so the mass that matters is the mass within radius r. A candidate who can say this, and can add that for uniform density that mass grows as r^3, has understood the structure of the graph rather than memorised two formulas.

Verification
The mass, the volume, the mean density, the interior and exterior field values and the escape speed were recomputed in Python from R = 3.0e6 m and g = 3.6 m/s^2 with G = 6.67e-11. The density was obtained twice, once as M/V and once from the interior gradient as 3g/(4 pi G R), and the two agree to four significant figures; the interior field was checked against the linear form g r/R, and the exterior values at 2R and 4R against the one-quarter and one-sixteenth ratios. The figure samples both branches from the same constants, so the kink is drawn at the radius the markscheme quotes.
