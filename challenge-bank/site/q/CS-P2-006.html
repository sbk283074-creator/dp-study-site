Migrating a policy administration system

An insurer administers 18 million policy records on a forty-year-old mainframe system written in COBOL, with data held in flat files and indexed by a bespoke access method. The system processes 240 000 transactions per night in a batch window and about 3 200 payment instructions per hour during the day. It is reliable but there are four staff in the country who can maintain it, all within ten years of retirement. The board has approved a migration to a relational database behind a service interface. Two proposals are on the table. Proposal A is a big-bang cutover on a single weekend: the new system is built in full, the old one is switched off, and if the new system fails the fallback is to restore the mainframe from the previous night's backup, which takes 14 hours. Proposal B is an incremental migration in which a facade routes each function either to the old or the new system, functions are moved one at a time, and both systems run in parallel until the last one is moved. The migration team estimates that dual running costs EUR 14 000 per month in additional infrastructure and that a reconciliation mismatch rate of 0.02% is achievable, each exception taking an average of 6 minutes to investigate. Analyst time is costed at EUR 65 per hour.

Question

Using the case study, answer all four parts. Assume a month is 30 nights of batch processing.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain why a big-bang cutover is a poor choice for this system, referring to specific figures in the case study rather than to general principles.

    (Explain)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Construct an incremental migration plan. State the mechanism that routes each request, the order in which you would move functions and why that order, and how the two systems are kept consistent while both are live.

    (Construct)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Calculate the total cost of nine months of dual running, including the analyst time spent on reconciliation. Show your working.

    (Calculate)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate the two proposals. If a failed big-bang cutover causes an 8-hour outage in which 3 200 payments per hour are missed and each missed payment costs EUR 120 in penalties and remediation, compare that with your answer to (c) and state which proposal you recommend.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) The decisive figure is the fallback: restoring from the previous night's backup takes 14 hours, during which roughly $14 \times 3200 \approx 45\,000$ payment instructions are not processed. (A1) A single failed cutover therefore risks a full working day of missed payments on a book of 18 million policies. (A1)

The staff position removes the option of a leisurely recovery. Only four people can maintain the old system, so if the new system fails and the old one must be restored and then kept running, there is almost no capacity to diagnose and fix the new one. (A1)

There is also no way to validate the new system at full scale before committing: 240 000 transactions per night cannot be simulated faithfully enough to be confident, and the first genuine run would also be the production run. (A1)

(b) *Mechanism:* place a facade in front of both systems. Every request arrives at the facade, which inspects the function being invoked and routes it either to the mainframe or to the new service. Callers never know which is serving them, so functions can be moved one at a time and moved back if necessary. (M1)(A1)

*Order:* move the read-mostly reference functions first, then simple queries, then writes, and leave the nightly batch and the payment instruction generation until last. (A1) The reason is that a read that goes wrong is visible immediately and is cheap to reverse, whereas a write that goes wrong corrupts data that may not be discovered for weeks; and the batch window is the most complex and least reversible part of the system, so it should be migrated only when everything feeding it is already new. (A1)

*Consistency while both are live:* run both systems for each migrated function and compare their outputs automatically every night, logging any disagreement as an exception for an analyst. The mainframe remains the system of record until a function has run clean for a sustained period, at which point the new system becomes the record for that function and the old one stops being authoritative for it. (A1)

(c) Infrastructure: $9 \times 14\,000 = \text{EUR }126\,000$. (A1)

Exceptions per night: $0.02\% \times 240\,000 = 48$. (M1)
Time per night: $48 \times 6 = 288$ minutes $= 4.8$ hours. (A1)
Over nine months, $9 \times 30 = 270$ nights: $270 \times 4.8 = 1296$ hours, at EUR 65 per hour:
$$1296 \times 65 = \text{EUR }84\,240$$
Total: $126\,000 + 84\,240 = \text{EUR }210\,240$. (A1)

(d) A failed cutover misses $8 \times 3200 = 25\,600$ payments, costing (M1)
$$25\,600 \times 120 = \text{EUR }3\,072\,000.$$
Comparing with the EUR 210 240 for nine months of dual running, a single failure costs about (A1)
$$\frac{3\,072\,000}{210\,240} \approx 15$$
times as much as the entire incremental programme. (A1)

Recommendation: Proposal B. Even if one assumed a failure probability for the big-bang cutover as low as one in ten, its expected cost would be about EUR 307 000, still above the full cost of dual running, and the incremental route has the further advantages of keeping the old system available as a live fallback for every function not yet moved, of building operational confidence gradually, and of allowing the four remaining experts to be consulted steadily rather than in a crisis. The one real drawback is the nine-month cost of running both, which the calculation shows to be small relative to the risk avoided.

Markscheme notes

(a) The marks are for using the figures; general assertions about risk earn nothing. The 14-hour restore and the four maintainers are the two that matter most, and a candidate who cites only the size of the dataset has missed the point, since size alone is not the difficulty. (b) Award (M1) for the facade concept regardless of the name used; a candidate who proposes a hard switch per function without a routing layer has not solved the problem. The order mark requires a reason, and the reason must be about reversibility or blast radius rather than convenience. The consistency mark is for dual-running with automated comparison and a defined point at which authority transfers; a candidate who says the two databases are kept synchronised by replication should be asked how, given that the schemas differ. (c) Accept a total between EUR 200 000 and EUR 220 000 depending on the assumption about nights per month; award the method marks for the mismatch count and the conversion to cost. (d) The comparison must be quantitative. Credit a candidate who goes further and computes an expected value or a break-even failure probability, since that is the strongest form of the argument. A recommendation of Proposal A is not creditable given these figures, but a candidate who argues for a shorter dual-running period, say four months, on the grounds that the mismatch rate would fall as the new system stabilises, should be credited.

Why this question is hard

Migration questions are usually answered with principles: do it incrementally, keep a fallback, test thoroughly. Those are all true and none of them is an argument. This question is built so that the argument is arithmetic. The cost of nine months of dual running, including every exception being investigated by a human being, is a little over two hundred thousand euro. The cost of one failed cutover, priced at the contractual penalty per missed payment, is over three million. That is a factor of about fifteen, and it means the incremental route is not merely more cautious but cheaper even if you assume the big-bang would usually succeed.

The design part is where the real difficulty sits, and it is not the routing mechanism, which is straightforward. It is deciding the order. The principle is that the cost of being wrong should rise as slowly as possible, which means moving functions whose failure is immediately visible and cheaply reversed before functions whose failure is silent and expensive. Read-only reference data is the obvious first candidate: if the new system returns the wrong address, someone notices the same day and nothing is corrupted. Writes are the opposite. And the nightly batch, which is the most intricate part and the one whose failure is discovered last, must go last, when everything that feeds it has already been proven.

Keeping the two systems consistent is the part students underestimate. It is not enough to say the databases are synced; the schemas differ, the semantics of a forty-year-old flat-file system rarely map cleanly onto a relational model, and the only practical approach is to run both and compare the outputs, treating every disagreement as an exception to be investigated. That is why the reconciliation cost is built into the calculation rather than treated as an afterthought: the 0.02 percent mismatch rate is not a rounding error, it is forty-eight investigations a night for nine months, and any plan that ignores it is not a plan.
