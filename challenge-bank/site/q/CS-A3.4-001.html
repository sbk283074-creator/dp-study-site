A relational store that cannot answer the question

Computer science HL · CS-A3.4-001

A3.4 Alternative databases and data warehouses (A3.4.1 NoSQL, cloud, spatial and in-memory databases, A3.4.2 objectives of data warehouses, A3.4.3 OLAP and data mining, A3.4.4 distributed databases and ACID)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedsqlExplainDesignCalculateJustify

Nightline Couriers

Nightline Couriers operates 340 vans from 12 depots. Every van reports its position, speed and load temperature once every 0.4 seconds while the ignition is on, an average of 14 hours a day. Each report is stored as a fixed-length record of 80 bytes. The reports are written to a relational database on a single server, and the same database holds the tables that the dispatch system reads and writes many times per second.

At the end of each month the operations director asks for a report showing, for every depot and every month, the number of completed deliveries, the average delivery time per parcel, the total distance driven and the number of temperature excursions, defined as any period in which a load temperature left the permitted range for more than 90 seconds.

The report currently takes nine hours to produce and, while it runs, drivers report that the dispatch screen stops responding.

Question

The operations director wants the monthly report produced without the dispatch system losing responsiveness. The data described above is available, and management accepts that the reporting and dispatch workloads must be separated.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain why the monthly report is slow and why running it affects the dispatch system, referring to the difference between the workloads the two stores must support.

    (Explain)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Design a star schema for the monthly report. State the grain of the fact table, name the fact table and three dimension tables, and give two measures that the fact table must hold.

    (Design)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Calculate the volume of raw report data generated in one year, and the volume if every record is stored with a replication factor of three. State one consequence of your answer for the choice of database.

    (Calculate)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Justify the use of a key-value or wide-column store for the incoming position reports rather than the relational database, and state one capability that is given up by doing so.

    (Justify)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The dispatch system performs online transaction processing: many small reads and writes, each touching a few rows, each required to complete almost immediately. The monthly report performs online analytical processing: it reads almost every row that exists and aggregates it, and it does not care whether the answer arrives in a millisecond. (M1)(A1)

The relational store is organised to make the first workload fast. Its indexes are built on the columns the dispatch system filters on, and those indexes are of no use to a query that must scan the whole of a large table. The monthly report therefore degenerates into a full scan of the position reports, and because the tables are also being written to continuously, the scan has to take locks or work against a moving target. (M1)

Running both workloads on one server means they compete for the same resources: the report's long scan occupies processor time, memory for buffers and disk bandwidth, so the transactional queries queue behind it and the dispatch screen stops responding. This is the standard argument for separating the operational store from a reporting store. (A1)

(b) The grain must be stated before anything else can be decided, because a fact table is only meaningful at one level of detail. Grain: one row per van per completed delivery. (M1)

Fact table: fact_delivery, with one row per delivery and foreign keys to each dimension. (M1)

Dimension tables: dim_date (day, month, year, weekday), dim_depot (depot identifier, depot name, region), and dim_van (van identifier, model, refrigeration capability). A fourth, dim_route, would be defensible if routes are named rather than being defined by origin and destination, and should be accepted. (M1)

Measures: at least two numeric, additive quantities such as delivery_duration_minutes and distance_km, together with temperature_excursions, which is stored as a count of qualifying events rather than as a duration so that it can be summed. (M1)(A1)

Storing the delivery duration as a measure rather than deriving it from timestamps is what makes the average delivery time per depot obtainable without touching the raw reports at all.

(c) 340 vans write one report every 0.4 seconds for 14 hours a day: each van produces $14\times 3600/0.4=126\,000$ reports per day, so the fleet produces $340\times 126\,000=42\,840\,000$ reports per day. (M1)

At 80 bytes each, that is $42\,840\,000\times 80=3.43\times 10^{9}$ bytes, which is 3.43 GB per day. (A1)

Over 365 days the raw volume is $3.43\times 365=1\,251$ GB, approximately 1.25 TB per year. (A1) With a replication factor of three the stored volume is about 3.75 TB. (A1)

One consequence follows directly: a single relational server cannot be the store for this data, because the write rate alone is over a million rows per minute across the fleet at peak and the table grows by terabytes annually. A distributed store that partitions the data across nodes is required, and retention policy becomes a design decision rather than an afterthought.

(d) The incoming reports have a very simple shape and an extremely high write rate, and the query pattern is almost always 'give me the reports for this van between these two times'. (M1) A key-value or wide-column store matches this exactly: the key is the van identifier combined with the timestamp, writes are append-only and can be spread across nodes, and a time-range read is a contiguous scan within one partition. No joins are required, so the relational model's main advantage is not being used.

The capability given up is transactional consistency across records: ACID guarantees are weakened in exchange for availability and partition tolerance, so a system reading the store may see data that is not yet complete or not yet consistent everywhere. (A1)(A1) For position reports this is acceptable; for the financial records of the same company it would not be, which is why the two stores coexist rather than one replacing the other.

Markscheme notes

Part (a): the essential contrast is workload shape — many small transactions against few large aggregations — rather than raw speed. A candidate who writes only 'the report is slow because there is a lot of data' earns one mark. The resource-contention mark requires the report and the transactions to be identified as competing for the same server. Part (b): a candidate who states the grain as 'per depot per month' has designed an aggregated table, not a star schema, and cannot earn the grain or the fact-table mark; the grain must be at the level of the individual event. The dimension requirement is for three sensible dimensions, not for specific names, but each must have plausible attributes and not be a copy of the fact table. Accept dim_route in place of dim_van if the delivery context makes it meaningful. Part (c): the calculation marks are for the daily figure, the annual figure and the replicated figure separately; a candidate who reaches 3.75 TB has all three and earns full marks. Follow-through applies to arithmetic. Reject an answer that ignores the 14 hours per day and assumes 24-hour reporting, though the method marks should still be awarded. Part (d): the justification must refer to the access pattern or the write rate; 'NoSQL is faster' alone is not a justification. The final mark is for a specific consistency, join or query capability that is lost, with ACID named or described.

Why this question is hard

The question turns on a distinction that is easy to state and hard to apply: the same data can require two different storage systems because the two workloads have opposite shapes. The dispatch system does many small operations that must each finish quickly; the monthly report does one enormous aggregation that nobody minds waiting for. A schema optimised for the first is actively hostile to the second, because the indexes that make row lookups fast are irrelevant to a full scan, and because the scan competes for resources with the transactions.

Part (b) is the part most likely to be attempted without the necessary discipline. A star schema is defined by its grain, and the grain must be the finest thing the measures are meaningful at. Writing 'per depot per month' produces a table that already contains the answer and cannot be re-aggregated differently, which defeats the purpose of a warehouse. Stating the grain as one row per van per completed delivery keeps the table re-aggregatable by any dimension, and that is precisely the flexibility a warehouse is bought for. The dimension tables exist so that the query can group by month, depot or van without a join to the operational database.

Part (c) is arithmetic with a design consequence. Roughly 43 million reports a day at 80 bytes gives about 3.4 GB daily and about 1.25 TB a year before replication; the moment a replication factor is added, the figure is well beyond what a single server should hold. The useful observation is not the number itself but what it rules out: the storage decision is forced by the write rate, not by a preference for novel technology.

Part (d) closes the argument. A time-series access pattern with no joins is exactly what a key-value or wide-column store is good at, and choosing it costs the transactional guarantees that the financial system still needs. Both stores therefore exist, and the fact that they hold overlapping data is a deliberate redundancy rather than a design failure.
