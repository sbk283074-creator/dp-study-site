5.2 The stage with the lowest stated capacity is not the bottleneck: quantifying what the wrong investment buys

Trentvale Components Ltd: three stages and two proposals

Trentvale Components Ltd makes a machined housing in three sequential stages. Cutting can process 100 units per hour and produces no scrap. Machining can process 90 units per hour, and 10% of the units it processes are scrapped and sold as swarf. Finishing can process 84 units per hour and produces no scrap. Every good unit that leaves finishing is sold immediately: demand is not a constraint, and the line runs for 40 hours a week for 48 weeks a year.

The housing sells for £40 and has a variable cost of £22, so each good unit contributes £18. Two proposals have been costed at £250 000 each.

Proposal 1 raises finishing capacity from 84 to 110 units per hour. Proposal 2 buys a better tool holder for machining, reducing the scrap rate there from 10% to 5%. The operations director argues that finishing must be extended first, because 84 units per hour is the lowest capacity in the factory.

Question

The diagram shows the three stages with their stated capacities. Answer every part, giving output to one decimal place and money to the nearest pound.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Determine the current maximum output of good units per hour and identify the stage that constrains it.

  2. [3 marks](b)

    Explain why finishing, which has the lowest stated capacity of the three stages, is not the constraint.

    (Explain)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Calculate the output of good units per hour under each proposal on its own and under both together, and calculate the cost per additional unit of hourly capacity in each case.

    (Calculate)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Evaluate which proposal, or pair of proposals, Trentvale should approve, using the payback period and two other factors.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) Cutting can supply 100 units an hour but machining can process only 90, so the line is throttled to 90 units an hour at machining (M1). Of those, 10% are scrapped, so the good output is 90 × 0.90 = 81 units an hour (A1). Finishing can handle 84 units an hour and receives 81, so it is not full. The maximum output of good units is therefore 81 per hour and the constraint is machining (A1).
(b) A stage constrains the line only if the good units arriving at it exceed what it can process. Finishing receives 81 good units an hour and can process 84, so it runs with three units an hour, or 3.6%, of spare capacity (M1)(A1). Its stated capacity is the lowest of the three only because it is quoted before the scrap that happens upstream of it; comparing stated capacities compares the stages at different points in the process, and the comparison that matters is between good units delivered and capacity available at the same point (A1).
(c) Proposal 1 alone leaves machining untouched, so output stays at 81 units an hour: the extra finishing capacity is starved and the proposal adds nothing at all (M1). Proposal 2 alone raises good output from machining to 90 × 0.95 = 85.5 units an hour, but finishing can take only 84, so the line delivers 84 units an hour, a gain of 3 units an hour (A1). Both together deliver 85.5 units an hour, a gain of 4.5 units an hour (A1). Cost per additional unit of hourly capacity: Proposal 1, £250 000 for no additional units, so the cost per unit is undefined — no price buys the extra capacity at any level of spending; Proposal 2, £250 000 ÷ 3 = £83 333; both, £500 000 ÷ 4.5 = £111 111, of which the marginal £250 000 buys only 1.5 units an hour, or £166 667 each (A1).
(d) Proposal 2 is the one to approve. It adds 3 units an hour, which is 3 × 40 × 48 = 5 760 units a year and 5 760 × £18 = £103 680 of additional contribution, so it pays back in £250 000 ÷ £103 680 = 2.41 years (M1)(A1). Proposal 1 on its own pays back never: it adds no output, so there is no additional contribution against which to recover the £250 000, and it would add depreciation to a stage that already has spare capacity (A1). Two further considerations qualify the recommendation. First, scrap is not only lost output: every scrapped unit has already absorbed £22 of material and machining time, so reducing the scrap rate also improves material productivity and reduces waste disposal, benefits that the contribution calculation does not capture (A1). Second, doing both proposals raises output by a further 1.5 units an hour but costs another £250 000 and stretches payback to 3.21 years; that is worth doing only if Trentvale is confident of selling the extra 8 640 units a year, and demand, not capacity, may then become the constraint (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for identifying that machining throttles the input to 90 units an hour, (A1) for 81 good units an hour, (A1) for naming machining as the constraint. Accept 81 arrived at as 90 − 9. Do not credit 84 (the lowest stated capacity) or 90 (the lowest stated processing capacity before scrap).
(b) (M1) for the principle that a stage binds only when arrivals exceed its capacity, (A1) for the figures 81 against 84, (A1) for the explanation that the stated capacities are measured at different points in the process. A candidate who says only that 'finishing has spare capacity' without quantifying it earns the first two marks but not the third.
(c) (M1) for recognising that Proposal 1 changes nothing while machining binds, (A1) for 84 units an hour under Proposal 2 with the reason (finishing now binds at 84, not 85.5), (A1) for 85.5 units an hour with both, (A1) for the three cost figures. The cost per unit for Proposal 1 must be stated as undefined or infinite; a candidate who writes £250 000 or zero has not understood the result and loses the mark.
(d) (M1) for converting the extra output into annual units, (A1) for £103 680 and 2.41 years, (A1) for the conclusion that Proposal 1 never pays back, (A1) for one further developed factor such as material productivity, and (A1) for a second, such as the marginal case for doing both or the risk that demand becomes the constraint. Credit the sequencing point — Proposal 1 has value only after Proposal 2 — wherever it appears.

Why this question is hard

Capacity questions invite one move: find the smallest number and call it the bottleneck. This item makes that move wrong in a way that is invisible in the data as presented, because the scrap rate is a property of one stage and its consequence lands on another. Finishing's 84 units an hour is measured in good units arriving; machining's 90 is measured in units started, of which a tenth never arrive. The two numbers are not comparable, and the whole item turns on converting them to a common basis.

The second design decision is to price the error rather than merely expose it. A question that asks only 'which stage is the bottleneck?' can be answered by luck; one that asks what £250 000 spent on the wrong stage buys forces the student to work out that it buys nothing at all — no output, no contribution, and therefore no payback period however long the horizon. That is a stronger and more practical idea than any definition of a bottleneck, and it is the reason the proposals cost the same: with equal price tags, the comparison is purely about what each one releases.

The sequencing result is the last turn. Proposal 1 is worthless alone and worth 1.5 units an hour once Proposal 2 is in place, so the value of a capital project here is not a property of the project but of the order in which it is done — which is exactly the situation the operations syllabus describes and the appraisal syllabus tends to ignore.

Challenge lever
Capacity is quoted per stage, and the smallest number looks like the constraint. It is not, because the scrap at machining is applied *before* the units reach finishing: machining delivers 81 good units an hour to a stage that could handle 84, so finishing is starved and has three units an hour of spare capacity while carrying the lowest stated number. The student has to convert each stage's nameplate capacity into good units delivered, and then price the error: the director's proposal buys zero extra output, and the same money spent on the scrap rate buys three units an hour immediately.

Inspiration
Operations management, the theory of constraints
Adaptation
The source tradition treats the constraint as the resource with the least capacity and teaches the sequence identify–exploit–subordinate–elevate. Here the identification step is made to fail on the textbook's own terms: a scrap rate sits upstream of the stage with the lowest stated capacity, so the constraint is one stage earlier than the numbers suggest, and the elevate step is then priced in two competing currencies so that spending on the wrong stage is shown to buy nothing rather than merely less.
Verification
Every figure was recomputed in Python. The flow through the line was simulated stage by stage, applying the scrap rate at machining and capping at each stage's capacity, and the output was confirmed to be 81 units an hour under the current settings. All four investment cases were simulated the same way rather than assumed, which is what produced the result that Proposal 1 alone leaves output unchanged and that Proposal 2 alone is capped at 84 by finishing. The payback figures were checked by dividing the investment by the annual contribution and by accumulating contribution year by year, and both routes agree. The figure draws its numbers from the same capacities.
