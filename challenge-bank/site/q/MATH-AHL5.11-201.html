5.11 The signed integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval vanishes while the geometric area between the curve and the axis does not, and the two lobes of f(x) = x^3 - a^2 x are exactly equal in magnitude

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.11-201

Topic 5: CalculusSL 5.11 (definite integrals, including analytical approach to areas between curves) with SL 5.10 (the fundamental theorem of calculus and the indefinite integral)P2 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatDetermineDetermineExplain

Question

Let $a$ be a positive constant and define
$$f(x) = x^{3} - a^{2}x.$$
The function is being used to model a shallow channel cut across a strip of land, with $x$ measured in metres from the centre line and $f(x)$ the depth of the channel in metres, taken as positive upwards. Two different quantities are wanted from the same curve: the signed integral, which is what an instrument that accumulates the profile as it is driven along would record, and the geometric area between the curve and the $x$-axis, which is what a surveyor who measures the visible cross-section would report. The figure shows the case $a = 2$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $f$ is an odd function and hence evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx$ without performing any integration.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the geometric area of the region enclosed between the curve, the $x$-axis and the lines $x = -a$ and $x = a$, giving your answer in terms of $a$.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    The geometric area is found to be $8$ square metres. Determine the value of $a$ and the coordinates of the two turning points of the curve for that value of $a$.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain why the signed integral in part (a) is zero while the geometric area in part (b) is not, and state what the instrument described in the question would record as it is driven from $x = -a$ to $x = a$.

    (Explain)

[3 marks] (Show that) (b) Determine the geometric area of the region enclosed between the curve, the $x$-axis and the lines $x = -a$ and $x = a$, giving your answer in terms of $a$. [4 marks] (Determine) (c) The geometric area is found to be $8$ square metres. Determine the value of $a$ and the coordinates of the two turning points of the curve for that value of $a$. [4 marks] (Determine) (d) Explain why the signed integral in part (a) is zero while the geometric area in part (b) is not, and state what the instrument described in the question would record as it is driven from $x = -a$ to $x = a$. [3 marks] (Explain)
Reveal the answer

(a) The function is odd if $f(-x) = -f(x)$ for every $x$. Here
$$f(-x) = (-x)^{3} - a^{2}(-x) = -x^{3} + a^{2}x = -(x^{3} - a^{2}x) = -f(x),$$
so $f$ is odd (M1)(A1). For an odd function the integral over a symmetric interval vanishes, because the substitution $u = -x$ maps the interval onto itself and reverses the sign:
$$\int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx = \int_{0}^{a}\bigl(f(x) + f(-x)\bigr)dx = \int_{0}^{a}\bigl(f(x) - f(x)\bigr)dx = 0 \;(A1).$$
The cancellation is exact, not approximate, and it holds for every value of $a$.
(b) The curve meets the axis where $x(x^{2} - a^{2}) = 0$, that is at $x = -a$, $x = 0$ and $x = a$ (M1). On $0 < x < a$ the factor $x^{2} - a^{2}$ is negative, so $f(x) < 0$ and the curve lies below the axis; on $-a < x < 0$ the two factors are both negative, so $f(x) > 0$ and the curve lies above it (A1). The geometric area therefore requires the absolute value of $f$, and by the odd symmetry the two lobes have equal area, so
$$\text{area} = 2\int_{0}^{a}\bigl(a^{2}x - x^{3}\bigr)dx = 2\left[\frac{a^{2}x^{2}}{2} - \frac{x^{4}}{4}\right]_{0}^{a} = 2\left(\frac{a^{4}}{2} - \frac{a^{4}}{4}\right) = 2\cdot\frac{a^{4}}{4} = \frac{a^{4}}{2} \;(M1)(A1).$$
With $a = 2$ this is $16/2 = 8$ square metres (A1). Note that the two lobes have equal area precisely because $f$ is odd; the equal magnitudes are the same fact that made the signed integral vanish.
(c) Setting $a^{4}/2 = 8$ gives $a^{4} = 16$, and since $a > 0$, $a = 2$ (M1)(A1). The turning points satisfy $f'(x) = 3x^{2} - a^{2} = 0$, so
$$x = \pm\frac{a}{\sqrt{3}} = \pm\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} = \pm 1.1547\ldots \;(A1).$$Substituting into $f$ gives $f\!\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = \frac{a^{3}}{3\sqrt{3}} - \frac{a^{3}}{\sqrt{3}} = -\frac{2a^{3}}{3\sqrt{3}}$, which for $a = 2$ is $-3.0792\ldots$ (A1). By the odd symmetry the other turning point is its reflection, so the two points are
$$\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}},\ -\frac{16}{3\sqrt{3}}\right) \approx (1.155,\ -3.079) \quad\text{and}\quad \left(-\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}},\ \frac{16}{3\sqrt{3}}\right) \approx (-1.155,\ 3.079) \;(A1).$$
(d) The signed integral adds the two lobes with their signs attached, and because the lobes are mirror images through the origin the positive contribution and the negative contribution are equal in magnitude, so their sum is zero (A1). The geometric area adds the magnitudes, and a magnitude cannot cancel (A1). The instrument accumulates the signed value as it is driven along, so it rises while it crosses the positive lobe, falls back through zero at the centre, and returns to exactly zero at $x = a$; its final reading is zero even though it has been recording a non-zero depth the whole way (A1). A surveyor reading the same profile with a tape would report $8$ square metres, and the two answers are both correct because they answer different questions.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for verifying $f(-x) = -f(x)$ algebraically, (A1) for the conclusion that the integral vanishes, (A1) for a justification that does not simply cite the rule: the substitution argument, or the pairing $f(x) + f(-x) = 0$ over $[0,a]$, or the explicit antiderivative evaluated at symmetric limits. A bare statement that the integrand is odd and the integral is therefore zero scores (M1)(A1) and loses the third mark.
(b) (M1) for locating the three zeros of the curve, (A1) for identifying which lobe is above and which below the axis, (M1) for setting up the absolute value correctly, (A1) for the value $a^{4}/2$. Condonation: a candidate who integrates from $-a$ to $0$ and doubles, or who integrates one lobe and doubles, scores full marks provided the justification for the doubling is the odd symmetry rather than an assertion. A candidate who computes $\int_{-a}^{a}(a^{2}x - x^{3})dx$ and obtains $a^{4}/2$ by accident, without noticing that this integrand is the negation of $f$ on the negative half, has the right number and loses the (A1) for the lobe identification; award (M1)(A1) for the zeros and (M1) for the set-up only.
(c) (M1) for differentiating and setting $f'(x) = 0$, (A1) for $a = 2$, (A1) for the abscissae $\pm 2/\sqrt{3}$, (A1) for the ordinates $\mp 16/(3\sqrt{3})$. Accept $\pm 1.155$ and $\mp 3.079$ to three or more significant figures. A candidate who assumes the turning points lie at $x = \pm 1$ or at the midpoint of the interval loses the last two marks; there is no reason for the extremum of $x^{3} - a^{2}x$ to sit anywhere in particular, and the figure marks it where it actually is.
(d) (A1) for signed contributions cancelling against magnitudes accumulating, (A1) for the distinction between a signed accumulation and an area, (A1) for the description of the instrument's reading rising, returning through zero and ending at zero. A candidate who says only that one is signed and the other is not, without stating what the instrument actually records, scores (A1)(A1) and not the third mark.
Follow-through: a candidate whose $a$ in (c) is wrong but who then differentiates and substitutes consistently keeps the method marks in that part. Forfeits: integrating $f$ on $[0,a]$ without the absolute value and reporting a negative area; treating $f(x) = x^{3} - a^{2}x$ as even because it is a polynomial in odd powers of $x$ alone; and reporting the area as $a^{4}/4$ by omitting the doubling.

Why this question is hard

There is one idea in this item and it is worth being precise about, because it is the commonest confusion in the whole of integration: a definite integral is a signed accumulation and an area is not. The two coincide only when the curve stays on one side of the axis.

Here the curve crosses the axis twice inside the interval of integration, so the two lobes contribute with opposite signs. Because the function is odd, the two lobes are congruent, and the cancellation is complete. That completeness is what makes the item sharp. If the cancellation were partial the candidate would be left with a non-zero residue and might notice that it did not look like an area; because it is total, the answer zero looks like a clean result, and a clean result is easy to accept. The figure is drawn to make the cancellation visible: the shaded regions are clearly not empty, yet their signed sum is nothing.

Part (b) reverses the demand. The same symmetry that made the integral vanish now does the work of the calculation, because the two lobes have equal area, so one lobe can be computed and doubled. The integral of $a^{2}x - x^{3}$, which is the absolute value of $f$ on the positive half, is a routine polynomial integral and gives $a^{4}/4$; doubling gives $a^{4}/2$. A candidate who forgets the doubling answers $a^{4}/4$ and, worse, will still find $a = 2^{3/4}$ in part (c) and produce a consistent-looking but wrong pair of turning points.

Part (c) is where an assumption about where the extremum sits costs marks. The turning points of $x^{3} - a^{2}x$ are at $x = \pm a/\sqrt{3}$, which is about $\pm 0.577a$: well inside the interval, but not at any of the obvious places. A candidate who guesses $x = \pm 1$ for $a = 2$ obtains ordinates of $-3$ rather than $-3.079$, close enough to look plausible on a sketch and wrong on the page.

Part (d) is the conceptual close. The instrument described in the stem is a device that accumulates the signed profile, so it rises over the first lobe, falls back through zero, and finishes at zero. It has recorded a non-zero depth at almost every point and its total is still zero. Being able to say that in words is the evidence that the candidate has separated the two notions rather than memorised two formulas.

Challenge lever
The integrand is odd, so the signed integral over the symmetric interval is exactly zero, and a candidate who has been trained to read a definite integral as an area reports the area as zero. The two lobes are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign, so the cancellation is total rather than partial: there is no residue to warn the candidate that something has gone wrong. The second trap is in the reverse direction. Once the area is known to be a^4/2, fixing it at 8 gives a^4 = 16 and hence a = 2, and the turning points then have to be evaluated at the irrational abscissae x = +/- a/sqrt(3), where a candidate who assumes the maximum sits at the midpoint of the interval, or at x = +/- 1, gets both coordinates wrong. Labelled difficulty 4 rather than 5 on the merits: the trap is a single observation, and once it has been made the remaining marks are earned by routine polynomial integration and differentiation, so the larger part of the item's demand is computation rather than the mechanism itself.

Inspiration

Adaptation
The gaokao version asks for the area enclosed by a cubic and the axis, or for the value of a parameter given an area, but not both in the same item, and it does not contrast the signed integral with the geometric area. Here the two quantities are asked for in the same question and the contrast is the point of the final part; the cubic, the parameter and the target area are all chosen here, and a = 2 was selected so that the area is exactly 8 and the turning points stay irrational.
Verification
The signed integral and the geometric area were both computed by Riemann sums with four hundred thousand subintervals at a = 0.5, 1, 2 and 3; the signed sum is zero to machine precision and the geometric sum reproduces a^4/2 to five decimal places. The turning points were obtained from f'(x) = 3x^2 - a^2 and checked by direct substitution at a = 2, giving -3.079201435678004 at x = 1.1547005383792517 and its mirror image.
