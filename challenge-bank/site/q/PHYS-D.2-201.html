D.2 A charged oil drop held at rest, and the p.d. that gives it an upward acceleration

Physics HL · PHYS-D.2-201

Theme D: FieldsD.2 Electric and magnetic fields (uniform field between parallel plates; force on a charge; equilibrium of a charged particle)P212 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateDetermineDetermineState

Question

Two large horizontal parallel plates are separated by $d = 12\ \mathrm{mm}$ and connected to a variable power supply. A small oil drop of mass $m = 3.27 \times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{kg}$ carries a charge $q$ and is observed to remain stationary between the plates when the potential difference is $V = 480\ \mathrm{V}$. Take $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and the elementary charge as $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$. The drop may be treated as a point charge and air resistance is negligible.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the magnitude of the electric field strength between the plates.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the magnitude of the charge on the drop, and hence determine how many elementary charges it carries.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    The potential difference is now adjusted so that the drop accelerates upwards with an acceleration of magnitude $g$. Determine the new potential difference, using your value of $q$ from part (b).

    (Determine)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    State one assumption made in treating the drop as stationary in equilibrium, and say how the answer to (b) would change if that assumption failed.

    (State)

Total: 12 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Two large horizontal parallel plates are separated by $d = 12\ \mathrm{mm}$ and connected to a variable power supply. A small oil drop of mass $m = 3.27 \times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{kg}$ carries a charge $q$ and is observed to remain stationary between the plates when the potential difference is $V = 480\ \mathrm{V}$. Take $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and the elementary charge as $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$. The drop may be treated as a point charge and air resistance is negligible. (a) Calculate the magnitude of the electric field strength between the plates. [3 marks] (Calculate) (b) Determine the magnitude of the charge on the drop, and hence determine how many elementary charges it carries. [4 marks] (Determine) (c) The potential difference is now adjusted so that the drop accelerates upwards with an acceleration of magnitude $g$. Determine the new potential difference, using your value of $q$ from part (b). [3 marks] (Determine) (d) State one assumption made in treating the drop as stationary in equilibrium, and say how the answer to (b) would change if that assumption failed. [2 marks] (State)
Reveal the answer

(a) The field between parallel plates is uniform, so (M1)(A1)
$$E = \frac{V}{d} = \frac{480}{12 \times 10^{-3}} = 4.0 \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$$

with the field directed downwards if the upper plate is positive (A1).

(b) For the drop to be stationary the electric force must balance the weight exactly (M1):
$$qE = mg \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q = \frac{mg}{E} = \frac{(3.27 \times 10^{-15})(9.81)}{4.0 \times 10^{4}}$$

$$q = \frac{3.208 \times 10^{-14}}{4.0 \times 10^{4}} = 8.02 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C} \tag{A1}$$

The number of elementary charges (M1):
$$n = \frac{q}{e} = \frac{8.02 \times 10^{-19}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}} = 5.01 \approx 5 \tag{A1}$$

The charge is $5e$, so the drop carries five excess electrons (or lacks five electrons, depending on the sign chosen).

(c) An upward acceleration of magnitude $g$ requires a net upward force equal to $mg$, so the electric force must be $2mg$ (M1):
$$qE' = 2mg \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E' = \frac{2mg}{q}$$

But $mg/q = E = 4.0 \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$ from part (b), so
$$E' = 2E = 8.0 \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}} \tag{A1}$$

$$V' = E'd = (8.0 \times 10^{4})(12 \times 10^{-3}) = 9.6 \times 10^{2}\ \mathrm{V} \tag{A1}$$

(d) The assumption is that the only forces are the electric force and the weight, so buoyancy and drag are negligible. If the drop also experienced an upward buoyant force $F_{b}$, the electric force required for equilibrium would be smaller, $qE = mg - F_{b}$, and the charge deduced in (b) would come out too small; the value of $n$ would then be less than $5$ and would no longer be an integer.

Part (c) can be seen without any arithmetic once the structure is clear. Equilibrium required the electric force to equal the weight, and an upward acceleration of magnitude $g$ requires a net force equal to the weight, so the electric force has to supply one weight to hold the drop up and a second weight to accelerate it. It must therefore double, and so must the field and the potential difference. The charge found in part (b) is then only needed to confirm the direction of the field, which is a useful reminder that a quantity can be known without being needed.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for $V/d$, A1 for $4.0 \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$, A1 for the direction. Accept $4 \times 10^{4}$ with the unit written as $\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$.

(b) M1 for equating $qE$ to $mg$, A1 for $8.02 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$, M1 for dividing by $e$, A1 for $n = 5$. A candidate who obtains $5.01$ and leaves it as $5.01$ without recognising that it must be an integer has missed the physics of charge quantisation and should be awarded the first A1 but not the second.

(c) M1 for requiring the net force to be $mg$ rather than the electric force to equal $mg$, A1 for $E' = 2E$, A1 for $960\ \mathrm{V}$. The commonest wrong answer is $480\ \mathrm{V}$, which is the p.d. already given in the stem: it satisfies $qE = mg$, which is equilibrium, not acceleration. A candidate who uses $q = 5e$ exactly obtains $961\ \mathrm{V}$; accept this, and note that $960\ \mathrm{V}$ follows from the charge found in (b), so the two are consistent to the precision of the data.

(d) The mark is for a named force that was neglected and a stated direction of the error. Accept buoyancy, drag, or the drop not being a point charge. A statement that the answer "would be different" without a direction earns 0.

Forfeits: using $g = 10\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, which loses the accuracy marks; forgetting to convert $12\ \mathrm{mm}$ to metres. Condonation: a candidate who carries $q = 8.02 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$ through (c) and obtains $960\ \mathrm{V}$, or who uses $5e$ and obtains $961\ \mathrm{V}$, should both be given full credit.

Why this question is hard

The item is a two-stage test of the same idea, and the second stage is where the marks are lost. In parts (a) and (b) the drop is stationary, so the electric force equals the weight and the charge follows from a straightforward balance. Almost every student who has met the Millikan experiment can do this, and the numbers are arranged so that the charge comes out at $5.01e$, close enough to an integer to be unmistakable but not so exact that it looks contrived.

Part (c) changes the question from "what force balances?" to "what force produces this acceleration?", and those are different questions. An upward acceleration of $g$ needs a net upward force of $mg$, so the electric force must supply $mg$ to hold the drop up and a further $mg$ to accelerate it, giving $2mg$ in total. The p.d. therefore doubles to $960\ \mathrm{V}$. The wrong answer is $480\ \mathrm{V}$, which is already printed in the question, and it is wrong for a specific and instructive reason: it satisfies the equilibrium condition that part (b) was about, and applying the earlier condition to a new question is exactly the habit this part is designed to detect.

Part (d) asks for an assumption and its consequence, which is a different kind of demand: it tests whether the student can see that "stationary" was a physical claim and not merely a description of the diagram. The most natural omission is buoyancy, and it is instructive that including it makes the deduced charge too small and destroys the integer, because that is precisely the kind of systematic error Millikan had to reason about. The item is arranged so that a student who understands the structure can see the answer to (c) without much arithmetic, and a student who does not will produce the number already given in the stem.
