Round-robin and first-come-first-served scheduling

Computer science HL · CS-P1A-403

Theme A: Concepts of computer scienceA1.3 Operating systems and control systems (A1.3.2 functions of an operating system, A1.3.3 comparison of scheduling approaches, A1.3.4 evaluation of polling and interrupt handling)P1 · Section A14 marksdifficulty 4technology: not applicableDetermineCalculateCalculateEvaluate

Question

A single-core processor runs three processes that are all held in the ready queue. The figure gives the arrival time and the burst time of each process, and the round-robin schedule that the scheduler produced with a time quantum of four units. The scheduler always adds a process that has just arrived to the back of the ready queue before it adds a process that has used its whole quantum, and a process that finishes inside its quantum is removed immediately. The operating system designer is choosing between this scheduler and a first-come-first-served scheduler, and wants to know which of the two gives the better average waiting time and which gives the better response for an interactive workload.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Determine the completion time of each of the three processes under the round-robin schedule shown in the figure. Show the order in which the processes are dispatched and the length of each dispatch.

    (Determine)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Calculate the waiting time of each process under this round-robin schedule and hence the average waiting time. Define the waiting time you use.

    (Calculate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Calculate the average waiting time if the same three processes were scheduled first-come-first-served in order of arrival, and state which scheduler gives the smaller average.

    (Calculate)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate which of the two schedulers is more suitable for an interactive system, using your results and considering how the two schedulers behave when a long process arrives before a short one.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) Under round-robin the ready queue is ordered by arrival, and each process runs for at most four units before returning to the back of the queue.

At time 0 only P1 has arrived, so P1 is dispatched and runs from 0 to 4, using a whole quantum and leaving 3 units of work (A1). P2 arrives at time 2 and P3 at time 4, so both are waiting when the quantum expires; P2 is added before P1 because P2 arrived first. P2 is dispatched from 4 to 8 and finishes inside its quantum, so it is removed at time 8 (A1). P3 is dispatched from 8 to 9, finishes inside its quantum, and is removed at time 9 (A1). P1 is dispatched again from 9 to 12 and finishes at time 12 (A1).

The completion times are therefore P2 at 8, P3 at 9 and P1 at 12.

(b) The waiting time of a process is the total time it spends in the ready queue, which is its completion time minus its arrival time minus its burst time.

For P1: $12 - 0 - 7 = 5$ units (M1). For P2: $8 - 2 - 4 = 2$ units (A1). For P3: $9 - 4 - 1 = 4$ units (A1).

The total waiting time is $5 + 2 + 4 = 11$ units, so the average is $11 \div 3 = 3.67$ units (A1).

Note that P3, the shortest process, has a longer waiting time than P2. It arrives at time 4, exactly when P1's first quantum expires, and P2 is placed ahead of it because P2 arrived earlier. This is a property of the queue order, not an error in the trace.

(c) First-come-first-served runs the processes to completion in order of arrival. P1 runs from 0 to 7 and finishes at 7 (A1). P2 runs from 7 to 11 and finishes at 11, so its waiting time is $11 - 2 - 4 = 5$ units (A1). P3 runs from 11 to 12 and finishes at 12, so its waiting time is $12 - 4 - 1 = 7$ units.

The total is $0 + 5 + 7 = 12$ units, so the average is $12 \div 3 = 4.00$ units (A1). Round-robin therefore gives the smaller average waiting time, 3.67 against 4.00.

(d) Round-robin is the more suitable scheduler for an interactive system, and the reason is the response time rather than the average waiting time. The response time of a process is the delay before it first receives the processor. Under round-robin P1 first runs at 0, P2 at 4 and P3 at 8, so the response times are 0, 2 and 4 units and the average is 2.0 units (A1). Under first-come-first-served P1 first runs at 0, P2 at 7 and P3 at 11, so the response times are 0, 5 and 7 units and the average is 4.0 units (A1). A user interacting with the system perceives the delay before the first response, so halving it is a substantial gain even though the two schedulers differ by only 0.33 units in average waiting time.

The behaviour when a long process arrives first is the decisive case. Under first-come-first-served a process that needs a long burst blocks every process behind it for the whole of that burst, so a short interactive job can be delayed by the full length of an unrelated long job; this is the convoy effect, and P3's waiting time of 7 units is a small example of it (A1). Round-robin bounds the delay before any waiting process is dispatched by one quantum, so a long job can delay a short one by at most the quantum rather than by its whole burst.

The cost of that bound is context switching, since every quantum boundary forces the processor to save one process and restore another, and a smaller quantum means more such switches (A1). Round-robin is therefore the better choice for interactivity, provided the quantum is large enough that the switching overhead stays small relative to the work done in a quantum.

Markscheme notes

Part (a): A1 for each of the four dispatches. Accept the completion times alone only if the dispatch order and the quantum lengths are shown; the marks are for the trace, not the three numbers.

The commonest error is to place P1 ahead of P2 when the first quantum expires, which produces completion times of 8, 9 and 12 in a different order and gives P1 a waiting time of 1. Award M1 for a self-consistent trace, but no A1 for the dispatch order.

Part (b): M1 for the definition of waiting time as completion minus arrival minus burst, A1 for P1 and P2, A1 for P3, A1 for the average 3.67. Accept 11/3. A candidate who uses turnaround time instead of waiting time obtains 7.67 and scores no A1, because the definition was asked for.

Part (c): A1 for each completion time and A1 for the average 4.00. A candidate who reuses the round-robin completion times here scores no marks.

Part (d): this part is about response time, not throughput. A1 for the response times 2.0 against 4.0, A1 for the convoy effect or the one-quantum bound, and A1 for the context-switching cost. A bare recommendation scores nothing; the evaluation must use the numbers.

Why this question is hard

Scheduling questions are usually answerable from a definition, because the marks are for naming the algorithm that suits a stated workload. This item makes the two candidate algorithms produce answers that are close enough that a slogan cannot separate them, and then asks a question whose answer is not the one the slogan predicts.

Round-robin gives an average waiting time of 3.67 units and first-come-first-served gives 4.00. The difference is small, and a candidate who reasons that first-come-first-served is unfair to short jobs would expect a larger gap. The reason the gap is small is that the trace itself has a surprise in it: P3, the shortest process, waits longer than P2, because it arrives exactly at the moment P1's first quantum expires and P2 has an earlier arrival time. The queue order, not the algorithm's reputation, determines that waiting time.

The part that separates the two schedulers properly is response time. Round-robin gives first responses at 0, 4 and 8, so the average is 2.0; first-come-first-served gives 0, 7 and 11, so the average is 4.0. Response time is what an interactive user experiences, and it is not the same quantity as waiting time, so a candidate who answers part (d) from the part (b) numbers alone will reach the right conclusion for the wrong reason and will not be able to say what the round-robin quantum is buying.

The convoy effect is the general statement of the difference. Under first-come-first-served a long burst delays everything behind it by its full length, so the worst-case delay is unbounded in the length of the longest job. Under round-robin the worst-case delay before a waiting process is dispatched is one quantum. That bound is what makes the algorithm suitable for interactive use, and it is paid for with context switches, which is the trade-off a complete answer names.

The figure is required for parts (a) to (c). The arrival and burst times appear only in the process table, and the round-robin schedule is drawn as a Gantt chart whose blocks give the dispatch order and the quantum boundaries directly. A candidate cannot trace the schedule without reading the chart, and the chart is generated from the same simulation the markscheme uses, so the drawn blocks and the quoted completion times cannot disagree.
