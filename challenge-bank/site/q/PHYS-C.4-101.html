C.4 Standing waves and resonance

Physics HL · PHYS-C.4-101

How sharp is the resonance of a driven mass-spring system?

A student drove a mass-spring system vertically with a vibration generator whose frequency she could set, and recorded the steady-state amplitude of oscillation with a position sensor. She kept the amplitude of the driving motion constant throughout and checked it on an oscilloscope before each reading. The sensor could be read to the nearest $0.01\ \mathrm{mm}$, and the student estimated the uncertainty in a single amplitude reading as $\pm 0.05\ \mathrm{mm}$ because the trace never settled completely. She intended to characterise the resonance by more than its position.

Question

Two quantities describe this system: the frequency at which it responds most strongly, and how sharply it responds. The table gives the first directly. The second has to be extracted.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Plot the data. Determine the resonant frequency and the maximum amplitude, explaining how you obtained them and why the resonant frequency is not simply the frequency of the largest recorded amplitude.

    (Determine)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Determine the two frequencies at which the amplitude has fallen to $A_{\max}/\sqrt{2}$, and hence determine the quality factor $Q$ of the system using $Q = f_{0}/\Delta f$, where $\Delta f$ is the width of the resonance at those two frequencies.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain what the quality factor measures about the system, and why a system with a large $Q$ takes a long time to stop oscillating once the driver is switched off.

    (Explain)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Identify the anomalous row, suggest a specific cause consistent with the readings either side of it, and state what the student should have done when it occurred.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The peak of the curve lies at $52\ \mathrm{Hz}$, where the amplitude is $12.00\ \mathrm{mm}$. (A1)(A1) The resonant frequency is the frequency at the maximum of a smooth curve drawn through the points, so it is found from the graph and not by picking the largest tabulated value; with readings every $2\ \mathrm{Hz}$ near the peak, the true maximum could lie anywhere between about $51$ and $53\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and the data as recorded cannot locate it more closely than that. (M1)(A1) Reading the largest tabulated amplitude gives the same number here only because a reading happens to sit at the top of the curve, and that is luck rather than method.

(b) $A_{\max}/\sqrt{2} = 12.00/1.414 = 8.49\ \mathrm{mm}$. (A1) Drawing a smooth curve through the points and reading off where it crosses $8.49\ \mathrm{mm}$, the lower crossing is at about $50.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and the upper at about $53.8\ \mathrm{Hz}$. (M1)(A1)(A1) Hence (M1)
$$\Delta f = 53.8 - 50.0 = 3.8\ \mathrm{Hz},\qquad Q = \frac{52}{3.8} \approx 14$$
(A1) Since both crossings have to be interpolated on the steepest part of the curve, the value is good to about two significant figures.

(c) $Q$ measures how sharply the system responds: it is a ratio of the energy stored in the oscillation to the energy lost per cycle, and it is large when the damping is light. (A1) A lightly damped system loses only a small fraction of its energy each cycle, so it needs many cycles before the amplitude has fallen appreciably. (A1) Quantitatively the amplitude decays as $e^{-\pi f_{0} t/Q}$, so the decay time is proportional to $Q$: this system rings on for roughly $Q$ radians of oscillation divided into $\pi f_{0}$, that is, of order $Q/f_{0}$ seconds, rather than stopping within a cycle or two. (A1)

(d) The anomalous row is $f = 72\ \mathrm{Hz}$, where the amplitude is $2.10\ \mathrm{mm}$. The readings either side are $1.39\ \mathrm{mm}$ at $66\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $0.63\ \mathrm{mm}$ at $80\ \mathrm{Hz}$, so a smooth curve through them would give about $0.93\ \mathrm{mm}$ at $72\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and the recorded value is more than twice that. (A1) Since the student reports having checked the drive amplitude before each reading, the most likely cause is that the output control on the signal generator was disturbed after that check, so the driving amplitude was larger for that one reading; a mains-frequency pickup would not produce a spike at $72\ \mathrm{Hz}$. (A1) She should have re-checked the drive amplitude, seen that it had changed, and re-taken the reading immediately; if that was not possible, the point should be excluded and the exclusion recorded in the log, not quietly dropped from the graph. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Accept $52\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $12.00\ \mathrm{mm}$. The explanation mark is for the point that the tabulated maximum is not the resonant frequency in general; a candidate who says the two are the same because the largest reading happens to be at the peak has not answered. (b) Accept $A_{\max}/\sqrt{2} = 8.49\ \mathrm{mm}$, crossings anywhere in $49.9$ to $50.3\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $53.5$ to $54.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and $Q$ from $13$ to $15$. Do not penalise a candidate who reads the crossings from a straight-line interpolation between adjacent points and gets $14.1$; note in feedback that the curve is not straight there and that linear interpolation is an approximation. (c) Accept any correct statement about the ratio of stored to lost energy per cycle; the mark for the decay is for linking the long ring-down to the small fractional loss, and a candidate who simply asserts that a large $Q$ rings for longer has not explained. (d) The cause must be tested against the number: credit any suggestion that would raise one reading while leaving its neighbours alone, and reject friction or damping answers, which would move the whole curve rather than one point.

Why this question is hard

The reason this item exists is that the two numbers a student can read straight off the table are the two that matter least. The resonant frequency and the peak amplitude say where the system responds and how strongly, but they say nothing about how selective that response is, and selectivity is what distinguishes a useful resonator from a useless one. A bell and a bowl of jelly can share a resonant frequency; what separates them is the width of the peak. Getting at the width requires the half-power points, which sit on the steepest part of the curve where interpolation is hardest, so the item forces the candidate to draw the curve rather than read the table.

The choice of $A_{\max}/\sqrt{2}$ rather than $A_{\max}/2$ is worth dwelling on in teaching, because it is the one place where the factor of two is routinely misplaced. Power is proportional to amplitude squared, so halving the power means dividing the amplitude by $\sqrt{2}$, not by $2$. Using $A_{\max}/2$ would give a wider interval, a larger $\Delta f$ and a smaller $Q$, and the error is invisible in the working because every step afterwards is correct. Part (b) is worth five marks largely because of that one factor.

Part (d) asks for a cause and then quietly requires it to be checked. The point at $72\ \mathrm{Hz}$ sits above its neighbours, and the tempting answer is to say the resonance is broad or that there is a second mode. Neither works: a second resonance would raise the readings around $72\ \mathrm{Hz}$, not just the one, and a broader peak would lift $66\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $80\ \mathrm{Hz}$ too. Only something that acts on that single reading will do, and the item supplies the clue by telling the candidate that the drive amplitude was checked before each reading but not after. The deeper lesson is about experimental bookkeeping: a control that is verified before a measurement but not after it is only half checked, and the row that gives the game away is the one nobody was looking at, far off resonance where the amplitude is small and the error is proportionally large.
