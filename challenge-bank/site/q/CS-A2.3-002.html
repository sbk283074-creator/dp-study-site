A2.3 Bandwidth-delay product: when increasing the link rate changes nothing

Computer science HL · CS-A2.3-002

A2.3 Data transmissions (transmission rate, bandwidth and latency; the effect of protocol overhead)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedCalculateDetermineExplainState

Question

A remote backup service transfers files between two sites. The connection has a round-trip
time of $40\ \mathrm{ms}$. The sending software uses a sliding window of $64\ \mathrm{kB}$
($65536$ bytes): it may send that much data before it must stop and wait for an
acknowledgement. The situation is shown in the diagram.

(a) Calculate the time taken to transmit one window at $100\ \mathrm{Mbps}$, and hence the
maximum throughput achievable with this window. \tag{3}

(b) The link is upgraded to $1\ \mathrm{Gbps}$. Calculate the new transmission time for one
window and the new maximum throughput. \tag{4}

(c) Determine the window size, in bytes, that would be needed to make full use of the
$1\ \mathrm{Gbps}$ link. \tag{5}

(d) Explain why the upgrade produced no improvement, and state what should be changed. \tag{4}

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the time to transmit one window at $100\ \mathrm{Mbps}$ and hence the maximum throughput achievable with this window.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Calculate the new transmission time for one window at $1\ \mathrm{Gbps}$ and the new maximum throughput.

    (Calculate)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Determine the window size, in bytes, needed to make full use of the $1\ \mathrm{Gbps}$ link.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Explain why the upgrade produced no improvement, and state what should be changed.

    (Explain)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) One window is $65536 \times 8 = 524288$ bits (A1). At $100\ \mathrm{Mbps}$ the
transmission time is

$$t = \frac{524288}{1.00 \times 10^{8}} = 5.24\ \mathrm{ms} \tag{A1}$$

The sender must then wait for the acknowledgement, so one window is delivered per round-trip
time and the maximum throughput is

$$\frac{524288\ \mathrm{bits}}{0.040\ \mathrm{s}} = 1.31 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{bps}
= 13.1\ \mathrm{Mbps} \tag{A1}$$

Note that this is $13.1\%$ of the link rate, even though the sender is idle for
$40 - 5.24 = 34.8\ \mathrm{ms}$ of every $40\ \mathrm{ms}$ cycle.

(b) At $1\ \mathrm{Gbps}$ the transmission time falls by a factor of ten (A1):

$$t = \frac{524288}{1.00 \times 10^{9}} = 0.524\ \mathrm{ms} \tag{A1}$$

The throughput is unchanged, because the amount sent per round trip has not changed (A1):

$$\frac{524288}{0.040} = 13.1\ \mathrm{Mbps} \tag{A1}$$

The sender is now idle for $39.5\ \mathrm{ms}$ of every $40\ \mathrm{ms}$ — it finishes sooner
and then waits longer, and the two effects cancel exactly.

(c) To keep the link busy the window must be at least the bandwidth-delay product (M1):

$$W = B \times \mathrm{RTT} = 1.00 \times 10^{9} \times 0.040 = 4.0 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{bits}
\tag{A1}$$

$$W = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{7}}{8} = 5.0 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{bytes} = 5.0\ \mathrm{MB}
\tag{A1}$$

Compared with the existing window this is (A1)

$$\frac{5.0 \times 10^{6}}{65536} = 76.3$$

times larger (A1). Equivalently, the existing window keeps the link busy for
$\frac{5.24}{40} = 13.1\%$ of the cycle at $100\ \mathrm{Mbps}$ and for
$\frac{0.524}{40} = 1.31\%$ at $1\ \mathrm{Gbps}$, so the utilisation falls by a factor of ten
exactly as the rate rises by a factor of ten, and the two effects cancel precisely.

Two checks are worth applying to the answer. First, the required window scales with the
round-trip time as well as with the rate, so halving the latency would halve the window
needed — a reminder that latency and bandwidth are separate constraints and can be traded
against each other. Second, $5.0\ \mathrm{MB}$ of unacknowledged data is a large amount to have
in flight, and in practice the window would be limited by the receiving system's buffer and by
congestion control rather than by the arithmetic here; the bandwidth-delay product gives the
minimum window that makes the link usable, not a window that any real system would necessarily
grant.

(d) The upgrade improved the rate at which data is placed on the link but not the amount that
may be in flight (A1). With a windowed protocol the throughput is
$\frac{\text{window}}{\mathrm{RTT}}$ whenever the window is smaller than the bandwidth-delay
product, and the link rate does not appear in that expression at all (R1). The window here,
$64\ \mathrm{kB}$, is well below the $5.0\ \mathrm{MB}$ bandwidth-delay product of the upgraded
link, so the link is idle for $98.7\%$ of each cycle and the tenfold increase in rate is
invisible (A1).

What should be changed is the window, not the link (A1): the sending software needs a window of
about $5\ \mathrm{MB}$ to use the $1\ \mathrm{Gbps}$ link, and with the existing $64\ \mathrm{kB}$
window there was no point buying the upgrade at all. The general diagnostic is to compute the
bandwidth-delay product before paying for bandwidth; if the window is smaller, the money is
being spent on the wrong thing.

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for converting the window to $524288$ bits. (A1) for $5.24\ \mathrm{ms}$.
(A1) for $13.1\ \mathrm{Mbps}$; accept $1.31 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{bps}$. A candidate who
divides the link rate by ten or otherwise tries to include the link rate in the throughput
earns at most the first (A1).

(b) (A1) for $0.524\ \mathrm{ms}$. (A1) for the observation that the window has not changed.
(A1) for $13.1\ \mathrm{Mbps}$. (A1) for the cancellation — the sender finishes sooner and waits
longer. A candidate who reports a higher throughput here has not understood windowing and loses
the last three marks.

(c) (M1) for the bandwidth-delay product. (A1) for $4.0 \times 10^{7}$ bits. (A1) for
$5.0 \times 10^{6}$ bytes. (A1) for the ratio $76.3$. (A1) for the utilisation argument.
Accept $5\ \mathrm{MB}$ or $4.77\ \mathrm{MiB}$ provided the unit is stated.

(d) (A1) for the distinction between the rate and the amount in flight. (R1) for the
throughput expression, which must be stated as window over round-trip time. (A1) for the
quantitative comparison with the bandwidth-delay product. (A1) for identifying the window as
what should change.

Condonation: allow a candidate who says "increase the window" without the figure, provided the
reasoning about the amount in flight is present.

Why this question is hard

The result is counter-intuitive enough that it is worth stating in one sentence: with a
windowed protocol, throughput is the amount you may have in flight divided by the time a
round trip takes, and the speed of the link does not appear in that expression at all until the
window exceeds the bandwidth-delay product.

The arithmetic makes it concrete. At $100\ \mathrm{Mbps}$ the $64\ \mathrm{kB}$ window takes
$5.24\ \mathrm{ms}$ to put on the wire, and then the sender sits idle for the remaining
$34.8\ \mathrm{ms}$ of the round trip. It is using the link $13.1\%$ of the time. Upgrade to
$1\ \mathrm{Gbps}$ and the same window takes $0.524\ \mathrm{ms}$ — the sender finishes sooner
and waits longer, and since the amount delivered per round trip has not changed, neither has the
throughput. Utilisation drops to $1.31\%$. The tenfold improvement in rate is exactly cancelled
by a tenfold drop in utilisation.

This is why the bandwidth-delay product is the first thing to compute. It is the amount of data
that must be in flight to keep the pipe full: rate times round-trip time. For the upgraded link
that is $1\ \mathrm{Gbps} \times 40\ \mathrm{ms} = 5.0\ \mathrm{MB}$, which is $76$ times the
window the software actually uses. Until the window is increased — in the protocol
configuration, not in the hardware — the upgrade buys nothing.

The practical lesson generalises well past this question. Latency and bandwidth are different
constraints and they bind at different times: if the window is small, latency binds and
bandwidth is irrelevant; if the window is large enough, bandwidth binds and latency stops
mattering. Buying more of the one that is not binding is the commonest way to spend money on a
network and get nothing for it, and the way to tell which one is binding is to compare the
window with the bandwidth-delay product.

The figure is there to make the idle time visible. Both rows show the same $64\ \mathrm{kB}$, and
what changes between them is only how much of the $40\ \mathrm{ms}$ is coloured in.

Metadata

Challenge lever
Doubling the link rate tenfold leaves the throughput unchanged, and the arithmetic that shows it is simple enough to be embarrassing. Throughput with a windowed protocol is window divided by round-trip time, and the link rate never appears in that expression once the window is smaller than the bandwidth-delay product. The data rate is a red herring, and the quantity that is actually binding — the window — is the one nothing in the stem draws attention to.

Adaptation
The source's transfer-time questions give a file size and a rate and ask for the time, so bandwidth is always the governing quantity and latency never enters. Here a sliding window is introduced, which makes the amount in flight the binding constraint and reduces the link rate to a quantity that cancels out. Parts (a) and (b) ask for the throughput at two rates tenfold apart and the answer is identical, which the source's framing can never produce, and part (c) asks for the bandwidth-delay product. Context, structure, given-versus-asked and the reasoning chain all differ.
Verification
Transmission times and throughputs are evaluated at both rates; the required window is computed as rate times round-trip time and compared with $65536$ bytes; utilisation is computed as the ratio of transmission time to round-trip time at each rate.
