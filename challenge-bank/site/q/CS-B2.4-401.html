B2.4 Binary search on an array that is ordered except for one transposed pair

Computer science HL · CS-B2.4-401

Question

The figure shows an array of twelve entries held in positions numbered 0 to 11. Reading from position 0 the values are 2, 5, 8, 21, 13, 34, 55, 89, 144, 233, 377 and 610.

Eleven of the twelve entries are in ascending order. Exactly one pair of adjacent entries has been transposed, and the two entries concerned are shaded in the figure. The array belongs to a frozen data set. Its contents may not be changed, no entry may be moved, and the array may not be re-sorted or copied into a new order.

A binary search is used to look for the target value 13. The implementation takes the middle position as the integer part of (lo + hi) divided by 2, compares the entry at that position with the target, moves lo to that position plus one when the entry is smaller than the target, and moves hi to that position minus one when the entry is larger. The figure traces the comparisons the search makes. An entry that has been compared once is never compared again.

(a) State the position and the value examined at each of the three comparisons, and state the position at which 13 is stored.

(b) Explain why the search reports that 13 is absent although 13 is stored in the array, and state the property of the data that the search relies on.

(c) Determine the number of comparisons a linear search from position 0 makes before it finds 13, and determine how many of the twelve stored values this binary search still locates.

Reveal the answer

(a) The first comparison is at position 5, holding 34. Because 34 is greater than the target, hi becomes 4. The second comparison is at position (0 + 4) / 2 = 2, holding 8. Because 8 is less than the target, lo becomes 3. The third comparison is at position (3 + 4) / 2 = 3, holding 21, and because 21 is greater than the target, hi becomes 2 (A1). The value 13 is stored at position 4, one place to the right of the entry it was compared against (A1).

(b) Discarding a half of the array is legitimate only when the entries are in ascending order. In that case an entry greater than the target proves that every entry to its right is at least as great and therefore also greater than the target, so the whole right-hand block can be abandoned with no risk of abandoning the target. That inference is what the third comparison makes, and here it is false: position 3 holds 21 and position 4 holds 13, so the entry immediately to the right is smaller than the entry that was compared, and the half thrown away contains the value being sought. After the third comparison hi has become 2 while lo is 3, so lo is greater than hi, the interval is empty, the loop terminates and the search reports that 13 is not present (M1). The search is behaving correctly as written; the array is not sorted, and sorted order, or at least non-decreasing order, is the precondition it depends on (A1). One adjacent transposition is enough to break it, and the figure places the transposition so that the third comparison lands on the larger of the two entries and draws the false inference from it.

(c) A linear search from position 0 compares 2 with the target, then 5, then 8, then 21, and only at the fifth comparison reaches position 4, where the entry is 13. It therefore makes five comparisons (A1). The binary search still locates eleven of the twelve stored values. Every value other than 13 is found at the position where it is stored, including 21 at position 3, because the comparison at position 3 succeeds and the search stops before any half is discarded. The single value that is unreachable is 13 itself, the smaller of the two transposed entries: it has moved to a position to the right of a larger value, so a comparison against that larger value consults the ordering, finds the ordering wrong, and discards the half that holds the target. The other transposed entry, 21, has moved one place to the left and remains reachable, which is why exactly one value, and not two, is lost (A1). In general a single swap that puts a larger entry before a smaller one can hide the smaller entry and only the smaller entry, because it is the smaller entry that now lies on the far side of a larger neighbour.

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for the three comparisons: position 5 holding 34, position 2 holding 8 and position 3 holding 21, in that order; (A1) for position 4 as the location of 13. Accept the comparisons quoted as values rather than positions provided the positions are attached to them. Do not accept a candidate who lists the comparisons out of order, because the order is what the trace in the figure establishes.

(b) (M1) for the argument that a comparison against a larger entry is used to justify discarding the right-hand half and that this inference requires the array to be in ascending order; (A1) for naming sorted or non-decreasing order as the precondition, and for connecting the failure to the pair 21, 13 rather than to the algorithm. A candidate who says the algorithm is faulty, or that the middle position is being computed wrongly, loses the second mark: the implementation is exactly as specified and every step of it is correct. Do not accept 'the array is not sorted' as the whole answer; the mark is for showing which discarded half contained the target and why the discard looked sound.

(c) (A1) for five comparisons, with the five entries identified; (A1) for eleven of the twelve, or equivalently for the statement that 13 alone is unreachable. Award the second mark to a candidate who identifies 13 as the lost value and explains that 21 survives because the comparison against 21 succeeds before a half is discarded.

Why this question is hard

The item is short and needs almost no arithmetic. What it needs is for the candidate to notice that the array is not what it appears to be, and that the trace in the figure is not an error but a consequence. Every textbook figure for binary search shows a sorted array, so the sortedness is read off the picture and never checked, and the precondition is learned as a remark attached to the algorithm rather than as a load-bearing assumption.

The trace is chosen so that the failure looks like ordinary progress. The first comparison discards the top half, which is correct and unremarkable. The second discards positions 0 and 1, which is also correct: 2 and 5 really are both less than 13, so those two entries are safely abandoned. The third comparison is the one that matters, and it reports 21, a value that is genuinely greater than the target. Every comparison has produced a true statement about the entry it examined. What the search then does with the last true statement is to generalise from it to a whole block of the array, and that generalisation is the only step that is false.

The count in part (c) is where the lesson becomes quantitative. Binary search does not merely fail here; it fails on one of twelve values while succeeding on the other eleven, and the value it loses is the smaller of the two transposed entries. So the damage from a single transposition is exactly one value, and it is predictable in advance: a swap that places a larger entry before a smaller one can hide the smaller entry, and nothing else. That is worth knowing because it tells you which way the failure will show up. A test suite that checks a handful of values chosen from the array will very likely pass, since only one value in twelve is affected, and the defect will surface in production on the one value nobody sampled.

It is also worth stating plainly why linear search is the right tool here. The array is frozen, it is unordered in one place, and no amount of cleverness in the search can recover an ordering that is not there. Five comparisons against a linear scan is the honest cost, and the same five comparisons do not depend on the array being sorted at all. When the data cannot be re-ordered and cannot be trusted to satisfy a precondition, the algorithm that does not need the precondition is the correct choice, whatever its asymptotic cost.

Here the array satisfies the precondition everywhere except at one adjacent pair, and that single transposition is enough to hide one value from the algorithm while leaving the other eleven reachable, so a candidate who trusts the array to be sorted reports a present value as absent. The trace in the figure is the whole argument: the third comparison lands on the larger of the two transposed entries, concludes from it that everything to its right must also be too large, and throws away the right half of the array, which contains the target. Nothing about the algorithm is wrong and nothing about the trace is wrong; what is wrong is the assumption the candidate brought to the array. The second half of the item then forces the candidate to say how much of the array survives the damage, which is the question that separates 'binary search needs sorted data' as a slogan from knowing what breaks when the data is not sorted.
