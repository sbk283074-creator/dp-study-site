P3 extended problem: series solution of a differential equation with no elementary solution

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-014

Topic 5: CalculusAHL 5.19 (Maclaurin series), AHL 5.18 (first order differential equations)P314 marksdifficulty 5technology: allowedShow thatHenceDetermine

Question

The function $y$ is defined for all real $x$ by
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = e^{-x^{2}}, \qquad y(0) = 0,$$
and $y$ has a Maclaurin series $y(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n x^{n}$ that converges for all real $x$. Write $t_k = \dfrac{(-1)^{k} x^{2k+1}}{k!\,(2k+1)}$ for $k \ge 0$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $a_n = 0$ for every even $n$, and that $a_{2k+1} = \dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{k!\,(2k+1)}$ for every integer $k \ge 0$, so that $y(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} t_k$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Show that $\left|\dfrac{t_{k+1}}{t_k}\right| = \dfrac{x^{2}}{k+1} \cdot \dfrac{2k+1}{2k+3}$, and deduce that $|t_{k+1}| < |t_k|$ for every $k \ge 0$ whenever $|x| \le 0.6$.

    (Show that)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Hence show that
    $$\left|\,y(0.6) - \sum_{k=0}^{3} t_k\,\right| < 5 \times 10^{-5}.$$

    (Hence)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Hence determine $y(0.6)$ correct to four decimal places, showing that your value is reliable at that accuracy.

    (Determine)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Write $y(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n x^{n}$, so $y'(x) = \sum_{n \ge 1} n\,a_n x^{n-1} = \sum_{n \ge 0} (n+1)a_{n+1} x^{n}$ (M1). Also $e^{-x^{2}} = \sum_{k \ge 0} \dfrac{(-1)^{k} x^{2k}}{k!}$, since replacing $x$ by $-x^{2}$ in the exponential series is valid for all real $x$ (A1). Equating coefficients of $x^{n}$ in $\sum_{n \ge 0} (n+1)a_{n+1}x^{n} = \sum_{k \ge 0} \dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{k!} x^{2k}$: the right-hand side has no odd powers, so $(n+1)a_{n+1} = 0$ whenever $n$ is odd, that is $a_n = 0$ for every even $n \ge 2$; and $a_0 = y(0) = 0$ (A1). When $n = 2k$ we get $(2k+1)a_{2k+1} = \dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}$, hence $a_{2k+1} = \dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{k!\,(2k+1)}$ for every $k \ge 0$ (A1). Substituting gives $y(x) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \dfrac{(-1)^{k} x^{2k+1}}{k!\,(2k+1)} = \sum_{k \ge 0} t_k$ (AG).

(b) Using $t_k = \dfrac{(-1)^{k}x^{2k+1}}{k!(2k+1)}$,
$$\frac{t_{k+1}}{t_k} = \frac{(-1)^{k+1}x^{2k+3}}{(k+1)!(2k+3)} \cdot \frac{k!(2k+1)}{(-1)^{k}x^{2k+1}} = -x^{2} \cdot \frac{2k+1}{(k+1)(2k+3)}$$ (M1),
so $\left|\dfrac{t_{k+1}}{t_k}\right| = \dfrac{x^{2}}{k+1} \cdot \dfrac{2k+1}{2k+3}$ (A1). Now $\dfrac{2k+1}{2k+3} < 1$ for every $k \ge 0$, so $\left|\dfrac{t_{k+1}}{t_k}\right| < \dfrac{x^{2}}{k+1} \le \dfrac{x^{2}}{1} = x^{2}$ when $k \ge 0$ and $|x| \le 0.6$; since $0.6^{2} = 0.36 < 1$, we get $|t_{k+1}| < |t_k|$ for every $k \ge 0$ (A1) (AG).

(c) From part (b) the terms $t_k$ alternate in sign, because of the factor $(-1)^{k}$, and their moduli decrease strictly. For such a series the truncation error is bounded by the modulus of the first omitted term (M1). We compute, at $x = 0.6$,
$$t_0 = 0.6, \quad t_1 = -\frac{0.216}{3} = -0.072, \quad t_2 = \frac{0.07776}{10} = 0.007776, \quad t_3 = -\frac{0.0279936}{42} = -0.000666514$$ (M1) (A1),
so $\sum_{k=0}^{3} t_k = 0.5351094857$ (A1). The first omitted term is $t_4 = \dfrac{0.6^{9}}{4! \cdot 9} = \dfrac{0.010077696}{216} = 4.6656 \times 10^{-5}$ (M1), and the alternating-series bound therefore gives
$$\left|\,y(0.6) - \sum_{k=0}^{3} t_k\,\right| \le |t_4| = 4.6656 \times 10^{-5} < 5 \times 10^{-5}$$ (A1) (AG).

(d) Four terms are not enough for four decimal places: the interval they determine is $(0.5351094857 - 0.000046656,\; 0.5351094857 + 0.000046656) = (0.5350628, 0.5351561)$, and this straddles the rounding boundary $0.53515$, so the fourth decimal place is not yet decided (M1). Adding the next term, $t_4 = 0.000046656$, gives $\sum_{k=0}^{4} t_k = 0.5351561417$ with the first omitted term now $|t_5| = \dfrac{0.6^{11}}{5! \cdot 11} = \dfrac{0.0036279706}{1320} = 2.7485 \times 10^{-6}$ (M1), so
$$0.5351533932 < y(0.6) < 0.5351561417,$$
and both endpoints round to $0.5352$; hence $y(0.6) = 0.5352$ to four decimal places (A1) (AG). (The exact value is $\tfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\,\mathrm{erf}(0.6) = 0.5351535268$, which lies inside the interval, as it must.)

Markscheme notes

(a) Accept the substitution performed in either order -- comparing coefficients of $y'$ with the expanded exponential is the expected route, but a candidate who differentiates the series twice and uses $y'' = -2x\,e^{-x^{2}} = -2xy'$ to obtain the recurrence $(n+2)(n+1)a_{n+2} = -2(n-1)a_{n-1}$ earns full marks provided the parity split is then carried out. The statement $a_0 = y(0) = 0$ must be justified from the initial condition, not assumed. Do not require the word 'induction' for the closed form: a correctly explained substitution is sufficient. A candidate who writes the series with the wrong parity, for example keeping even powers, forfeits the final (A1).

(b) The ratio must be derived, not quoted. Award (M1) for forming $t_{k+1}/t_k$ and (A1) for the simplified modulus. The deduction that $|t_{k+1}| < |t_k|$ needs the numerical fact $x^{2} \le 0.36 < 1$; a bare assertion that the ratio is less than one, without evaluating it at $|x| = 0.6$, earns nothing. Condone the weaker but still valid observation that $\dfrac{2k+1}{2k+3} \le \dfrac{1}{3}$ for $k \ge 0$, since $0.36 \times \tfrac13 < 1$.

(c) The marks are for the alternating-series bound (M1), the four terms (M1)(A1), their sum (A1), the first omitted term (M1) and the comparison (A1). Do not award the final (A1) for a bound obtained by 'the terms get small'; the error bound for an alternating series must be the first omitted term, and a candidate who sums more terms and quotes a difference between successive partial sums has not bounded anything. Follow-through: a candidate whose part (b) is wrong but who correctly identifies the first omitted term and compares its modulus with $5 \times 10^{-5}$ may still earn the last two marks.

(d) Award (M1) for recognising that four terms are insufficient, (M1) for extending to five terms with a new bound, and (A1) for $0.5352$. A candidate who simply writes $0.5352$ from a calculator without an interval earns no marks, because the question asks for reliability at that accuracy. Accept $0.5351$ only if the accompanying interval is genuinely contained in $(0.53505, 0.53515)$; it cannot be, since the true value is $0.5351535$.

Why this question is hard

This item is built on a reversal. The equation is separable and the solution is, in principle, available in closed form: $y(x) = \int_0^{x} e^{-t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t$. A strong candidate will find that in the first thirty seconds and feel that the problem is over. It is not, because that integral has no elementary antiderivative -- it is the error function -- so the closed form cannot produce the number that part (d) asks for. The method that looks like a concession to approximation is the only one that can answer the question.

The coefficient extraction is routine: substituting a power series into the differential equation and comparing coefficients with the exponential series gives a recurrence in which every even coefficient vanishes, because the right-hand side $e^{-x^{2}}$ has only even powers while the derivative of a power series carries the index shift. The surviving coefficients are $\dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{k!(2k+1)}$, which is the series of the error function in disguise.

What makes the item difficult is the demand for an error bound, and this is where the two halves of the series have to be handled differently. The signs alternate because of the $(-1)^{k}$, and the moduli decrease because the ratio is $\dfrac{x^{2}}{k+1}\cdot\dfrac{2k+1}{2k+3} < \dfrac{x^{2}}{k+1} \le 0.36$ throughout the region of interest. An alternating series with strictly decreasing moduli has its truncation error bounded by the first omitted term -- nothing more and nothing less -- so the whole problem reduces to evaluating one term. That is a strong result and it is why the 'approximate' method wins.

The subtlety that separates a good answer from a correct one is part (d). Four terms give $0.5351095 \pm 0.0000467$, and that interval contains $0.53515$, the boundary between $0.5351$ and $0.5352$. So four terms do not determine the fourth decimal place, even though the error is small. A candidate who quotes $0.5351$ because the first four terms give it will be wrong; a candidate who quotes $0.5352$ from a calculator will be right for the wrong reason. Five terms give $0.5351534 < y(0.6) < 0.5351561$, and now both endpoints round to $0.5352$, which is an answer with a reason attached. The habit being tested is asking how many terms the requested accuracy actually requires, rather than assuming that a small error is a sufficient error.

Topic 5: Calculus — P3 extended problem: series solution of a differential equation with no elementary solution
Challenge lever
The differential equation is separable, so the first move a prepared student makes is to separate it and integrate -- and that move is correct and useless, because the integral of e^{-x^2} is not elementary, so the exact solution produces no number at all. The route that looks like a retreat, grinding out coefficients of a Maclaurin series, is the one that yields a value with a defensible error bound, because the series alternates with decreasing terms and therefore bounds its own tail. The item inverts the usual status ordering of the two methods: the exact method dead-ends exactly where a number is required, and the 'approximate' method is the rigorous one.

Adaptation
The source genre contrasts an exact-but-useless closed form with an approximate-but-rigorous series, and asks for a value to a stated accuracy. Three of the permitted changes were made. First, the integrand was chosen as e^{-x^2}, so that the alternating series arises from the expansion of a single exponential and the ratio of consecutive terms has the elementary form x^2/(k+1) times (2k+1)/(2k+3); this keeps the whole problem inside AHL 5.19. Second, the evaluation point was fixed at x = 0.6 rather than a natural value such as 1, because at x = 0.6 four terms are deliberately insufficient for four decimal places while five terms suffice -- the source genre typically asks for a value at which the truncation is comfortable. Third, the ratio and the monotonicity of the moduli were made explicit parts, so that the alternating-series bound is derived rather than quoted. The context and all numerical data are original.
Verification
