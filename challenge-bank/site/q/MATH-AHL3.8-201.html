3.8 The number of solutions of sin 2x = k cos x on 0 <= x <= 2pi as a function of k: the count is four while 0 < |k| < 2, five at k = 0 because the branch sin x = 0 reaches both endpoints, and two once |k| >= 2 because that branch loses its solutions or merges into the other one

Question

Let $k$ be a real constant and consider the equation
$$\sin 2x = k\cos x, \qquad 0 \le x \le 2\pi.$$
Write $S(k)$ for the number of distinct values of $x$ in that interval that satisfy the equation. The interval is closed at both ends, so a value of $x$ equal to $0$ or to $2\pi$ counts as a solution when it satisfies the equation. The figure shows the two curves $y = \sin 2x$ and $y = k\cos x$ for $k = 2.5$ and for $k = 0.75$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the equation is equivalent to $\cos x\,(2\sin x - k) = 0$, and state the two solutions of $\cos x = 0$ on the interval $0 \le x \le 2\pi$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine $S(k)$ when $0 < |k| < 2$, justifying why neither solution of $2\sin x = k$ coincides with either of the two solutions you found in part (a).

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine $S(0)$, and explain why it is greater than the value of $S(k)$ you found in part (b) even though $k = 0$ satisfies $0 < |k| < 2$.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine $S(k)$ when $k = 2$, when $k = -2$ and when $|k| > 2$, and explain what happens to the two solutions of $2\sin x = k$ as $k$ reaches each of the values $-2$ and $2$.

    (Determine)

Reveal the answer

(a) Using the double angle identity $\sin 2x = 2\sin x\cos x$, the equation becomes
$$2\sin x\cos x = k\cos x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \cos x\,(2\sin x - k) = 0.$$
This is an identity, not an approximation: the two equations have exactly the same solution set, because subtracting the right side from the left gives $\cos x\,(2\sin x - k)$ identically (M1). The factorisation is what makes the problem finite, since a product is zero exactly when one of its factors is (A1).
Setting $\cos x = 0$ gives $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ and $x = \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$ on $0 \le x \le 2\pi$ (A1). These two values do not depend on $k$ at all, and they are solutions of the original equation for every value of $k$, because when $\cos x = 0$ the left side $\sin 2x = 2\sin x\cos x$ is also zero.
(b) The second branch is $2\sin x = k$, that is $\sin x = k/2$. For $0 < |k| < 2$ the number $k/2$ lies strictly between $-1$ and $1$, so $\sin x = k/2$ has exactly two solutions in one full period (M1). On $0 \le x \le 2\pi$ they are
$$x = \arcsin\!\left(\frac{k}{2}\right) \quad\text{and}\quad x = \pi - \arcsin\!\left(\frac{k}{2}\right).$$
Write $\alpha = \arcsin(k/2)$, so that $0 < |\alpha| < \pi/2$ (A1). If $k > 0$ then $\alpha \in (0, \pi/2)$ and $\pi - \alpha \in (\pi/2, \pi)$; if $k < 0$ then $\alpha \in (-\pi/2, 0)$, which puts the first solution at $2\pi + \alpha \in (3\pi/2, 2\pi)$ and the second at $\pi - \alpha \in (\pi, 3\pi/2)$. In both cases the two values avoid $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$ strictly: coincidence would need $\sin(\pi/2) = 1 = k/2$ or $\sin(3\pi/2) = -1 = k/2$, that is $|k| = 2$, which part (b) excludes (A1). So the two branches contribute disjoint sets, and $S(k) = 2 + 2 = 4$ (A1).
(c) At $k = 0$ the second branch reads $\sin x = 0$. On the closed interval $0 \le x \le 2\pi$ this has three solutions, $x = 0$, $x = \pi$ and $x = 2\pi$ (M1), because the equation is satisfied at both endpoints as well as in the interior. Combining with the two roots of $\cos x = 0$ gives
$$S(0) = 5, \qquad x \in \left\{0,\ \frac{\pi}{2},\ \pi,\ \frac{3\pi}{2},\ 2\pi\right\} \;(A1).$$
The reason the count rises is not that the branch gains a root in any general sense, but that the two roots of $\sin x = k/2$ move onto the endpoints of the interval as $k \to 0$: $\alpha \to 0$ and $\pi - \alpha \to \pi$, and the endpoint $2\pi$ is reached from below by $2\pi + \alpha$ when $k$ approaches zero through negative values (A1). Nothing new appears; the same two roots sit on the boundary, where they still count, and the point $x = 2\pi$ now coincides with a root that was previously outside the interval (A1).
(d) At $k = 2$ the branch is $\sin x = 1$, whose only solution is $x = \pi/2$; that value is already one of the roots of $\cos x = 0$, so it adds nothing. At $k = -2$ the branch is $\sin x = -1$, whose only solution is $x = 3\pi/2$, again already counted (A1). Hence $S(2) = S(-2) = 2$ (A1).
For $|k| > 2$ the number $k/2$ lies outside $[-1, 1]$ and $\sin x = k/2$ has no solution at all (A1), so the solution set is exactly $\{\pi/2,\ 3\pi/2\}$ and $S(k) = 2$ (A1). Geometrically the two roots of the branch move towards $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$ as $k$ increases to $2$, meet them at $k = 2$, and then leave the interval $[-1,1]$ altogether, which is why the count is unchanged from $k = 2$ onwards.
Collecting the regimes: $S(k) = 5$ at $k = 0$; $S(k) = 4$ for $0 < |k| < 2$; and $S(k) = 2$ for $|k| \ge 2$.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for applying the double angle identity to $\sin 2x$ and factorising, (A1) for $\cos x\,(2\sin x - k) = 0$, (A1) for the pair $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$. A candidate who divides both sides by $\cos x$ has destroyed the branch that carries the whole question and cannot score the final (A1); dividing by $\cos x$ is the single most damaging move available here, and it also silently discards the roots $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$.
(b) (M1) for solving $\sin x = k/2$ on the stated interval, (A1) for naming the two solutions $\alpha$ and $\pi - \alpha$, (A1) for the argument that they cannot coincide with $\pi/2$ or $3\pi/2$ unless $|k| = 2$, (A1) for $S(k) = 4$. The justification mark requires the candidate to say *why* the four values are distinct, not merely to assert it: an answer that writes the four values and adds four is worth (M1)(A1) and nothing more. Accept any correct argument, including comparing the ranges of $\alpha$ and $\pi - \alpha$ or observing that $\sin(\pi/2) = 1$.
(c) (M1) for solving $\sin x = 0$ and finding three roots on the closed interval, (A1) for $S(0) = 5$ with the solution set listed, (A1) for explaining that the two roots of the branch have moved onto the endpoints, (A1) for identifying $x = 2\pi$ as the value that was outside the interval for every other $k$. A candidate who answers $4$ has counted the roots of $\sin x = 0$ on $0 < x < 2\pi$ instead of on the closed interval; that answer scores the first (M1) only. A candidate who answers $6$ has double-counted $x = 0$ as both a root of $\cos x = 0$ and a root of $\sin x = 0$, which is false, and scores nothing beyond (M1).
(d) (A1) for $S(2) = 2$ with the reason that $\sin x = 1$ gives only $\pi/2$, (A1) for $S(-2) = 2$ with the reason that $\sin x = -1$ gives only $3\pi/2$, (A1) for $S(k) = 2$ when $|k| > 2$ with the reason that $\sin x = k/2$ has no solution, (A1) for the geometric description of the two roots merging and then leaving $[-1,1]$. Condonation: a candidate who writes a single case $|k| \ge 2$ and gives the reason for each of the three sub-cases inside it scores all four marks.
Follow-through: a candidate who mislabels the regime boundaries in (b) and then works consistently in (c) and (d) keeps the method marks in those parts. Forfeits: treating the interval as open and answering $S(0) = 4$; answering $S(k) = 4$ for all $k$ by adding two roots from each factor without checking that they are distinct; and claiming that $\sin x = k/2$ has two solutions for $|k| > 2$.

Why this question is hard

This item is a case analysis, and the case analysis is the mathematics. Nothing in it is hard to compute: one identity, one factorisation, and then the reading of a single trigonometric equation. What it tests is whether the candidate treats the factorised form as two independent equations with a fixed number of roots each, or as two equations whose root counts depend on the parameter.

The factorisation is the hinge. From $\cos x\,(2\sin x - k) = 0$ the solutions come from two sources, and the first source, $\cos x = 0$, is completely rigid: it always supplies $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$, whatever $k$ is. The second source, $\sin x = k/2$, is completely flexible. It supplies two roots while $|k| < 2$, one root at $|k| = 2$, and none at all beyond that. The natural error is to notice that two plus two is four and to stop, reporting four for every value of $k$. That answer is right for the whole open range $0 < |k| < 2$ and wrong everywhere else, which is exactly the kind of answer that is dangerous in an examination: it is right often enough to feel confirmed.

The value $|k| = 2$ is where the two sources touch. At $k = 2$ the root $\arcsin(k/2)$ is $\pi/2$, which is already a root of $\cos x = 0$; the two branches do not simply stop contributing, they merge, and merging is not adding. A candidate who counts the branches separately and adds will report four solutions for $k = 2$ where there are two. The same thing happens at $k = -2$ with $3\pi/2$. This is the reason the question asks for the count at the boundary values separately from the count beyond them.

The case $k = 0$ is the third regime and the one most likely to be missed, because it does not involve the parameter leaving a range at all; it involves the interval. At $k = 0$ the second branch is $\sin x = 0$, whose solutions are $0$, $\pi$ and $2\pi$. The interval is closed, so both endpoints count, and the count is three rather than two. The point is not that a new solution has appeared from nowhere: it is that the two solutions of $\sin x = k/2$ migrate to the endpoints as $k$ goes to zero, and a solution sitting on the boundary of a closed interval is still a solution. Reading the interval carefully is part of reading the equation.

The figure is the fastest way to see all of this at once. In the left panel the dashed curve is taller than the solid one and the two meet only on the axis, at the two points where $\cos x = 0$; there is no crossing anywhere else because $\sin x$ never reaches $1.25$. In the right panel the dashed curve is shorter and two extra crossings appear. Everything the item asks about is the transition between those two pictures.

Challenge lever
The equation factorises into two branches and the whole item is about the second one. For 0 < |k| < 2 the branch sin x = k/2 supplies two solutions that are genuinely distinct from the roots of cos x = 0, giving four. At |k| = 2 those two solutions collapse onto x = pi/2 and x = 3pi/2 and are already counted, so the total drops to two; for |k| > 2 the branch has no solution at all. The case k = 0 is a third regime that neither of the other two covers: sin x = 0 has three solutions on the closed interval, because x = 0 and x = 2pi are both included and both satisfy the equation. A candidate who counts the branch's solutions generically answers four for every k.

Adaptation
The gaokao archetype asks for the number of roots of a trigonometric equation containing a parameter, and answers it with a single interval for the parameter. Here the answer is deliberately split across three regimes, and the third one is created by the closed interval rather than by the parameter, so the candidate has to examine both the parameter range and the endpoints. The k = 0 case is not present in the source material at all.
Verification
The three regimes were confirmed by a numerical scan of sin 2x - k cos x over four million subintervals of 0 <= x <= 2pi at k = -3, -2, -1.5, 0, 0.5, 1.5, 2 and 2.5, with the roots clustered and counted. The scan returns 2 solutions for |k| >= 2 and 4 for 0 < |k| < 2. At k = 0 the scan detects 0, pi/2, pi and 3pi/2 as sign changes and x = 2pi as an exact endpoint zero, giving 5 once the endpoint is included, which the closed interval requires. Every value asserted in the answer was checked directly against the original equation.
