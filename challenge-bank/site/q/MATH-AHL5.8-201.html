5.8 Stationary points of f read off the graph of f', including a stationary point of inflection that the standard test misclassifies

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.8-201

Topic 5: CalculusSL 5.7 (the second derivative; concavity and points of inflection); SL 5.8 (local maximum and minimum points; use of f'(x) = 0 and the second derivative test); SL 5.11 (definite integrals, including analytical approach)P318 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatDetermineFindFind

Question

The diagram shows the graph of $y = f'(x)$, where $f$ is a function defined for all real values of $x$. The graph is a cubic curve. It passes through $(0,\,16)$, meets the $x$-axis at $x = 2$ where it touches the axis without crossing it, and meets the $x$-axis again at $x = 4$ where it crosses from above to below. The curve continues with the same shape outside the window shown, and it has no other intersections with the $x$-axis.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $f'(x) = (x-2)^{2}(4-x)$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the $x$-coordinate of each stationary point of $f$ and determine the nature of each. You must justify each answer by reference to the sign of $f'(x)$ immediately on either side of the point.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Find the $x$-coordinates of the points of inflection of the curve $y = f(x)$.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Given that $f(-1) = 0$, find the values of $f(4)$ and $f(5)$.

    (Find)
  5. [4 marks](e)

    Hence find the total area enclosed between the graph of $y = f'(x)$ and the $x$-axis for $-1 \le x \le 5$.

    (Hence)

Total: 18 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The graph meets the $x$-axis at $x = 2$ and at $x = 4$, so $f'(2) = 0$ and $f'(4) = 0$ (M1). At $x = 2$ the curve touches the axis and turns back, while at $x = 4$ it crosses; so $2$ is a repeated root of $f'$ and $4$ is a simple root, and $f'(x) = k(x-2)^{2}(4-x)$ for some constant $k$ (A1). Since the curve is below the axis for $x > 4$ we have $k > 0$. From the graph the $y$-intercept is $16$, so $16 = k(0-2)^{2}(4-0) = 16k$ and therefore $k = 1$ (AG). Expanding, $f'(x) = -x^{3} + 8x^{2} - 20x + 16$.

(b) The stationary points of $f$ are the solutions of $f'(x) = 0$, namely $x = 2$ and $x = 4$ (A1). For $x < 4$ with $x \ne 2$ the factor $(x-2)^{2}$ is positive and $(4-x)$ is positive, so $f'(x) > 0$; for instance $f'(1) = 3$ and $f'(3) = 1$ (M1). Since $f'$ is positive immediately on both sides of $x = 2$, the function $f$ is increasing as it passes through $x = 2$, so that point is a stationary point of inflection and not a maximum or a minimum (A1). For $x > 4$ the factor $(4-x)$ is negative, so $f'(x) < 0$; for instance $f'(5) = -9$. The derivative therefore changes from positive to negative at $x = 4$, so $f$ has a local maximum there (A1).

(c) Inflection points of $f$ are where $f''$ changes sign. Differentiating the factorised form with the product rule gives $f''(x) = (x-2)(10-3x)$ (M1), whose zeros are $x = 2$ and $x = \tfrac{10}{3}$ (A1). Numerically $f''(1.9) = -0.43$ and $f''(2.1) = +0.37$, while $f''(3.2) = +0.48$ and $f''(3.5) = -0.75$, so $f''$ changes sign at each value and both are inflection points (A1). The point $x = 2$ is therefore both stationary and an inflection point.

(d) An antiderivative of $f'$ is $F(x) = -\tfrac{x^{4}}{4} + \tfrac{8x^{3}}{3} - 10x^{2} + 16x$ (M1). By the fundamental theorem of calculus $f(4) - f(-1) = F(4) - F(-1) = \tfrac{32}{3} + \tfrac{347}{12} = \tfrac{475}{12}$ (A1), and since $f(-1) = 0$ we obtain $f(4) = \tfrac{475}{12} \approx 39.58$ (A1). Similarly $f(5) - f(4) = F(5) - F(4) = \tfrac{85}{12} - \tfrac{32}{3} = -\tfrac{43}{12}$, so $f(5) = \tfrac{475}{12} - \tfrac{43}{12} = \tfrac{432}{12} = 36$ (A1). The value $f(4)$ is the local maximum found in (b), and $f(5)$ is smaller because $f$ is decreasing beyond $x = 4$.

(e) The graph of $f'$ lies above the $x$-axis on $-1 \le x < 4$ and below it on $4 < x \le 5$, so the two regions must be treated separately (M1). The first area is $f(4) - f(-1) = \tfrac{475}{12}$ and the second is $f(4) - f(5) = \tfrac{43}{12}$ (A1). The total area is therefore $\tfrac{475}{12} + \tfrac{43}{12} = \tfrac{518}{12} = \tfrac{259}{6} \approx 43.2$ (A1)(A1). A candidate who evaluates the single integral from $-1$ to $5$ obtains $36$ instead, because the negative region cancels part of the positive one.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for identifying both zeros of $f'$ from the graph, (A1) for the repeated root at $x = 2$ and the simple root at $x = 4$, (AG) for $k = 1$ obtained from the intercept $16$. Do not award the final mark for a curve fitted to the picture without the intercept being used: the intercept is the only datum that fixes the vertical scale, and a candidate who ignores it has three unknowns and two conditions.
(b) (A1) for both $x$-coordinates, (M1) for testing the sign of $f'$ on either side, (A1) for the stationary point of inflection at $x = 2$, (A1) for the local maximum at $x = 4$. The word 'inflection' or an unambiguous equivalent, such as 'neither a maximum nor a minimum', is required at $x = 2$; 'turning point' scores no mark there. Condone a candidate who uses the second derivative test correctly, since $f''(2) = 0$ and $f''(4) = -4$; but a candidate who concludes from $f''(2) = 0$ alone that the point is an inflection point has not established the change of sign and loses that (A1) unless a sign check is shown.
(c) (M1) for differentiating, (A1) for both zeros, (A1) for establishing that $f''$ changes sign at each. Accept $3.33$ or $\tfrac{10}{3}$ for the second value. A candidate who lists $x = 2$ only once, under either (b) or (c), forfeits the (A1) for the missing occurrence: the point is both, and the question asks for the inflection points independently of (b).
(d) (M1) for a correct antiderivative, (A1) for $f(4) - f(-1) = \tfrac{475}{12}$, (A1) for $f(4) = \tfrac{475}{12}$, (A1) for $f(5) = 36$. Follow-through applies throughout from an incorrect $k$ in (a): carry the candidate's constant through and mark (d) and (e) on its consequences. Decimal answers are accepted to three significant figures only if the exact fraction is also shown.
(e) (M1) for splitting the range at $x = 4$, (A1) for the two separate areas, (A1)(A1) for the total $\tfrac{259}{6}$. A candidate who writes $36$ has computed the signed integral and scores (M1) only. Accept $\tfrac{259}{6}$, $43.2$ or $43.17$; do not accept a negative total area.

Why this question is hard

This item is about the difference between a zero of $f'$ and a change of sign in $f'$. The graph is drawn so that the two zeros look alike: the curve comes down to the axis at $x = 2$ and at $x = 4$ and turns, and at a glance both look like turning points of $f$. Only one of them is. At $x = 2$ the factor $(x-2)^{2}$ does not change sign, so $f'$ stays positive through the point, $f$ keeps increasing, and the correct classification is a stationary point of inflection. At $x = 4$ the factor $(4-x)$ does change sign and $f$ has an ordinary local maximum.

The reason the trap works is that the standard procedure, 'set $f'(x) = 0$ and apply the second derivative test', is exactly what a well-prepared candidate has drilled. Applied here it produces $f''(2) = 0$, which is the one outcome that test cannot resolve. A candidate who has learned that $f'' = 0$ means an inflection point will get the right label for the wrong reason, while a candidate who has learned nothing about that case will usually guess 'minimum', because the curve visibly dips. Both routes have to be checked by hand before the mark can be awarded.

Part (d) is where the sign structure pays off. Because $f' > 0$ on the whole interval from $-1$ to $4$, the change in $f$ over that interval is the area under the graph, and the antiderivative returns $\tfrac{475}{12}$ with no cancellation. From $4$ to $5$ the graph is below the axis, so the same antiderivative returns a negative number, $-\tfrac{43}{12}$, and the value of $f$ falls from $39.58$ to $36$. Part (e) then separates the two: a total area is a sum of absolute values, and the signed integral $\int_{-1}^{5} f' = 36$ is not it. The gap between $36$ and $\tfrac{259}{6} \approx 43.2$ is the quantity that catches the candidate who integrates once and reports the result.

There is one more structural point worth noticing, because it is the honest reason the item is hard rather than merely long. The function $f$ is not given. Everything about $f$ here is recovered from $f'$ together with a single value, and that value is the only datum tying the two together. A candidate who treats the picture as a picture, rather than as a source of numerical conditions, will produce a plausible-looking answer to (a) and then find that (d) cannot be started.
