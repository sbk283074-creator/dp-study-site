D.3 A velocity selector, and the circular path of a singly and a doubly charged ion

Physics HL · PHYS-D.3-201

Theme D: FieldsD.3 Motion in electromagnetic fields (velocity selector; charged particle in a uniform magnetic field; radius of the circular path)P2 · Section B12 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedShow thatDetermineDetermineExplain

Question

A velocity selector consists of a region in which a uniform electric field of magnitude $E = 2.5 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$ and a uniform magnetic field of magnitude $B = 0.50\ \mathrm{T}$ are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of motion of a beam of ions. Ions that pass through undeflected then enter a second region containing only the same magnetic field, where they move in circular arcs.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that an ion passes through the crossed fields undeflected only if its speed is $v = E/B$, and calculate that speed.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    A singly charged ion of mass $3.32 \times 10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$ enters the second region with the speed found in (a). Determine the radius of its circular path.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    A doubly charged ion of the same mass enters the second region at the same speed. Determine the radius of its path and compare it with the radius in (b).

    (Determine)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    Explain why the speed selected by the crossed fields does not depend on the mass or the charge of the ion.

    (Explain)

Total: 12 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) In the crossed fields the electric force $qE$ and the magnetic force $qvB$ act in opposite directions (M1). For the ion to pass undeflected the resultant must be zero:
$$qE = qvB \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \frac{E}{B} \tag{AG}$$

The charge cancels, so the condition is independent of $q$. Numerically (A1)(A1):
$$v = \frac{2.5 \times 10^{5}}{0.50} = 5.0 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$$

(b) In the second region the magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it supplies the centripetal force (M1):
$$qvB = \frac{mv^{2}}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{mv}{qB} \tag{A1}$$

With $q = e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$ (M1):
$$r = \frac{(3.32 \times 10^{-26})(5.0 \times 10^{5})}{(1.602 \times 10^{-19})(0.50)} = \frac{1.66 \times 10^{-20}}{8.01 \times 10^{-20}} = 0.207\ \mathrm{m} \tag{A1}$$

(c) For the doubly charged ion, $q = 2e$, so (M1)
$$r_{2} = \frac{mv}{2eB} = \frac{r}{2} = \frac{0.207}{2} = 0.104\ \mathrm{m} \tag{A1}$$

The radius is exactly halved. The same speed and the same mass produce the same momentum, so doubling the charge doubles the centripetal force available and the ion curves twice as tightly (A1).

(d) The two forces in the selector are both proportional to the charge: the electric force is $qE$ and the magnetic force is $qvB$. Setting them equal divides the charge out, leaving $v = E/B$, which contains neither $q$ nor $m$. The selection is therefore a statement about speed alone, and ions of different mass and different charge all pass undeflected provided they are travelling at that one speed. This is what makes the device useful as the first stage of a mass spectrometer: it delivers a beam of known speed, so the radius in the second region can be interpreted as a measurement of $m/q$.

The halving in part (c) is exact rather than approximate, and that exactness is itself a check on the reasoning. Only one quantity changed, and the charge appears to the first power in the expression for the radius, so the radius must change by exactly the same factor. A student who obtains anything other than a clean factor of two has usually applied the magnetic force to the wrong region, or has forgotten that the force in the second region is the one supplying the centripetal acceleration.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for setting $qE = qvB$ with the two forces opposed, AG for $v = E/B$, A1A1 for $5.0 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. A candidate who writes $qvB = qE$ without stating that the forces oppose has the right algebra but should be asked to justify the directions; award the M1 and the AG, and reserve one A1.

(b) M1 for $qvB = mv^{2}/r$, A1 for $r = mv/(qB)$, M1 for substituting $q = e$, A1 for $0.207\ \mathrm{m}$. Accept $0.21\ \mathrm{m}$. Follow-through applies from an incorrect speed in (a).

(c) M1 for doubling the charge, A1 for $0.104\ \mathrm{m}$, A1 for the statement that the radius halves or the reasoning in terms of force. A candidate who recomputes from scratch with $q = 2e$ should be given full credit; a candidate who writes "the radius is unchanged because the mass and speed are the same" has not seen that the force also doubles and scores 0.

(d) The mark is for noting that both forces are proportional to the charge and that the charge therefore cancels. A statement that "the fields balance" without the cancellation earns 0; a statement that the ratio $E/B$ has units of speed earns the first mark only.

Forfeits: using the mass of an electron instead of the given ion mass; treating the magnetic force in the selector as centripetal, which is the error of a candidate who has not noticed that the ion is undeflected and therefore not moving in a circle. Condonation: a candidate who quotes $r = 0.21\ \mathrm{m}$ and $r_{2} = 0.10\ \mathrm{m}$ with the halving stated explicitly should be given full credit.

Why this question is hard

The item is about a fact that is easy to state and easy to lose sight of: the velocity selector selects a speed and nothing else. Part (a) derives it, and the derivation is short, which is the point, because the charge cancels in the first line and the mass never appears at all. The two later parts then test whether that cancellation was understood rather than merely written down.

Part (c) is where the understanding is separated from the memorisation. A student who has learned $r = mv/(qB)$ as a formula will substitute $q = 2e$ and get the right answer, which is fine. A student who reasons physically, however, should notice that the momentum is unchanged and the available centripetal force has doubled, so the arc must be twice as tight. Both routes arrive at $0.104\ \mathrm{m}$, and the wrong answer, that the radius is unchanged because nothing about the motion changed, comes from treating the charge as a spectator when in fact it appears in both forces.

The second region also has a structural role. The ion arrives with a known speed, so measuring the radius measures $m/q$, and that is the whole principle of the mass spectrometer. Part (d) asks for the reason the first stage works at all, and the answer is a cancellation rather than a coincidence: both forces are proportional to the same charge, so their ratio is a property of the fields. The item deliberately gives a mass that is not a familiar particle mass, so that the student cannot shortcut the calculation by recognising the ion.
