4.6 Conditioning on the complement changes the denominator, so P(P given Q') is not P(P given Q) and the two conditionals differ in both directions

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-4.6-601

Topic 4: Statistics and probabilitySL 4.6 Use of Venn diagrams, tree diagrams and sample space to solve problems; SL 4.5 concepts of trial, outcome and equally likely outcomes; SL 4.7 conditional probabilityP2 · Section A6 marksdifficulty 5technology: requiredFindFindDetermine

Question

In a survey, each of $120$ people was asked whether they had visited website $P$ and whether they had visited website $Q$. The results are shown in the Venn diagram, where the numbers are the numbers of people in each region.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Find the probability that a randomly chosen person had visited $P$, given that they had not visited $Q$.

    (Find)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Find the probability that a randomly chosen person had visited $Q$, given that they had not visited $P$.

    (Find)
  3. [2 marks](c)

    Determine whether the events 'had visited $P$' and 'had visited $Q$' are independent. Justify your answer.

    (Determine)

Total: 6 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

In a survey, each of $120$ people was asked whether they had visited website $P$ and whether they had visited website $Q$. The results are shown in the Venn diagram, where the numbers are the numbers of people in each region. (a) Find the probability that a randomly chosen person had visited $P$, given that they had not visited $Q$. [2 marks] (Find) (b) Find the probability that a randomly chosen person had visited $Q$, given that they had not visited $P$. [2 marks] (Find) (c) Determine whether the events 'had visited $P$' and 'had visited $Q$' are independent. Justify your answer. [2 marks] (Determine)
Reveal the answer

(a) From the diagram, the number who visited $P$ is $40 + 30 = 70$ and the number who visited $Q$ is $20 + 30 = 50$, so $70$ people did not visit $Q$. Of those $70$, the number who also visited $P$ is $40$, the region of $P$ outside $Q$ (M1). Therefore $P(P \mid Q') = \frac{40}{70} = \frac{4}{7} \approx 0.571$ (A1).

(b) The number who did not visit $P$ is $120 - 70 = 50$. Of those $50$, the number who visited $Q$ is $20$ (M1). Therefore $P(Q \mid P') = \frac{20}{50} = \frac{2}{5} = 0.4$ (A1). The two answers are different, and it is worth seeing why: the first conditions on the $70$ people outside $Q$ while the second conditions on the $50$ people outside $P$, so the denominators are different and neither is $120$.

(c) The events are independent exactly when $P(P \cap Q) = P(P)\,P(Q)$ (M1). Here $P(P) = \frac{70}{120} = \frac{7}{12}$ and $P(Q) = \frac{50}{120} = \frac{5}{12}$, so $P(P)\,P(Q) = \frac{35}{144} \approx 0.243$. But the diagram gives $P(P \cap Q) = \frac{30}{120} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$. Since $0.243 \neq 0.25$, the two events are not independent (A1). The same conclusion follows from either conditional probability, because $P(P \mid Q) = \frac{30}{50} = 0.6$ and $P(P) = \frac{7}{12} \approx 0.583$ are unequal, and equally $P(Q \mid P) = \frac{30}{70} \approx 0.429 \neq \frac{5}{12} \approx 0.417$. The gap is small, and that is deliberate. The two quantities compared in part (c) are $\frac{35}{144}$ and $\frac{36}{144}$, which are one part in one hundred and forty-four apart, so a candidate who keeps the fractions exact will see the difference at once while one who rounds both to two decimal places sees $0.24$ against $0.25$ and has to decide whether that is a real gap or a rounding artefact.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for using $70$ rather than $120$ as the denominator, or for identifying the region of $P$ outside $Q$ as $40$; (A1) for $\frac{4}{7}$, or the decimal $0.571$ to three significant figures. A candidate who answers $\frac{40}{120}$ has conditioned on the whole sample rather than on the complement and should receive no marks: the conditional probability is a proportion of the $70$, and the command is 'given that they had not visited $Q$'.

(b) (M1) for the denominator $50$, or for the numerator $20$; (A1) for $\frac{2}{5}$ or $0.4$. A candidate who gives $\frac{4}{7}$ again has not distinguished the two conditionals, and the two parts exist to make that distinction visible. Both parts are independent, so a candidate may earn (a) without (b).

(c) (M1) for the product $P(P)P(Q)$, or for comparing a conditional probability with the corresponding marginal; (A1) for the conclusion that they are not independent with the comparison shown. Do not accept a bare 'no' or a bare 'yes': the command term is 'determine', and the part asks for the justification explicitly. Accept any one of the three valid comparisons -- $P(P)P(Q)$ against $P(P \cap Q)$, $P(P \mid Q)$ against $P(P)$, or $P(Q \mid P)$ against $P(Q)$ -- provided the two numbers are both stated.

Why this question is hard

The item exists because conditional probability is the place where the denominator, and not the numerator, carries the idea. The Venn diagram gives four counts and no probabilities, so every probability in the item has to be built by choosing what to divide by, and the two parts of the question choose differently. A candidate who divides by the sample size every time will produce $\frac{1}{3}$ and $\frac{1}{6}$ and will not notice anything wrong, because both answers are between zero and one and both are computed from the diagram.

Asking for the two conditional probabilities in sequence is deliberate. They are $P(P \mid Q')$ and $P(Q \mid P')$, which condition on complementary events, so their denominators are $70$ and $50$ and their numerators are $40$ and $20$. Neither is the converse of the other, and neither equals the other, and a candidate who has not separated the two directions of conditioning will give the same answer twice. The figure makes the distinction available without making it: the four regions are labelled with counts, so the candidate can read the $70$ and the $50$ straight off, but nothing in the picture says which one belongs to which part.

Part (c) then asks for a judgement rather than a number, and the numbers are arranged so that the judgement is close but not ambiguous. The product $\frac{35}{144}$ and the intersection $\frac{1}{4}$ differ by about $0.007$, which is small enough that a candidate who rounds early may conclude that they are equal. That is why the exact fractions are given in the answer: $\frac{35}{144}$ against $\frac{36}{144}$ is a difference of one part in a hundred and forty-four, and it is visible without a calculator only if the comparison is kept exact.
