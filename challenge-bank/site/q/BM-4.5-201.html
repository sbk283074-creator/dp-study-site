4.5 A revenue-positive, profit-negative price cut: price elasticity of demand governs turnover, but contribution per member governs profit once a variable cost sits under the price

Business management SL · BM-4.5-201

A members' gym considering a price cut

Highfield Fitness is a single-site gym with a maximum capacity of $500$ memberships. It currently charges $£40$ per month and has $300$ members, so it is running at three-fifths of its capacity. The variable cost of serving a member is $£6$ per month, covering cleaning, utilities and consumables, and the gym's fixed costs are $£8{,}000$ per month, covering rent, staff salaries and equipment leases regardless of how many members it has.

The manager is concerned that the gym is not full and proposes cutting the monthly fee by $15\%$ to $£34$. A trial in a neighbouring town suggested that such a cut raises membership by about $20\%$, a figure the manager believes would hold here. The manager's argument is that revenue would rise because the gym would sell a fifth more memberships, and that this on its own makes the proposal worthwhile. The gym can admit up to $500$ members without any increase in fixed costs.

Question

The manager of Highfield Fitness wants to fill the gym by cutting the monthly fee, and argues that the extra memberships will raise revenue. Using the figures in the stimulus, calculate the present revenue, contribution and profit; calculate the price elasticity of demand implied by the proposal; show what the cut would do to revenue and to profit; determine the membership the gym would actually need to hold profit where it is; and evaluate whether the cut should be made.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the gym's current monthly revenue, its contribution per member, its total monthly contribution and its monthly profit.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the price elasticity of demand implied by the manager's figures, and state whether demand is price-elastic or price-inelastic over this price range.

    (Calculate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Show that the price cut raises monthly revenue to $£12{,}240$ but reduces monthly profit to $£2{,}080$, and comment on why revenue and profit move in opposite directions.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine the number of members the gym would need at the new monthly fee to leave its monthly profit unchanged, and compare that number with the $360$ members the manager expects.

    (Determine)
  5. [4 marks](e)

    Evaluate whether Highfield Fitness should cut its monthly fee, referring both to the figures you have calculated and to the assumptions behind the manager's expectation.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) Current monthly revenue:
$$300 \times \u00a340 = \u00a312{,}000 \;\; (A1).$$
Contribution per member is the price less the variable cost of serving that member:
$$\u00a340 - \u00a36 = \u00a334 \;\; (A1).$$
Total monthly contribution is $300 \times \u00a334 = \u00a310{,}200$, and after fixed costs the monthly profit is
$$\u00a310{,}200 - \u00a38{,}000 = \u00a32{,}200 \;\; (A1).$$
Note that the $£6$ per member is a *variable* cost: it is incurred only for members the gym actually has, which is why it is deducted from the price rather than lumped with the rent.

(b) The proposal changes the price by $-15\%$ and, on the manager's own figure, changes the quantity demanded by $+20\%$. So
$$\text{PED} = \frac{\text{percentage change in quantity demanded}}{\text{percentage change in price}} = \frac{+20\%}{-15\%} = -1.33 \;\; (M1)(A1).$$
The *magnitude* is greater than one, $|{-1.33}| > 1$, so demand is price-elastic over this range $(A1)$. That is exactly the condition the manager is relying on: with elastic demand, a fall in price raises total revenue. The candidate should be able to state that conclusion and it is correct.

(c) At the new fee the gym has $300 \times 1.20 = 360$ members and charges $\u00a340 \times 0.85 = \u00a334$, so
$$\text{new revenue} = 360 \times \u00a334 = \u00a312{,}240,$$
a rise of $\u00a3240$ a month on the $£12{,}000$ in part (a) $(A1)$.

Profit falls, however, because the contribution per member falls with the price while the variable cost does not. The new contribution per member is $\u00a334 - \u00a36 = \u00a328$, so
$$\text{new total contribution} = 360 \times \u00a328 = \u00a310{,}080,$$
and
$$\text{new profit} = \u00a310{,}080 - \u00a38{,}000 = \u00a32{,}080 \;\; (A1).$$
That is $\u00a3120$ *less* than the present profit of $\u00a32{,}200$.

Revenue and profit move in opposite directions because they are governed by different quantities $(A1)$. Revenue depends only on price times volume, and elasticity is precisely a statement about revenue; profit depends on the *contribution* each member makes, and the six-pound cut in price cuts contribution per member by the same six pounds, from $\u00a334$ to $\u00a328$, a fall of $17.6\%$. A $20\%$ rise in volume cannot offset a fall of that size in contribution per unit, so the total contribution falls even though the revenue rises. Elasticity is silent about profit because it works with revenue alone; the variable cost is what breaks the coincidence between the two.

(d) To hold profit where it is, the new total contribution must still be $\u00a310{,}200$, so the gym needs
$$\frac{\u00a310{,}200}{\u00a328} = 364.3 \;\; (M1)(A1).$$
Membership comes in whole numbers, so the gym would need $365$ members $(A1)$. The manager expects $360$ members, which is five short of the membership required, and at $360$ members the profit is the $\u00a32{,}080$ of part (c). The comparison to state is that the proposal misses its own break-even point by about five memberships, not that it is close to it: five members is a small number of people but it is the whole of the margin.

(e) On the figures given, the gym should not cut the fee. The decision is not close: every member the gym gains at $\u00a334$ adds $\u00a328$ of contribution, and the fee cut removes $\u00a36$ of contribution from each of the $300$ members who would have paid $\u00a340$ anyway. The gain from fifty more members (about $360 - 300 = 60$ extra, worth $60 \times \u00a328 = \u00a31{,}680$) is outweighed by the $300 \times \u00a36 = \u00a31{,}800$ given up on the members who were already there $(A1)$, and the two figures have been arranged so that this is exactly the $\u00a3120$ shortfall. A cut of this size cannot pay for itself unless almost every existing member stays and the new members arrive in numbers the trial does not promise.

The weaknesses in the manager's expectation should be identified rather than assumed away $(A1)$. The $20\%$ figure comes from a *neighbouring town*, so it is evidence about a different location, a different competing set of gyms and a different population; the manager's belief that it would hold here is an assumption, not a measurement $(A1)$. The trial also tells the manager what happened to membership when the price was cut elsewhere, with no indication of how long the effect lasted; a promotional price often attracts members who leave when it rises, which would raise the variable-cost base without building the membership the gym wants.

A judgement is required, and it should be a clear one $(A1)$. The strongest honest case for the cut is not in the figures but beside them: if the extra members raise the gym's utilisation, its classes fill, its atmosphere improves and its retention improves, then part of the benefit would appear *later* in lower turnover among existing members, which the item's numbers do not capture. That argument is real, but it is not what the manager offered, and it would have to be large enough to close a gap of at least $\u00a3120$ a month and probably much more. On the arithmetic presented, the recommendation is to keep the fee at $\u00a340$ and look elsewhere for the members: the gym has $200$ memberships of headroom and the cheapest way to fill them is not to reduce the contribution of the members it already has.

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for revenue $£12{,}000$, (A1) for contribution per member $£34$, (A1) for profit $£2{,}200$ (the total contribution of $£10{,}200$ may be shown as working). A candidate who treats the $£6$ as a fixed cost and deducts it after fixed costs will be wrong and forfeits the profit mark; the mark is for recognising that it is variable.

(b) (M1) for forming the ratio of the two percentage changes, (A1) for $-1.33$ or $-4/3$, (A1) for stating elastic with the magnitude test $|\text{PED}| > 1$. A candidate who writes $1.33$ without the sign and then calls it elastic has the substance and should keep the marks.

(c) (A1) for new revenue $£12{,}240$, (A1) for new profit $£2{,}080$, (A1) for the explanation that the contribution per member has fallen faster than the volume has risen. The explanation mark needs the per-member contribution, not "costs went up": a candidate who says only "revenue rose but costs rose more" without naming the $£28$ contribution has the first two marks and not the third.

(d) (M1) for setting the new total contribution equal to $£10{,}200$, (A1) for $364.3$, (A1) for rounding up to $365$ and comparing with $360$. Accept a candidate who works from the contribution lost on existing members and the contribution gained on new ones; the answer is the same. A candidate who answers $360$ because that is the forecast has not answered the question.

(e) (A1) for a reasoned recommendation against the cut, supported by the shortfall computed in (d). (A1) for identifying the transferability of the trial as an assumption. (A1) for identifying at least one further assumption (the duration of the trial's effect, or retention, or the absence of any change in variable cost per member). (A1) for a defensible judgement that weighs the qualitative case against the arithmetic rather than ignoring it. Full marks do not require the candidate to agree with the model answer: a clear case *for* the cut built on stated qualitative benefits, with the $£120$ gap acknowledged and argued, can score the fourth mark.

Forfeits: using $£40 - \u00a36$ as the new contribution per member after the cut, which double-counts the price change; computing elasticity from $£40$ and $£34$ directly rather than as a percentage change (that gives $-6/40 = -15\%$, which is the price change and no elasticity); stating the profit effect as positive because revenue rose. Condonation: contribution may be called "unit contribution" or "contribution per member"; $£2{,}080$ may be shown as $£2{,}080$ or "about $£2{,}100$" provided the exact figure appears once.

Why this question is hard

This item is built around the single most common error in pricing work, which is to answer a revenue question when a profit question was asked. The two are not the same, and the difference between them is a variable cost.

Price elasticity of demand is a statement about revenue, and it is a good one: if the magnitude is greater than one, a fall in price raises total revenue, and the numbers here are arranged so that this is true. The manager is not wrong about that at all -- revenue does rise, from $\u00a312{,}000$ to $£12{,}240$ a month. A candidate who stops at that point has done the elasticity correctly and has still given the wrong answer, because the proposal has to be judged on profit, and profit depends on contribution per unit, not on revenue.

The arithmetic that separates them is one line long. Cutting the price by $£6$ cuts the contribution of every member by $£6$, from $\u00a334$ to $\u00a328$; a $£6$ cut on $300$ members costs $\u00a31{,}800$ a month in contribution on the very members the gym already has, and the $60$ new members contribute $60 \times \u00a328 = \u00a31{,}680$. The gap is the $\u00a3120$ by which profit falls, and it is not a rounding error or a modelling artefact: it is the whole point. Elasticity could not have caught it, because elasticity compares percentage changes and does not know that the price has a cost underneath it.

Part (d) turns the same arithmetic into a target. The membership that holds profit is $31$ more than the forecast, so the proposal fails by five members, which sounds trivial and is not: the gym would have to reach $365$ members to stand still, and the trial it is relying on promises $360$. The distinction between "almost break-even" and "short of break-even" is the distinction between a project that should be reconsidered with better research and one that should be refused on the present numbers.

Only the evaluation part opens the qualitative door, and it is deliberately narrow. There is a genuine argument for the cut -- a fuller gym is a better gym, and happier existing members are less likely to leave -- but it is not the argument the manager made, and it does not appear anywhere in the figures. The mark is for noticing that the honest counter-argument rests on retention benefits the item has not quantified, and for refusing to let the assumption of a $20\%$ response from another town carry a decision worth more than the gap it has to close.

Challenge lever
Every figure the manager offers is correct. The elasticity really is elastic, membership really does rise by a fifth, and revenue really does rise. The error is in what the decision turns on: an elastic demand makes a price cut revenue-positive, and says nothing about whether it is profit-positive once the variable cost of serving each member is deducted from the lower price. The item also removes the usual qualitative escape, because the membership needed to hold profit is well inside the gym's capacity, so "we could not fit them in anyway" is not available.

Adaptation
Original. Price elasticity of demand is routinely taught alongside the rule that a price cut raises revenue when demand is elastic; the scenario here places a variable cost under the price so that the revenue conclusion and the profit conclusion diverge, and fixes the numbers so that the membership needed to hold profit sits above the forecast but below capacity.
Verification
Every figure was recomputed from the stated price, variable cost, fixed cost and membership: revenue, contribution per member, total contribution and profit at both the present and the proposed price, and the price elasticity of demand from the two percentage changes. The membership needed to hold profit was obtained by dividing the present total contribution by the new contribution per member and was checked against the profit at 364, 365 and 366 members. The shortfall was cross-checked by computing the contribution lost on the three hundred existing members and the contribution gained on the sixty new ones.
