3.18 Three planes whose behaviour changes at one value of a parameter, at which the system becomes inconsistent rather than underdetermined and the three pairwise intersections become parallel lines

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL3.18-202

Topic 3: Geometry and trigonometryAHL 3.17 (vector equation of a plane) with AHL 3.18 (intersections of three planes) and AHL 1.16 (solutions of systems of linear equations)P1 · Section B13 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatDetermineShow thatDetermine

Question

Three planes are given by
$$\Pi_{1}: x + y + z = 4, \qquad \Pi_{2}: x + 2y + 3z = 7, \qquad \Pi_{3}: 2x + 3y + kz = 12,$$
where $k$ is a real constant.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the determinant of the system is $k - 4$, and hence state the single value of $k$ at which the planes fail to meet at a unique point.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    For $k \ne 4$, determine the unique point of intersection in terms of $k$, and verify your expression by substituting $k = 5$.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Show that at $k = 4$ the three planes have no common point, and that each pair meets in a line parallel to the other two.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Write down the point common to all three planes for $k \ne 4$ in the limit as $k$ becomes large, and use it to determine the constant on the right-hand side of $\Pi_{3}$ for which the three planes would meet in a line when $k = 4$.

    (Determine)

Total: 13 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Three planes are given by $$\Pi_{1}: x + y + z = 4, \qquad \Pi_{2}: x + 2y + 3z = 7, \qquad \Pi_{3}: 2x + 3y + kz = 12,$$ where $k$ is a real constant. (a) Show that the determinant of the system is $k - 4$, and hence state the single value of $k$ at which the planes fail to meet at a unique point. [3 marks] (Show that) (b) For $k \ne 4$, determine the unique point of intersection in terms of $k$, and verify your expression by substituting $k = 5$. [4 marks] (Determine) (c) Show that at $k = 4$ the three planes have no common point, and that each pair meets in a line parallel to the other two. [3 marks] (Show that) (d) Write down the point common to all three planes for $k \ne 4$ in the limit as $k$ becomes large, and use it to determine the constant on the right-hand side of $\Pi_{3}$ for which the three planes would meet in a line when $k = 4$. [3 marks] (Determine)
Reveal the answer

(a) The directions normal to the three planes are $(1,1,1)$, $(1,2,3)$ and $(2,3,k)$. Expanding along the first row (M1):
$$\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & k \end{vmatrix}
= 1(2k - 9) - 1(k - 6) + 1(3 - 4) = 2k - 9 - k + 6 - 1 = k - 4 \;\; (A1).$$
The normals are linearly dependent, and the system fails to have a unique solution, exactly when $k - 4 = 0$, that is at $k = 4$ (A1).

(b) From $\Pi_{1}$ and $\Pi_{2}$, subtracting gives
$$y + 2z = 3, \qquad \text{so} \qquad y = 3 - 2z,$$
and then $x = 4 - y - z = 4 - (3 - 2z) - z = 1 + z$ (M1).

Parameterise with $z = t$: the intersection of $\Pi_{1}$ and $\Pi_{2}$ is the line $(x,y,z) = (1 + t,\; 3 - 2t,\; t)$ (A1). Substituting into $\Pi_{3}$ (M1):
$$2(1 + t) + 3(3 - 2t) + kt = 2 + 2t + 9 - 6t + kt = 11 + (k - 4)t = 12,$$
so
$$t = \frac{1}{k - 4}, \qquad (x, y, z) = \left(1 + \frac{1}{k-4},\; 3 - \frac{2}{k-4},\; \frac{1}{k-4}\right) \;\; (A1).$$

At $k = 5$ this gives $t = 1$ and the point $(2, 1, 1)$. Check: $2 + 1 + 1 = 4$; $2 + 2 + 3 = 7$; $2(2) + 3(1) + 5(1) = 12$. All three equations are satisfied, so the expression is correct.

(c) At $k = 4$, $\Pi_{3}$ becomes $2x + 3y + 4z = 12$. On the line $(1 + t, 3 - 2t, t)$ found in (b),
$$2x + 3y + 4z = 2 + 2t + 9 - 6t + 4t = 11$$
for every $t$ (M1). The constant on the left is $11$ at every point of the line and $12$ is required, so no point of the line lies on $\Pi_{3}$ and the three planes have no common point (A1). Note that $2x + 3y + 4z = 11$ is itself a plane, and it is precisely the plane that contains the whole of $\Pi_{1} \cap \Pi_{2}$.

The pairwise intersections are all parallel to that line. In vector form the direction of $\Pi_{1} \cap \Pi_{2}$ is $(1, -2, 1)$. For $\Pi_{1} \cap \Pi_{3}$: subtracting twice $\Pi_{1}$ from $\Pi_{3}$ gives $y + 2z = 12 - 8 = 4$, so $y = 4 - 2z$ and $x = 4 - y - z = z$, that is $(0, 4, 0) + t(1, -2, 1)$. For $\Pi_{2} \cap \Pi_{3}$: subtracting twice $\Pi_{2}$ from $\Pi_{3}$ gives $-y - 2z = 12 - 14 = -2$, so $y + 2z = 2$, $y = 2 - 2z$ and $x = 7 - 2y - 3z = 3 + z$, that is $(3, 2, 0) + t(1, -2, 1)$ (A1).

Three distinct parallel lines, no two of which meet: the three planes form a triangular prism and the system is inconsistent, not underdetermined.

(d) From (b), as $k$ increases without bound,
$$x \to 1, \qquad y \to 3, \qquad z \to 0, \qquad \text{so the point tends to } (1, 3, 0) \;\; (A1).$$
That is the point of $\Pi_{1} \cap \Pi_{2}$ with $z = 0$, the anchor of the line, and it is the point that every member of the family of intersections as $k$ varies shares (M1).

At $k = 4$ the three planes meet in that line exactly when $\Pi_{3}$ contains it. The line $(1 + t, 3 - 2t, t)$ satisfies $2x + 3y + 4z = 11$ identically, so the required right-hand side is $11$ (A1), that is $\Pi_{3}: 2x + 3y + 4z = 11$. With that constant, $\Pi_{3}$ is the plane spanned by the line and one further direction, the three equations reduce to two independent ones, and the solution set is the line $(1, 3, 0) + t(1, -2, 1)$.

The pattern across the two cases is worth stating: at $k = 4$ the three planes share a line only if the constant is $11$, and if it is anything else they share nothing. The determinant alone told us that something degenerates at $k = 4$; it did not tell us which.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for a determinant expansion, (A1) for $k - 4$ exactly, (A1) for $k = 4$. A candidate who gets $4 - k$ scores the first two marks and the third, since the condition is the same; a candidate who uses row reduction instead of a determinant is given full credit if the row reduction is correct and the singular value is identified.

(b) (M1) for eliminating between $\Pi_{1}$ and $\Pi_{2}$ to get the line, (A1) for the parameterisation, (M1) for substituting into $\Pi_{3}$ and forming the single equation in $t$, (A1) for the point in terms of $k$. The verification at $k = 5$ is required by the wording and must be present to earn the fourth mark if the expression is given without the check; a candidate who writes $(1 + \tfrac{1}{k-4},\, 3 - \tfrac{2}{k-4},\, \tfrac{1}{k-4})$ and checks it scores all four.

(c) (M1) for evaluating $2x + 3y + 4z$ on the line and obtaining the constant $11$ independently of $t$, (A1) for the conclusion that the system is inconsistent because $11 \ne 12$, (A1) for the three pairwise lines with a common direction. Answers that assert "infinitely many solutions" because the determinant is zero forfeit all three marks: the determinant says only that the solution set is not a single point. A candidate who finds one pairwise line and states that the other two are parallel to it by symmetry of the elimination scores the third mark.

(d) (A1) for the limit point $(1, 3, 0)$, (M1) for connecting it to the family of intersections, (A1) for the constant $11$. The limit need not be written as a limit: "as $k$ grows the term in $k$ forces $z \to 0$, so the solution approaches $(1,3,0)$" is enough. A candidate who answers "$k = 4$ has no solution for any constant" has missed that the third plane can be made to contain the line, and forfeits the last mark.

Forfeits throughout: assuming that a zero determinant implies infinitely many solutions. Condonation: a candidate who writes the line as $(1, 3, 0) + t(1, -2, 1)$ and the point as $(1,3,0) + \tfrac{1}{k-4}(1,-2,1)$ should be given full credit in (b) and (d); the two forms are the same statement.

Why this question is hard

The determinant is one number and it answers exactly one question: does the system have a unique solution? It is silent on the other question, which is what happens when it does not, and the gap between those two questions is where this item lives.

The reflexive move, and the one almost every candidate makes, is: the determinant is $k - 4$, so at $k = 4$ the planes must be in a sheaf and the solution set must be a line. That conclusion needs a second fact that has not been checked, namely whether the third plane's constant is compatible with the line the first two already determine. Here the first two planes fix the line $(1 + t, 3 - 2t, t)$, and on that entire line $2x + 3y + 4z = 11$ -- the normal vector $(2, 3, 4)$ is the sum of $(1, 1, 1)$ and $(1, 2, 3)$, so the expression collapses to $4 + 7$ with no $t$ surviving. The constant in the actual third plane is $12$. Eleven is not twelve, and the system is inconsistent: three planes forming a prism, each pair meeting in a line parallel to the other two. Nothing meets anything.

The structure that makes this checkable rather than a matter of luck is that the left-hand side collapses. Whenever the third normal vector is a combination of the first two, the left-hand side of the third equation is fixed on the intersection line, and the whole question reduces to comparing one number with one other number. Part (d) makes that explicit by asking what the constant would have to be, and the answer -- $11$ -- is precisely the value that was computed and discarded in part (c).

The other thing worth noticing is part (b)'s form. The solution is $(1, 3, 0) + \tfrac{1}{k-4}(1, -2, 1)$: the solution point slides along the line direction as $k$ changes, and as $k$ becomes large it stops at the anchor $(1, 3, 0)$. That is the geometric content of the whole question, because $(1, 3, 0)$ is where the degenerate line would have to pass. The algebra of the determinant and the geometry of the prism are two views of one statement, and a candidate who sees the limit will not make the error in part (c).

Challenge lever
The determinant vanishes at exactly one value of $k$, and the reflex every candidate has been trained to perform is to conclude that the singular case gives infinitely many solutions. It does not: the third plane's constant is $12$ while the line common to the first two satisfies $2x + 3y + 4z = 11$, so at $k = 4$ the three planes are mutually parallel about three distinct parallel lines and the system has no solution at all. The parameter that produces the degeneration and the parameter that decides consistency are different, and only the second one determines the answer.
