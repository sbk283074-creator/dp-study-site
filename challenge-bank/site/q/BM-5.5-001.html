5.5 Break-even analysis; 3.3 Costs and revenues

Business management SL · BM-5.5-001

5.5 Break-even analysis (Unit 5); 3.3 Costs and revenues; 3.2 Sources of finance (not required); Toolkit: decision treesP2 · Section A15 marksdifficulty 4CalculateEvaluateRecommend

Verdana Foods Ltd (VF)

VF produces chilled plant-based ready meals at a single factory in Porto, Portugal, and sells through two channels: to supermarket chains, and directly to consumers through its own website. The two channels carry very different cost structures — supermarket orders leave the factory in full pallets, while online orders are picked, packed and delivered chilled to individual households.

VF's marketing director has proposed a campaign to shift the sales mix towards the online channel, which she describes as "obviously better, because the contribution per unit is higher and it must therefore lower our break-even". The finance director is less sure. She estimates a 0.6 probability that total volume remains at 620 000 units and a 0.4 probability that it falls to 480 000 units, because some supermarket buyers may delist VF if it starts promoting its own website to their customers.

All figures below are for the year ended 31 March 2026. Total sales volume was 620 000 units and total fixed costs were €486 000.

Question

The proposed campaign would add €54 000 per year to fixed costs and is expected to change the sales mix to 60% supermarket / 40% online.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate VF's weighted average contribution per unit at the current sales mix, and hence its break-even level of output.

    (Calculate)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Calculate the break-even level of output and the margin of safety at a volume of 620 000 units if the campaign achieves the expected 60 / 40 mix. Comment on why this result differs from the marketing director's claim.

    (Calculate)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    Using the finance director's probabilities, calculate the expected annual profit both with and without the campaign. Evaluate, with reference to one stakeholder group and one non-financial factor, whether VF should launch the campaign.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Contribution per unit = selling price − all variable costs.

Supermarket: $3.20-1.85-0.00=\textbf{€1.35}$ (A1)

Online: $5.00-1.85-1.10=\textbf{€2.05}$ (A1)

Weighted average contribution $=0.78(1.35)+0.22(2.05)=1.053+0.451=\textbf{€1.504 per unit}$ (M1)

$$\text{Break-even output}=\frac{\text{fixed costs}}{\text{contribution per unit}}=\frac{486\,000}{1.504}=323\,138\approx\textbf{323 000 units}$$ (A1)

(b) New weighted average contribution $=0.60(1.35)+0.40(2.05)=0.81+0.82=\textbf{€1.63 per unit}$ (M1)

New fixed costs $=486\,000+54\,000=\textbf{€540 000}$ (A1)

$$\text{New break-even output}=\frac{540\,000}{1.63}=331\,288\approx\textbf{331 000 units}$$ (A1)

$$\text{Margin of safety}=\frac{620\,000-331\,288}{620\,000}\times100=\textbf{46.6\%}$$ (A1)

The marketing director is right that contribution per unit rises, from €1.504 to €1.63. She is wrong that break-even falls: it rises, from about 323 000 to about 331 000 units, because fixed costs increase by 11.1% while weighted contribution increases by only 8.4%. The margin of safety also falls, from 47.9% to 46.6%. (A1)

(c) Profit = (volume × weighted contribution) − fixed costs.

*Without the campaign (contribution €1.504, fixed €486 000):*

  • at 620 000 units: $620\,000(1.504)-486\,000=932\,480-486\,000=\textbf{€446 480}$
  • at 480 000 units: $480\,000(1.504)-486\,000=721\,920-486\,000=\textbf{€235 920}$

$$\text{EV}=0.6(446\,480)+0.4(235\,920)=267\,888+94\,368=\textbf{€362 256}$$ (M1)(A1)

*With the campaign (contribution €1.63, fixed €540 000):*

  • at 620 000 units: $620\,000(1.63)-540\,000=1\,010\,600-540\,000=\textbf{€470 600}$
  • at 480 000 units: $480\,000(1.63)-540\,000=782\,400-540\,000=\textbf{€242 400}$

$$\text{EV}=0.6(470\,600)+0.4(242\,400)=282\,360+96\,960=\textbf{€379 320}$$ (M1)(A1)

The campaign has the higher expected profit by €17 064, about 4.7% of the expected profit without it. (A1)

*Evaluation.* On expected value the campaign is justified, and it is more robust than the margin of safety suggests: because the campaign is better in both states of the world (€470 600 > €446 480, and €242 400 > €235 920), the decision does not actually depend on which probability is correct. The break-even volume *of the change itself* is $54\,000\div(1.63-1.504)=428\,571$ units, and both volume scenarios exceed it. (A1)

However the expected-value calculation hides a step change rather than a smooth one. Supermarket buyers are the stakeholder group at risk: they supply 60% of volume even after the shift, and their reaction to a supplier promoting direct-to-consumer sales is not a 40%-chance event — it is a decision they either make or do not. If delisting removed the supermarket channel entirely, the 480 000-unit scenario would be optimistic. Set against that, a non-financial factor pulls the other way: chilled single-household delivery carries a markedly higher carbon footprint per unit than palletised supermarket distribution, which sits awkwardly with the sustainability positioning of a plant-based brand and could damage the very customers the campaign targets.

Conclusion (AO3): a supported recommendation. The expected gain of €17 064 is thin relative to a channel-specific risk that the model does not capture, so a full campaign is hard to justify on these numbers; the stronger recommendation is to test the online offer in a limited region, or to grow online volume without advertising it to supermarket customers, so that the contribution gain is captured without triggering the delisting risk. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) only if the two channel contributions are *weighted* by the sales mix; an unweighted mean of €1.70 is a common error and forfeits the method mark. Accept answers in the range 322 000–324 000.

(b) The final (A1) is for the comment, and it must identify the *relative* size of the two changes — 'contribution went up but fixed costs went up more' in words is sufficient, a bare 'she is wrong' is not. Candidates who compute the margin of safety at 480 000 units should be followed through if stated.

(c) Award the method marks for weighting both outcomes by their probabilities; a candidate who weights the two volumes by their probabilities (564 000) and then computes a single profit figure at that volume has not used the decision tree and forfeits both method marks. The evaluation mark requires a *supported* recommendation — credit any reasonable conclusion, including 'launch', provided it is justified with reference to the figures and to at least one stakeholder or non-financial factor. Do not credit generic factors ('the weather', 'the economy') or a stakeholder named without an explanation of their interest.

Note on Toolkit use: the expected-value calculation is the decision-tree tool applied with the branches implicit. Candidates who draw the tree explicitly should be credited equally.

Why this question is hard

This question is built around a trap that is invisible until the arithmetic is done. Almost every student — and, in the scenario, the marketing director — reasons that a higher contribution per unit must lower break-even. It does not, because the campaign buys that higher contribution with €54 000 of extra fixed costs, and break-even is a ratio of the two. Break-even rises and the margin of safety falls, which looks like a clear argument against the campaign.

The twist is that break-even is the wrong decision criterion here. VF sells 620 000 units against a break-even of about 330 000, so it operates far above the break-even point; in that region what matters is total contribution, not the level at which profit becomes zero. Expected profit is higher with the campaign in *both* volume states, which means the conclusion is insensitive to the probability estimates — a much stronger argument than the €17 064 figure alone suggests.

The evaluation mark separates description from judgement. Naming 'supermarket buyers' as a stakeholder earns nothing; observing that their response is a step change the probability model cannot represent does. And the sustainability point is not decoration: chilled home delivery is genuinely more carbon-intensive per unit than palletised freight, so the campaign works against the positioning of the product it is promoting.

The indifference volume makes the robustness claim exact, and it is worth stating. Because the same contribution and fixed costs apply in both volume states, the campaign's advantage does not depend on the probabilities at all: it is the better choice at any volume above the point where $1.63V - 540\,000 = 1.504V - 486\,000$, that is $V = 54\,000 / 0.126 = 428\,571$ units. Both states lie above that threshold, so the recommendation is robust - but the low state of 480 000 units sits only about 12% clear of it, which is why the answer is a qualified yes rather than a comfortable one. A candidate who reaches this point has shown that the decision turns on the volume the campaign can hold, not on the probability of the downturn.

Classic wrong turns: using €1.70 (the unweighted average of €1.35 and €2.05); treating the 22% online share as a cost rather than a mix weight; and concluding 'yes' purely from the higher expected value without acknowledging the channel risk.

Metadata

Challenge lever
Two defensible-looking measures point in opposite directions: the campaign raises contribution per unit yet also raises break-even output and lowers the margin of safety, while still increasing expected profit. The student must decide which measure is decision-relevant and say why.
