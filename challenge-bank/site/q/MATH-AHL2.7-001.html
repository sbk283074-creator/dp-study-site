An inequality that holds only for a range of its own parameter

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL2.7-001

SL 2.7 (solution of quadratic equations and inequalities) and SL 2.6 (the quadratic function and its discriminant)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatDetermineFind

Question

For each real number $k$, define $E(x)=(k+1)x^{2}-2(k+2)x+(k+5)$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that the discriminant of $E(x)$ is equal to $-8k-4$.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Determine the set of values of $k$ for which $E(x)>0$ for every real number $x$.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Find the values of $x$ satisfying $E(x)>0$ when $k=-1$, where $E$ is no longer quadratic.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the value of $k$ for which $E(x)=0$ has exactly one real solution, and state that solution.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Here $a=k+1$, $b=-2(k+2)$ and $c=k+5$, so the discriminant is $b^{2}-4ac$ (M1) $=4(k+2)^{2}-4(k+1)(k+5)$ (A1). Expanding, $(k+2)^{2}=k^{2}+4k+4$ and $(k+1)(k+5)=k^{2}+6k+5$ (A1), so the discriminant equals $4[(k^{2}+4k+4)-(k^{2}+6k+5)]=4(-2k-1)=-8k-4$ (A1) *(AG)*. The $k^{2}$ terms cancel, which is why the discriminant is linear in $k$ rather than quadratic, and this feature is what makes the later parts tractable.

(b) For $E(x)>0$ for every real $x$ the parabola must open upwards and have no real roots, so $a>0$ and the discriminant must be negative (M1). The discriminant $-8k-4$ is negative precisely when $-8k<4$, that is $k>-\frac{1}{2}$ (A1)(A1). For such values $k+1>\frac{1}{2}>0$, so the leading coefficient is automatically positive (R1), and the condition is exactly $k>-\frac{1}{2}$ (A1). No separate condition on $k$ is required beyond the discriminant inequality, because the boundary value of $k$ is greater than minus one and therefore keeps the leading coefficient positive.

(c) When $k=-1$ the coefficient of $x^{2}$ vanishes, so $E(x)=-2x+4$ (M1)(A1). Then $-2x+4>0$ gives $x<2$ (A1). The inequality is strict, so the endpoint $x=2$ is excluded and the solution set is the open ray to the left of two.

(d) A repeated root occurs when the discriminant is zero, so $-8k-4=0$ and $k=-\frac{1}{2}$ (M1)(A1). Substituting, $E(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}-3x+\frac{9}{2}=\frac{1}{2}(x-3)^{2}$ (A1), so the single solution is $x=3$ (A1). The completed square shows that the expression is non-negative for every real $x$, and it is equal to zero only at the single point just found. The three answers form a consistent picture: below the boundary the expression is always positive, at the boundary it touches zero once, and beyond the boundary it changes sign.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for quoting $b^{2}-4ac$ with the correct coefficients. The expansion marks are independent: a candidate who expands correctly but makes a sign slip while collecting terms can still earn the first two. The final (A1) requires the exact form $-8k-4$; the unsimplified $4(-2k-1)$ is acceptable.

(b) The key is that 'positive for every real $x$' is a two-condition statement. A candidate who only requires the discriminant to be negative reaches $k>-\frac{1}{2}$, which happens to be correct, but the (R1) is awarded only if the leading coefficient is also verified. Award full marks to a candidate who argues that the discriminant condition already forces $k+1>0$.

(c) This is a deliberate degenerate case. The (M1) is for recognising that the quadratic term disappears, the (A1) for the correct linear expression, and the final (A1) for the strict inequality $x<2$. Do not accept $x\le2$.

(d) The (M1) is for setting the discriminant to zero and the (A1) for $k=-\frac{1}{2}$. Completing the square earns the third mark and the value $x=3$ the fourth. A candidate who uses the quadratic formula and obtains the double root earns full credit.

Note: the boundary value $k=-\frac{1}{2}$ belongs to (d) rather than (b), since there $E$ has a zero.

Why this question is hard

The item is built around a single expression whose sign behaviour changes at two different parameter values. Part (a) is mechanical but sets up everything: the discriminant is linear in $k$, which is unusual for a quadratic family and is what makes the later parts tractable. Candidates who expect a quadratic discriminant and try to factor it waste time.

Part (b) is where the reasoning lives. The phrase 'for every real $x$' requires two separate conditions: the parabola must open upwards and must not touch the axis. Many candidates remember only the discriminant condition. Here the trap is benign, because the discriminant condition $k>-\frac{1}{2}$ already implies $k+1>0$, but the mark scheme rewards the candidate who notices this rather than one who assumes it. A candidate who writes $k>-\frac{1}{2}$ with no justification loses the reasoning mark.

Part (c) breaks the family. Setting $k=-1$ removes the quadratic term, and the expression becomes the decreasing line $-2x+4$. Candidates who blindly apply the discriminant to a line will divide by zero. This part exists to test whether the algebra is being watched.

Part (d) reuses the discriminant at its zero. The double root at $x=3$ is the natural bridge between the strict inequality of part (b) and the degenerate case of part (c). The classic errors are forgetting that $a>0$ is required in part (b); solving $-2x+4>0$ as $x>2$; and treating the repeated root as two coincident solutions. Plotting the family for a negative value of $k$ shows the parabola crossing the axis twice, which is precisely the case that the inequality in part (b) is designed to exclude. A useful sanity check is that the expression is a perfect square at the boundary, so its sign never becomes negative.
