D.1 Gravitational fields

Physics HL · PHYS-D.1-101

Theme D: FieldsD.1 (Gravitational fields: potential, field strength, escape speed, orbital energy)P1 · Section A5 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedCalculateDetermineState

Question

Five independent multiple-choice questions on gravitational fields. Each one stands alone. The gravitational potential is taken to be zero at infinity throughout, so the potential at any finite distance from a mass is negative. $G$ is the gravitational constant.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    Two point masses, each of mass $M$, are separated by a distance $2d$. What is the gravitational potential at the midpoint between them?

    (Determine)
  2. [1 mark](b)

    The escape speed from the surface of a planet of radius $R$ is $v$. What is the escape speed from a point at a distance $2R$ from the centre of the same planet?

    (Calculate)
  3. [1 mark](c)

    A satellite of mass $m$ is in a circular orbit of radius $r$ around a planet of mass $M$. What is the total energy of the satellite?

    (Determine)
  4. [1 mark](d)

    A satellite is moved from a circular orbit of radius $R$ to a circular orbit of radius $2R$ around the same planet of mass $M$. What is the change in the total energy of the satellite?

    (Determine)
  5. [1 mark](e)

    A planet of mass $M$ and radius $R$ has a uniform density. Let $g$ be the gravitational field strength at its surface. What is the gravitational field strength at a distance $R/2$ from its centre?

    (Calculate)

Total: 5 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) A. Gravitational potential is a scalar, so contributions add without any direction: each mass is a distance $d$ from the midpoint and contributes $-GM/d$, giving $-2GM/d$. (A1) Option B is the signature error of this subtopic, because it is the field strength that cancels at the midpoint by symmetry, not the potential.

(b) A. Equating kinetic energy to the depth of the potential well, $\tfrac12 mv_{e}^{2}=GMm/r$, gives $v_{e}=\sqrt{2GM/r}$. At $r=2R$ this is $\sqrt{2GM/(2R)}=v/\sqrt{2}=0.707\,v$. (A1) The square root is what B misses.

(c) A. For a circular orbit $\tfrac12 mv^{2}=GMm/(2r)$, so the kinetic energy is $+GMm/(2r)$ and the potential energy is $-GMm/r$; the total is $-GMm/(2r)$. (A1) The result is negative, which is the defining feature of a bound orbit, and the factor of two is the relationship students most often misremember.

(d) A. With $E=-GMm/(2r)$, the energy changes from $-GMm/(2R)$ to $-GMm/(4R)$. The change is $-GMm/(4R)-(-GMm/(2R))=+GMm/(4R)$, an increase. (A1) The sign is the point: raising an orbit requires an input of energy even though the satellite ends up moving more slowly.

(e) A. For a uniform sphere the enclosed mass within radius $r$ is $M r^{3}/R^{3}$, so $g(r)=G(Mr^{3}/R^{3})/r^{2}=GMr/R^{3}$, which rises linearly from zero at the centre to $g$ at the surface. At $r=R/2$ it is $g/2$. (A1) B is the inverse-square law applied where it does not hold.

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking. (a) Accept $-2GM/d$; a candidate who explains that the field cancels while the potential does not has demonstrated the key idea even if the sign slips. (b) Accept $0.71\,v$ or $v/\sqrt{2}$; candidates often reach for the inverse-square law here, so credit should follow the square root rather than any particular rounding. (c) The negative sign is part of the answer and a positive magnitude alone does not earn the mark. (d) Accept the modulus with a clear statement of the direction; note the well-known paradox that the satellite slows down while gaining energy, and do not penalise a candidate who flags it. (e) Accept $g/2$ or $GM/(2R^{2})$; this item depends on the uniform-density assumption, which is stated in the stem, and a candidate who objects that real planets are not uniform is right but has not answered the question as set.

Why this question is hard

Gravitational fields are the subtopic where the most confident wrong answers come from applying a correct formula in the wrong regime, and this cluster is built almost entirely on that. In (e) the inverse-square law gives $4g$ at half the radius, which is badly wrong because inside a uniform sphere the enclosed mass falls faster than the distance does. In (b) the same instinct produces $0.500\,v$, because escape speed looks as though it should scale like the field strength, but the square root from the energy argument softens the dependence. In (a) the field strength and the potential behave in opposite ways at the midpoint between two equal masses, and the fact that one cancels while the other adds is the cleanest test of whether a student knows which is a vector.

The two orbital items, (c) and (d), probe the sign structure of orbital energy, which is the hardest part of the topic. A circular orbit has negative total energy, equal to half the potential energy, and moving outwards makes it less negative: the satellite gains energy and loses speed at the same time. That combination is counter-intuitive enough that a student who has only memorised the formula will often report the change in (d) with the wrong sign, and the $GMm/(2R)$ option is there for the candidate who compares magnitudes instead of taking a difference. Together the five items reward a student who checks which regime a formula belongs to before using it.
