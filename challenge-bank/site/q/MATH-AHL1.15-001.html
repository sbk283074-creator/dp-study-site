An induction that only closes once the hypothesis is strengthened

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.15-001

AHL 1.15 (proof by mathematical induction)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatExplainProveDetermine

Question

For each integer $n\ge1$, let $S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{k^{2}+1}$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $\dfrac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}\le\dfrac{1}{k}-\dfrac{1}{k+1}$ for every integer $k\ge1$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    A student tries to prove by induction that $S_n<\dfrac32$ for all $n\ge1$. Explain why the inductive step cannot succeed when the hypothesis is only $S_k<\dfrac32$, writing down the strongest inequality that the step actually gives.

    (Explain)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Prove by induction that $S_n\le\dfrac32-\dfrac1n$ for every integer $n\ge1$, and deduce that $S_n<\dfrac32$ for every integer $n\ge1$.

    (Prove)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine the greatest real constant $c$ for which the inequality $S_n\le\dfrac32-\dfrac{c}{n}$ holds for every integer $n\ge1$, justifying your answer.

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) For $k\ge1$,
$$\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k+1}=\frac{(k+1)-k}{k(k+1)}=\frac{1}{k(k+1)}.$$ (M1) Now $(k+1)^{2}+1=k^{2}+2k+2$ and $k(k+1)=k^{2}+k$, so $$(k+1)^{2}+1-k(k+1)=k+2>0,$$ hence $(k+1)^{2}+1>k(k+1)$ and therefore $$\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}<\frac{1}{k(k+1)}=\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}.$$ (A1)(A1) *(AG)*

(b) With the hypothesis $S_k<\frac32$ the step gives $$S_{k+1}=S_k+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}<\frac32+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}.$$ (M1)(A1) The right-hand side is strictly greater than $\frac32$ because $\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}>0$, so this does not establish $S_{k+1}<\frac32$. (R1) No rearrangement of a hypothesis of the form $S_k<\frac32$ can remove a positive increment, so the hypothesis has to be strengthened to one that leaves a gap larger than the increment. (A1)

(c) Claim: $S_n\le\frac32-\frac1n$ for all $n\ge1$. Base case $n=1$: $S_1=\frac12$ and $\frac32-1=\frac12$, so the claim holds, with equality. (M1) Inductive step: assume $S_k\le\frac32-\frac1k$. Then $$S_{k+1}=S_k+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}\le\frac32-\frac1k+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}.$$ (M1) By part (a), $\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}\le\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}$, so $$S_{k+1}\le\frac32-\frac1k+\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}=\frac32-\frac1{k+1}.$$ (A1) This is the claim with $n=k+1$, so by induction the claim holds for every $n\ge1$. (A1) *(AG)* Finally $\frac1n>0$, so $S_n\le\frac32-\frac1n<\frac32$ for every $n\ge1$. (A1)

(d) We want the largest $c$ with $S_n\le\frac32-\frac{c}{n}$ for all $n\ge1$. (M1) The case $n=1$ reads $\frac12\le\frac32-c$, that is $c\le1$, so no $c>1$ is admissible; part (c) shows that $c=1$ is admissible. (A1) Therefore the greatest such constant is $c=1$. (A1) For example, $c=\frac{3}{2}$ would demand $S_1\le0$, which is false because $S_1=\frac12$, so values above $1$ genuinely fail and the answer is not merely the largest value tested.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for combining the right-hand side into the single fraction $\frac{1}{k(k+1)}$; the two (A1) marks are for the comparison $(k+1)^{2}+1>k(k+1)$ and for concluding the inequality. A candidate who checks several values of $k$ without proving the general comparison earns at most (M1): the point is the identity $(k+1)^{2}+1-k(k+1)=k+2>0$. Accept the equivalent form $\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}\le\frac{1}{k(k+1)}$ with no further work required.

(b) The step must be written out with the positive increment visible. Award (M1) for the decomposition $S_{k+1}=S_k+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}$, (A1) for substituting the hypothesis, (R1) for observing that the resulting bound exceeds $\frac32$, and the final (A1) for the structural remark that a strictly positive increment cannot be absorbed by a bound with no slack. A candidate who writes only 'it does not work' with no inequality earns just the first two marks.

(c) The strengthened statement must be stated, not assumed. The base case is checked with equality, which is acceptable. Award (M1) for the base case, (M1) for the inductive hypothesis and the decomposition, (A1) for applying the inequality from (a), (A1) for the algebra reaching $\frac32-\frac1{k+1}$, and (A1) for the concluding deduction $S_n<\frac32$. Follow-through is allowed: a candidate who carries an incorrect constant from (a) but applies it consistently keeps the method marks.

(d) The base case gives $c\le1$ and part (c) gives admissibility at $c=1$. A candidate who simply states $c=1$ without the inequality $\frac12\le\frac32-c$ loses the (M1). Condone a brief note that smaller values of $c$ give a weaker, hence also true, inequality; the question asks for the greatest constant.

Why this question is hard

Part (a) is a small algebraic fact, but it is the engine of the whole question: it is exactly the telescoping difference $\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}$, which is slightly larger than the increment $\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}$. The denominator $k^{2}+1$ was chosen so that $(k+1)^{2}+1=k^{2}+2k+2$ exceeds $k(k+1)=k^{2}+k$ by exactly $k+2$, giving the clean comparison. Had the denominator been $k^{2}$, the comparison would point the wrong way and the argument would need a different tail entirely.

Part (b) is the conceptual heart. Induction is usually presented as 'assume it for $k$, prove it for $k+1$', and students expect the hypothesis to be used verbatim. Here the hypothesis $S_k<\frac32$ gives $S_{k+1}<\frac32+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}+1}$, a bound that has moved the wrong way. The bound is not merely inconvenient: because the increment is strictly positive, no amount of algebra starting from $S_k<\frac32$ can produce $S_{k+1}<\frac32$. The only way forward is to prove something stronger, a statement with a margin that grows and can therefore absorb the increment.

Part (c) supplies that margin. The tail $-\frac1n$ is chosen so that the slack between consecutive values, $\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}$, is at least the increment; part (a) is precisely that statement. The base case holds with equality and the step preserves the bound, so the stronger claim is established. Part (d) pins the constant: the base case forces $c\le1$, so $c=1$ is the best possible tail of this shape, and larger constants fail immediately at $n=1$.

The classic wrong turns are: assuming the hypothesis $S_k<\frac32$ is enough and writing a circular step; treating the inequality in (a) as an equality; trying to prove the weaker $S_n<2$ instead, which is true but does not answer the question; and in (d) forgetting that the base case, not the step, is what bounds $c$ from above. A useful check is that $S_1=\frac12$ exactly, which is why the constant $\frac32$ and the tail $\frac1n$ meet at $n=1$.

Metadata

Challenge lever
The natural inductive hypothesis $S_n<\frac32$ is too weak to carry the step, because the step adds a strictly positive term; the student must notice this, construct the stronger statement $S_n\le\frac32-\frac1n$ whose tail absorbs the increment, and prove that stronger statement instead of the one asked for.
