A segment that needs the left branch and a shifted substitution

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.16-001

AHL 5.16 (integration by substitution), AHL 5.17 (area of the region enclosed by a curve and the y-axis)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatExplainDetermineHence

Question

The curve $C$ has equation $x^{2}+y^{2}-4x-6y-3=0$. The region $R$ is the region enclosed by $C$ and the $y$-axis.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    By completing the square, show that $C$ is a circle and state the coordinates of its centre and the value of its radius. Hence find the $y$-coordinates of the two points where $C$ meets the $y$-axis.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Explain why the region $R$ requires the branch $x=2-\sqrt{16-(y-3)^{2}}$ rather than the positive square root, and hence write down a definite integral with respect to $y$ whose value is the area of $R$.

    (Explain)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Using the substitution $y-3=4\sin\theta$, determine the value of $\displaystyle\int_{3-2\sqrt{3}}^{3+2\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{16-(y-3)^{2}}\,dy$, showing clearly how the limits of integration change.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Hence determine the exact area of the region $R$, giving your answer in the form $a\pi+b\sqrt{3}$ where $a$ and $b$ are integers.

    (Hence)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Completing the square in $x$ and in $y$,

$$x^{2}-4x+y^{2}-6y-3=(x-2)^{2}-4+(y-3)^{2}-9-3=(x-2)^{2}+(y-3)^{2}-16.$$ (M1)

Setting this equal to zero gives $(x-2)^{2}+(y-3)^{2}=16$, so $C$ is the circle with centre $(2,3)$ and radius $4$. (A1) On the $y$-axis $x=0$, so $4+(y-3)^{2}=16$, giving $(y-3)^{2}=12$ and therefore $y=3\pm2\sqrt{3}$. (A1)

(b) Solving $(x-2)^{2}+(y-3)^{2}=16$ for $x$ gives $x=2\pm\sqrt{16-(y-3)^{2}}$, so for each $y$ between $3-2\sqrt{3}$ and $3+2\sqrt{3}$ the circle has two points with the same $y$-coordinate. (M1) The centre has $x$-coordinate $2>0$, so the segment cut off by the $y$-axis lies to the left of that axis, where $x<0$; the branch with the negative square root, $x=2-\sqrt{16-(y-3)^{2}}$, is the only one that can produce $x<0$, and it is the boundary of $R$. (A1) The $y$-axis itself contributes $x=0$, so the area is the integral of $0-x$ over the $y$-range of the segment: (M1)

$$A(R)=\int_{3-2\sqrt{3}}^{3+2\sqrt{3}}\Bigl(\sqrt{16-(y-3)^{2}}-2\Bigr)\,dy.$$ (A1) *(AG)*

(c) Let $y-3=4\sin\theta$ with $-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le\theta\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}$, so $dy=4\cos\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{16-(y-3)^{2}}=\sqrt{16-16\sin^{2}\theta}=4\cos\theta$, since $\cos\theta\ge0$ on this interval. (M1) When $y=3-2\sqrt{3}$ we have $\sin\theta=-\tfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{4}=-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, so $\theta=-\tfrac{\pi}{3}$; when $y=3+2\sqrt{3}$ we have $\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{3}$. (A1) Hence

$$\int_{3-2\sqrt{3}}^{3+2\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{16-(y-3)^{2}}\,dy=\int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3}(4\cos\theta)(4\cos\theta)\,d\theta=16\int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3}\cos^{2}\theta\,d\theta.$$ (A1)

Using $\cos^{2}\theta=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\cos 2\theta)$,

$$16\int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3}\cos^{2}\theta\,d\theta=16\left[\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}\right]_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3}=16\left(\frac{\pi}{3}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}\right)=\frac{16\pi}{3}+4\sqrt{3}.$$ (M1)(A1)

(d) From part (b), $A(R)$ is the value just found minus the contribution of the $y$-axis boundary, so

$$A(R)=\int_{3-2\sqrt{3}}^{3+2\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{16-(y-3)^{2}}\,dy-\int_{3-2\sqrt{3}}^{3+2\sqrt{3}}2\,dy.$$ (M1) The first integral is $\frac{16\pi}{3}+4\sqrt{3}$ from part (c), and the second is $2\bigl(4\sqrt{3}\bigr)=8\sqrt{3}$. (A1) Therefore

$$A(R)=\frac{16\pi}{3}+4\sqrt{3}-8\sqrt{3}=\frac{16\pi}{3}-4\sqrt{3}.$$ (A1)

This is the area of a circular segment, and it is consistent with the sector minus triangle formula $\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}(\theta-\sin\theta)$ for $r=4$ and $\theta=\tfrac{2\pi}{3}$.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for completing the square, which must be visible; a candidate who writes down the centre and radius with no rearrangement earns only the accuracy marks. The centre is $(2,3)$, not $(-2,-3)$: the signs come from $x^{2}-4x=(x-2)^{2}-4$ and $y^{2}-6y=(y-3)^{2}-9$. The final (A1) requires $(y-3)^{2}=12$, so $y=3\pm2\sqrt{3}$; accept the exact surd form and do not penalise a decimal that rounds correctly.

(b) The essential marks are for identifying that the region lies to the left of the $y$-axis and that the left boundary is the branch with the negative square root. A candidate who writes $x=2+\sqrt{16-(y-3)^{2}}$ has chosen the arc on the right and is integrating the wrong region; that forfeits the second (A1), but follow-through is allowed if the remaining geometry is correct. The integrand $0-x$ must appear; a candidate who integrates $x$ directly and reports a negative quantity should still earn the method marks if the final area is then taken positive. The *(AG)* requires the integral with the correct limits to be stated.

(c) The substitution must be carried through with the shift. A candidate who sets $y=4\sin\theta$ and leaves the constant $3$ out will obtain incorrect limits and should not earn the limit (A1); the method (M1) for the substitution may still be awarded. The identity $\cos^{2}\theta=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\cos 2\theta)$ must be used correctly, and the evaluation may be done by symmetry since the integrand is even in $\theta$. A candidate who substitutes $u=16-(y-3)^{2}$ cannot remove the stray factor of $y-3$ and gains nothing beyond the initial (M1).

(d) The (M1) is for splitting off the constant term, and the two (A1) marks for the value $\frac{16\pi}{3}+4\sqrt{3}$ carried through and the final $\frac{16\pi}{3}-4\sqrt{3}$. A candidate who leaves the answer as an unsimplified difference of integrals has not answered the question. The exact value is about $9.83$, so a numerical check against the circular segment formula is a sensible verification.

Why this question is hard

Two separate ideas are needed here, and neither works without the other. The first is that an area with respect to the $y$-axis is computed by integrating a horizontal strip: for a region bounded on the right by the $y$-axis and on the left by a curve, the area is $\int(0-x)\,dy$, taken over the $y$-range that the region occupies. The second is that the curve must first be solved for $x$, and when the curve is not single-valued in $y$ the solution has two branches.

Here the curve is a circle written in general form. Completing the square shows that its centre is $(2,3)$ and its radius is $4$. Because the centre is to the right of the $y$-axis and the radius exceeds the distance from the centre to that axis, the $y$-axis cuts the circle into two segments, and the region enclosed by the curve and the axis is the segment on the left. Solving for $x$ gives $x=2\pm\sqrt{16-(y-3)^{2}}$; only the branch with the minus sign can give $x<0$, so the student must reject the other branch. This is the first lever, and it is easy to get wrong by reflex, because the positive square root is the one usually written first.

The second lever is the integration itself. The natural attempt is to substitute $u=16-(y-3)^{2}$, which is exactly the expression under the root, but that leaves a stray factor of $y-3$ that cannot be removed. A student may also try $y=4\sin\theta$, forgetting that the circle is not centred on the $y$-axis; this produces incorrect limits because the substitution must be $y-3=4\sin\theta$, carrying the shift of the centre into the new variable. With the correct substitution the root becomes $4\cos\theta$, the limits become $\pm\tfrac{\pi}{3}$, and the integral reduces to a standard $\cos^{2}\theta$ integral.

The final area is $\frac{16\pi}{3}-4\sqrt{3}$, which is a circular segment: it is the sector of angle $\tfrac{2\pi}{3}$ minus the triangle of base $4\sqrt{3}$ and height $2$. Recognising that is a good check. The classic wrong turns are choosing the wrong branch, omitting the shift in the substitution, and forgetting to subtract the constant $2$ that comes from the $y$-axis boundary.
