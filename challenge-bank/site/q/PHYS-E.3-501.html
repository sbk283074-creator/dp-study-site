E.3 Radioactive decay: how a constant background changes every fold-change in a measured count rate

Physics HL · PHYS-E.3-501

Theme E: Nuclear and quantum physicsE.3 (Radioactive decay: activity and the decay constant, the half-life, the exponential decay of activity and of the number of undecayed nuclei, and the correction of a measured count rate for background radiation)P1 · Section A5 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateDetermine

Question

A radioactive source is placed in front of a detector. With the source in position the detector records 960 counts during a measuring interval of 120 s. With the source removed, but with the detector, its shielding and the room exactly as they were, it records 120 counts during a second interval of the same length. The detector registers 4.0% of the decays that occur in the source; the remainder are missed because the radiation is emitted in all directions and much of it never reaches the detector. The source consists of a single nuclide of half-life 12 h and emits no other radiation. Questions (a) to (e) all refer to this arrangement. Each is worth one mark and numerical answers should be given to three significant figures.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    Calculate the background-corrected count rate at the start of the measurements, in counts per second.

    (Calculate)
    1. A. 1.00 s$^{-1}$
    2. B. 6.00 s$^{-1}$
    3. C. 7.00 s$^{-1}$
    4. D. 8.00 s$^{-1}$
  2. [1 mark](b)

    Calculate the activity of the source at the start of the measurements, in becquerels.

    (Calculate)
    1. A. 70.0 Bq
    2. B. 175 Bq
    3. C. 200 Bq
    4. D. 7.00 Bq
  3. [1 mark](c)

    Calculate the count rate that the detector will record, in counts per second, 24 h after the start of the measurements.

    (Calculate)
    1. A. 2.00 s$^{-1}$
    2. B. 1.75 s$^{-1}$
    3. C. 4.00 s$^{-1}$
    4. D. 2.75 s$^{-1}$
  4. [1 mark](d)

    Determine the time, in hours, at which the count rate recorded by the detector has fallen to one half of the rate it recorded at the start of the measurements.

    (Determine)
    1. A. 14.7 h
    2. B. 12.0 h
    3. C. 6.00 h
    4. D. 24.0 h
  5. [1 mark](e)

    Calculate the number of nuclei of the nuclide present in the source at the start of the measurements.

    (Calculate)
    1. A. $6.23 \times 10^{6}$
    2. B. $2.18 \times 10^{6}$
    3. C. $1.09 \times 10^{7}$
    4. D. $1.25 \times 10^{7}$

Reveal the answer

(a) C. Both totals have to become rates before they can be compared, and both
were taken over the same 120 s. The gross rate is $960/120 = 8.00$ s$^{-1}$ and the
background rate is $120/120 = 1.00$ s$^{-1}$, so the rate due to the source alone is
$8.00 - 1.00 = 7.00$ s$^{-1}$. (A1)

(b) B. The detector registers only 4.0% of the decays, so the corrected count rate
is 0.040 of the activity: $A = 7.00/0.040 = 175$ Bq. The activity is a property of the
source and does not depend on how well it is being watched, which is why the efficiency
has to be divided out. (A1)

(c) D. Twenty-four hours is two half-lives, so the activity falls by a factor of
four to $175/4 = 43.75$ Bq. The corrected count rate is then
$43.75 \times 0.040 = 1.75$ s$^{-1}$. But the detector cannot distinguish a decay from
a background event, so it records $1.75 + 1.00 = 2.75$ s$^{-1}$. The recorded rate has
fallen by a factor of $8.00/2.75 = 2.91$, not 4. (A1)

(d) A. The recorded rate starts at 8.00 s$^{-1}$ and has to fall to 4.00 s$^{-1}$,
which means the corrected rate must fall from 7.00 to 3.00 s$^{-1}$ — and the
background has to be removed before this can be seen. Solving
$7.00 \times 2^{-t/12} = 3.00$ gives $2^{t/12} = 7/3$, so
$t = 12\log_{2}(7/3) = 12 \times 1.222 = 14.7$ h. The recorded rate takes almost
fifteen hours to halve even though the activity halves in twelve. (A1)

(e) C. The half-life must be in seconds before it gives a decay constant in
reciprocal seconds: $\lambda = \ln 2/(12 \times 3600) = 1.60\times10^{-5}$ s$^{-1}$.
Then $A = \lambda N$ gives $N = 175/1.60\times10^{-5} = 1.09\times10^{7}$ nuclei.
(A1)

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking, and no method marks: the key is the answer.

(a) Accept 7.00 s⁻¹. An answer of 8.00 s⁻¹ has left the background in and 1.00 s⁻¹ has
kept only the background; 6.00 s⁻¹ mixes a per-second rate with a per-minute one.

(b) Accept 175 Bq. An answer of 200 Bq shows the gross rate being used, so the
background has been treated as decays from the source. An answer of 7.00 Bq has
confused a count rate with an activity.

(c) Accept 2.75 s⁻¹. This is the discriminating question. Do not accept 2.00 s⁻¹,
which applies the decay factor to the recorded rate, and do not accept 1.75 s⁻¹, which
is the corrected rate with the background not put back.

(d) Accept 14.7 h; accept 14.6 h or 14.8 h from a rounded logarithm. An answer of
12.0 h has halved the activity rather than the recorded rate, and is the commonest
wrong answer. An answer of 6.00 h has the background acting in the wrong direction.

(e) Accept 1.09 × 10⁷. Allow answers in the range 1.08 × 10⁷ to 1.10 × 10⁷. An answer
of 2.18 × 10⁶ usually means the half-life was used in seconds without converting from
hours, giving a decay constant sixty times too large; a fifth suggests a different
slip, and is worth checking individually.

Why this question is hard

There are two quantities in this cluster and the whole difficulty is keeping them
apart. The first is the activity of the source, 175 Bq. It obeys $A = \lambda N$ and
$A = A_{0}e^{-\lambda t}$, it halves every 12 h, and nothing that happens in the
laboratory changes it. The second is the count rate the detector records. It is
$0.040A + b$, where $b$ is the background, and it is the only one of the two that
anyone actually measures.

The background is a shift, not a scaling, and that is the entire point. Multiplying by
0.040 commutes with the exponential: the corrected count rate falls by exactly the same
factor as the activity, so over 24 h it really does quarter, from 7.00 to 1.75 s⁻¹.
Adding the background afterwards does not commute with anything. So the recorded rate
falls from 8.00 to 2.75 s⁻¹, a factor of 2.91 rather than 4, and the difference
between 2.91 and 4 is entirely the extra 1.00 s⁻¹ that was there at the start and is
still there at the end.

Part (d) turns the same observation into the question it is really about. If the
recorded rate halved after one half-life, then adding a constant would have no effect
on a fold-change, which is false: a constant offset always slows a fall towards it. The
recorded rate is heading for 1.00 s⁻¹, not for zero, so it covers the first half of its
fall more slowly than the second half of it in proportional terms — and it takes 14.7 h
rather than 12 h. Notice that the answer is *longer* than a half-life. An offset that
does not decay can only ever delay a fall, never hasten it, so any candidate who
produces a time shorter than 12 h has made an error of direction as well as of
arithmetic.

Part (b) and part (e) are the two conversions that bracket the calculation, and both
are places where a quantity changes units. Turning a count rate into an activity
divides by 0.040, because a detector that sees one decay in twenty-five is not a
detector that sees one decay in ten. Turning a half-life into a decay constant requires
the half-life in seconds, because $\lambda$ is a rate; 12 h is 43 200 s, not 12 s.
Neither step is difficult and both are routinely skipped.

One practical note worth making explicit: the background was measured with the source
removed but everything else unchanged. That is what makes $b$ the same 1.00 s⁻¹ in
every part. If the shielding or the geometry had moved between the two measurements,
none of the subtraction would be valid — the correction is only as good as the claim
that the two intervals differ in exactly one respect.

Challenge lever
The exponential law governs the activity and governs nothing else in this cluster. What the detector records is the activity scaled by an efficiency and then shifted by a constant, and a shift does not decay. So the recorded rate does not fall by the same factor as the activity, does not halve after one half-life, and never falls below the background however long the wait. Every distractor is the number the exponential alone would give, and every one of them forgets a quantity that was stated once, in one sentence, and appears in no formula.
