The greatest coefficient, attained twice

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.9-001

SL 1.9 (the binomial theorem: expansion of (a + b)^n and the general term)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatFindHence

Question

In the expansion of $(1 + x/2)^n$, where $n$ is a positive integer, the coefficients of $x^4$ and $x^5$ are equal.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Given that the coefficients of $x^4$ and $x^5$ are equal, show that $n = 14$.

    (Show that)
  2. [6 marks](b)

    Find the greatest coefficient in the expansion, stating the term or terms at which it occurs.

    (Find)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    Hence find the coefficient of $x^6$ in the expansion of $(1 + x/2)^{14}(1 - x)^3$.

    (Hence)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The general term of the expansion is $\binom{n}{r}(x/2)^r$, so the coefficient of $x^r$ is $\binom{n}{r}/2^r$. (M1) Equating the coefficients of $x^4$ and $x^5$ gives $\binom{n}{4}/2^4 = \binom{n}{5}/2^5$. (M1) Multiplying by $2^5$ and dividing by $\binom{n}{5}$ gives $\binom{n}{4}/\binom{n}{5} = 1/2$. (M1) Now $\binom{n}{4}/\binom{n}{5} = 5/(n-4)$, so $5/(n-4) = 1/2$. (A1) Hence $n - 4 = 10$ and $n = 14$, as required. (AG)

(b) Write $t_r = \binom{14}{r}/2^r$ for the coefficient of $x^r$. (M1) The ratio of consecutive coefficients is $t_{r+1}/t_r = (1/2)\binom{14}{r+1}/\binom{14}{r} = (1/2)(14-r)/(r+1)$. (M1) This ratio is at least $1$ exactly when $14 - r \ge 2(r+1)$, that is $14 - r \ge 2r + 2$, so $r \le 4$. (A1) The coefficients therefore increase up to $r = 4$, and at $r = 4$ the ratio equals $1$, so $t_4 = t_5$; for $r > 4$ the ratio is below $1$ and the coefficients decrease. (R1) The greatest coefficient is thus $t_4 = \binom{14}{4}/2^4 = 1001/16 = 62.5625$. (A1) It is attained twice, by the terms in $x^4$ and $x^5$, since $t_5 = \binom{14}{5}/2^5 = 2002/32 = 1001/16$. (A1) Because the ratio equals exactly $1$ at $r = 4$, the two largest coefficients are equal and every other coefficient is strictly smaller. For comparison, $t_3 = 364/8 = 45.5$ and $t_6 = 3003/64 = 46.921875$, both smaller. (R1)

(c) Expanding, $(1 - x)^3 = 1 - 3x + 3x^2 - x^3$. (M1) If $a_r$ denotes the coefficient of $x^r$ in $(1 + x/2)^{14}$, then the coefficient of $x^6$ in the product is $a_6 - 3a_5 + 3a_4 - a_3$. (M1) Substituting, this is $3003/64 - 3(1001/16) + 3(1001/16) - 364/8$. (A1) The two middle terms cancel exactly, leaving $3003/64 - 364/8$. (A1) Writing over the common denominator $64$, this is $3003/64 - 2912/64 = 91/64$. (A1) Hence the coefficient is $91/64 = 1.421875$. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a) awards (M1) for writing the general term $\binom{n}{r}(x/2)^r$, (M1) for equating the two coefficients, and (M1) for using the ratio $\binom{n}{4}/\binom{n}{5} = 5/(n-4)$; the (A1) is for $n - 4 = 10$ and the (AG) confirms $n = 14$. A candidate who equates $\binom{n}{4}$ and $\binom{n}{5}$ without the factors $2^4$ and $2^5$ obtains $n = 9$ and loses the (A1) and the (AG). In part (b) the (M1) marks are for defining $t_r$ and for forming the ratio $t_{r+1}/t_r = (1/2)(14-r)/(r+1)$, and the (A1) is for the inequality $r \le 4$. The (R1) is for the deduction that the coefficients rise to $r = 4$ and fall afterwards, which forces $t_4 = t_5$; a candidate who merely states a maximum at $r = 4$ without the increase-then-decrease argument loses that mark. The two (A1) marks are for the value $1001/16$ and for naming both terms, and the final (R1) rewards the numerical comparison with $t_3$ and $t_6$. Part (c) awards (M1) for the expansion of $(1-x)^3$ and (M1) for the convolution $a_6 - 3a_5 + 3a_4 - a_3$; the (A1) marks are for the correct substitution, the exact cancellation of the two middle terms, and the value $91/64$. A candidate who drops the signs on the odd terms obtains a value near $125$, so the signs must be preserved.

Why this question is hard

The stem is a coefficient-matching problem, but the equality of two coefficients is only the entry point; the real work is deciding where the coefficients stop growing. In part (a) the factors $2^r$ from $(x/2)^r$ must be carried into the comparison, and it is the ratio $\binom{n}{4}/\binom{n}{5} = 5/(n-4)$ that reduces the condition to a one-line equation. The common error is to compare binomial coefficients alone, which shifts the answer from $n = 14$ to $n = 9$ and destroys the later parts.

Part (b) turns on the ratio of consecutive coefficients, $t_{r+1}/t_r = (1/2)(14-r)/(r+1)$. The coefficients increase while this ratio exceeds $1$ and decrease once it falls below $1$, and the critical case is when the ratio equals $1$ exactly: that happens at $r = 4$, so the maximum is shared by the terms in $x^4$ and $x^5$. This tie is the twist, because a candidate who assumes a single greatest term will report only one of the two.

Part (c) hides a designed cancellation. Multiplying by $(1-x)^3$ mixes four consecutive coefficients with alternating signs, and because $a_4 = a_5$ the two middle contributions cancel exactly, leaving only $a_6 - a_3$. The result $91/64$ is far smaller than the individual coefficients, which is a strong signal that the cancellation was intended. Truncating the product or mis-signed terms give answers of a completely different size. The candidate is also expected to recognise that the tie at $r = 4$ is exact rather than approximate, because the ratio equals $1$ precisely there; this is why the maximum is shared by two terms and not attained at a single index. Reporting only $t_4$ or only $t_5$ therefore answers only half of the question, and the markscheme requires both to be named explicitly.
