Measuring the moment of inertia of a disc

A disc is mounted on a horizontal axle of radius $25.0\ \mathrm{mm}$ and can rotate in bearings that are not frictionless. A light string is wound round the axle and passes over a pulley; a slotted mass hanger is attached to the free end. The hanging mass is released from rest and the angular acceleration of the disc is determined from a rotary motion sensor, which has a resolution of $0.01\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$ and an uncertainty of $\pm 0.03\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$. The hanging mass is varied and the angular acceleration recorded. The student assumes that the tension in the string equals the weight of the hanging mass and that the mass of the string and the inertia of the pulley are negligible. During one run the string was seen to slip on the axle; the student noted this in the log book but kept the reading.

Question

Use $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. The applied torque has been calculated for you as $mgr$, where $m$ is the hanging mass in kilograms.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Verify the applied torque for the $60.0\ \mathrm{g}$ hanging mass, and state why the torque is calculated from the hanging mass rather than measured directly at the axle.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Plot a graph of applied torque against angular acceleration, excluding any point you have reason to distrust. Determine the gradient of the best-fit line and state what physical quantity it represents.

    (Determine)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Determine the intercept on the torque axis and explain what it represents. Hence explain why calculating the moment of inertia as the ratio of torque to angular acceleration for a single pair of readings gives a different answer for each row of the table, and state whether that ratio would be too large or too small.

    (Explain)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate the reading taken during the run in which the string slipped. State how the point appears on your graph, why the slip produces that appearance, and what the student should have done.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) $\tau = mgr = 0.0600\times9.81\times0.0250 = 1.4715\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{N\,m} = 14.7\ \mathrm{mN\,m}$, in agreement with the table. (M1)(A1) The torque is calculated rather than measured because there is no instrument in the apparatus that measures the force at the axle directly; the tension is inferred from the weight of a known mass on the assumption, stated in the stimulus, that the string is light and the pulley has negligible inertia. (A1)

(b) The point at $60.0\ \mathrm{g}$ is excluded for the reason given in (d), leaving four points. Plotting $\tau$ against $\alpha$ gives a straight line. (M1) Taking two widely separated points on the best-fit line, for example $(\alpha,\tau) = (2.17,\,4.905\ \mathrm{mN\,m})$ and $(11.99,\,24.525\ \mathrm{mN\,m})$: (M1)

$$\text{gradient} = \frac{(24.525-4.905)\times10^{-3}}{11.99-2.17} = \frac{19.620\times10^{-3}}{9.82} = 2.00\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{2}}$$ (A1)

The gradient is the moment of inertia of the disc about the axle, since $\tau_{\text{applied}} = I\alpha + \tau_{f}$ and the gradient of $\tau$ against $\alpha$ is $I$. (A1)

(c) Extrapolating the best-fit line back to $\alpha = 0$ gives an intercept of about (M1)

$$\tau_{f} \approx 4.905\times10^{-3} - (2.00\times10^{-3})(2.17) = 4.905\times10^{-3}-4.340\times10^{-3} = 5.7\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{N\,m}$$ (A1)

so the intercept is about $0.6\ \mathrm{mN\,m}$ and represents the frictional torque in the bearings: this much applied torque is needed before the disc accelerates at all. (A1) If a student computes $\tau/\alpha$ for a single row, they are computing $I + \tau_{f}/\alpha$, because the frictional torque has been attributed to the acceleration instead of to the bearings. (M1) Since $\tau_{f}/\alpha$ is positive but falls as $\alpha$ grows, every value is an overestimate of $I$, and the overestimate is largest for the smallest hanging mass: at $20.0\ \mathrm{g}$ the ratio gives $2.26\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{2}}$ and at $100.0\ \mathrm{g}$ it gives $2.05\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{2}}$, against a true value of $2.00\times10^{-3}$. (A1)

(d) The point at $60.0\ \mathrm{g}$ lies well above the line through the other four, that is, at a larger torque than the line predicts for the angular acceleration recorded. (A1) If the string slips on the axle, less torque is transmitted to the disc than $mgr$, so the angular acceleration is lower than it should be for the nominal applied torque, and the point moves to the left of the line while the plotted torque is unchanged. (A1) The student should have discarded that run, re-wound the string so that it gripped the axle, and repeated the measurement; keeping a reading known to be faulty and simply plotting it is not acceptable. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Accept $14.7\ \mathrm{mN\,m}$ or $1.47\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{N\,m}$. The justification mark is for noting that no sensor measures the axle force and that the tension is inferred from the hanging weight under stated assumptions. (b) Accept a gradient between $1.9$ and $2.1\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{2}}$ provided the anomalous point is excluded; a candidate who includes all five points obtains a gradient near $2.2\times10^{-3}$ and should be credited for method but not for value. The gradient must be taken from separated points on the line, not from two adjacent rows. (c) Accept an intercept between $0.5$ and $0.7\ \mathrm{mN\,m}$; the interpretation mark is for identifying it as the frictional torque that must be overcome. The direction question is the discriminator: the ratio is too large, and a candidate who argues that friction reduces the acceleration and therefore the ratio is unaffected has not separated the constant from the gradient. (d) The point must be located above the line, and the explanation must refer to reduced transmitted torque. Accept any sensible remedy that involves repeating the run with the fault fixed.

Why this question is hard

This is the classic systematic-error structure of a mechanics experiment and it is worth understanding in general form. The relationship being tested is $\tau = I\alpha$, but the apparatus contains a constant frictional torque, so what is actually measured is $\tau = I\alpha + \tau_{f}$. That single extra term changes the interpretation of the graph completely: the moment of inertia is the gradient, not the ratio of coordinates, and the intercept, which a student is tempted to dismiss as experimental error, carries the physical information about the bearings.

The consequence is quantified in (c). Computing $\tau/\alpha$ for one row gives $I + \tau_{f}/\alpha$, so every such estimate is too large and the error is worst where the acceleration is smallest. The data bear this out: the ratio falls from $2.26\times10^{-3}$ at the lightest load to $2.05\times10^{-3}$ at the heaviest, converging towards the true $2.00\times10^{-3}$ as the constant term becomes a smaller share of the total. A student who computes the ratio for each row and averages them has produced a number that depends on which loads happened to be used, which is the defining symptom of an uncorrected systematic error.

The seeded anomaly in (d) is deliberately of a different kind from the systematic error under investigation, so the two are not confused. The frictional torque is a genuine feature of every run and belongs in the model; the slipped string is a fault in one run and does not. Recognising which is which is the difference between a systematic effect and a mistake, and the question asks for the remedy in each case to make sure the distinction is understood.
