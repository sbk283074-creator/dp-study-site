B.4 Efficiency of a rectangular pV cycle, and why the Carnot value is not the answer

Physics HL · PHYS-B.4-201

Theme B: The particulate nature of matterB.4 Thermodynamics (first law; work done in a cyclic process; thermal efficiency; Carnot cycle as an upper bound)P2 · Section B12 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedDetermineDetermineCalculateExplain

Question

A fixed mass of ideal monatomic gas is taken round the closed cycle A$\rightarrow$B$\rightarrow$C$\rightarrow$D$\rightarrow$A shown in the table of states. The gas has molar heat capacities $C_{V} = \tfrac{3}{2}R$ and $C_{P} = \tfrac{5}{2}R$ and behaves ideally throughout.

| State | $p\,/\,10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa}$ | $V\,/\,10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^{3}}$ |
|---|---|---|
| A | 3.0 | 1.0 |
| B | 3.0 | 3.0 |
| C | 1.0 | 3.0 |
| D | 1.0 | 1.0 |

A$\rightarrow$B is isobaric, B$\rightarrow$C is isochoric, C$\rightarrow$D is isobaric and D$\rightarrow$A is isochoric.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Determine the net work done by the gas in one complete cycle, and state how this follows from the shape of the cycle on a $pV$ diagram.

    (Determine)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the total thermal energy supplied to the gas during the cycle, taking care to identify which legs supply energy rather than remove it.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Calculate the thermal efficiency of the cycle, and calculate the efficiency of a Carnot engine operating between the highest and lowest temperatures reached by this gas.

    (Calculate)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    Explain why the Carnot value cannot be the efficiency of this cycle, however ideal the gas.

    (Explain)

Total: 12 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) On a $pV$ diagram the work done in a cycle is the area enclosed. The cycle is a rectangle spanning $\Delta p = 2.0 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa}$ and $\Delta V = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^{3}}$ (M1), so
$$W = \Delta p\,\Delta V = (2.0 \times 10^{5})(2.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 400\ \mathrm{J} \tag{A1}$$

The cycle is traced clockwise, so the gas does net work on the surroundings; the isochoric legs contribute nothing because $\Delta V = 0$ (R1).

(b) Because $pV \propto T$, the four states have $pV$ products in the ratio $300 : 900 : 300 : 100$ in units of $\mathrm{J}$.

On the isobaric legs $Q = \tfrac{5}{2}\Delta(pV)$ and on the isochoric legs $Q = \tfrac{3}{2}\Delta(pV)$:

A$\rightarrow$B: $Q = \tfrac{5}{2}(900 - 300) = +1500\ \mathrm{J}$ (M1)(A1)
B$\rightarrow$C: $Q = \tfrac{3}{2}(300 - 900) = -900\ \mathrm{J}$
C$\rightarrow$D: $Q = \tfrac{5}{2}(100 - 300) = -500\ \mathrm{J}$
D$\rightarrow$A: $Q = \tfrac{3}{2}(300 - 100) = +300\ \mathrm{J}$ (M1)

The legs that supply energy are A$\rightarrow$B and D$\rightarrow$A, so
$$Q_{\mathrm{in}} = 1500 + 300 = 1800\ \mathrm{J} \tag{A1}$$

The check that the bookkeeping is right: the signed sum is $1500 - 900 - 500 + 300 = +400\ \mathrm{J}$, which is exactly $W$ from part (a), as the first law requires for a cycle.

(c) Thermal efficiency (M1)(A1):
$$\eta = \frac{W}{Q_{\mathrm{in}}} = \frac{400}{1800} = 0.222 = 22.2\ \%$$

Highest and lowest temperatures: $pV$ is greatest at B ($900$) and least at D ($100$), so the Carnot bound is (M1)
$$\eta_{C} = 1 - \frac{T_{D}}{T_{B}} = 1 - \frac{100}{900} = 0.889 = 88.9\ \%$$

(d) The Carnot efficiency is the bound for a cycle made of two isothermal and two adiabatic legs, which is the most efficient cycle possible between two reservoirs. This cycle has no isothermal legs at all: A$\rightarrow$B and C$\rightarrow$D are isobaric and B$\rightarrow$C and D$\rightarrow$A are isochoric. Heat is therefore transferred across a continuously changing temperature range rather than at two fixed temperatures, and part of the heat transfer is irreversible. The Carnot expression is an upper bound that this cycle does not approach, and quoting it as the answer confuses the bound with the value.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for the rectangle area $\Delta p\,\Delta V$ or for summing $\int p\,dV$ leg by leg, A1 for $400\ \mathrm{J}$, R1 for noting that the isochoric legs do no work or that the cycle is clockwise. Accept the sign as positive with "work done by the gas".

(b) M1 for using $\tfrac{5}{2}\Delta(pV)$ on the isobaric legs and $\tfrac{3}{2}\Delta(pV)$ on the isochoric legs, A1 for $+1500\ \mathrm{J}$ on A$\rightarrow$B, M1 for identifying the two legs that supply heat, A1 for $1800\ \mathrm{J}$. A candidate who sums all four legs and reports $400\ \mathrm{J}$ as the heat supplied has confused net work with heat input and scores 0 for (b); the distinction between $Q_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $|Q_{\mathrm{net}}|$ is the substance of the part.

(c) M1 for $\eta = W/Q_{\mathrm{in}}$, A1 for $22.2\ \%$, M1 for $1 - T_{D}/T_{B}$. Follow-through applies from incorrect values in (a) and (b) provided the ratio is formed correctly.

(d) The mark is for identifying that the Carnot cycle requires two isothermal legs and this cycle has none. A bare statement that "Carnot is the maximum" without saying why this cycle is not Carnot earns at most one of the two marks. Accept a reference to the reversibility of the heat transfer, or to the fact that heat is transferred over a range of temperatures.

Forfeits: using $C_{V} = \tfrac{5}{2}R$ for the isochoric legs (a diatomic slip); treating $pV$ as proportional to $T$ but forgetting the factor $nR$, which is harmless here because only ratios are needed. Condonation: a candidate who computes the efficiency from $1 - Q_{\mathrm{out}}/Q_{\mathrm{in}}$ with $Q_{\mathrm{out}} = 1400\ \mathrm{J}$ should be given full credit.

Why this question is hard

The trap is the presence of a memorable formula that fits the shape of the question but not its content. Given a cycle with a hot and a cold extreme, $1 - T_{c}/T_{h}$ is the first thing a prepared student reaches for, and here it returns $88.9\ \%$ against a true value of $22.2\ \%$. The two numbers are far enough apart that a student who checks the result against the first law will notice, which is the point: the item rewards checking and punishes pattern-matching.

The real work is in part (b). Efficiency is $W/Q_{\mathrm{in}}$, and $Q_{\mathrm{in}}$ is not the sum of the four heat terms, two of which are negative. Identifying which legs supply heat is the discriminating step: A$\rightarrow$B and D$\rightarrow$A, both of which raise $pV$. A candidate who sums the magnitudes, or who uses the net heat, gets a different denominator and therefore a different efficiency. Part (a) is placed first precisely so that the student has $W = 400\ \mathrm{J}$ in hand and can use the cyclic identity $\sum Q = W$ to check part (b); that identity is exact here and catches the error immediately.

Part (d) asks for the reason rather than the rule. The Carnot efficiency is the efficiency of a specific cycle, not a general bound that any cycle approaches; the gap here is large because the isobaric legs transfer heat across a range of temperatures, and the isochoric legs transfer heat with no work at all. Understanding that the bound is unattainable without the two isothermal legs is the difference between knowing a formula and knowing what it is for.
