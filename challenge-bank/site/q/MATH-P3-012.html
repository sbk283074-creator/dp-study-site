5.19 Maclaurin series used to solve a first order differential equation

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-012

Topic 5: CalculusAHL 5.19 (Maclaurin series), AHL 5.18 (first order differential equations)P314 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatHenceDetermine

Question

A function $f$ satisfies
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = 1 + xy, \qquad y = f(x), \qquad f(0) = 1,$$
and $f$ has a Maclaurin series $f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n x^n$ that converges for all real $x$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $a_0 = 1$, that $a_1 = 1$, and that $(n+1)a_{n+1} = a_{n-1}$ for every integer $n \ge 1$. Hence write down $a_2$, $a_3$ and $a_4$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that $a_{2k} = \dfrac{1}{2^k k!}$ and that $a_{2k+1} = \dfrac{1}{(2k+1)!!}$ for every integer $k \ge 0$, where $(2k+1)!! = 1 \times 3 \times 5 \times \cdots \times (2k+1)$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Hence show that, for all real $x$,
    $$f(x) = e^{x^2/2} + \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!!}.$$

    (Hence)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine $f(1)$ to four significant figures, showing that your answer is reliable to that accuracy.

    (Determine)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

A function $f$ satisfies $$\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = 1 + xy, \qquad y = f(x), \qquad f(0) = 1,$$ and $f$ has a Maclaurin series $f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n x^n$ that converges for all real $x$. (a) Show that $a_0 = 1$, that $a_1 = 1$, and that $(n+1)a_{n+1} = a_{n-1}$ for every integer $n \ge 1$. Hence write down $a_2$, $a_3$ and $a_4$. [3 marks] (Show that) (b) Show that $a_{2k} = \dfrac{1}{2^k k!}$ and that $a_{2k+1} = \dfrac{1}{(2k+1)!!}$ for every integer $k \ge 0$, where $(2k+1)!! = 1 \times 3 \times 5 \times \cdots \times (2k+1)$. [4 marks] (Show that) (c) Hence show that, for all real $x$, $$f(x) = e^{x^2/2} + \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!!}.$$ [4 marks] (Hence) (d) Determine $f(1)$ to four significant figures, showing that your answer is reliable to that accuracy. [3 marks] (Determine)
Reveal the answer

(a) With $f(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n$ we have $f'(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}na_nx^{n-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)a_{n+1}x^n$ (M1), while $1+xf(x)=1+\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^{n+1}=1+\sum_{n\ge1}a_{n-1}x^n$ (M1). Equating constant terms gives $a_1=1$ (A1) and equating coefficients of $x^n$ for $n\ge1$ gives $(n+1)a_{n+1}=a_{n-1}$ (A1). Since $f(0)=1$ we also have $a_0=1$. Therefore $2a_2=a_0=1$, so $a_2=\tfrac12$; $3a_3=a_1=1$, so $a_3=\tfrac13$; and $4a_4=a_2=\tfrac12$, so $a_4=\tfrac18$ (A1).

(b) Putting $n=2k-1$ in the recurrence gives $2k\,a_{2k}=a_{2k-2}$, that is $a_{2k}=\dfrac{a_{2k-2}}{2k}$ for $k\ge1$ (M1). Iterating down to $a_0=1$ gives $a_{2k}=\dfrac{1}{(2k)(2k-2)\cdots 2}=\dfrac{1}{2^k\,k!}$ (A1) (AG), because the descending product of $k$ even numbers carries a factor $2$ from each of them. Putting $n=2k$ gives $(2k+1)a_{2k+1}=a_{2k-1}$, that is $a_{2k+1}=\dfrac{a_{2k-1}}{2k+1}$ (M1), and iterating down to $a_1=1$ gives $a_{2k+1}=\dfrac{1}{(2k+1)(2k-1)\cdots 3\cdot 1}=\dfrac{1}{(2k+1)!!}$ (A1) (AG), with the convention $(-1)!!=1$ covering $k=0$.

(c) Split the convergent series into its even-indexed and odd-indexed parts (M1):
$$f(x)=\sum_{k\ge0}a_{2k}x^{2k}+\sum_{k\ge0}a_{2k+1}x^{2k+1}.$$
The even part is $\sum_{k\ge0}\dfrac{x^{2k}}{2^k k!}=\sum_{k\ge0}\dfrac{(x^2/2)^k}{k!}$ (M1), which is the exponential series evaluated at $x^2/2$ and so equals $e^{x^2/2}$ (A1). The odd part is $\sum_{k\ge0}\dfrac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!!}$ (A1). Adding the two parts gives the stated result (AG).

(d) At $x=1$ the even part is $e^{1/2}=1.6487212707$ (A1) and the odd part is $1+\tfrac13+\tfrac1{15}+\tfrac1{105}+\tfrac1{945}+\tfrac1{10395}+\tfrac1{135135}+\tfrac1{2027025}+\tfrac1{34459425}$ (M1), which evaluates to $1.4106861$ (A1). Consecutive odd terms satisfy $\dfrac{t_{k+1}}{t_k}=\dfrac{1}{2k+3}$, so beyond the last term retained every ratio is at most $\tfrac1{19}$ and the discarded tail is smaller than $\dfrac{1}{34459425}\times\dfrac{19}{18}=3.1\times10^{-8}$ (R1). The tail is therefore far too small to disturb the fourth significant figure, and $f(1)=1.6487212707+1.4106861=3.0594074$, so $f(1)=3.059$ to four significant figures (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) Accept the recurrence stated with the index shifted, for example $(n+2)a_{n+2}=a_n$ for $n\ge0$, provided the range of validity is given. A candidate who writes the derivative as $\sum na_nx^n$ forfeits the first (M1) but may earn the later marks on follow-through from their own (wrong) recurrence, so long as it is used consistently. $a_0=1$ must be justified from $f(0)=1$, not assumed.

(b) The closed forms must be established, not merely conjectured from the first few terms: a candidate who states $a_{2k}=1/(2^kk!)$ without the iteration forfeits (A1). Condone $\dfrac{1}{2\cdot4\cdots(2k)}$ for $a_{2k}$ and any correct descending odd product for $a_{2k+1}$. Do not require the word 'induction'; a correctly explained iteration is sufficient.

(c) The exponential series must be identified as such: writing $\sum (x^2/2)^k/k!$ without naming $e^{x^2/2}$ earns (M1) only. Follow-through applies to a candidate whose part (b) is wrong, provided they split the series into two parts and sum the one they have.

(d) Award (M1) for setting up the odd sum with at least five terms, (A1) for a value correct to five decimal places, and (R1) only for a genuine bound on the discarded tail — a bare assertion that 'the terms get small' earns nothing. Accept any correct alternative route, including numerical quadrature of $e^{1/2}\left(1+\int_0^1 e^{-t^2/2}\,dt\right)$, provided an error estimate accompanies it.

Why this question is hard

The trap is that this differential equation is exactly the kind a strong candidate solves without thinking: it is linear, so the integrating factor is $e^{-x^2/2}$ and the solution is $f(x)=e^{x^2/2}\left(1+\int_0^x e^{-t^2/2}\,dt\right)$. That is correct and completely useless for part (d), because $\int e^{-t^2/2}\,dt$ is not elementary — it is an error-function integral. The item is built so that the 'exact' method dead-ends at precisely the point where a number is required.

The recurrence route looks like a retreat to approximation and is in fact the stronger method, because it separates into two subseries with completely different characters. The even part is the exponential series in $x^2/2$ and sums in closed form; the odd part is a double-factorial series that does not, but whose consecutive-term ratio $1/(2k+3)$ shrinks fast enough to bound its own tail. Recognising that one half of the series is elementary and the other half is not — and then treating each with the tool that suits it — is the whole problem.

The classic wrong turn is to compute $a_0, a_2, a_4$ first, spot $1,\tfrac12,\tfrac18$, recognise $e^{x^2/2}$, and carry that through as the whole answer, reporting $f(1)=1.649$. That discards the odd subseries, which at $x=1$ contributes $1.4107$ — nearly as much again. A second wrong turn is to sum the two subseries together under one rule and produce a single spurious closed form. Thirdly, candidates who do reach the correct series often quote $f(1)$ with no error bound, which is not an answer to a question that asks for a stated accuracy.
