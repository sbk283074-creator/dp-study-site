6.7 An open-ended final class: the mean is not estimable and the median is, and the target has to be judged on the second

Ashgrove Logistics: absence days by employee

Ashgrove Logistics employs 70 drivers and warehouse staff. The HR department records the number of days each employee was absent last year and groups them for the annual report.

The board has set a target that *average* absence must be below six days per employee. The HR director reports that the mean absence is 5.71 days, calculated from the grouped table, and that the target has therefore been met.

The table is an extract from the report. The final class is recorded as '12 or more' because six employees had long-term sickness and their records were not broken down further.

Question

The histogram shows the same distribution, with the open-ended final class drawn as a faded bar. Use the n/2 convention for the median and quartiles of a grouped distribution, and give all answers to two decimal places.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Reproduce the HR director's estimate of the mean from the grouped table, stating the assumption you have to make about the final class in order to produce it.

    (Estimate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the median and the interquartile range of the distribution, and state what assumption, if any, the open-ended class forces you to make in order to find them.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Show that no upper bound can be placed on the mean from this table, and determine the greatest lower bound that the table does guarantee.

    (Show that)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Evaluate the HR director's claim that the mean absence is 5.71 days and that the six-day target has been met, and recommend how absence should be reported instead.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) Using midpoints 1, 4, 7 and 10 for the first four classes and assuming the last class has the same width as the others, so a midpoint of 13 (M1), the estimated mean is (14 × 1 + 23 × 4 + 18 × 7 + 9 × 10 + 6 × 13) ÷ 70 = (14 + 92 + 126 + 90 + 78) ÷ 70 = 400 ÷ 70 = 5.71 days (A1). The assumption is that the six employees in the open class were each absent for about 13 days; nothing in the table supports it, and the class was left open precisely because those six records were not broken down (A1).
(b) With 70 employees the median is the 35th value. Cumulative frequencies are 14, 37, 55, 64 and 70, so the 35th value lies in the class 3 – 5, whose lower boundary is 2.5 and whose width is 3: median = 2.5 + ((35 − 14) ÷ 23) × 3 = 2.5 + 2.74 = 5.24 days (M1)(A1). The lower quartile is the 17.5th value, also in 3 – 5: 2.5 + ((17.5 − 14) ÷ 23) × 3 = 2.96 days. The upper quartile is the 52.5th value, in 6 – 8: 5.5 + ((52.5 − 37) ÷ 18) × 3 = 8.08 days. The interquartile range is 8.08 − 2.96 = 5.13 days (A1). No assumption is needed: these are positional measures, and the six employees in the open class affect them only by standing above them in the ordering.
(c) The six employees in the final class each have at least 12 days, but the table places no ceiling on them. If they each had 60 days, the mean would be (14 + 92 + 126 + 90 + 6 × 60) ÷ 70 = 682 ÷ 70 = 9.74 days (M1); if they each had 200 days it would be 21.74 days (A1). Since the assumed value can be increased without limit, no upper bound on the mean can be derived from the table (A1). The smallest the mean can be is when all six are at the foot of their class, 12 days: (14 + 92 + 126 + 90 + 72) ÷ 70 = 394 ÷ 70 = 5.63 days, so the table guarantees only that the mean is at least 5.63 days (A1).
(d) The claim is unsound in two ways. The figure of 5.71 days is not an estimate of the mean at all: it is the value obtained when the six longest absences are each assumed to be 13 days, and the same table is consistent with a mean of 9.74 days or of 21 days, so the mean cannot be compared with the target (M1)(A1). What the table does support is a different statement: the median is 5.24 days and therefore below the target, 8.6% of the workforce (6 of 70) were absent for at least 12 days, and the distribution is right-skewed with an interquartile range of 5.13 days — so the typical employee is comfortably inside the target and a small group is far outside it (A1). The recommendation is to report absence with positional measures and the tail: the median, the interquartile range and the percentage of employees above a threshold such as twelve days, together with the exact figures for the six long-term cases, which the HR department already holds (M1)(A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for using midpoints at all, (A1) for 5.71 days, (A1) for naming the assumption explicitly. A candidate who writes 5.71 with no comment on the open class earns the method mark only; the point of the part is that the number carries an invisible assumption.
(b) (M1) for locating the median class by cumulative frequency, (A1) for 5.24 days, (A1) for the quartiles and the interquartile range of 5.13 days. Accept quartile positions of (n+1)/4 and 3(n+1)/4 if applied consistently, with quartiles of 2.99 and 8.21 and an IQR of 5.22; do not penalise the alternative convention, but do penalise a candidate who mixes them. The statement that no assumption is required is worth credit here even if it is not separately marked.
(c) (M1) for substituting a large value into the open class, (A1) for a second such value or for a general argument, (A1) for the conclusion that no upper bound exists, (A1) for the lower bound of 5.63 days. The lower bound must use 12, the smallest value the class permits; a candidate who uses 11.5 has misread the class and loses this mark.
(d) (M1) for distinguishing an estimate that depends on the assumption from one that does not, (A1) for the conclusion that the claim cannot be tested from the mean, (A1) for the supportable statements — median 5.24, 8.6% at twelve days or more — and (M1)(A1) for a reporting recommendation. Do not credit 'collect better data' on its own: the data already exist, and the fault is in the summary chosen.

Why this question is hard

Every grouped-data question a student meets asks for an estimated mean, and the method — midpoint times frequency, summed and divided — becomes a reflex. This item is built so that the reflex produces a number that looks exactly like the right kind of answer and cannot be right, because one of the inputs does not exist.

The open-ended class is doing two jobs. Mechanically, it removes the midpoint, so the mean is not approximate but underivable, and the student has to say what the table still guarantees instead — a one-sided bound. Conceptually, it removes the *wrong* summary from the board's hands: the six employees who caused the class to be left open are the ones the business needs to manage, and the mean is the one statistic that lets them disappear into an average. The median and the interquartile range are unaffected by how large their values are, which is precisely why they are the right measures for a skewed distribution with a long tail.

The judgement in (d) is therefore not an add-on. A student who simply reports that the target is unmet has missed the more useful finding: the typical employee is well inside the target, 8.6% are far outside it, and those are different problems requiring different responses. Reporting the median does not conceal those six cases: it reports them as a percentage of the workforce rather than averaging them into a figure that no longer describes anybody, which is what the board's target actually needs.

Challenge lever
Grouped-data questions always ask for an estimated mean, and the routine is to take midpoints. Here the final class has no upper end, so it has no midpoint: whatever value the student substitutes is an assumption about six named people, and because those six are the ones with the longest absences, changing the assumption alone can push the mean above any number. The mean is therefore not merely inaccurate but underivable, while the median and quartiles are fixed by position alone and survive unchanged.

The business setting is added so that the choice between the mean and the median has a consequence — a target that is met or not met — rather than being a preference.
Verification
Every figure was recomputed in Python, and the arithmetic was cross-checked two ways for each statistic: the median and quartiles by the interpolation formula and by brute-force expansion of a synthetic data set consistent with the table.
