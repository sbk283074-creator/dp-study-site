A quadratic whose second root the sum to infinity forbids

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.8-001

SL 1.8 (sum of infinite convergent geometric sequences) and SL 1.3 (geometric sequences and series)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatHenceDetermineFind

Question

A geometric series has second term $-6$ and sum to infinity $8$. Its first term is $a$ and its common ratio is $r$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Given that the second term is $-6$ and the sum to infinity is $8$, show that $4r^2 - 4r - 3 = 0$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Hence find the two possible values of the common ratio $r$.

    (Hence)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Determine which of these two values of $r$ is admissible for a convergent series, and find the corresponding first term $a$ and third term $ar^2$.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Find the sum of the first ten terms of the convergent series, giving your answer as an exact fraction.

    (Find)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The second term is $ar = -6$, so $a = -6/r$. (M1) The two relations $ar = -6$ and $a/(1-r) = 8$ are the only information available, so eliminating $a$ is the natural first move. Substituting this into the sum to infinity $a/(1-r) = 8$ gives $-6/r = 8(1-r)$, so $-6 = 8r(1-r)$. (M1) Expanding the right-hand side, $-6 = 8r - 8r^2$, and bringing every term to one side gives $8r^2 - 8r - 6 = 0$. (A1) Dividing through by $2$ yields $4r^2 - 4r - 3 = 0$, as required. (AG)

(b) Factorising, $4r^2 - 4r - 3 = (2r - 3)(2r + 1)$. (M1) Setting each factor to zero gives $2r - 3 = 0$ or $2r + 1 = 0$, so $r = 3/2$ or $r = -1/2$. (A1) Both values satisfy the quadratic, which substitution confirms. (A1)

(c) A geometric series converges to a finite sum only when $|r| < 1$. (R1) For $r = 3/2$ we have $|r| = 1.5 > 1$, so this series diverges and cannot have sum to infinity $8$; that root must be rejected. (R1) For $r = -1/2$ we have $|r| = 0.5 < 1$, so this is the admissible value. (A1) Then $a = -6/(-1/2) = 12$, and the third term is $ar^2 = 12(1/4) = 3$. (A1) Checking the sum, $12/(1 - (-1/2)) = 12/(3/2) = 8$, as required. (A1)

(d) The sum of the first $n$ terms is $S_n = a(1 - r^n)/(1 - r)$. (M1) With $a = 12$, $r = -1/2$ and $n = 10$, this is $S_{10} = 12(1 - (-1/2)^{10})/(1 + 1/2)$. (M1) Now $(-1/2)^{10} = 1/1024$ and $1 + 1/2 = 3/2$, so $S_{10} = 12(1023/1024)/(3/2) = 8(1023/1024)$. (A1) Hence $S_{10} = 8184/1024 = 1023/128$. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a) carries two (M1) marks: the first is for translating the second term into $ar = -6$ and hence $a = -6/r$, and the second is for substituting that expression into the sum-to-infinity formula. The (A1) is for reaching $8r^2 - 8r - 6 = 0$ in a correct unsimplified form, and the (AG) confirms the printed answer, so a candidate who simply quotes $4r^2 - 4r - 3 = 0$ without the substitution earns nothing. In part (b) the (M1) is for a valid factorisation or for applying the quadratic formula, and the two (A1) marks are for the two roots; a candidate who finds only $r = 3/2$ loses the second (A1). Part (c) is the discriminating part: the first (R1) is for stating the convergence condition $|r| < 1$, the second (R1) is for rejecting $r = 3/2$ because $|3/2| > 1$ even though it satisfies the quadratic, and the three (A1) marks are for the admissible ratio, the first term $a = 12$ and the third term $ar^2 = 3$. Part (d) awards (M1) for quoting the finite-sum formula, (M1) for the substitution, and (A1)(A1) for the simplification and the exact fraction $1023/128$; a decimal such as $7.99$ loses the final (A1) because the question demands an exact fraction.

Why this question is hard

The item is built so that the algebra of part (a) and the convergence condition of part (c) pull in opposite directions. The substitution $ar = -6$ into $a/(1-r) = 8$ is standard, and it produces a quadratic whose two roots are both genuine roots of that quadratic; nothing in the algebra of parts (a) and (b) can tell the candidate which one is wanted. The decisive fact is that a geometric series has a finite sum to infinity only when $|r| < 1$, so the root $r = 3/2$ is a valid solution of the equation but not a valid common ratio for a convergent series. This is the trap: a candidate who stops at part (b) and reports both roots has answered a different question from the one asked.

The negative ratio $r = -1/2$ then has to be carried forward correctly. The first term follows from $a = -6/r = 12$, the third term from $ar^2 = 3$, and the check $12/(1 + 1/2) = 8$ confirms that the reconstructed series really does have the stated sum. Part (d) rewards exactness: the alternating ratio makes $(-1/2)^{10}$ positive, so the finite sum is slightly below $8$, and the exact value is $1023/128$. A candidate who works with decimals will see $7.9921875$ and may not recognise the fraction, which is why the command term insists on an exact fraction. The rejection of the positive root is the whole point of the exercise: an examiner expects the candidate to say explicitly that $|r| > 1$ prevents convergence, not merely to discard the root because it looks large. Both roots solve the quadratic, so the arithmetic alone cannot separate them, and a final check that the surviving series really does sum to $8$ closes the argument.
