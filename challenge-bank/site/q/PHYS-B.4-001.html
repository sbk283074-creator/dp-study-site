B.4 Thermodynamics

Physics HL · PHYS-B.4-001

B.3 Gas laws (ideal gas equation), B.4 Thermodynamics (AHL): first law, work, internal energy, heat engine efficiencyP216 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatCalculateDetermine

Stimulus

A fixed amount of monatomic ideal gas is taken round the rectangular cycle ABCDA shown in the table. Each leg is either isochoric (constant volume) or isobaric (constant pressure).

| State | $p$ / Pa | $V$ / m³ | $T$ / K |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $2.0\times10^{5}$ | $5.0\times10^{-3}$ | $300$ |
| B | $4.0\times10^{5}$ | $5.0\times10^{-3}$ | |
| C | $4.0\times10^{5}$ | $1.0\times10^{-2}$ | |
| D | $2.0\times10^{5}$ | $1.0\times10^{-2}$ | |

For a monatomic ideal gas, $U=\dfrac32 nRT$. The amount of gas is $n=0.40\ \mathrm{mol}$ and $R=8.31\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$.

Question

The cycle runs A → B → C → D → A.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that $T_{B}=600\ \mathrm{K}$, $T_{C}=1200\ \mathrm{K}$ and $T_{D}=600\ \mathrm{K}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the net work done by the gas in one complete cycle.

    (Calculate)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Calculate the total thermal energy transferred to the gas during one complete cycle.

    (Calculate)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the efficiency of this cycle as a heat engine, and compare it with the efficiency of a Carnot engine operating between the same maximum and minimum temperatures.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) From $pV=nRT$, with $n$ and $R$ constant, $T\propto pV$.

A → B is isochoric, so $T\propto p$: $\dfrac{T_{B}}{T_{A}}=\dfrac{p_{B}}{p_{A}}=\dfrac{4.0}{2.0}=2 \Rightarrow T_{B}=600\ \mathrm{K}$. (M1)(A1)

B → C is isobaric, so $T\propto V$: $\dfrac{T_{C}}{T_{B}}=\dfrac{V_{C}}{V_{B}}=\dfrac{1.0\times10^{-2}}{5.0\times10^{-3}}=2 \Rightarrow T_{C}=1200\ \mathrm{K}$. (A1)

C → D is isochoric: $\dfrac{T_{D}}{T_{C}}=\dfrac{p_{D}}{p_{C}}=\dfrac{2.0}{4.0}=\dfrac12 \Rightarrow T_{D}=600\ \mathrm{K}$. (A1)

(Consistency check: D → A is isobaric with $\frac{V_A}{V_D}=\frac12$, giving $T_{A}=300\ \mathrm{K}$ as stated.)

(b) Work is done only on the two isochoric-free legs:

B → C: $W=p\,\Delta V=4.0\times10^{5}(1.0\times10^{-2}-5.0\times10^{-3})=4.0\times10^{5}(5.0\times10^{-3})=2000\ \mathrm{J}$ (A1)

D → A: $W=p\,\Delta V=2.0\times10^{5}(5.0\times10^{-3}-1.0\times10^{-2})=-1000\ \mathrm{J}$ (A1)

$$W_{\text{net}}=2000-1000=1000\ \mathrm{J}$$ (A1)

(Equivalently, the area of the rectangle: $(4.0-2.0)\times10^{5}\times(1.0-0.5)\times10^{-2}=1000\ \mathrm{J}$.)

(c) Heat flows in during the two legs where the temperature rises: A → B and B → C.

A → B (isochoric): $W=0$, so by the first law $Q=\Delta U$.

$$Q_{AB}=\tfrac32 nR\,\Delta T=\tfrac32(0.40)(8.31)(600-300)=1.5(0.40)(8.31)(300)=1496\ \mathrm{J}$$ (M1)(A1)

B → C (isobaric): $\Delta U=\tfrac32 nR\,\Delta T=\tfrac32(0.40)(8.31)(1200-600)=2992\ \mathrm{J}$ (A1)

$W=2000\ \mathrm{J}$ from (b), so by the first law $Q=\Delta U+W$:

$$Q_{BC}=2992+2000=4992\ \mathrm{J}$$ (M1)(A1)

$$Q_{\text{in}}=1496+4992=6488\approx6.5\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{J}$$

(d)

$$\eta=\frac{W_{\text{net}}}{Q_{\text{in}}}=\frac{1000}{6488}=0.154=15.4\%$$ (A1)

A Carnot engine between $T_{\max}=1200\ \mathrm{K}$ and $T_{\min}=300\ \mathrm{K}$ has

$$\eta_{\text{Carnot}}=1-\frac{T_{\min}}{T_{\max}}=1-\frac{300}{1200}=0.75=75\%$$ (M1)(A1)

So this cycle achieves only about one fifth of the Carnot efficiency. (A1) The reason is that heat is taken in over a whole *range* of temperatures — including during the isochoric leg A → B, where the gas is still relatively cool — and rejected over a range as well (C → D and D → A). A Carnot engine takes in all of its heat at the single highest temperature and rejects it all at the single lowest; any engine that absorbs heat while its temperature is changing must do worse.

Markscheme notes

(a) Credit either the ratio argument or direct substitution into $T=pV/nR$. Using $R=8.31$ and $n=0.40$ gives $T_{A}=301\ \mathrm{K}$ rather than exactly $300\ \mathrm{K}$; this is rounding in the data and must not be penalised. Award full marks for temperatures given as 600, 1200, 600 K.

(b) Award (A1) for each leg's work with the correct sign, (A1) for the net. Credit the 'area of the p–V loop' method fully if the area is correctly identified as $(p_{2}-p_{1})(V_{2}-V_{1})$.

(c) The key discrimination is *which* legs to include. Candidates who sum $\Delta U$ over the whole cycle get zero (correctly, since it returns to A) and conclude $Q_{\text{in}}=W_{\text{net}}$, which is wrong — heat is also *rejected*. Award (M1) for applying $Q=\Delta U+W$ on a heating leg. Condone 6.4–6.5 kJ given rounding.

(d) Accept 15%–16%. The Carnot comparison must use the *extreme* temperatures of the cycle (300 K and 1200 K), not the mean. The final (A1) is for an explanation in terms of heat being absorbed/rejected across a temperature range; 'real engines are always less efficient' is not sufficient.

Why this question is hard

This question is assembled from three ideas students usually meet separately, and the joins are where the marks are lost.

First, the temperatures. No absolute calculation is needed if you notice that each leg holds either $p$ or $V$ fixed, so $T\propto pV$ collapses to a simple ratio. Candidates who insist on computing $T=pV/nR$ for every state do more work and accumulate rounding error (the data are only self-consistent to about 0.3%).

Second, and this is the real trap: the net work is not the net heat. Over a complete cycle $\Delta U=0$, so $W_{\text{net}}=Q_{\text{in}}-Q_{\text{out}}$ — but 'the total heat transferred to the gas' means $Q_{\text{in}}$ only, i.e. the two legs on which the temperature rises. A very common answer to (c) is $1000\ \mathrm{J}$, arrived at by applying $\Delta U=0$ to the whole cycle and forgetting the rejected heat entirely. The efficiency then comes out as 100%, which should be an immediate alarm bell.

Third, the Carnot comparison. Students often reach for the temperatures of the *isothermal* legs — but there are none here. The correct bound uses the extreme temperatures the working substance actually reaches, 300 K and 1200 K, giving 75%. The gap between 15% and 75% is then the physical point: a cycle that absorbs heat while warming up is inherently wasteful, which is why real engines use regeneration and why the Carnot ideal insists on isothermal heat exchange.

Classic wrong turns: including C → D in $Q_{\text{in}}$; using $W=p\Delta V$ on the isochoric legs; and quoting the Carnot efficiency with temperatures in the wrong order (giving a negative value).
