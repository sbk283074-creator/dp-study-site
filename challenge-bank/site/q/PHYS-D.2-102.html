D.2 Electric and magnetic fields

Physics HL · PHYS-D.2-102

Theme D: FieldsD.2 (Electric and magnetic fields)P216 marksdifficulty 5technology: allowedShow thatCalculateDetermineEvaluate

Question

A mass spectrometer uses a velocity selector followed by a deflecting chamber. In the selector, a uniform electric field of strength $E = 8.0 \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$ and a uniform magnetic field of flux density $B = 0.40\ \mathrm{T}$ are at right angles to each other and to the beam, arranged so that the electric and magnetic forces on a positive ion oppose. Ions that pass through the selector without deflection enter a second chamber containing only a uniform magnetic field of flux density $B' = 0.80\ \mathrm{T}$ perpendicular to their velocity. Take $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$ and the unified atomic mass unit as $1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that an ion passes through the selector undeflected only if its speed is $v = E/B$, and calculate that speed. State, with a reason, whether this speed depends on the mass or the charge of the ion.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    In the second chamber an ion describes a semicircular path and strikes a detector. Show that the distance between the entry point and the point of impact is $d = \dfrac{2mv}{qB'}$, and calculate this distance for a singly ionised ion of uranium-238.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    The beam also contains singly ionised uranium-235. Determine the separation of the two impact points on the detector, and determine the ratio of the two path radii.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Evaluate whether this instrument can be used to separate the two isotopes in practice, referring to your answer to (c) and to one further feature of the apparatus that the calculation does not include.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) For an undeflected path the net force is zero, so the electric and magnetic forces are equal in magnitude: (M1)
$$qE = Bqv \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \frac{E}{B} = \frac{8.0 \times 10^{4}}{0.40} = 2.0 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$$ (A1)(A1)
The speed does not depend on the mass or on the magnitude of the charge, because $q$ cancels in $qE = Bqv$; it depends only on the two field strengths. (A1) Doubling the charge doubles both forces and leaves the balance untouched. The selector therefore transmits ions of one speed and rejects all others, whatever their mass.

(b) In the second chamber the magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it provides the centripetal force and the path is an arc of a circle: (M1)
$$B'qv = \frac{mv^{2}}{r} \quad\Rightarrow\quad r = \frac{mv}{qB'}$$
The ion travels a semicircle, so the distance from entry to impact is the diameter $d = 2r$: (M1)
$$d = \frac{2mv}{qB'} = \frac{2 \times 238 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} \times 2.0 \times 10^{5}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.80}$$
$$d = \frac{1.58 \times 10^{-19}}{1.28 \times 10^{-19}} = 1.23\ \mathrm{m}$$ (A1)(A1)

(c) The mass of a uranium-235 ion is $\dfrac{235}{238}$ of the uranium-238 mass, and since $r \propto m$ at fixed $v$, $q$ and $B'$, the radii scale in the same ratio. (M1) For uranium-238, $r = d/2 = 0.617\ \mathrm{m}$, so for uranium-235 (M1)
$$r_{235} = 0.617 \times \frac{235}{238} = 0.610\ \mathrm{m}$$
The impact points are separated by twice the difference in radii: (A1)
$$\Delta = 2(0.6173 - 0.6095) = 1.6 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m} = 16\ \mathrm{mm}$$
and the ratio of the radii is $\dfrac{238}{235} = 1.013$. (A1)

(d) A separation of $16\ \mathrm{mm}$ on a path about $1.2\ \mathrm{m}$ long is measurable, so in principle the two isotopes can be distinguished. (A1) The ratio of $1.013$ is the more telling number: the two radii differ by only about $1.3\%$, so the apparatus has very little margin, and anything that blurs the impact point by more than a few millimetres destroys the separation. (A1) One feature the calculation ignores is that the beam entering the selector is not monoenergetic — the ion source produces a spread of speeds, and even the transmitted ions have a small spread because the selector has a finite width and the cancellation is never perfect for ions slightly off the axis. This thermal spread alone typically broadens each impact point by more than $16\ \mathrm{mm}$ unless the ions are first accelerated through a well-defined potential difference, which is why real instruments do so. (A1) A second consideration is that a practical separator must also produce useful quantities: the beam current that can be pushed through a $16\ \mathrm{mm}$ slit is small, so separating macroscopic amounts of uranium-235 this way would be extremely slow, which is precisely why isotope separation for fuel is done by other means. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for equating the two forces and (A1) for the speed; the final mark is for stating that the speed is independent of mass and charge *with the reason that q cancels*, and 'it depends only on the fields' without the cancellation is not enough. (b) Accept $1.22$ to $1.24\ \mathrm{m}$. Candidates who use the diameter and candidates who use the radius should be distinguished: the question asks for the distance from entry to impact, so a candidate who computes $0.617\ \mathrm{m}$ has found the radius and should lose one mark even though the physics is right. (c) Accept $15$ to $17\ \mathrm{mm}$ and a ratio of $1.01$. The scaling argument $r \propto m$ is worth more than recomputing from scratch, and should be credited even if the arithmetic slips. (d) Credit must be given for engaging with the $16\ \mathrm{mm}$ figure rather than asserting that the separation is small; the strongest additional point is the spread of speeds in the beam, and the strongest objection of all is throughput. Do not accept 'friction' or 'air resistance' — the chamber is evacuated.

Why this question is hard

The velocity selector is the conceptual heart of this item, and it is worth being clear about why. Two fields act on the same ion and their forces oppose, and the balance condition $qE = Bqv$ does not contain $q$ or $m$ at all. Every ion that emerges from the selector is therefore travelling at exactly the same speed, $2.0 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, regardless of what it is. Students find this surprising because they expect a mass spectrometer to select by mass, and it does not: the selector fixes the speed, and the deflection chamber then converts mass into radius precisely *because* the speed is already known. Without the selector, $r = mv/qB'$ has two unknowns and nothing can be inferred.

Part (c) is where the item earns its marks. The two uranium isotopes differ by three atomic mass units, which sounds like a great deal, and the resulting radii differ by a factor of $238/235 = 1.013$, which is very little. The detector separation is $16\ \mathrm{mm}$ on a $1.2\ \mathrm{m}$ path. Both of those numbers are needed to evaluate the instrument, and they pull in different directions: $16\ \mathrm{mm}$ is comfortably measurable, and $1.3\%$ is uncomfortably tight. Holding the two together is the actual judgement.

Part (d) asks what the calculation leaves out, and the honest answer is the spread of speeds in the beam. The entire derivation assumes every ion moves at exactly $v = E/B$, and a real ion source produces a distribution. Because the separation is only $1.3\%$ in radius, a spread of more than about half a per cent in speed washes it out completely, which is why real instruments accelerate the ions through a fixed potential difference before they enter. That is a genuine engineering point that follows directly from the numbers the candidate has just computed, and it is a better lesson than any amount of description of how a mass spectrometer works.
