B.3 Testing the ideal gas equation against data; uncertainty and systematic error

Physics HL · PHYS-B.3-101

Theme B: The particulate nature of matterB.3 (Gas laws)P1 · Section B13 marksdifficulty 5technology: allowedCalculateEstimateIdentifySuggest

Student investigation: testing the ideal gas equation

A student traps a fixed amount of gas in a sealed syringe connected to a pressure gauge and placed in a water bath. The pressure, the volume and the temperature of the water bath are recorded for six different settings. The pressure gauge reads to the nearest 1 kPa, the syringe scale to the nearest 1 cubic centimetre, and the thermometer to the nearest 0.5 K. The student wants to know whether the data support the ideal gas equation, and to determine the amount of gas in the syringe.

Question

The readings are shown in the table. Take the molar gas constant as $8.31$ J per mole per kelvin.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the value of $\dfrac{pV}{T}$ for each of the six settings, stating the unit, and hence determine the amount of gas in the syringe in moles.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Estimate the percentage uncertainty in $\dfrac{pV}{T}$ for the first row of the table, using the resolutions given in the method.

    (Estimate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Identify which row is not consistent with the other five, and state the temperature that would bring it into line with them.

    (Identify)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Suggest a physical cause for the inconsistency, and describe a change to the experimental method that would remove it.

    (Suggest)

Total: 13 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

[3 marks] (Estimate) (c) Identify which row is not consistent with the other five, and state the temperature that would bring it into line with them. [3 marks] (Identify) (d) Suggest a physical cause for the inconsistency, and describe a change to the experimental method that would remove it. [4 marks] (Suggest)
Reveal the answer

(a) With $p$ in kPa and $V$ in cubic centimetres, $pV$ is in units of $10^{3}\times10^{-6}=10^{-3}$ J, so the unit of $\dfrac{pV}{T}$ is $10^{-3}$ J per kelvin. The six values are:

$$166.3,\;166.1,\;166.4,\;166.2,\;166.3,\;161.2\quad(10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}).$$

(M1)(A1) Excluding the last row, the mean is $166.3\times10^{-3}$ J per kelvin. Since $\dfrac{pV}{T}=nR$, the amount of gas is

$$n=\frac{166.3\times10^{-3}}{8.31}=2.00\times10^{-2}\text{ mol}.$$

(M1)(A1)

(b) For the first row, the fractional uncertainties are $\dfrac{1}{100}=1.0\%$ in the pressure, $\dfrac{1}{499}=0.20\%$ in the volume and $\dfrac{0.5}{300}=0.17\%$ in the temperature. (M1)(A1) Adding these in quadrature,

$$\sqrt{1.0^{2}+0.20^{2}+0.17^{2}}=\sqrt{1.00+0.040+0.029}=1.0\%.$$

(A1)

(c) The last row is the outlier: its value of $161.2\times10^{-3}$ J per kelvin lies about $3\%$ below the others, against a measurement uncertainty of about $0.6\%$ for that row, so the discrepancy is several times larger than the uncertainty and cannot be put down to precision. (M1)(A1) The temperature that would bring it into line is $T=\dfrac{pV}{nR}=\dfrac{200\times332\times10^{-3}}{8.31\times2.00\times10^{-2}}=399$ K, so about $400$ K rather than $412$ K. (A1)

(d) The most likely cause is that the thermometer was not measuring the temperature of the gas. (A1) If the bulb or probe was resting outside the water bath, in the laboratory air, or in direct sunlight, or was touching the bench, then it reads something other than the gas temperature, and the recorded $412$ K is an over-read of about $12$ K. (A1) This is a systematic error, not a random one: it does not average out over repeats, and it grows with the setting, which is exactly what a sunlit probe would do as the water bath is heated. (A1) The change to the method is to immerse the temperature probe in the water bath alongside the syringe and allow enough time for thermal equilibrium before each reading, to shield the bath from direct radiation, and to stir the bath so that it is isothermal; a further improvement is to read the temperature with the probe in the bath rather than from a sensor clipped to the outside of the syringe. (A1)

Markscheme notes

In (a) allow values within one or two units in the last place and accept the unit expressed as kPa cubic centimetres per kelvin, which is equivalent. The amount of gas should be reported as $2.00\times10^{-2}$ mol; allow $0.0200$ to $0.0201$. In (b) allow the uncertainties to be added linearly, giving about $1.4\%$, provided the method is stated; the quadrature answer is $1.0\%$. In (c) the justification must compare the discrepancy with the uncertainty, not merely point at the row that looks different, and the corrected temperature should be $399$ to $400$ K. In (d) credit any physically sensible cause that is consistent with a temperature over-read, but do not credit 'human error' or 'the reading was wrong'; the improvement must address the cause that has been named. A candidate who suggests that the gas leaked should be asked where the gas went, since the syringe is sealed and the other five rows are consistent. A further condonation is worth recording. Some candidates will compute the invariant as $pV$ against $T$ and attempt a gradient through the origin; this is a perfectly good alternative and should be credited in full provided the last row is excluded from the fit and that exclusion is justified, because a best-fit line through all six points is dragged by the outlier and gives an amount of substance about one per cent low. Others will convert volumes to cubic metres at the start; this is more laborious and introduces more rounding, but is not wrong. What should not be credited is a conclusion reached by eye.

Why this question is hard

This item is built as a deliberate contrast with the two data-based questions elsewhere in the bank, both of which ask the student to linearise a relationship and take a gradient. Here there is nothing to plot: the invariant $pV/T$ can be formed directly from each row, so the question tests whether a student recognises an invariant when there is no graph to draw, and whether they know what to do with it afterwards. The real discrimination is in (b) and (c) together. Computing the combined uncertainty is routine; the hard step is using it to decide whether the last row is anomalous or merely imprecise, and that decision cannot be made without the number from (b). A candidate who simply says the row looks wrong has skipped the reasoning entirely. Part (d) then asks for a cause that is consistent with the direction of the error, and the answer has to be about the thermometer rather than about the gas, because the five consistent rows show the gas is behaving properly. The pedagogical point worth carrying forward is that a systematic error in one instrument shows up in every derived quantity and does not improve with repetition, which is why the fix is a change of method rather than more readings. There is a second lesson hiding in the table, and it is worth pointing out to a class that has finished. The pressure readings rise in equal steps of 20 kPa, so the experimenter has varied the independent variable uniformly; that is reasonable but not optimal, because the percentage uncertainty in pressure is largest at the low-pressure end, where it is a full one per cent, and smallest at the top, where it is half that. Spacing the settings so that the percentage uncertainty is roughly constant, or simply taking more readings where the uncertainty is worst, would improve the test at no cost. Candidates who have internalised that uncertainties, and not the readings themselves, should drive the design of an experiment will suggest it without being asked, and the suggestion is a reliable marker of the top grade boundary.
