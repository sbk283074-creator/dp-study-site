C.4 Standing waves on a string fixed at both ends

Physics HL · PHYS-C.4-102

Question

A string of length 1.20 m and mass 3.60 g is fixed at both ends and set vibrating by a small oscillator attached at one end. The figure shows the mode of vibration that is produced when the oscillator is driven at 240 Hz. The string is under tension and vibrates in the plane of the page. The mass of the string is small compared with the masses that maintain the tension, so the tension may be taken as constant. Answer all parts with reference to the figure, and give numerical answers to three significant figures.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Determine the wavelength of the waves travelling along the string in this mode of vibration.

    (Determine)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Calculate the speed of the transverse waves travelling along the string.

    (Calculate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Calculate the tension in the string, stating the value of the mass per unit length that you use.

    (Calculate)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    Determine the frequency of the fundamental mode of this string.

    (Determine)
  5. [4 marks](e)

    The oscillator frequency is now changed to 320 Hz while the tension is kept the same. Determine the harmonic that is produced, and explain why this frequency, and no neighbouring frequency, can produce a standing wave on this string.

    (Explain)

Reveal the answer

(a) The string is fixed at both ends, so both ends must be nodes and the length of the string must contain a whole number of half-wavelengths. (M1) Counting the antinodes in the figure gives three, so the string holds three half-wavelengths and $3\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right) = 1.20$ m. Therefore $\lambda = \frac{2 \times 1.20}{3} = 0.800$ m. (A1) The nodes lie at the two ends and at 0.400 m and 0.800 m from one end, which is what the figure shows.

(b) The wave equation for a travelling wave is $v = f\lambda$, and the frequency of the standing wave equals the frequency of the travelling waves that compose it, so $v = 240 \times 0.800 = 192$ m s$^{-1}$. (M1)(A1) This is the speed at which a disturbance travels along the string, not the speed of any material point of the string, whose motion is transverse and much slower.

(c) The mass per unit length of the string is $\mu = \frac{m}{L} = \frac{3.60 \times 10^{-3}}{1.20} = 3.00 \times 10^{-3}$ kg m$^{-1}$. (M1) For a stretched string the wave speed is related to the tension by $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$, so $T = \mu v^2$. (M1) Substituting, $T = (3.00 \times 10^{-3})(192)^2 = (3.00 \times 10^{-3})(3.6864 \times 10^4) = 111$ N. (A1) A tension of about a hundred newtons is plausible for a string weighing a few grams, which is a useful check on the arithmetic.

(d) The fundamental mode is the longest wavelength the string can support, with a single antinode and one half-wavelength across the fixed length. (M1) Hence $\lambda_1 = 2L = 2.40$ m and $f_1 = \frac{v}{\lambda_1} = \frac{192}{2.40} = 80.0$ Hz. (A1) Equivalently, the mode shown is the third harmonic, so $f_1 = \frac{240}{3} = 80.0$ Hz, and the two routes must agree.

(e) The standing waves possible on a string fixed at both ends have frequencies $f_n = \frac{nv}{2L}$, which are the integer multiples of the fundamental 80.0 Hz. (M1) Since $\frac{320}{80.0} = 4$ exactly, the driving frequency coincides with the fourth harmonic, so a standing wave with four half-wavelengths and four antinodes is produced. (A1) At any other frequency the waves reflected from the fixed ends return out of phase with the driver. (M1) The reflected wave then interferes destructively at the driver instead of reinforcing it, so the amplitude does not build up and only a small forced vibration remains. (A1) Resonance requires the round trip along the string to be a whole number of wavelengths, which is exactly the condition that the driving frequency be a harmonic; a frequency a few hertz away would leave the string forced but not resonant.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for using the fixed-end boundary condition, either by counting three half-wavelengths in the figure or by writing $L = n\lambda/2$ with $n = 3$; A1 for 0.800 m. A candidate who answers 0.400 m has read the figure as three whole wavelengths rather than three half-wavelengths, and scores 0 of 2. (b) M1 for using $v = f\lambda$, A1 for 192 m s$^{-1}$; follow through from the candidate's own wavelength in (a). (c) M1 for the linear density in kg m$^{-1}$, M1 for $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ rearranged or quoted correctly, A1 for 111 N. A candidate who uses the mass in grams obtains $1.11 \times 10^5$ N and scores 1 of 3. Accept 110.6 N to 111 N. (d) M1 for $\lambda_1 = 2L$ or for dividing the third-harmonic frequency by three, A1 for 80.0 Hz. Accept a route through $f_1 = f_3/3$. (e) M1 for stating that only integer multiples of the fundamental are supported; A1 for identifying the fourth harmonic, with the ratio $320/80$ shown; M1 for explaining the phase condition between the reflected wave and the driver; A1 for a conclusion that makes clear that a non-harmonic frequency produces forced vibration without resonance. Do not award the final A1 for a statement that the string would vibrate at the driving frequency regardless.

Why this question is hard

The figure gives the shape of a mode, not a measurement, so the first thing to do is to read it as a boundary-condition statement. Both ends are fixed, so both are nodes, and the number of half-wavelengths that fit between them is an integer. Counting the antinodes in the figure gives three, so the string holds three half-wavelengths, and since the string is 1.20 m long each half-wavelength is 0.400 m and the wavelength is 0.800 m. A candidate who reads three wavelengths instead of three half-wavelengths gets 0.400 m, which then makes the speed wrong by a factor of two.

With the wavelength and the driving frequency the wave equation gives the speed immediately, and with the speed and the linear density it gives the tension. The linear density is where the units do the damage: the string has a mass of 3.60 g, which is $3.60 \times 10^{-3}$ kg, and dividing by 1.20 m gives $3.00 \times 10^{-3}$ kg m$^{-1}$. Using grams throughout gives a tension a hundred thousand times too large, which is the sort of answer a candidate should notice is absurd for a string.

Part (d) reverses the harmonic series. The mode that is drawn is the third harmonic at 240 Hz, so the fundamental is 240/3 = 80.0 Hz, and the string can vibrate at 80, 160, 240, 320 Hz and so on.

Part (e) is the conceptual centre of the item. The temptation is to assume that a string driven at any frequency will vibrate at that frequency, which is true of the forced vibration but not of the standing wave. A standing wave requires the waves reflected from the ends to return in phase with the driver, and that happens only when the round-trip path length is a whole number of wavelengths. Here 320 Hz is exactly four times the fundamental, so it is the fourth harmonic and a standing wave with four antinodes appears. A frequency of 310 Hz would force the string to move, but the reflected waves would arrive out of phase, the amplitude would not build up, and no stable standing wave would form.
