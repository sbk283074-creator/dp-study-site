P3 extended problem: bounding a series by term-wise telescoping

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-017

Topic 1: Number and algebraAHL 1.15 (proof by mathematical induction), SL 1.6 (simple deductive proof), AHL 1.8 (sum of infinite convergent geometric sequences)P314 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatHenceCalculate

Question

For an integer $n \ge 1$ write $S_n = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{k^{2}}$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that, for every integer $k \ge 3$,
    $$\frac{1}{k^{2}} < \frac{1}{k - \frac{1}{2}} - \frac{1}{k + \frac{1}{2}}.$$

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Hence show that $S_n < \dfrac{7}{4}$ for every integer $n \ge 1$.

    (Hence)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Show that the alternative estimate $\dfrac{1}{k^{2}} < \dfrac{1}{k(k-1)}$, valid for $k \ge 2$, gives $S_n < 2 - \dfrac{1}{n}$, and explain why this estimate cannot establish the bound in part (b) for $n \ge 4$.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Calculate $S_{20}$ correct to six decimal places, and verify that it is consistent both with the bound proved in part (b) and with the tighter value $\dfrac{33}{20} = 1.65$ that your proof in part (b) actually establishes.

    (Calculate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

For an integer $n \ge 1$ write $S_n = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{k^{2}}$. (a) Show that, for every integer $k \ge 3$, $$\frac{1}{k^{2}} < \frac{1}{k - \frac{1}{2}} - \frac{1}{k + \frac{1}{2}}.$$ [4 marks] (Show that) (b) Hence show that $S_n < \dfrac{7}{4}$ for every integer $n \ge 1$. [4 marks] (Hence) (c) Show that the alternative estimate $\dfrac{1}{k^{2}} < \dfrac{1}{k(k-1)}$, valid for $k \ge 2$, gives $S_n < 2 - \dfrac{1}{n}$, and explain why this estimate cannot establish the bound in part (b) for $n \ge 4$. [3 marks] (Show that) (d) Calculate $S_{20}$ correct to six decimal places, and verify that it is consistent both with the bound proved in part (b) and with the tighter value $\dfrac{33}{20} = 1.65$ that your proof in part (b) actually establishes. [3 marks] (Calculate)
Reveal the answer

(a) Combining the two fractions on the right-hand side over a common denominator,
$$\frac{1}{k - \frac{1}{2}} - \frac{1}{k + \frac{1}{2}} = \frac{\left(k + \frac12\right) - \left(k - \frac12\right)}{\left(k - \frac12\right)\left(k + \frac12\right)} = \frac{1}{k^{2} - \frac14}$$ (M1) (A1).
Since $k \ge 3$ we have $k^{2} - \tfrac14 < k^{2}$, and both quantities are positive, so taking reciprocals reverses the inequality (M1):
$$\frac{1}{k^{2}} < \frac{1}{k^{2} - \frac14} = \frac{1}{k - \frac{1}{2}} - \frac{1}{k + \frac{1}{2}}$$ (A1).

(b) For $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ we have $S_1 = 1$ and $S_2 = 1 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac54 = 1.25$, both less than $\tfrac74 = 1.75$ (A1).

Now let $n \ge 3$. By part (a),
$$\sum_{k=3}^{n} \frac{1}{k^{2}} < \sum_{k=3}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{k - \frac12} - \frac{1}{k + \frac12}\right)$$ (M1).
The right-hand sum telescopes: writing out the first few terms, $\left(\frac{1}{2.5} - \frac{1}{3.5}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{3.5} - \frac{1}{4.5}\right) + \cdots + \left(\frac{1}{n - 0.5} - \frac{1}{n + 0.5}\right)$, every intermediate term cancels (M1), leaving
$$\sum_{k=3}^{n} \frac{1}{k^{2}} < \frac{1}{\frac52} - \frac{1}{n + \frac12} = \frac{2}{5} - \frac{1}{n + \frac12} < \frac{2}{5} = 0.4$$ (A1).
Therefore
$$S_n = 1 + \frac14 + \sum_{k=3}^{n}\frac{1}{k^{2}} < 1 + \frac14 + \frac25 = \frac{20 + 5 + 8}{20} = \frac{33}{20} = 1.65 < \frac74$$ (A1) (AG).
Since $\tfrac{33}{20} < \tfrac74$, this establishes $S_n < \tfrac74$ for every $n \ge 3$, and the cases $n = 1, 2$ were settled separately, so the bound holds for every integer $n \ge 1$ (AG).

(c) For $k \ge 2$ we have $k(k-1) < k^{2}$, so $\dfrac{1}{k^{2}} < \dfrac{1}{k(k-1)}$ (A1). Also $\dfrac{1}{k(k-1)} = \dfrac{1}{k-1} - \dfrac{1}{k}$, so
$$\sum_{k=2}^{n} \frac{1}{k^{2}} < \sum_{k=2}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{k-1} - \frac{1}{k}\right) = 1 - \frac{1}{n}$$ (M1),
the sum telescoping to $\left(1 - \frac12\right) + \left(\frac12 - \frac13\right) + \cdots + \left(\frac{1}{n-1} - \frac{1}{n}\right)$. Hence $S_n < 1 + 1 - \dfrac{1}{n} = 2 - \dfrac{1}{n}$ (A1).

But $2 - \dfrac{1}{n} \ge \dfrac74$ exactly when $\dfrac{1}{n} \le \dfrac14$, that is when $n \ge 4$. So for $n \ge 4$ this estimate bounds $S_n$ only by a quantity that is at least $\tfrac74$, and therefore cannot show $S_n < \tfrac74$ (AG). The estimate is not wrong -- it is a valid upper bound -- it is simply too weak: every estimate of the form $\dfrac{1}{k^{2}} < \dfrac{1}{(k-a)(k-b)}$ with $ab = 0$ produces a telescoping sum whose total is exactly $2$, and no member of that family can reach $1.75$.

(d) Summing directly,
$$S_{20} = 1 + \frac14 + \frac19 + \cdots + \frac{1}{400} = 1.5961632439\ldots$$ (M1) (A1), so $S_{20} = 1.596163$ to six decimal places (A1). This is consistent with the two bounds: $1.596163 < 1.65 < 1.75$ (AG). Note that the proof of part (b) gives the stronger bound $\tfrac{33}{20}$, and the true limit of the series is $\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{6} = 1.644934\ldots$, which also lies below both bounds, so the numerical value is consistent with the theory as it must be.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for combining the fractions, (A1) for the simplified form $1/(k^{2}-\tfrac14)$, (M1) for the observation that $k^{2}-\tfrac14 < k^{2}$, and (A1) for reversing the inequality correctly. The reversal is the point of the part: a candidate who writes $1/k^{2} > 1/(k^{2}-\tfrac14)$ has the inequality the wrong way round and forfeits the last (A1). Do not accept a numerical check at $k = 3$ as a proof for all $k \ge 3$.

(b) The cases $n = 1, 2$ must be handled separately, because the telescoping identity of part (a) requires $k \ge 3$; a candidate who applies the telescoping sum from $k = 1$ and writes $\frac{1}{0.5} - \frac{1}{1.5} = 2 - \frac23$ for the first term forfeits the (A1) for the base cases and will obtain a wrong constant. Award (M1) for summing the inequality, (M1) for the telescoping cancellation, (A1) for the value $2/5$, and (A1) for the final constant. The value $\tfrac{33}{20}$ need not be simplified to a decimal, but the comparison with $\tfrac74$ must be made explicitly: the question asks for $\tfrac74$, and obtaining a different (larger) bound without comparing it to $\tfrac74$ is not an answer.

(c) Award (A1) for the comparison $k(k-1) < k^{2}$, (M1) for the telescoping sum, (A1) for $2 - \tfrac1n$, and (AG) for the inequality $2 - \tfrac1n \ge \tfrac74 \iff n \ge 4$. The explanation must identify the *reason* the estimate fails -- that its bound tends to $2$ and so cannot ever certify a target below $2$ -- and not merely state that $2 - \tfrac1n$ is larger than $\tfrac74$ at $n = 4$.

(d) Award (M1) for an explicit summation, (A1) for the value to six decimal places, and (A1) for the comparison. Accept the value obtained from a calculator, provided the six decimal places are stated correctly; a candidate who reports $1.5961632$ (seven places) has not answered to the required accuracy but should not be penalised heavily. Follow-through applies to a candidate whose constant in part (b) differs from $\tfrac{33}{20}$: the comparison must then be made against their own constant.

Why this question is hard

This problem is about a specific and very common failure of reasoning: having found a bound that is true, concluding that it is the bound you need. The estimate $\dfrac{1}{k^{2}} < \dfrac{1}{k(k-1)}$ is the standard one, it appears in every textbook treatment of the convergence of this series, and it works perfectly for its usual purpose -- showing that the series converges, since it gives $S_n < 2 - 1/n < 2$. What it cannot do is show $S_n < \tfrac74$, because it is an estimate whose total is exactly $2$ and $\tfrac74$ is smaller than $2$. No amount of careful algebra applied to that estimate will produce the target, and this is the sense in which the constraint binds.

The escape is to compare against a slightly wider denominator. Instead of $k(k-1)$ one uses $k^{2} - \tfrac14 = \left(k - \tfrac12\right)\left(k + \tfrac12\right)$, which is larger than $k^{2}$ and still splits into two telescoping pieces. The gain is not large -- the tail from $k = 3$ onwards is bounded by $\tfrac25$ instead of $1$ -- but it is exactly enough, and it is enough because the first two terms are handled exactly rather than estimated. That combination, a sharp estimate where it is available and exact arithmetic where the estimate does not apply, is the transferable technique.

The restriction $k \ge 3$ is not incidental. At $k = 1$ the comparison $1/(k^{2}-\tfrac14) > 1/k^{2}$ would give $\tfrac{1}{0.75} = \tfrac43 > 1$, which is a much weaker statement than the trivial equality $1/1^{2} = 1$; at $k = 2$ it would give $\tfrac{1}{3.75} = 0.2667$ against the exact $0.25$. So the sharpened estimate is worse than useless at the start of the series and essential later, and knowing where to switch from exact values to estimates is the real content of part (b). A candidate who applies the new estimate from $k = 1$ obtains a bound larger than $\tfrac74$ and concludes, wrongly, that the method has failed.

Part (d) is a reality check. The true sum is $\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{6} = 1.6449\ldots$, comfortably below $1.65$, so the bound proved here is not merely valid but reasonably tight -- the gap is about $0.005$. Seeing that the bound is nearly attained is what makes the earlier failure of the $k(k-1)$ estimate understandable rather than mysterious: the true value sits so close to $\tfrac{33}{20}$ that any estimate with slack of order $0.35$ was never going to be good enough.

Challenge lever
The series of reciprocal squares has no elementary closed form, so the only available route is comparison, and the standard comparison is available to every prepared student: 1/k^2 < 1/(k(k-1)) telescopes to give the partial sum below 2 - 1/n. That estimate is correct, it is the one the student has been taught, and it is not strong enough for the target 7/4, because 2 - 1/n exceeds 7/4 as soon as n reaches 4. The constraint binds: the target is tighter than the whole family of estimates of the obvious type, so the student must find a different comparison, valid only from k = 3, and combine it with exact values for the first two terms. Part (c) makes the failure of the standard estimate an explicit object of study rather than something the student has to notice.
