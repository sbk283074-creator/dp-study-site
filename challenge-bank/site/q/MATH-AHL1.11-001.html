Partial fractions that do not telescope in the obvious form

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.11-001

AHL 1.11 (partial fractions)P1 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatHenceWrite downDetermine

Question

For each integer $n\geq1$, let $u_n=\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $u_n$ can be written in the form $\dfrac{A}{n}+\dfrac{B}{n+1}+\dfrac{C}{n+2}$ and state the values of $A$, $B$ and $C$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Hence show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_n=\dfrac14-\dfrac{1}{2(N+1)(N+2)}$ for every positive integer $N$.

    (Show that)
  3. [2 marks](c)

    Hence write down the value of the infinite sum $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_n$.

    (Write down)
  4. [5 marks](d)

    Hence determine the value of $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}$.

    (Determine)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Write $\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}=\dfrac{A}{n}+\dfrac{B}{n+1}+\dfrac{C}{n+2}$. Multiplying through by $n(n+1)(n+2)$ gives

$$1=A(n+1)(n+2)+Bn(n+2)+Cn(n+1).$$ (M1)

Setting $n=0$: $1=2A$, so $A=\tfrac12$. Setting $n=-1$: $1=(-1)(1)B$, so $B=-1$. Setting $n=-2$: $1=(-2)(-1)C$, so $C=\tfrac12$. (A1)(A1)

Hence $u_n=\dfrac{1}{2n}-\dfrac{1}{n+1}+\dfrac{1}{2(n+2)}$. (A1) *(AG)*

(b) The three-term form does not telescope cleanly, so regroup it. Note that

$$\frac{1}{n(n+1)}-\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac{(n+2)-n}{n(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac{2}{n(n+1)(n+2)}.$$ (M1)

Therefore

$$u_n=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{n(n+1)}-\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}\right),$$ (A1)

a difference of two consecutive values of $w_n=\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)}$. Summing,

$$\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_n=\frac12\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(w_n-w_{n+1}\right)=\frac12\left(w_1-w_{N+1}\right)$$ (M1)

$$=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{1\cdot2}-\frac{1}{(N+1)(N+2)}\right)=\frac14-\frac{1}{2(N+1)(N+2)}.$$ (A1) *(AG)*

(c) As $N\to\infty$ the fraction $\dfrac{1}{2(N+1)(N+2)}$ tends to $0$, so the tail disappears. (A1) Hence $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_n=\frac14$. (A1)

(d) Let $v_n=\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}$. Use the same device one order higher, taking the difference of two consecutive blocks of three factors:

$$\frac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}-\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}=\frac{(n+3)-n}{n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}=\frac{3}{n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}.$$ (M1)

Hence $v_n=\dfrac13\left(\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}-\dfrac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}\right)$. (A1) Summing from $n=1$ to $N$ telescopes to

$$\sum_{n=1}^{N}v_n=\frac13\left(\frac{1}{1\cdot2\cdot3}-\frac{1}{(N+1)(N+2)(N+3)}\right).$$ (M1)

As $N\to\infty$ the second term vanishes, so $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}v_n=\frac13\cdot\frac16=\frac{1}{18}$. (A1)(A1)

Edge-case checks. In (b), at $N=1$ the formula gives $\tfrac14-\tfrac1{12}=\tfrac16$, matching the single term $u_{1}=\tfrac16$. In (d), at $N=1$ it gives $\tfrac13\!\left(\tfrac16-\tfrac{1}{24}\right)=\tfrac1{24}=v_{1}$; only this first term survives as $N\to\infty$.

Markscheme notes

(a) The cover-up (substitution) method is intended, but credit any correct route, including solving the resulting $3\times3$ system. The *(AG)* requires the three coefficients to be exhibited and the identity stated; a candidate who gives $A=\tfrac12$, $B=-1$, $C=\tfrac12$ without restating the identity still earns the accuracy marks.

(b) This is where the marks are won or lost. The three-term form from (a) does telescope, but only with careful bookkeeping of the $\tfrac12$ coefficients; a candidate who telescopes it directly and correctly should receive full credit. The cleaner route is the regrouping displayed above, in which $u_n$ is the difference of two consecutive values of $w_n=\frac{1}{n(n+1)}$. Award (M1) for the regrouping, (A1) for the constant $\tfrac12$, (M1) for recognising the telescoping, and (A1) for the closed form. Condone $N\geq1$ written as $N>0$.

(c) The two (A1) marks are for taking the limit and for the value $\frac14$. A candidate who substitutes $N=\infty$ without comment keeps both marks.

(d) The same construction one order higher: the difference of two consecutive *triple* blocks has numerator $3$, giving the factor $\tfrac13$. Follow-through applies from (b): a candidate whose method is structurally correct but whose constant is wrong keeps the two method marks. The common errors are using $\tfrac12$ instead of $\tfrac13$, and telescoping over the wrong block, which leaves the wrong first term. The final answer $\frac{1}{18}$ follows from $\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac16$.

Why this question is hard

The question looks like a routine partial-fractions exercise, and part (a) is exactly that. The difficulty begins in part (b), and it is a difficulty of structure rather than of algebra.

The three-term decomposition produced in (a) is correct, but it is not the form in which the sum collapses. Summing $\frac{1}{2n}-\frac{1}{n+1}+\frac{1}{2(n+2)}$ from $n=1$ to $N$ does telescope, but three interleaved sequences have to be tracked and the boundary terms are easy to mis-assign. The intended observation is that the product $n(n+1)(n+2)$ can be split as a difference of two consecutive blocks, $\frac{1}{n(n+1)}-\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}$. This is one quantity evaluated at $n$ and at $n+1$, so the sum over $n$ collapses at once to first term minus last term.

This is the lever. The student must construct a decomposition that part (a) did not ask for, and must recognise that a partial-fraction decomposition and a telescoping decomposition are not the same thing. Many candidates never make this distinction and grind through the three-term sum, usually losing a boundary term.

Part (d) tests whether the construction was understood or merely memorised. The four-factor denominator needs the same idea one order up: the difference of two consecutive blocks of three factors has numerator $3$, so the constant is $\tfrac13$, and the surviving first term is $\frac{1}{1\cdot2\cdot3}=\frac16$. A candidate who has memorised the answer $\frac14$ will reach for a similar-looking fraction and produce nonsense.

The classic wrong turns are: treating the three-term form as already telescoping and dropping the $n+2$ tail; forgetting that the first term is the only survivor as $N\to\infty$; and, in (d), reusing the constant $\tfrac12$ from part (b). A useful check is the general identity $\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{1}{n(n+1)\cdots(n+k)}=\frac{1}{k\cdot k!}$; for $k=2$ it gives $\frac14$ and for $k=3$ it gives $\frac{1}{18}$, both confirmed by the parts above.
