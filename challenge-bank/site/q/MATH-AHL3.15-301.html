3.15 Two aircraft whose flight paths intersect at one point but who are never there at the same instant: the same-parameter test gives no solution and misleads

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL3.15-301

Topic 3: Geometry and trigonometryAHL 3.14 (vector equation of a line in two and three dimensions) with AHL 3.15 (coincident, parallel, intersecting and skew lines)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedCalculateShow thatDetermineDetermine

Question

Two aircraft, A and B, fly at constant velocities along straight paths. Positions are measured in kilometres east, north and up from a control tower at the origin, and time $t$ is measured in minutes after both aircraft pass their respective starting points. Their position vectors are
$$\mathbf{r}_{A} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} + t\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\\2\end{pmatrix} \quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf{r}_{B} = \begin{pmatrix}13\\-5\\4\end{pmatrix} + s\begin{pmatrix}-2\\4\\1\end{pmatrix},$$
where $t$ is the time for aircraft A and $s$ is the time for aircraft B. Both start at $t = 0$ and $s = 0$ respectively, and start at the same instant. Speeds are constant and the two paths are straight over the interval considered.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the exact speed of each aircraft in kilometres per minute, and hence decide which aircraft is travelling faster.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine whether the two flight paths intersect, by allowing the parameters $t$ and $s$ to take independent values. If they do intersect, find the coordinates of the point of intersection and the time at which each aircraft reaches it.

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine whether the two aircraft collide, and justify your answer by comparing the two times found in part (b). If they do not collide, state what change to the speed of aircraft B would make them collide at the point found in part (b).

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Write down an expression for the separation of the two aircraft at a single common time $t$, and hence determine the time at which the separation is least and the value of that least separation.

    (Determine)
  5. [2 marks](e)

    Explain why writing $\mathbf{r}_{A}(t) = \mathbf{r}_{B}(t)$ with a single parameter was not the correct test for whether the paths intersect, and say what question that single-parameter test does answer.

    (Explain)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The velocity vector of A is $(3,\,1,\,2)$, so its speed is
$$\lvert \mathbf{v}_{A} \rvert = \sqrt{3^{2} + 1^{2} + 2^{2}} = \sqrt{14} = 3.74 \;\text{km}\,\text{min}^{-1} \;(M1)(A1),$$
and for B, with velocity $(-2,\,4,\,1)$,
$$\lvert \mathbf{v}_{B} \rvert = \sqrt{4 + 16 + 1} = \sqrt{21} = 4.58 \;\text{km}\,\text{min}^{-1} \;(A1),$$
so aircraft B is travelling faster.
(b) Allowing $t$ and $s$ to vary independently and equating components,
$$3t = 13 - 2s, \qquad t = -5 + 4s, \qquad 2t = 4 + s \;(M1).$$
From the second equation $t = 4s - 5$. Substituting into the third,
$$2(4s - 5) = 4 + s \;\Longrightarrow\; 8s - 10 = 4 + s \;\Longrightarrow\; 7s = 14 \;\Longrightarrow\; s = 2 \;(A1),$$
whence $t = 4(2) - 5 = 3$. Checking these in the first equation, which has not yet been used,
$$3t = 3(3) = 9 \quad\text{and}\quad 13 - 2s = 13 - 4 = 9 \;(A1),$$
which are equal, so the three equations are consistent and the paths do intersect. The point is found from either position vector:
$$\mathbf{r}_{A}(3) = \begin{pmatrix}9\\3\\6\end{pmatrix} \quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf{r}_{B}(2) = \begin{pmatrix}13 - 4\\-5 + 8\\4 + 2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}9\\3\\6\end{pmatrix} \;(A1),$$
so the paths intersect at $(9,\,3,\,6)$, which aircraft A reaches at time $t = 3$ minutes and aircraft B at time $s = 2$ minutes.
(c) The aircraft collide only if they are at the same point at the same instant, which requires $t = s$. Here the times are $3$ and $2$ minutes respectively, and $3 \neq 2$, so the aircraft do not collide (A1) even though their paths cross. For aircraft B to arrive at $(9,\,3,\,6)$ at time $t = 3$ it must cover its fixed distance to that point three times as slowly, so its speed must be multiplied by the factor
$$\frac{2}{3} \;(M1)(A1),$$
giving a speed of $\tfrac{2}{3}\sqrt{21} = 3.06$ km min$^{-1}$. (Equivalently, B must delay its departure by one minute, or follow the same path at any speed such that it arrives three minutes after A starts.)
(d) At a single common instant both parameters equal the same value $t$, so the separation vector is
$$\mathbf{w}(t) = \mathbf{r}_{B}(t) - \mathbf{r}_{A}(t) = \begin{pmatrix}13 - 2t \\ -5 + 4t \\ 4 + t\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}3t\\t\\2t\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}13 - 5t\\ -5 + 3t\\ 4 - t\end{pmatrix} \;(M1).$$
The squared separation, which is minimised at the same $t$ as the separation, is
$$\begin{aligned} \lvert\mathbf{w}\rvert^{2} &= (13 - 5t)^{2} + (3t - 5)^{2} + (4 - t)^{2} \\ &= \bigl(169 - 130t + 25t^{2}\bigr) + \bigl(9t^{2} - 30t + 25\bigr) + \bigl(t^{2} - 8t + 16\bigr) \\ &= 35t^{2} - 168t + 210 \;(A1). \end{aligned}$$
Since this is a quadratic in $t$ with positive leading coefficient, its minimum occurs at
$$t = \frac{168}{2 \times 35} = \frac{168}{70} = 2.4 \;\text{minutes} \;(A1),$$
at which the separation vector is $\mathbf{w} = (1,\,2.2,\,1.6)$ and
$$\lvert\mathbf{w}\rvert^{2} = 1 + 4.84 + 2.56 = 8.4, \quad \lvert\mathbf{w}\rvert = \sqrt{8.4} = 2.90 \;\text{km} \;(A1),$$
to three significant figures. Note that the least separation is not zero: zero is the distance between the two infinite lines, whereas this is the least distance between the two aircraft sharing one clock.
(e) $\mathbf{r}_{A}(t) = \mathbf{r}_{B}(t)$ uses one time variable, so it asks whether the two aircraft are at the same point at the *same* instant -- that is, whether they collide (M1). Whether their paths intersect is a question about the two infinite straight lines, which is settled by allowing two independent parameters, as in part (b); the lines here meet even though the aircraft never do (A1).

This last mark requires both times: a candidate who finds the point but not the times loses it, because part (c) turns on exactly that comparison. Follow-through: a candidate whose algebra gives another consistent pair of times keeps every mark here provided the check on the unused equation is carried out.
(c) (A1) for the conclusion that they do not collide, with the times $3$ and $2$ identified as the reason, (M1) for reasoning that B must cover the same fixed distance in three minutes instead of two, (A1) for the factor $\tfrac{2}{3}$ or an equivalent statement such as a one-minute delay. Accept any equivalent sincere answer: a speed of $\tfrac{2}{3}\sqrt{21}$, a delay of one minute, or a statement that B must reduce its speed by one third. Forfeits: answering 'no, because the paths do not cross', which contradicts part (b).
(d) (M1) for forming the separation vector using a single shared time, (A1) for the correct quadratic $35t^{2} - 168t + 210$, (A1) for $t = 2.4$ minutes, (A1) for $2.90$ km. Accept a candidate who minimises by completing the square or by using a calculator rather than differentiating. Follow-through applies to a wrong but quadratic expression: if the minimum yields a negative squared separation, that is a signal the expansion is wrong and no further marks are available until it is corrected. Note that the minimum separation is not zero even though the paths intersect -- the mark is awarded for a value near $2.9$ km, and a candidate who returns zero has minimised over two parameters rather than one.
(e) (M1) for identifying that the single-parameter test governs simultaneity rather than intersection, (A1) for stating that the lines meet although the aircraft do not. Accept 'it tests whether they collide' as the second half, provided it is linked to the single time variable.

Why this question is hard

The whole item turns on one suppressed step: choosing how many time variables to introduce. It feels like notation rather than mathematics, which is exactly why it is missed. Two people asked whether two straight lines meet will happily write two different parameters; the same two people asked about two moving aircraft will reach for one, because 'the time' reads as a single thing when both start together.

The consequence is asymmetric and worth stating plainly. With two parameters the system is three equations in two unknowns, so it is over-determined and consistency has to be checked; here it is consistent, and the extra unused equation becomes a genuine verification. With one parameter it is three equations in one unknown, and an inconsistency means only that the two are not coincident at a shared instant -- which everyone already knew, and which says nothing whatever about the geometry of the two lines.

Part (d) is where the two questions separate cleanly, and the arithmetic is a trap in itself. Because the paths intersect, the minimum distance between the two *lines* is zero, and that is the number a candidate is most likely to carry into this part. But the question asks for the minimum separation of two objects sharing a clock, so the minimisation runs over one parameter, not two, and the answer is $2.90$ km rather than zero. The expansion of the squared separation is deliberately fiddly -- three squared binomials, coefficients $35$, $168$ and $210$ -- so that the check against a negative value in the working above is not cosmetic. Any error in collecting the $t^{2}$ terms shows up as a negative squared length, which is a signal a candidate can act on.

Finally, part (c) is useful because it converts the abstract distinction into a number. Since B must cover a fixed distance in three minutes instead of two, its speed must fall to two thirds. The aircraft do not fail to collide for any interesting dynamical reason; they fail because their arrival times differ by one minute, and the fix is arithmetic. That makes concrete what 'same point, different time' means, and it is a good test of whether the candidate has actually separated the two questions or has merely produced a correct coordinate pair by algebra.

Challenge lever
Whether two moving objects occupy the same point is a question about one shared instant, so it needs one parameter; whether their paths cross is a question about two possibly different instants, so it needs two. Candidates collapse the two into one, set the two position vectors equal with the same parameter $t$, obtain three equations in one unknown with no simultaneous solution, and conclude that the paths do not cross. That conclusion is false: the paths meet at exactly one point, which one aircraft reaches three minutes after departure and the other two minutes after it. The constraint the candidate imposes -- identical times -- is not in the question, and imposing it destroys the quantity being asked about.

Adaptation
The source story is a collision question: two bodies, one shared time, decide whether they meet. Here that question is split deliberately into two, and the one the source asks is deferred to part (c) while part (b) asks a different one that needs two parameters. The context is rebuilt around a shared origin and a single added variable -- arrival time -- so that the paths cross and the bodies do not, which the source arrangement never produces. Both structures, the given-versus-asked, and the reasoning chain are changed; all vector data are original and were chosen so that the intersection point is reached at whole minute three and minute two, and the least separation comes out as the square root of eight point four.
Verification
Speeds were computed as the moduli of the two velocity vectors: sqrt(14) = 3.7417 and sqrt(21) = 4.5826. The three component equations were solved by substituting t = 4s - 5 into 2t = 4 + s, giving s = 2, t = 3, and the unused x-equation was confirmed as 9 = 9. Both position vectors were evaluated at their own times and agree at (9,3,6). The common-time separation vector was expanded twice after the first collection gave a negative squared length, arriving at 35t^2 - 168t + 210, whose minimum sits at t = 2.4 with value 8.4; the root was taken as 2.89828. The slowing factor 2/3 was checked by confirming (2/3)*3 = 2.
