A card game whose expected value hides in a parameter

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.14-001

AHL 4.14 (variance of a discrete random variable; E(X^2) - (E(X))^2), SL 4.7 (concept of discrete random variables and their probability distributions)P2 · Section A15 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatFindDetermine

Question

A bag contains five cards numbered $1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4$ and $k$, where $k$ is an integer greater than $4$. Two cards are drawn at random without replacement, and $S$ denotes the sum of the numbers on the two cards.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    The two cards are drawn at random and the sum is recorded. Show that the expected value of this sum is $\mathrm{E}(S)=4+\dfrac{2k}{5}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Find the least value of $k$ for which the expected value of $S$ exceeds $10$.

    (Find)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    For the case $k=10$, calculate the variance of the sum $S$, showing the values of $\mathrm{E}(S)$ and $\mathrm{E}(S^{2})$ that you use.

    (Calculate)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    For $k=10$ a player pays $9$ (in the currency units of the game) to play and receives the value of $S$. Determine whether the game favours the player, and find the probability that the player makes a profit.

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) There are $\dbinom{5}{2}=10$ equally likely pairs, so each has probability $0.1$. (M1)

The six pairs that avoid $k$ give sums $1+2=3$, $1+3=4$, $1+4=5$, $2+3=5$, $2+4=6$, $3+4=7$, whose total is $30$. (M1)

The four pairs containing $k$ give $(1+k)+(2+k)+(3+k)+(4+k)=10+4k$. (A1)

So $\displaystyle\sum S=30+10+4k=40+4k$ and $\mathrm{E}(S)=\dfrac{40+4k}{10}=4+\dfrac{2k}{5}$. (AG)

Check, by linearity of expectation. Each of the five values appears in $4$ of the $10$ pairs, so the two drawn cards have the same marginal distribution; its mean is $\dfrac{1+2+3+4+k}{5}=\dfrac{10+k}{5}$. Hence

$$\mathrm{E}(S)=2\times\frac{10+k}{5}=4+\frac{2k}{5}\quad\checkmark$$

(b) $4+\dfrac{2k}{5}>10\Rightarrow \dfrac{2k}{5}>6\Rightarrow k>15$. (M1)(A1) As $k$ is an integer, the least such value is $k=16$. (A1)

(c) With $k=10$ the ten sums are $3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 11,\ 5,\ 6,\ 12,\ 7,\ 13,\ 14$, so $\mathrm{E}(S)=\dfrac{80}{10}=8$. (M1)

$\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(S^{2})=\dfrac{9+16+25+121+25+36+144+49+169+196}{10}=\dfrac{790}{10}=79$. (M1)(A1)

$\mathrm{Var}(S)=\mathrm{E}(S^{2})-\left[\mathrm{E}(S)\right]^{2}=79-64=15$. (M1)(A1)

(d) $\mathrm{E}(S)=8<9$, so the player pays more on average than they receive and the game favours the organiser. (A1) The organiser's expected margin is $9-8=1$ unit per game, and over many plays the player's losses converge to that figure.

The expectation is therefore below the stake, and the arithmetic has not yet said anything about how often a player wins.

A profit requires $S>9$, that is $S\in\{11,12,13,14\}$, which occurs in $4$ of the $10$ equally likely outcomes. (M1) So $\mathrm{P}(\text{profit})=0.4$. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a): the (AG) requires the total to be exhibited, not merely asserted. A candidate who lists the distribution correctly earns both (M1) marks even if the algebra afterwards slips; do not condone an answer that treats the draw as ordered and then divides by 20 as well. Part (b): accept $k=15$ only if the candidate explicitly notes that the strict inequality excludes it — otherwise the answer mark is lost. Part (c): accept the grouped distribution $\mathrm{P}(S=5)=0.2$, $\mathrm{P}(S=3)=\mathrm{P}(S=4)=\mathrm{P}(S=6)=\mathrm{P}(S=7)=0.1$, $\mathrm{P}(S=11)=\mathrm{P}(S=13)=0.1$ and so on, provided the total probability is 1. Follow-through: a candidate whose $\mathrm{E}(S)$ from part (a) is wrong but consistent still earns the two method marks and the first accuracy mark in (c). A very common error is to compute $\mathrm{E}(S^{2})$ with $\mathrm{E}(S)^{2}$ substituted — this forfeits both (M1) marks in (c). In (d) accept a correct comparison of the expectation with the stake in either direction; the probability mark is independent of the judgement.

Why this question is hard

There is no distribution to look up. The random variable comes from a physical process, so the first task is combinatorial: ten equally likely pairs, four of which contain $k$ and six of which do not. The parameter then behaves in a way that rewards a student who keeps it symbolic rather than substituting early, because the pairs not containing $k$ contribute a constant $30$ and only the pair sums involving $k$ change.

Part (b) tests a small but real distinction: the inequality is strict, and since $k$ is an integer the answer is $16$ rather than $15$. This is the kind of boundary case in which a technically correct piece of algebra produces the wrong answer if the domain of $k$ is forgotten.

Part (c) is where most of the marks sit, and it is deliberately unglamorous. The distribution of $S$ is not uniform — the value $5$ occurs twice — and $\mathrm{E}(S^{2})$ must be formed from the ten ungrouped values or from a carefully grouped table. Students who reach for the binomial variance formula or who assume $\mathrm{Var}(S)=\mathrm{Var}(X_{1})+\mathrm{Var}(X_{2})$ for the two drawn cards get a wrong answer, because the draws are without replacement and therefore not independent. That is the classic wrong turn and the most useful thing in the question: it separates students who understand what variance of a sum requires from students who have memorised a formula.

The final part makes a point that matters beyond the examination. An expected value below the stake tells you the game is unfavourable on average, but it says nothing about how often a player wins: here a player profits 40% of the time and still loses money overall. That gap between a favourable expectation and a likely outcome is exactly the gap that makes gambling attractive, and a question that stops at '$8<9$, do not play' has missed half of it.

Metadata

Challenge lever
The distribution is never written down: it must be constructed from ten equally likely draws, and the parameter then has to be carried through the expectation before the variance can be evaluated at a specific value. The final part inverts the problem, asking what the expectation being below the stake does and does not tell you about the chance of profit.

Adaptation
Expected-value games are common in IB statistics, but they almost always supply the probability distribution as a table. Here the distribution must be built from a without-replacement draw, the expectation is required in terms of a parameter, and the final part is inverted to separate the mean from the probability of profit. The card set, the parameter k, the value k = 10 and the profit probability are original; every figure was recomputed in exact rational arithmetic.
Verification
All ten pair sums were enumerated exactly. E(S) = (40 + 4k)/10 was checked symbolically and at k = 10, giving 8. E(S^2) = 790/10 = 79 and Var(S) = 79 - 64 = 15, sd = 3.8730. P(S > 9) = 4/10.
