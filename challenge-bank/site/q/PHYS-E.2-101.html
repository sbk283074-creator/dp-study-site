E.1 / E.2 Photons, the photoelectric effect and matter waves

Physics HL · PHYS-E.2-101

Theme E: Nuclear and quantum physicsE.1 (Structure of the atom), E.2 (Quantum physics)P1 · Section A5 marksdifficulty 5technology: allowedCalculateDetermine

Question

Each question below is self-contained. Take the Planck constant as $6.63\times10^{-34}$ J s, the speed of light in vacuum as $3.00\times10^{8}$ m per second, the elementary charge as $1.60\times10^{-19}$ C, the electron mass as $9.11\times10^{-31}$ kg, and $1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.60\times10^{-19}$ J.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    Light of wavelength 420 nm is incident on a metal surface in a darkened room. What is the energy carried by a single photon of this light, and what is that energy when it is expressed in electron-volts rather than in joules?

    (Calculate)
  2. [1 mark](b)

    A metal has a work function of $2.28$ eV. Light of frequency $8.00\times10^{14}$ Hz is shone on it. What is the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons?

    (Calculate)
  3. [1 mark](c)

    For the metal and the illumination described in the previous question, what stopping potential would be measured?

    (Determine)
  4. [1 mark](d)

    An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of $150$ V. What is its de Broglie wavelength?

    (Calculate)
  5. [1 mark](e)

    A laser emits $2.0$ mW of light at a wavelength of $640$ nm. How many photons does it emit each second?

    (Calculate)

Total: 5 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) $E=\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}=\dfrac{6.63\times10^{-34}\times3.00\times10^{8}}{420\times10^{-9}}=4.74\times10^{-19}$ J, which is option A. (A1) Option B is the same number in electron-volts, so it catches anyone who converts and then forgets which unit they are in; option C comes from multiplying by the wavelength; option D is the frequency, one step short. (a) A

(b) $hf=6.63\times10^{-34}\times8.00\times10^{14}=5.304\times10^{-19}$ J $=3.315$ eV. Subtracting the work function gives $K_{\max}=3.315-2.28=1.035$ eV $=1.66\times10^{-19}$ J, option A. (A1) The trap in option B is subtracting $2.28\times10^{-19}$ J, which is not the same quantity as $2.28$ eV. (b) A

(c) $eV_s=K_{\max}$ in electron-volts, so $V_s=1.035$ V, option A. (A1) The rule is that one electron-volt corresponds to one volt for a singly charged particle, which is why the conversion is trivial once (b) is in eV and impossible if it is left in joules. (c) A

(d) $p=\sqrt{2\,m\,eV}=\sqrt{2\times9.11\times10^{-31}\times1.60\times10^{-19}\times150}=6.61\times10^{-24}$ kg m per second, so $\lambda=\dfrac{h}{p}=\dfrac{6.63\times10^{-34}}{6.61\times10^{-24}}=1.00\times10^{-10}$ m, option A. (A1) The factor of two is the discriminator here, and it is worth noting that $0.10$ nm is of the order of an atomic spacing, which is why electron diffraction works. (d) A

(e) $E=\dfrac{6.63\times10^{-34}\times3.00\times10^{8}}{640\times10^{-9}}=3.11\times10^{-19}$ J, so the rate is $\dfrac{2.0\times10^{-3}}{3.11\times10^{-19}}=6.4\times10^{15}$ per second, option A. (A1) Option B is the most dangerous distractor in the cluster because the arithmetic is identical and only the prefix differs. (e) A

Markscheme notes

One mark per item and there is no negative marking, so a candidate should be advised to commit to an answer rather than leave a blank. In (a) condone $4.7$ to $4.8\times10^{-19}$ J. In (b) allow the answer in eV provided it is identified as such, and note that a candidate who works entirely in joules must convert the work function first; that conversion is where the mark is actually won or lost. In (d) allow $0.10$ nm or $1.0\times10^{-10}$ m, and credit a candidate who reaches $1.42\times10^{-10}$ m only if they show the factor of two explicitly, since otherwise the working is wrong even though the answer looks close. In (e) accept $6.4\times10^{15}$ to $6.5\times10^{15}$. It is worth telling candidates explicitly that the electron-volt is doing real work in this cluster rather than being decoration: in (b) the subtraction is only possible after the work function has been brought into the same unit as the photon energy, and in (c) the answer in volts is the answer in electron-volts, which is the sense in which the unit simplifies rather than complicates the arithmetic.

Why this question is hard

The cluster is deliberately built so that four of the five items are won or lost on a conversion rather than on an idea. Every candidate can state that $E=hf$ and that $K_{\max}=hf-\phi$; far fewer can hold onto the units while doing it, and the distractors are engineered so that each wrong option is a specific, nameable slip — quoting electron-volts as joules, subtracting $2.28\times10^{-19}$ J as though it were $2.28$ eV, dropping the factor of two in $p=\sqrt{2meV}$, reading nanometres as micrometres. This matters beyond the marks: in the examined practical work the same conversions recur constantly, and a student who loses them here will lose them when they are carrying three other uncertainties at the same time. A guessing candidate is unlikely to land on the key in more than one of these, because the wrong options are not random numbers but the results of real errors, which is the design target for a Paper 1A cluster. A useful post-mortem is to ask the class which of the four distractors they actually chose. The distribution is informative: the micrometre slip in (e) and the dropped factor of two in (d) are both errors of attention rather than of understanding, and they respond to a checklist, whereas the electron-volt slips in (a) and (b) usually indicate that the student has not yet internalised what the unit means and needs the conversion written out in full until it becomes automatic.
