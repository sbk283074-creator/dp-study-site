A join where continuity and differentiability impose independent relations

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.12-001

AHL 5.12 (informal understanding of continuity and differentiability), AHL 5.15 (derivatives of tan x, sec x, csc x, cot x, a^x, log_a x)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatDetermineExplainHence

Question

Let $a>0$ with $a\ne1$, and let $p,q,r,s,t$ be real constants. Define $$f(x)=\begin{cases} a^{x}+\log_a x+p, & 0<x\le1,\\[4pt] q\tan(x-1)+r, & 1<x\le1+\frac{\pi}{4},\\[4pt] s\csc(x-1)+t, & 1+\frac{\pi}{4}<x<1+\pi. \end{cases}$$

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that $f$ is continuous at $x=1$ exactly when $a+p=r$, and that $f$ is continuous at $x=1+\frac{\pi}{4}$ exactly when $q+r=s\sqrt2+t$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    By differentiating each branch, show that $f$ is differentiable at $x=1$ exactly when $a+p=r$ and $q=a\ln a+\frac{1}{\ln a}$, and that $f$ is differentiable at $x=1+\frac{\pi}{4}$ exactly when $q+r=s\sqrt2+t$ and $s=-\sqrt2\,q$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine all parameter choices for which $f$ is continuous at $x=1$ but not differentiable there, and explain why no choice of parameters makes $f$ differentiable at $x=1$ while failing to be continuous there.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Taking $a=e$ and $r=0$, determine the coordinates of the stationary point of $f$ on the third branch when $f$ is differentiable at both joins, and justify that this point is a local maximum.

    (Hence)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) At $x=1$ the left limit is $a^{1}+\log_a1+p=a+p$, while the right-hand value is $q\tan0+r=r$. (M1)(A1) These agree exactly when $a+p=r$. (AG) At $x=1+\frac{\pi}{4}$ the left limit is $q\tan\frac{\pi}{4}+r=q+r$ and the right-hand value is $s\csc\frac{\pi}{4}+t=s\sqrt2+t$; these agree exactly when $q+r=s\sqrt2+t$. (M1)(A1) *(AG)*

(b) The first branch has derivative $a^{x}\ln a+\frac{1}{x\ln a}$, equal at $x=1$ to $a\ln a+\frac{1}{\ln a}$; the second has derivative $q\sec^{2}(x-1)$, equal at $x=1$ to $q$. (M1)(A1) Differentiability at $x=1$ needs continuity and equality of these one-sided derivatives, giving $a+p=r$ and $q=a\ln a+\frac{1}{\ln a}$. (A1) At $x=1+\frac{\pi}{4}$ the second branch has derivative $q\sec^{2}\frac{\pi}{4}=2q$, and the third has derivative $-s\csc(x-1)\cot(x-1)$, equal there to $-s\sqrt2$. (M1) So differentiability at that join requires $q+r=s\sqrt2+t$ together with $2q=-s\sqrt2$, that is $s=-\sqrt2\,q$. (A1) *(AG)*

(c) Continuity at $x=1$ is the single condition $a+p=r$; differentiability adds the independent condition $q=a\ln a+\frac{1}{\ln a}$. (M1) Hence $f$ is continuous but not differentiable at $x=1$ precisely when $a+p=r$ and $q\ne a\ln a+\frac{1}{\ln a}$, for any admissible $a$. (A1) The reverse cannot occur: a function differentiable at a point must be continuous there, since a differentiable function has a tangent line and cannot break. (R1)(A1)

(d) With $a=e$ we have $\ln a=1$, so $q=e+1$, then $s=-\sqrt2(e+1)$ and $t=q+r-s\sqrt2=3(e+1)$. (M1) On the third branch $f'(x)=-s\csc(x-1)\cot(x-1)=\sqrt2(e+1)\csc(x-1)\cot(x-1)$, which vanishes when $\cot(x-1)=0$, that is at $x=1+\frac{\pi}{2}$. (A1) There $f=s\csc\frac{\pi}{2}+t=s+t=(3-\sqrt2)(e+1)$, so the stationary point is $\left(1+\frac{\pi}{2},\,(3-\sqrt2)(e+1)\right)$. Since $f'$ is positive just left of $x=1+\frac{\pi}{2}$ and negative just right, the point is a local maximum. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for evaluating each one-sided limit and the (A1) for the resulting relation; accept $\log_a1=0$ used without comment. A candidate who checks continuity by substituting $x=1$ into one branch only, without comparing the branches, earns at most the (M1).

(b) The derivative marks depend on the correct rules: $\frac{d}{dx}a^{x}=a^{x}\ln a$, $\frac{d}{dx}\log_a x=\frac{1}{x\ln a}$, $\frac{d}{dx}\tan u=\sec^{2}u$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\csc u=-\csc u\cot u$, with $u=x-1$. Award (A1) for $a\ln a+\frac{1}{\ln a}$ and for $-s\sqrt2$. Condone writing $\frac{1}{\ln a}$ as $\log_a e$. Differentiability must be stated to require continuity as well; a candidate who equates the derivatives but omits $a+p=r$ loses the final (A1).

(c) The (M1) is for separating the continuity relation from the differentiability relation; the answer must be given as a family, not one choice. The (R1) is for the implication differentiable implies continuous, and the final (A1) for completing the argument. Accept an equivalent remark that the differentiability condition is strictly stronger.

(d) The (M1) is for substituting $a=e$ and propagating $q$, $s$ and $t$; follow-through is allowed from values carried consistently from (b). The (A1) needs both coordinates, and the second (A1) needs the sign argument, not merely the assertion that the point is a maximum.

Why this question is hard

The function is assembled so that each branch forces a different derivative rule, and so that the two conditions imposed at each join are logically independent. That independence is the point: continuity and differentiability are often conflated, and here they are made to disagree in a visible, parameterised way.

At $x=1$ the left branch $a^{x}+\log_a x+p$ contributes the rules $\frac{d}{dx}a^{x}=a^{x}\ln a$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\log_a x=\frac{1}{x\ln a}$, so its slope is $a\ln a+\frac{1}{\ln a}$, while the right branch $q\tan(x-1)+r$ has slope $q$. Continuity matches the values and gives $a+p=r$; differentiability additionally matches the slopes and gives the second, independent condition. No value condition can produce a slope condition, so for a whole family of parameters the graph is continuous at $x=1$ yet has a corner there. This is exactly the informal relationship the syllabus describes: continuity is necessary for differentiability but not sufficient.

The second join repeats the pattern with the reciprocal functions. Differentiating $q\tan(x-1)$ at $x=1+\frac{\pi}{4}$ uses $\sec^{2}$, giving $2q$, while differentiating $s\csc(x-1)$ uses $-\csc\cot$, giving $-s\sqrt2$; equality of slopes then fixes $s$ in terms of $q$, and continuity fixes $t$. Because $\sec^{2}$ and $-\csc\cot$ are genuinely different, the two joins do not merely repeat one calculation.

Part (d) tests whether the parameter relations can be carried through to a concrete graph. With $a=e$ the logarithm becomes natural, $q=e+1$, and the third branch has a stationary point where $\cot(x-1)=0$. The classic wrong turns are equating values instead of slopes, forgetting that differentiability requires continuity, differentiating $\log_a x$ as $\frac{1}{x}$, and losing the factor $\sqrt2$ from $\csc\frac{\pi}{4}$.

Challenge lever
Continuity at each join forces one relation on the parameters while differentiability forces a second relation coming from the derivative rules, and because the value condition cannot produce the slope condition the student must separate the two and describe the whole family of parameter choices that are continuous but not differentiable, rather than solve one equation and assume smoothness.

Adaptation
Built from the AHL 5.12 bullet on continuity and differentiability together with the AHL 5.15 bullet on the derivatives of tan, sec, csc, cot, a^x and log_a x. The design uses a three-branch piecewise function whose two joins each carry an independent continuity relation and differentiability relation, so the student must describe the family of parameter choices that are continuous but not differentiable, and then carry the relations into a stationary-point question on the csc branch. The function, the two joins, the parameter relations and the local maximum are all constructed here, and every value was checked numerically.
Verification
Numerical verification: each branch was evaluated and differentiated at the joins; the derivative relations q = a ln a + 1/ln a at a=e and s = -sqrt(2) q were checked numerically; the relation t = 3(e+1) with r=0 was confirmed; the vanishing of the third-branch derivative at x=1+pi/2 and the sign change across that point were checked; and a counterexample a=2, q=0 was used to confirm the continuous-but-not-differentiable family.
