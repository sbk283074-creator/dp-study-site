Student investigation: the period of a loaded spring

A student hangs a spring from a rigid support and suspends a mass hanger from its lower end. She pulls the hanger a short distance below its equilibrium position and releases it, so that the system oscillates vertically. She measures the time for 20 complete oscillations using a stopwatch that reads to 0.01 s, and divides by 20 to obtain the period. She estimates her reaction time as 0.2 s at both the start and the stop. She repeats the measurement for seven different loads. The masses are known to the nearest gram, so the uncertainty in the load is negligible.

Question

The student intends to test the relationship $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$ and to determine the spring constant $k$ from her data.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    State the uncertainty in a single value of the period $T$, and explain how you obtained it from the information given about the timing.

    (State)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Describe how the student could reduce the uncertainty in the period using the same stopwatch, and state one further change to the apparatus that would reduce it more.

    (Describe)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain why the student should plot a graph of $T^2$ against $m$ rather than $T$ against $m$, and state what feature of the resulting graph would support the proposed relationship.

    (Explain)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the spring constant $k$ from the data. You should make it clear which points you used to fix your best-fit line and why, and give $k$ with its unit.

    (Determine)
  5. [3 marks](e)

    Evaluate whether the data support the proposed relationship. Identify any anomalous reading, suggest a plausible experimental cause for it, and state what the student should have done at the time.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The reaction time contributes at both the start and the stop, so combining in quadrature gives $\Delta t = \sqrt{0.2^2 + 0.2^2} = 0.28\ \mathrm{s}$ for the timing of 20 oscillations. Since $T = t/20$, the uncertainty in the period is $\Delta T = 0.28/20 = 0.014\ \mathrm{s}$. (M1) for dividing the timing uncertainty by the number of oscillations, (A1) for a value of about $0.01\ \mathrm{s}$ to $0.02\ \mathrm{s}$. Adding the two reaction times directly instead of in quadrature gives $0.4/20 = 0.020\ \mathrm{s}$, which is also acceptable; the stopwatch resolution of $0.01\ \mathrm{s}$ is far smaller than either and can be ignored.

(b) Timing a larger number of oscillations divides the same timing uncertainty by a larger number, so 50 oscillations rather than 20 would give $\Delta T \approx 0.006\ \mathrm{s}$. (A1) A much larger reduction comes from removing the human element: a position sensor or a video recording analysed frame by frame gives a period limited only by the sampling rate. (A1) Note that timing more oscillations is free, which is why it should always be the first thing a student does.

(c) The relationship $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ is not linear in $m$, so a graph of $T$ against $m$ would be a curve and a curve cannot confirm a hypothesis by eye. Squaring gives $T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{k}\,m$, which is the equation of a straight line through the origin with gradient $4\pi^2/k$. (M1) The relationship is supported if the plotted points lie close to a straight line (A1) and if that line passes through, or very close to, the origin (A1) — a non-zero intercept would indicate that the spring's own mass is contributing to the inertia of the system.

(d) The point at $m = 0.300\ \mathrm{kg}$ lies well above the trend and must be excluded from the fit (R1); the reason is given in part (e). Using the remaining six points, a least-squares fit of $T^2$ against $m$ gives a gradient of $1.56\ \mathrm{s^2\ kg^{-1}}$ and an intercept of about $0.005\ \mathrm{s^2}$, effectively zero. (M1) Hence $k = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{\text{gradient}} = \dfrac{39.5}{1.56} = 25.3\ \mathrm{N\ m^{-1}}$. (A1) A hand-drawn best-fit line will give a gradient anywhere in the range $1.53$ to $1.60\ \mathrm{s^2\ kg^{-1}}$, so any value of $k$ from $24.7$ to $25.8\ \mathrm{N\ m^{-1}}$ should be credited. (A1)

(e) With the anomalous point removed, the remaining six points lie on a straight line through the origin to within the scatter expected from the timing uncertainty, so the data do support the relationship. (A1) The reading at $m = 0.300\ \mathrm{kg}$ is the anomalous one: the model predicts $T = 2\pi\sqrt{0.300/25} = 0.688\ \mathrm{s}$, whereas $0.730\ \mathrm{s}$ was recorded, some $6\%$ high, which is far outside the $2\%$ timing uncertainty at that load. (M1) A plausible cause is that the hanger caught on the support or that an extra mass was left on the hanger for that run. The student should have repeated that measurement immediately, while the apparatus was still set up, rather than processing a single suspicious value. (A1)

Markscheme notes

In (a) accept any value from 0.014 s to 0.020 s; credit the method of dividing by 20 even if the combination of the two reaction times is done by simple addition. Do not credit the stopwatch resolution of 0.01 s alone, which ignores the dominant error. In (b) the first mark is for timing more oscillations; the second must be a change of technique, not simply 'be more careful' or 'use a better stopwatch'. In (c) all three marks are for the linearisation argument: award the first for stating that the untransformed graph is curved, the second for the straight-line prediction, and the third for the through-the-origin test. In (d) allow follow-through from a gradient obtained with the anomalous point included, provided the point is not silently absorbed; a candidate who includes it and comments on the resulting poor fit can still earn the reasoning marks. In (e) the anomaly must be identified by its load, and the suggested cause must be physically plausible rather than 'human error' alone. A candidate who argues that the point should simply be deleted without a reason earns no credit.

Why this question is hard

This item is built around a distinction that Paper 1B rewards and that coursework often does not: the difference between calculating a result and interrogating a dataset. The linearisation in (c) is the first hurdle, and it is the one that separates candidates who treat a graph as a formality from those who use it as a test — a curve can be fitted to almost anything, and only a straight line lets you judge a hypothesis by eye. The seeded anomaly in (d) and (e) is the second hurdle, and it is deliberately placed in the middle of the range rather than at an end, so that an endpoint calculation absorbs it silently while a best-fit line exposes it: a candidate who uses only the first and last rows obtains a gradient of about 1.55 and never notices anything is wrong, whereas one who plots the points sees immediately that the fifth sits above the trend. The final trap is in (e), where the natural answer is to delete the point and move on; what the markscheme wants is a stated physical cause and the correct experimental response, which is to repeat the measurement at once rather than to discard inconvenient data after the fact. A further check that this dataset invites, and that examiners return to, is the intercept. Had the best-fit line cut the T squared axis clearly above zero, the natural conclusion would be that the spring's own mass is oscillating along with the load, contributing roughly one third of its mass to the effective inertia. The data here were generated with a negligible intercept so that this complication does not obscure the main test, but a candidate who comments on the size of the intercept rather than ignoring it is displaying exactly the judgement Paper 1B is designed to reward.
