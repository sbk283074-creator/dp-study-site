C.4 Resonance positions in a closed tube, and the end correction that cancels

Physics HL · PHYS-C.4-201

Theme C: Wave behaviourC.4 Standing waves and resonance (resonance in a tube closed at one end; end correction)P2 · Section B11 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedDetermineCalculateDetermineExplain

Question

A tuning fork of frequency $f = 512\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is held over the open upper end of a vertical tube closed at the lower end by a movable piston. The air column is adjusted until resonance is heard, and the three shortest resonating lengths are found to be $L_{1} = 0.160\ \mathrm{m}$, $L_{2} = 0.500\ \mathrm{m}$ and $L_{3} = 0.840\ \mathrm{m}$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Determine the wavelength of the sound in the tube, explaining why the three lengths are used in the way you choose rather than $L_{1}$ alone.

    (Determine)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the speed of sound in the air column.

    (Calculate)
  3. [2 marks](c)

    Determine the end correction of the tube, and state its value to two significant figures.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain why using $L_{1}$ alone would give an incorrect speed of sound, and state by what percentage the value would be wrong.

    (Explain)

Reveal the answer

(a) A tube closed at one end resonates when its length is an odd number of quarter wavelengths, but the antinode at the open end lies a small distance $e$ beyond the tube, so
$$L_{n} = (2n-1)\frac{\lambda}{4} - e$$

Subtracting consecutive lengths removes $e$ exactly (M1):
$$L_{2} - L_{1} = \frac{\lambda}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda = 2(L_{2} - L_{1}) = 2(0.500 - 0.160) = 0.680\ \mathrm{m} \tag{A1(A1)}$$

Using $L_{1}$ alone would require knowing $e$, which is not given; the difference is used precisely because the unknown cancels.

(b) (M1)(A1)
$$v = f\lambda = 512 \times 0.680 = 348\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$$

to three significant figures (A1).

(c) From the first resonance (M1):
$$e = \frac{\lambda}{4} - L_{1} = \frac{0.680}{4} - 0.160 = 0.170 - 0.160 = 0.010\ \mathrm{m} \tag{A1}$$

The end correction is $0.010\ \mathrm{m}$, or $10\ \mathrm{mm}$, of the order of the tube radius.

The data are consistent: $\dfrac{3\lambda}{4} - e = 0.510 - 0.010 = 0.500\ \mathrm{m} = L_{2}$ and $\dfrac{5\lambda}{4} - e = 0.850 - 0.010 = 0.840\ \mathrm{m} = L_{3}$, both exact.

(d) Using $L_{1}$ alone assumes the antinode is exactly at the open end, so it takes $\lambda = 4L_{1} = 0.640\ \mathrm{m}$ (M1). Then
$$v = 512 \times 0.640 = 328\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \tag{A1}$$

The percentage error is
$$\frac{348.16 - 327.68}{348.16} \times 100 = 3.0\ \%$$

too low. The error is entirely in the wavelength, because the neglected end correction shortens the apparent quarter wavelength by $0.010\ \mathrm{m}$ out of $0.170\ \mathrm{m}$, which is $5.9\ \%$ (A1).

The strongest evidence that the model is the right one is that two fitted numbers, the wavelength and the end correction, reproduce all three measured resonance lengths exactly. Three independent measurements have been reduced to two parameters with nothing left over, which a wrong model would not achieve. That is also why the data were given as three lengths rather than one: a single length can always be explained by a single fitted quantity, but it cannot be checked.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for using a difference of two resonance lengths, A1A1 for $\lambda = 0.680\ \mathrm{m}$. The reasoning must mention that the end correction is the same for every resonance and therefore cancels; an answer that simply computes $2(L_{2}-L_{1})$ with no justification earns M1 and the first A1 only. Accept the use of $L_{3} - L_{2}$ instead, which gives the same wavelength.

(b) M1 for $v = f\lambda$, A1 for $348\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Follow-through applies from an incorrect $\lambda$.

(c) M1 for $\lambda/4 - L_{1}$ or for any consistent route, A1 for $0.010\ \mathrm{m}$. Accept a value between $0.008$ and $0.012\ \mathrm{m}$; the tube radius is not given, so a candidate who argues the correction should be about $0.6r$ is reasoning correctly and should not be penalised.

(d) M1 for recognising that $\lambda = 4L_{1}$ ignores the end correction, A1 for $328\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ or for the $3.0\ \%$ error, A1 for attributing the whole discrepancy to the wavelength. A candidate who quotes $2.8\ \%$ (the error in $v$ measured against $328$) should be credited if the working is consistent.

Forfeits: treating the tube as open at both ends, which gives $\lambda = L_{2} - L_{1}$ and a speed half the correct value; assuming the first resonance is at $\lambda/2$ rather than $\lambda/4$. Condonation: quoting the speed as $348\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ or $3.5 \times 10^{2}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ are both acceptable; a candidate who then compares it with the accepted $343\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and comments on the discrepancy should be credited for the judgement.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on a single structural fact about closed tubes: the three resonance lengths are equally spaced, because successive resonances differ by half a wavelength, and the end correction is the same for each. That means the unknown appears with the same sign and the same value in every measurement, so subtracting two of them annihilates it. This is the standard experimental trick for measuring a wavelength in a closed tube, and it is the reason the data contain three lengths rather than one.

The trap is the natural first move. Given a frequency and a resonance length, the tempting route is $\lambda = 4L_{1}$ and then $v = f\lambda$, which uses one number and requires no thought about the open end. It is also wrong, because the antinode is not at the open end but slightly outside it: the air just beyond the tube also moves. With these numbers the omission produces $328\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ against $348\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, a discrepancy of three per cent that is larger than the year-to-year spread of the accepted value, so it matters.

Part (d) asks for the size of the error rather than just its existence, which forces the student to actually compute the wrong answer and compare it. That comparison is what makes the item diagnostic: a student who writes "it would be slightly wrong" has not understood that the correction is a fixed length, and a fixed length is a much larger fraction of the shortest resonance than of the longest. The check at the end of part (c), reproducing both $L_{2}$ and $L_{3}$ from the fitted $\lambda$ and $e$, is there so that the student can see the model is exactly consistent with the data rather than approximately so.
