The append that copies half the array

Computer science HL · CS-P2B-003

Theme B: Computational thinking and problem-solvingB2.4 (Programming algorithms: algorithm design, efficiency, and the analysis of running time)P215 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedCalculateDetermineExplainJustify

A list that grows itself

A hospital records a reading every second. The readings are held in a structure that starts empty with room for one reading. When a reading arrives and the structure is full, the structure allocates a new region twice as large, copies every reading it already holds into the new region, and then stores the new reading. Copying one reading costs one operation.

A shift lasts 1024 seconds, so 1024 readings are appended, one per second. The monitoring software runs on a device that must respond within a fixed deadline, and the engineers have been told that the structure is cheap because "the average append costs about one operation".

Question

You are asked to check the engineers' claim and to decide whether the structure is safe for a device with a response deadline. Work in operations, where copying one reading is one operation, and assume the first reading is appended to a structure with room for one.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    State the capacities of the structure after each of the first eight appends, and identify the appends at which a copy takes place during the 1024-second shift.

    (Calculate)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Determine the total number of copy operations performed across the whole shift, and hence the average number of copy operations per append. Give your average to three decimal places.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the largest number of copy operations performed by any single append during the shift, state which append that is, and explain why the average you found in part (b) does not bound it.

    (Explain)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    The engineers propose to keep the doubling rule but to pre-allocate room for 4096 readings before the shift begins. Justify whether that change removes the risk of missing the response deadline, and state what the change costs in operations at start-up.

    (Justify)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

Part (a). The structure doubles when full, so the capacity sequence is 1, then 2, then 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128 and so on. (M1) After the first append the capacity is 1; after the second it becomes 2; after the third, 4; after the fifth, 8; after the ninth, 16. So after the first eight appends the capacities are 1, 2, 4, 4, 8, 8, 8, 8. (A1)

A copy happens when an append finds the structure already full, which is exactly the appends numbered 2, 3, 5, 9, 17, 33, 65, 129, 257 and 513. (A1) There are ten such appends in the shift, and the other 1014 appends copy nothing.

Part (b). At append number 2 the structure holds one reading and copies it; at append 3 it holds two; at append 5, four; and in general the append numbered 2^k + 1 copies 2^k readings. (M1) The copy counts are therefore 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256 and 512. (A1)

The total is 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 + 32 + 64 + 128 + 256 + 512 = 1023 operations. (M1)(A1)

The average per append is 1023 / 1024 = 0.999 operations, to three decimal places. (A1) So the engineers' claim is arithmetically sound: on average an append costs about one operation, and in fact slightly less.

Part (c). The largest single copy is 512 operations, performed by append number 513. (M1)(A1)

The average does not bound it because an average is a statement about a total divided by a count, and it is compatible with almost any single value in the sequence. Here the 1023 operations are concentrated in ten appends out of 1024, and one of those ten carries half of the entire total on its own. (M1) The average is a property of the run as a whole, while the deadline is threatened by one append, so the two quantities answer different questions. (A1)

It is worth being precise about why the engineers' claim and the objection to it can both be true at once. The claim is about the total cost of the shift, and it is correct: 1023 operations across 1024 appends really is about one operation each, and no arrangement of the same appends can make that total much larger. The objection is about the largest cost of any single second, and it is also correct. A quantity that describes a sum cannot also describe a maximum unless the sum happens to be spread evenly, and here it is spread as unevenly as it could be: ten appends carry the whole cost and the other 1014 carry none of it. If the device's deadline is expressed per second, then it is the maximum that must be compared with the deadline, and the average is simply the wrong statistic for the question being asked. (R1)

Part (d). Pre-allocating room for 4096 readings does remove the risk, because a shift only ever appends 1024 readings and 1024 is less than 4096, so the structure is never full during the shift and no append copies anything. (M1) The total copy work during the shift falls from 1023 operations to 0. (A1)

The change is not free. The start-up cost is that the device must obtain room for 4096 readings at once, and if it must be initialised by copying, the cost is 4096 operations in a single step, which is eight times worse than the worst append it was trying to avoid. (M1) The trade is therefore between a predictable large cost before the shift and an unpredictable large cost during it, and which is preferable depends on whether the deadline applies at start-up; a candidate may reasonably conclude either way provided the trade is stated.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for the doubling capacity sequence. A1 for the capacities 1, 2, 4, 4, 8, 8, 8, 8 after the first eight appends. A1 for the copy appends 2, 3, 5, 9, 17, 33, 65, 129, 257, 513; award this even if the capacity list is wrong, provided the two are consistent. Do not require the list to be exhaustive for full marks in (a); the exhaustive list is credited in (b).

(b) M1 for the pattern that append 2^k + 1 copies 2^k. A1 for the ten counts. M1 for summing them. A1 for 1023. A1 for 0.999 to three decimal places; accept 0.9988 or 1.0 only if the candidate's own total supports it. A candidate who reports the total as 2046 by assuming every doubling copies the *new* capacity rather than the old one scores the M1s but neither of the last two A1s.

(c) M1 for identifying that the largest copy is at the last doubling. A1 for 512 and for append 513; both are required, since 512 without the append number does not locate the event. M1 for the explanation that an average does not bound an individual value. A1 for the sharper statement that the total is concentrated in ten appends. An answer that says only "the average is not the worst case" earns the M1 but not the A1.

(d) M1 for the observation that 1024 is below 4096 so the structure never fills. A1 for the shift's copy total falling to 0. M1 for identifying the start-up cost. A1 is not available for claiming the change is free. Accept a conclusion either way provided the trade between a predictable and an unpredictable large cost is stated.

Throughout, accept 512 as "half the array" and do not require the phrase "amortised".

Why this question is hard

The item isolates a single idea: an amortised cost and a worst-case cost are different quantities, and a system with a deadline is governed by the second.

The arithmetic is deliberately gentle. The student computes 1023 copies over 1024 appends and gets an average of 0.999, which *confirms* the engineers' claim rather than refuting it. That is the design: the question is not whether the average is right, it is whether the average is the number that matters. A student who stops at part (b) has done everything correctly and still not answered the question the hospital is asking.

The mechanism is concentration. The 1023 operations are not spread evenly; they sit in ten appends, and the last of those carries 512 of them. The gap between the average and the maximum is a factor of about 512, and no amount of averaging will expose it, because averaging is precisely the operation that destroys that information.

Part (a) is a scaffolding step and is worth noticing as such: it asks for capacities after the first eight appends, which is where the doubling pattern is visible but the numbers are small enough to list. The trap is to write the capacities as 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, which is the sequence of *doublings* rather than the sequence of *capacities after each append*. Both are natural readings of the question and only one is right.

The classic wrong turn in part (b) is to assume that each doubling copies the new capacity rather than the old one, giving 2046 instead of 1023 and an average of about 2. That answer is doubly wrong, because it both doubles the total and, ironically, makes the average look *worse* than it is, which would have led the student to reject a claim that is in fact correct.
