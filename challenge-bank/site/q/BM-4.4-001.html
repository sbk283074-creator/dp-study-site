4.4 Purchase-intent research read with 6.7 weighted and standardised rates

Business management SL · BM-4.4-001

4.4 Market research (sampling, bias); 6.7 Descriptive statisticsP215 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedCalculateShow thatEvaluate

Purchase intent by age band, waves 1 and 2

Positive responses are respondents who said they would definitely or probably buy. Note that the two waves did not interview the same number of people in each band.

Question

Nordvik Drinks commissions two waves of purchase-intent research for a new low-sugar cordial. Respondents are asked whether they would definitely or probably buy the product at its intended price.

Wave 1 is an in-store intercept survey carried out at weekends. Wave 2 is an online panel carried out on weekdays.

The company's target market for the launch is 900 000 adults, split 30% / 40% / 30% between the 18–34, 35–54 and 55+ bands. Each buyer is expected to make one purchase in the launch year, and the gross margin is £1.40 per unit.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the overall positive response rate for each wave.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Calculate the positive response rate within each of the three age bands for each wave, and state what your results show that the comparison in part (a) conceals.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Using the target market mix of 30% / 40% / 30%, show that the age-standardised positive rate is 25.0% for wave 1 and 30.0% for wave 2.

    (Show that)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    The marketing director proposes to forecast launch-year sales using the wave 2 overall rate from part (a). Evaluate this proposal, quantifying its effect on the launch decision.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Wave 1: $\dfrac{118}{400} \times 100 = 29.5\%$. Wave 2: $\dfrac{153}{600} \times 100 = 25.5\%$. On this comparison purchase intent appears to have fallen by $4.0$ percentage points between the two waves.

(b) Wave 1. 18–34: $\dfrac{80}{200} = 40.0\%$. 35–54: $\dfrac{30}{120} = 25.0\%$. 55+: $\dfrac{8}{80} = 10.0\%$.

Wave 2. 18–34: $\dfrac{54}{120} = 45.0\%$. 35–54: $\dfrac{54}{180} = 30.0\%$. 55+: $\dfrac{45}{300} = 15.0\%$.

Intent rose in every single band — by 5.0, 5.0 and 5.0 percentage points respectively. The overall comparison in part (a) conceals this completely, because the two waves did not interview the same mix of people: wave 2 drew 300 of its 600 respondents from the 55+ band, against 80 of 400 in wave 1, and that band has the lowest intent in both waves. The headline fall is an artefact of who was asked, not a change in what people think.

(c) Applying the target mix to the wave 1 band rates: $0.30(40.0) + 0.40(25.0) + 0.30(10.0) = 12.0 + 10.0 + 3.0 = 25.0\%$. Applying it to the wave 2 band rates: $0.30(45.0) + 0.40(30.0) + 0.30(15.0) = 13.5 + 12.0 + 4.5 = 30.0\%$. Both waves are therefore reweighted onto the same population, and on that like-for-like basis intent rose by $5.0$ percentage points.

(d) The proposal should be rejected. The wave 2 overall rate of 25.5% is computed on a sample that is not representative of the target market: it over-weights the 55+ band by a factor of roughly five relative to the target mix. Applying it gives $900\,000 \times 0.255 = 229\,500$ buyers. Applying the standardised wave 2 rate of 30.0% gives $900\,000 \times 0.30 = 270\,000$ buyers. The difference is $40\,500$ units, and at a gross margin of £1.40 that is $40\,500 \times 1.40 = £56\,700$ of margin left out of the forecast. That is large enough to change the decision if the launch has a fixed cost close to the projected margin — a project that looks marginal at 229 500 units may clear its threshold at 270 000. Against this, the standardised figure is not free of doubt: wave 2 was an online panel taken on weekdays, so it may under-represent people who shop in stores at weekends, and stated purchase intent reliably overstates actual buying. The defensible conclusion is that the director's method is wrong in a known direction and by a known amount, and that the forecast should use the standardised rate while being presented as an upper estimate.

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for each rate and one for the comparison. Accept rates given to one decimal place. Do not penalise a candidate who comments that the two rates are not comparable — that is creditworthy here and is developed in later parts.

(b) One mark for each wave's set of three band rates (two marks total), one for the observation that intent rose in every band, and one for identifying the differing age mix as the cause. A candidate who computes the band rates but does not draw the conclusion earns the first two marks only.

(c) One mark for setting up the weighted sum with the target mix, one for each standardised rate, and one for the resulting comparison. Candidates may use either the percentages or the counts; both are acceptable. Follow-through applies from part (b).

(d) This is an AO3 part: marks are for the judgement and its support, not for the arithmetic alone. Award one mark for rejecting the proposal, one for each of the two forecasts (four marks total across the set), one for the quantified difference in units and one for the money figure. A candidate who reaches the correct numbers but does not say what they mean for the decision is capped at three. Conversely, a candidate who rejects the proposal with sound reasoning but whose arithmetic slips may still earn three, provided the direction of the error is identified. Credit the counter-argument about stated intent overstating actual purchase where it is developed rather than merely listed.

Why this question is hard

This is a descriptive-statistics item in which the obvious statistic is the wrong one. Both headline rates are arithmetically correct — 29.5% and 25.5% — and both are misleading, because the denominator changed shape between the waves. Wave 2 interviewed 300 over-55s out of 600; wave 1 interviewed 80 out of 400. Since that band is the least enthusiastic in both waves, moving it from a fifth to a half of the sample drags the overall figure down even though enthusiasm went up everywhere.

The skill being tested is knowing which tool to reach for. A simple overall rate is the right tool for a representative sample and the wrong tool for two samples with different compositions; the right tool here is a standardised rate, which reweights each wave onto the same reference population. Once that is done the sign of the change flips: the raw comparison says intent fell by 4.0 points, and the standardised comparison says it rose by 5.0. That reversal is not a curiosity — it is the whole decision, because the forecast built on the raw figure is short by 40 500 buyers and £56 700 of margin.

The most common error is to pool the two waves into a single sample of 1 000 and quote 27.1%, which combines the two sampling biases rather than removing either. A second is to notice that the samples differ but then adjust in the wrong direction, or to dismiss wave 2 entirely as 'biased' without recognising that both waves can be used once they are standardised. A third, subtler, error is to treat the standardised 30.0% as a reliable forecast in itself: stated purchase intent is systematically optimistic, and the online weekday panel carries its own coverage problem. The best answers quantify the director's error and then say what the corrected figure is still worth.

Challenge lever
Both headline figures are correct and both point the wrong way. The obvious tool — the overall positive rate for each wave — is the wrong one, because the two waves did not sample the same population: wave 2 interviewed three times as many over-55s, the band with the lowest intent in both waves. The composition difference, not any change of opinion, produces the apparent fall, so the item can only be answered with an age-standardised rate, and the cost of using the obvious tool is a forecast that is short by 40 500 buyers.
