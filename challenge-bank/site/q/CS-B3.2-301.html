Sharing projectors between meeting rooms

A company has 8 meeting rooms and is buying projectors for them. Each projector costs 2 400.

Under a composition relationship each Room object creates and owns its own Projector when the room is constructed, and the projector is destroyed with the room. Under an aggregation relationship the projectors are created separately and passed to the rooms that use them, so one projector can serve more than one room. The two relationships are drawn in the figure.

Under aggregation the company buys 5 projectors. Three of them are used by two rooms each and two of them are used by one room each, which covers all 8 rooms. Every room has exactly one projector, and a projector that fails makes every room that depends on it unusable.

Question

Evaluate the two relationships as a basis for the purchasing decision. Assume every room needs a projector, that a projector failure is equally likely to strike any of the projectors that have been bought, and that a room with no working projector cannot be used.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the capital cost of the projector fleet under each relationship, and state the saving that aggregation offers as a percentage of the composition cost.

    (Calculate)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Determine the expected number of rooms that become unusable when one projector fails, under each relationship, and express the aggregation figure as a percentage change from the composition figure.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Explain why the two relationships give different answers in part (b) even though both use the same projectors at the same price.

    (Explain)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Justify which relationship you would choose if the rooms must remain usable, and state what the choice costs in capital.

    (Justify)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

Part (a). Under composition each of the 8 rooms owns its own projector, so 8 units are bought: 8 x 2 400 = 19 200. (M1)(A1)

Under aggregation 5 units are bought: 5 x 2 400 = 12 000. (A1)

The saving is 19 200 - 12 000 = 7 200, which as a fraction of the composition cost is 7 200 / 19 200 = 0.375, or 37.5%. (A1)

Equivalently, aggregation buys 5 / 8 = 62.5% of the projectors, so it saves the remaining 37.5%.

Part (b). Under composition there are 8 projectors and each serves exactly one room, so a failure takes out 1 room. (M1)(A1)

Under aggregation the five projectors serve 2, 2, 2, 1 and 1 rooms. If a failure is equally likely to strike any of the five, the expected number of rooms lost is the mean of those five values: (2 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1) / 5 = 8 / 5 = 1.6 rooms. (M1)(A1)

The change from 1.0 to 1.6 is an increase of (1.6 - 1.0) / 1.0 = 0.60, or 60%. (A1)

Part (c). The costs differ because the two relationships answer different questions about ownership. Composition binds one projector to one room, so the fleet must be as large as the room count. Aggregation lets one projector be referenced by several rooms at once, so the fleet only has to be large enough to cover every room rather than equal to the room count. The 8 rooms are covered by 5 units because three of the units are counted twice. (M1)(A1)

The exposure differs for the same reason read in the other direction. Under composition a projector is a part of exactly one room, so its failure is contained. Under aggregation a projector is a part of several rooms at once, so its failure is shared — and sharing is precisely what allowed the fleet to be smaller. (A1)

The two effects are not independent, and this is the point of the part. Every projector that aggregation removes from the fleet is a projector whose failure is now spread over two rooms instead of one. The 37.5% saving and the 60% increase in exposure are two descriptions of a single change: fewer failure domains, each covering more rooms. (A1)

Part (d). I would choose composition for rooms that must remain usable. (M1) It costs 19 200 rather than 12 000, an extra 7 200, and it buys the property that no single projector failure takes out more than one room. (A1)

The choice is worth stating as a rate rather than as a total. The extra 7 200 buys a reduction of 0.6 rooms of expected exposure per failure. With two failures a year, aggregation exposes 3.2 rooms a year against composition's 2.0, so the extra capital buys about 1.2 room-outages a year, which is 6 000 per outage avoided. (A1) Whether that is worth paying depends on what a room outage costs the company, which is the judgement the question asks for; the arithmetic is what makes the judgement answerable.

It is worth saying plainly what the choice does *not* buy. Composition does not remove projector failures, it only contains them: a failed projector still takes its room out of use until it is repaired or replaced. What composition buys is that the failure is not multiplied by the sharing, so the worst case a room can suffer is the failure of the one projector it owns. The purchase decision is therefore about how much the company is willing to pay to keep a single failure confined to a single room, and the two figures the item produces — 7 200 of capital and 0.6 rooms of expected exposure — are the two sides of that price.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for 8 units under composition, A1 for 19 200. A1 for 12 000. A1 for 37.5%. Accept 0.375 or 7 200 stated as the saving with the percentage. Do not award the final A1 for a saving expressed as a fraction of the aggregation cost, which gives 60% and is the common error.

(b) M1 for recognising that each composition projector serves one room, A1 for 1.0. M1 for the mean over the five projectors, A1 for 1.6. A1 for 60%. Accept 1.6 with or without the working, provided the distribution 2, 2, 2, 1, 1 appears or is clearly implied. A candidate who divides by 8 instead of 5 and obtains 1.0 has not weighted by projector and should lose the second A1.

(c) M1 for the ownership or part-count distinction on cost, A1 for the fleet being smaller because units are counted twice. A1 for the exposure argument. A1 for the link between the two, namely that the same sharing produces both effects. A candidate who explains each effect separately but never connects them earns three of the four marks.

(d) M1 for choosing composition. A1 for 7 200 of extra capital. A1 for a justification that weighs the two quantities against each other rather than restating one of them. Accept aggregation with full marks if the candidate argues that the rooms need not all remain usable, or that the exposure is acceptable at the stated failure rate; the mark is for the weighing, not for the choice.

Why this question is hard

Composition and aggregation are usually taught as a vocabulary question — which diamond is filled, which is hollow — and candidates learn to label the diagrams without learning what the labels cost. This item makes the two relationships produce opposite answers to two questions that sound like the same question, and it is the opposition that carries the difficulty.

The numeric trap is in the denominator of part (a). The saving is 7 200, and 7 200 expressed against the composition cost is 37.5%, while against the aggregation cost it is 60%. Both are correct arithmetic on the same numbers, and the question asks for the saving "as a percentage of the composition cost" precisely so that the wrong denominator produces a number that also appears later in the item — as the exposure figure in part (b). A candidate who mixes the denominators will find 60% twice and read that agreement as confirmation.

Part (b) is where the item is genuinely hard, because the quantity is an expectation over an unstated distribution. The candidate has to notice that the five projectors are not interchangeable: three of them carry two rooms and two carry one, so the expected number of rooms lost is the mean of 2, 2, 2, 1 and 1 rather than the mean of 1 and 2 or the ratio 8/5 arrived at by a different route. The answer is the same as 8/5, which means a candidate who reaches 1.6 by an unprincipled shortcut gets the right number, and the markscheme has to look for the distribution rather than the value.

Part (c) is the conceptual centre. The saving and the exposure are not two facts about the design; they are one fact seen twice. Fewer projectors means each projector covers more rooms, and covering more rooms is exactly what makes a failure more damaging. A candidate who explains the two effects as separate consequences of ownership has understood the vocabulary but not the mechanism, and the markscheme separates the two answers.

Part (d) is deliberately short and deliberately open. The item does not have a preferred answer — composition is defensible because the rooms must stay usable, and aggregation is defensible if room outages are cheap. What the part tests is whether the candidate can put a price on the trade rather than restating one side of it.
