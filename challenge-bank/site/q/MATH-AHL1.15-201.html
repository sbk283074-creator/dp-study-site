1.15 An induction whose step is valid only for indices above a threshold: n! > 3^n first holds at n = 7 because n = 6 falls short by nine, and the inductive step needs n > 2, so the base case must be chosen above that threshold rather than at the smallest index

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.15-201

Topic 1: Number and algebraAHL 1.15 (proof by mathematical induction, including the choice of a suitable base case) with SL 1.8 (factorials and their growth) and AHL 5.13 (limits of sequences)P314 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedDetermineProveExplainExplain

Question

Let $a_{n} = n!$ and $b_{n} = 3^{n}$ for positive integers $n$. The two sequences are compared below, and the comparison is then used to make a claim about the infinite series $\displaystyle\sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{3^{n}}{n!}$. Note that the inequality $n! > 3^{n}$ is not true for small $n$; it is the point of the question to establish exactly where it begins to hold, and to say why the proof must begin there and nowhere earlier.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Determine, by evaluating both $n!$ and $3^{n}$ at each integer from $1$ upwards, the smallest positive integer $N$ for which $n! > 3^{n}$ holds at $n = N$.

    (Determine)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Prove by induction that $n! > 3^{n}$ for all integers $n \ge N$, stating precisely the inequality that your inductive step requires of $k$ and verifying that the base case satisfies it.

    (Prove)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain why the induction cannot be started at $n = 3$ or at $n = 4$ even though the algebra of the inductive step is valid for every $k \ge 3$.

    (Explain)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    A student argues that because $n! > 3^{n}$ for $n \ge N$, every term of $\displaystyle\sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{3^{n}}{n!}$ is less than $1$, and the series therefore converges. Explain why this argument does not establish convergence, and determine by a valid test whether the series converges, giving the limit of the ratio of successive terms.

    (Explain)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Let $a_{n} = n!$ and $b_{n} = 3^{n}$ for positive integers $n$. The two sequences are compared below, and the comparison is then used to make a claim about the infinite series $\displaystyle\sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{3^{n}}{n!}$. Note that the inequality $n! > 3^{n}$ is not true for small $n$; it is the point of the question to establish exactly where it begins to hold, and to say why the proof must begin there and nowhere earlier. (a) Determine, by evaluating both $n!$ and $3^{n}$ at each integer from $1$ upwards, the smallest positive integer $N$ for which $n! > 3^{n}$ holds at $n = N$. [2 marks] (Determine) (b) Prove by induction that $n! > 3^{n}$ for all integers $n \ge N$, stating precisely the inequality that your inductive step requires of $k$ and verifying that the base case satisfies it. [5 marks] (Prove) (c) Explain why the induction cannot be started at $n = 3$ or at $n = 4$ even though the algebra of the inductive step is valid for every $k \ge 3$. [3 marks] (Explain) (d) A student argues that because $n! > 3^{n}$ for $n \ge N$, every term of $\displaystyle\sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{3^{n}}{n!}$ is less than $1$, and the series therefore converges. Explain why this argument does not establish convergence, and determine by a valid test whether the series converges, giving the limit of the ratio of successive terms. [4 marks] (Explain)
Reveal the answer

(a) Evaluating both expressions from $n = 1$:
$$1! = 1 < 3, \quad 2! = 2 < 9, \quad 3! = 6 < 27, \quad 4! = 24 < 81, \quad 5! = 120 < 243, \quad 6! = 720 < 729, \quad 7! = 5040 > 2187 \;(M1).$$
At $n = 6$ the factorial falls short by $729 - 720 = 9$, which is the last failure, and the inequality first holds at $n = 7$. So $N = 7$ (A1).
(b) Claim: $n! > 3^{n}$ for every integer $n \ge 7$.
Base case $n = 7$: $7! = 5040$ and $3^{7} = 2187$, so $5040 > 2187$ and the claim holds (B1).
Inductive step. Assume for some integer $k \ge 7$ that $k! > 3^{k}$ (M1). Then
$$(k+1)! = (k+1)\cdot k! > (k+1)\cdot 3^{k} \;(A1).$$
The right-hand side exceeds $3^{k+1} = 3 \cdot 3^{k}$ exactly when $(k+1) > 3$, that is when $k > 2$ (A1). Since $k \ge 7$ certainly satisfies $k > 2$, the required inequality holds, and combining the two steps gives
$$(k+1)! > (k+1)\cdot 3^{k} > 3\cdot 3^{k} = 3^{k+1} \;(A1).$$
So the claim passes from $k$ to $k+1$ for every $k \ge 7$, and with the base case at $n = 7$ it holds for all $n \ge 7$ by induction (A1).
The inequality the step requires of $k$ is $k + 1 > 3$, and it is worth recording that this is the only place where the number $3$ in $3^{n}$ enters the proof in a way that constrains the index. Had the comparison been with $2^{n}$ the condition would have been $k + 1 > 2$, that is $k > 1$.
(c) The inductive step is valid for every $k \ge 3$, because that is exactly the range in which $k + 1 > 3$ (A1). Validity of the step is not enough on its own: an induction needs a true base case, and the step then propagates truth upwards from it. At $n = 3$ the claim reads $6 > 27$, which is false, and at $n = 4$ it reads $24 > 81$, which is also false (A1). Starting at either would be starting from a false statement, and a false statement can imply anything, so nothing would be proved. The base case must be chosen both above the threshold the step imposes and at an index where the claim is actually true; the smallest index satisfying both conditions is $N = 7$ (A1).
(d) The argument is invalid because the bound $3^{n}/n! < 1$ is uniform and says nothing about the size of the terms as $n$ grows. The harmonic series $\sum 1/n$ has every term less than $1$ and diverges, so a bound of this kind cannot establish convergence (A1).
A valid test is the ratio test. Writing $u_{n} = \dfrac{3^{n}}{n!}$,
$$\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_{n}} = \frac{3^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\cdot\frac{n!}{3^{n}} = \frac{3}{n+1} \;(M1),$$
so
$$\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_{n}} = \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{3}{n+1} = 0 \;(A1).$$
Since $0 < 1$, the ratio test gives convergence (A1). The ratio is the quantity the inequality of part (b) cannot supply: it shows that successive terms shrink by a factor that itself tends to zero, which is far stronger than each term being less than one.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for evaluating both expressions at successive integers, (A1) for $N = 7$. A candidate who stops at $n = 6$ and concludes the inequality is false scores nothing; a candidate who tests only $n = 1$ and $n = 7$ and asserts that $7$ is the smallest has not excluded $6$ and loses the (A1) unless the value at $6$ is shown.
(b) (B1) for the base case with both numbers evaluated, (M1) for the inductive assumption stated for a general $k$ in the stated range, (A1) for the step $(k+1)! > (k+1)3^{k}$, (A1) for identifying the condition $k > 2$, (A1) for the completed chain, (A1) for the conclusion over all $n \ge 7$. A candidate who writes the chain without mentioning the condition $k + 1 > 3$ loses that mark even though the chain is correct for the stated range: the question asks for the condition to be stated. Accept \"since $k \ge 7 > 2$ the factor $k+1$ exceeds $3$\" as a statement of it.
(c) (A1) for the observation that the step needs $k > 2$ and is therefore valid from $k = 3$ onwards, (A1) for showing that the claim is false at $n = 3$ and at $n = 4$ with the values, (A1) for the conclusion that the base case must be both above the threshold and true. A candidate who answers only that the base case is false at $n = 3$ scores the second (A1) and not the third, because the part asks why the *step* is not the obstacle.
(d) (A1) for the harmonic-series counterexample or an equivalent statement that a uniform bound below one is not sufficient, (M1) for forming the ratio $3/(n+1)$, (A1) for its limit $0$, (A1) for the conclusion that the series converges. A candidate who quotes the ratio test without computing the ratio scores (A1) for the counterexample only. Condonation: a candidate who identifies the series as the expansion of $e^{3} - 1$ and concludes convergence from that scores full marks, provided the ratio is also given.
Follow-through: a candidate whose $N$ in (a) is wrong but who then runs a correct induction from that value keeps the method marks in (b). Forfeits: assuming the base case is $n = 1$; writing $(k+1)! > (k+1)3^{k} > 3^{k+1}$ without noticing that the second inequality needs a condition on $k$; and concluding convergence from the bound $3^{n}/n! < 1$ alone.

Why this question is hard

This item is about the two places an induction can fail, and it is constructed so that both are present at once.

The first place is the base case. A candidate who has learned induction as a template reaches for $n = 1$ without looking at whether the claim is true there. Here it is false at $n = 1$ through $n = 6$, and the last of those failures is uncomfortably close: $6! = 720$ against $3^{6} = 729$. A shortfall of nine is small enough that a candidate who rounds, or who estimates $6!$ as "about seven hundred", may well conclude that the inequality holds from $n = 6$. Evaluating both sides exactly is the only safe route, and it is why part (a) asks for the value at every integer rather than for a judgement.

The second place is the step, and this is the subtler of the two. The manipulation $(k+1)! = (k+1)k! > (k+1)3^{k}$ is correct for every $k$, and it is tempting to write the next inequality, $(k+1)3^{k} > 3^{k+1}$, as though it followed automatically. It does not: it is equivalent to $k + 1 > 3$. For $k = 1$ and $k = 2$ it is false, and a candidate who starts the induction at $n = 1$ or $n = 2$ is relying on a step that does not hold at the values being used. The item asks for that condition to be stated, which means the candidate has to notice that the comparison with $3^{n}$ imposes a threshold on the index at all. With $2^{n}$ the threshold would be $k > 1$; with $4^{n}$ it would be $k > 3$. The threshold is a property of the constant, and it is the reason the base case cannot simply be the smallest index for which the claim happens to be true.

Part (c) joins the two. The step is valid from $k = 3$ upwards, so an induction starting at $n = 3$ would propagate correctly if it could get started; but the claim is false at $n = 3$ and at $n = 4$, so there is nothing to propagate. The base case has to satisfy two conditions, not one, and part (c) exists to make the candidate say so.

Part (d) closes a loophole that a candidate might think the earlier parts opened. The inequality $n! > 3^{n}$ says that $3^{n}/n! < 1$ from $n = 7$ onwards. It is natural to read that as convergence, and it is wrong: the harmonic series is the standard counterexample, with every term below one and no finite sum. What the inequality does give is a bound, and a bound below one is not a rate. The ratio test supplies the rate, and the rate is $3/(n+1)$, which tends to zero -- far stronger than anything the inequality provides. The contrast between the two is the point of putting them in the same question.

Challenge lever
The inductive step is a one-line manipulation: if k! > 3^k then (k+1)! = (k+1)k! > (k+1)3^k, and (k+1)3^k > 3^{k+1} exactly when k > 2. That condition is invisible to a candidate who writes the chain of inequalities without checking it, because the chain looks valid for every k. The second half of the trap is the base case. The inequality is false for n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 -- n = 6 gives 720 against 729, a shortfall of nine -- and first holds at n = 7. A candidate who assumes the base case is n = 1, or who tests a few values and picks the smallest that works without noticing that the step requires n > 2, produces an induction that does not run. The final part closes the trap from the other side: the inequality makes the terms of the series 3^n/n! smaller than one, and terms smaller than one do not converge to a finite sum, so the convergence has to be settled by a test rather than by the inequality. Labelled difficulty 4 rather than 5 on the merits: the whole item turns on one inequality and a one-line manipulation, so its total demand is smaller than that of the other items in this batch even though the trap is a genuine one.

The gaokao version asks for an induction proof of an inequality between a factorial and an exponential and does not examine the threshold condition in the inductive step. Here the base case is asked for by exhaustive evaluation, the condition the step imposes on the index is a separate mark, the impossibility of starting at a smaller index is a part in its own right, and the convergence of the associated series is added so that the inequality is not mistaken for a convergence argument. The constant three was chosen so that the last failure at n = 6 is a near miss.
Verification
Both sequences were evaluated exactly for n = 1 to 15 and the crossover confirmed at n = 7, with n = 6 giving 720 against 729. The condition required by the inductive step was derived symbolically as k + 1 > 3 and checked at k = 1, 2 and 3. The ratio of successive terms of the series was formed exactly as 3/(n+1) and its limit confirmed numerically at n = 1000, where it is 2.997e-3.
