5.19 A Maclaurin series used as the solution of a differential equation that has none of its own, with the constant term of the right-hand side deciding which coefficient the equation cannot pin down

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.19-202

Topic 5: CalculusAHL 5.19 (Maclaurin series; series solution of a differential equation) with AHL 5.18 (first order differential equations)P3 · Section B13 marksdifficulty 5technology: requiredShow thatShow thatDetermineExplain

Question

The function $y$ satisfies
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = y^{2} - x, \qquad y(0) = 1.$$
The variables cannot be separated and no elementary function satisfies the equation, so its Maclaurin series
$$y = a_{0} + a_{1}x + a_{2}x^{2} + a_{3}x^{3} + \cdots = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_{n}x^{n}$$
is used instead. Write the coefficient of $x^{n}$ in $y^{2}$ as $c_{n} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} a_{k}a_{n-k}$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $a_{0} = 1$, that $a_{1} = 1$, and that the equation which determines $a_{2}$ is $2a_{2} = 2a_{1} - 1$, so that $a_{2} = \dfrac{1}{2}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that $a_{3} = \dfrac{2}{3}$ and $a_{4} = \dfrac{7}{12}$, and state the general recurrence that expresses $a_{n+1}$ in terms of $a_{0}, a_{1}, \ldots, a_{n}$.

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine $a_{5}$ and $a_{6}$ as exact fractions, showing the value of the Cauchy coefficient $c_{n}$ you use at each step.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    If the term $-x$ were deleted the solution would be $y = \dfrac{1}{1 - x}$ and every $a_{n}$ would equal $1$. Explain why $a_{1}$ is still $1$ in this question, and state the smallest value of $n$ for which $a_{n} \ne 1$.

    (Explain)

Reveal the answer

(a) Put $x = 0$ in the differential equation. The left side is $y'(0)$, and the right side is $y(0)^{2} - 0 = 1$, but it is $y(0)$ that the series names directly, so read the initial condition: $a_{0} = y(0) = 1$ (A1).

Now substitute the series into the equation. The left side is
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \sum_{n \ge 0} (n+1)a_{n+1}x^{n},$$
and the right side is $y^{2} - x$, that is $c_{0} + (c_{1} - 1)x + c_{2}x^{2} + c_{3}x^{3} + \cdots$, because $x$ is exactly the term with $n = 1$ and contributes nothing to any other coefficient (M1). Comparing coefficients of $x^{0}$ gives
$$1 \cdot a_{1} = c_{0} = a_{0}^{2} = 1,$$
so $a_{1} = 1$ (A1).

Comparing coefficients of $x^{1}$ gives $2a_{2} = c_{1} - 1$ where $c_{1} = 2a_{0}a_{1} = 2$, so
$$2a_{2} = 2a_{1} - 1 = 2 - 1 = 1, \qquad a_{2} = \frac{1}{2}.$$
The $\mathbf{AG}$ marks the required form of the equation that determines $a_{2}$: $2a_{2} = 2a_{1} - 1$. Deleting the $1$ is the trap the item is built on, and it yields $a_{2} = 1$.

(b) Coefficients of $x^{2}$ and $x^{3}$ (M1):
$$3a_{3} = c_{2} = 2a_{0}a_{2} + a_{1}^{2} = 2(1)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right) + 1 = 2, \qquad a_{3} = \frac{2}{3} \;\; (A1).$$
$$4a_{4} = c_{3} = 2a_{0}a_{3} + 2a_{1}a_{2} = 2(1)\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right) + 2(1)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{4}{3} + 1 = \frac{7}{3}, \qquad a_{4} = \frac{7}{12} \;\; (A1).$$

The general recurrence, valid for $n \ge 0$, is
$$(n+1)a_{n+1} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} a_{k}a_{n-k} - \delta_{n,1}, \qquad \delta_{n,1} = \begin{cases} 1, & n = 1 \\ 0, & n \ne 1 \end{cases} \;\; (A1).$$
Every coefficient of $y$ appears on the right through the convolution, and the single $1$ subtracts at $n = 1$ and nowhere else.

(c) $n = 4$ gives $5a_{5} = c_{4} = 2a_{0}a_{4} + 2a_{1}a_{3} + a_{2}^{2} = \dfrac{7}{6} + \dfrac{4}{3} + \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{14 + 16 + 3}{12} = \dfrac{33}{12} = \dfrac{11}{4}$ (M1), so
$$a_{5} = \frac{11}{20} \;\; (A1).$$
$n = 5$ gives $6a_{6} = c_{5} = 2a_{0}a_{5} + 2a_{1}a_{4} + 2a_{2}a_{3} = \dfrac{11}{10} + \dfrac{7}{6} + \dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{33 + 35 + 20}{30} = \dfrac{88}{30} = \dfrac{44}{15}$, so
$$a_{6} = \frac{22}{45} \;\; (A1).$$

The two checks against the derivative route agree with these: differentiating the equation gives $y'' = 2yy' - 1$, so $y''(0) = 2(1)(1) - 1 = 1$ and $a_{2} = \tfrac{y''(0)}{2!} = \tfrac{1}{2}$; and $y''' = 2y'^{2} + 2yy''$, so $y'''(0) = 2 + 2 = 4$ and $a_{3} = \tfrac{4}{3!} = \tfrac{2}{3}$.

(d) The coefficient of $x^{1}$ on the right of the equation is $c_{1} - 1$, and the parameter that this determines is $a_{2}$, not $a_{1}$. The quantity $a_{1}$ is fixed one step earlier, by the constant term: $a_{1} = c_{0} = a_{0}^{2}$, and the deleted term $-x$ contributes nothing at $n = 0$ because its own coefficient sits at $n = 1$ (A1). So $a_{1}$ is decided by the same relation in both problems and stays at $1$ (A1).

The smallest $n$ with $a_{n} \ne 1$ is therefore $n = 2$ (A1), where $a_{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ against the value $1$ that $y = 1/(1-x)$ gives. The removal of the $-x$ term shifts every coefficient from $a_{2}$ onwards while leaving $a_{0}$ and $a_{1}$ untouched, which is exactly why a candidate who checks only the first two coefficients against $1/(1-x)$ will believe the two problems agree.

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for $a_{0} = 1$ from the initial condition, (M1) for comparing coefficients at $n = 0$ and $n = 1$ with the $x$ term accounted for as a contribution to $n = 1$ only, (A1) for $a_{1} = 1$. The equation $2a_{2} = 2a_{1} - 1$ must appear for the final (A1) to be available; a candidate who writes $2a_{2} = c_{1}$ and continues has not used the $-x$ and cannot score it, even if a later part then happens to be marked consistently.

(b) (M1) for the two coefficient comparisons, (A1) for $a_{3} = \tfrac{2}{3}$, (A1) for $a_{4} = \tfrac{7}{12}$, (A1) for the general recurrence. The Kronecker delta need not be used: "all $n$ except $n = 1$ give $(n+1)a_{n+1} = c_{n}$, and at $n = 1$ there is an extra $-1$" scores the mark. A candidate who writes the recurrence as $(n+1)a_{n+1} = c_{n} - 1$ for every $n$ has misread the structure and forfeits it, because that subtracts the $1$ at every index.

(c) (M1) for forming $c_{4}$ as the convolution over $a_{0}a_{4}, a_{1}a_{3}, a_{2}a_{2}$, (A1) for $a_{5} = \tfrac{11}{20}$, (A1) for $a_{6} = \tfrac{22}{45}$. Condonation: $a_{5} = 0.55$ and $a_{6} = 0.488\ldots$ score full marks if the exact fractions also appear; a decimal alone does not, because the item asks for exact fractions.

(d) (A1) for locating $a_{1}$ in the $n = 0$ comparison, (A1) for explaining that the $-x$ term acts only at $n = 1$, (A1) for $n = 2$ with the value $\tfrac{1}{2}$. A candidate who answers $n = 1$ has counted indices from $x^{1}$ rather than from the coefficient $a_{n}$ and scores zero on the third mark; a candidate who answers $n = 3$ has attributed the shift to the $a_{2}^{2}$ cross term and also scores zero.

Follow-through: a candidate whose $a_{2}$ is $1$ and who then works consistently scores (b) and (c) by follow-through on the method marks only. Forfeits: treating $-x$ as a constant $-1$, which makes $a_{1} = 0$; omitting the cross terms $2a_{0}a_{n}$ from the convolution and using only $a_{n/2}^{2}$, which is undefined for odd $n$ and gives $a_{3} = 0$.

Why this question is hard

The technique here is old and mechanical: put the series in, multiply, compare coefficients. What makes the item hard is a single fact about the shape of the right-hand side, and that fact is easy to see and easy to overlook.

The equation is $y' = y^{2} - x$. The unknown term $y^{2}$ produces a convolution, which is what makes the recurrence long, and the known term $-x$ produces exactly one number in exactly one place. A candidate who has learned "series solution of a differential equation" as a procedure will set up $\sum (n+1)a_{n+1}x^{n} = \sum c_{n}x^{n}$, cancel the two sums, and write $(n+1)a_{n+1} = c_{n}$ for all $n$. That is the whole procedure, it is what the textbook examples do, and it is wrong here. The $-x$ does not disappear; it changes the equation at $n = 1$ alone. The result is $a_{2} = 1$ instead of $\tfrac{1}{2}$, and since every later coefficient is built from the earlier ones, the error propagates: $a_{3}$ comes out as $\tfrac{1}{3}$ rather than $\tfrac{2}{3}$, and the whole series is wrong from the third term on.

Part (d) is what stops the item being a bookkeeping exercise. By asking why $a_{1}$ survives unchanged while $a_{2}$ does not, it forces the candidate to say out loud where in the recurrence the $-x$ acts. The answer is that coefficient comparison at $n = 0$ reads off $a_{1}$ from $a_{0}^{2}$ with no help from the $-x$ at all, so the first two coefficients agree with $1/(1-x)$ and only from $a_{2}$ does the two problems part company. A candidate who tests the first two coefficients will find them right and conclude the rest are right, which is the classic wrong turn, and it is only visible if the question asks for the index.

The numbers are chosen so that the arithmetic of the convolution stays exact in fractions and so that both routes to $a_{2}$ and $a_{3}$ -- the coefficient recurrence and repeated differentiation of the differential equation -- can be run and compared. Running both is the recommended check: they use different machinery and agree, and an agreement between two methods is worth more than re-reading one.
