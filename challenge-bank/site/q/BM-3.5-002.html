3.5 When the ratios improve and the cash position does not: a financing decision the numbers cannot settle

Business management SL · BM-3.5-002

Unit 3: Finance and accounts3.5 Profitability and liquidity ratio analysisP2 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateAnalyseEvaluateState

Question

Ravensworth Ltd is a wholesaler. Extracts from its accounts for two years are shown below, together with the figures for a proposed expansion.

| | Year 1 (\$000) | Year 2 (\$000) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | 2 400 | 3 200 |
| Gross profit | 900 | 1 120 |
| Net profit before interest and tax | 288 | 352 |
| Capital employed | 1 800 | 1 920 |
| Inventory | 300 | 420 |
| Other current assets | 320 | 280 |
| Current liabilities | 310 | 500 |

The directors propose an expansion costing \$180 000, which would be financed entirely by an increase in the bank overdraft. The expansion would increase inventory by \$180 000 and is expected to raise annual net profit before interest and tax by \$46 000. No other figure in the table would change immediately.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    For each of Year 1 and Year 2, calculate the gross profit margin, the net profit margin, the return on capital employed and the current ratio. Give ratios to two decimal places and percentages to one decimal place.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Calculate the acid test ratio for each year. Hence analyse whether the financial performance of Ravensworth Ltd improved in Year 2.

    (Analyse)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Calculate, for the proposed expansion: the annual percentage return on the \$180 000 invested, the payback period, and the current ratio and acid test ratio immediately after the expansion is financed by the overdraft.

    (Calculate)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate whether the directors should proceed with the expansion on the terms described. There is no single correct answer: marks are awarded for the reasoning, including the use of the figures you have calculated.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Year 1 (A1 for the year):

  • Gross profit margin $= 900 \div 2400 = 37.5\%$
  • Net profit margin $= 288 \div 2400 = 12.0\%$
  • Return on capital employed $= 288 \div 1800 = 16.0\%$
  • Current ratio $= (300 + 320) \div 310 = 620 \div 310 = 2.00$

Year 2 (A1 for the year):

  • Gross profit margin $= 1120 \div 3200 = 35.0\%$
  • Net profit margin $= 352 \div 3200 = 11.0\%$
  • Return on capital employed $= 352 \div 1920 = 18.3\%$
  • Current ratio $= (420 + 280) \div 500 = 700 \div 500 = 1.40$

One mark is available for each of the four ratios being correct in both years (A1 each).

(b) The acid test ratio is $320 \div 310 = 1.03$ for Year 1 and $280 \div 500 = 0.56$ for Year 2 (A1).

The assessment has to be given in two directions, and a candidate who gives only one of them has not finished (A1). Profitability as a return on the resources employed improved: ROCE rose from $16.0\%$ to $18.3\%$ and absolute net profit rose from \$288 000 to \$352 000 (A1). But profitability as a margin, and liquidity, both deteriorated: gross margin fell from $37.5\%$ to $35.0\%$, net margin from $12.0\%$ to $11.0\%$, the current ratio from $2.00$ to $1.40$, and the acid test ratio from $1.03$ to $0.56$ (A1).

The acid test figure is the one that matters. At $0.56$ the business cannot cover its current liabilities out of current assets without selling inventory, and the fall from $1.03$ to $0.56$ means it has moved from comfortable to exposed in a single year. The most likely explanation is that rapid revenue growth of $33\%$ has been financed by current liabilities, which rose from \$310 000 to \$500 000, rather than by new capital.

(c) Annual return $= 46 000 \div 180 000 = 25.6\%$ (A1). Payback period $= 180 000 \div 46 000 = 3.9$ years (A1).

After the expansion, financed by the overdraft: other current assets stay at \$280 000, inventory rises to $420 + 180 = \$600 000$, so current assets total \$880 000; current liabilities rise to $500 + 180 = \$680 000$.

  • Current ratio $= 880 \div 680 = 1.29$ (A1)
  • Acid test ratio $= (880 - 600) \div 680 = 280 \div 680 = 0.41$ (A1)

(d) A developed answer distinguishes the project from the method of finance, and that is the insight the item is built around (A1).

The project looks sound on its own terms. A return of $25.6\%$ exceeds the current ROCE of $18.3\%$, so the expansion would raise the average return on capital employed, and a payback period of $3.9$ years is reasonable for a wholesaler investing in stock-holding capacity. On the quantitative evidence alone the expansion earns more than it costs.

The method of finance does not. Overdraft finance is repayable on demand and is a current liability, so it pushes the current ratio down from $1.40$ to $1.29$ and the acid test ratio from $0.56$ to $0.41$ (A1). At $0.41$ the business would be holding \$600 000 of inventory against \$680 000 of liabilities falling due within the year, and the additional inventory is precisely the asset the acid test excludes. Financing a three-to-four-year investment with money that can be recalled at short notice is a maturity mismatch, and the ratios are right to flag it.

The conclusion candidates should reach is that the expansion should proceed but should not be financed this way. If the same \$180 000 were raised as a long-term loan, current liabilities would stay at \$500 000 and the current ratio would be $880 \div 500 = 1.76$, with the acid test ratio unchanged at $0.56$ -- that is, the expansion would improve the current ratio rather than damage it. The objection is to the overdraft, not to the investment (A1).

A candidate who reaches the opposite conclusion -- that the business should not expand at all -- can still be credited fully if the judgement is supported by the figures, for example by arguing that an acid test ratio of $0.56$ before any expansion is already too low to take on further fixed commitments, and that the business should rebuild liquidity first. What earns no credit is letting the $25.6\%$ return settle the question on its own, because that number is silent about when the money has to be repaid.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award one (A1) for each of the five ratios calculated correctly for BOTH years, up to a maximum of five marks. Ratios to two decimal places and percentages to one decimal place, as instructed; condone a correctly calculated ratio given to a different number of decimal places. Accept ROCE calculated on capital employed as given. Do not award a mark where one year is correct and the other is not.
(b) Award (A1) for recognising that the answer is two-directional, (A1) for the improvement in ROCE and in absolute profit, (A1) for the deterioration in the two margins and in the two liquidity ratios. A candidate who says only 'it improved' or only 'it deteriorated' is capped at one mark regardless of how many correct figures are quoted.
(c) Award (A1) for 25.6 per cent, (A1) for 3.9 years (accept 3.91), (A1) for the current ratio 1.29 and (A1) for the acid test ratio 0.41. Follow-through: a candidate who wrongly leaves current liabilities at 500 obtains 1.76 and 0.56 and should still be awarded the two marks for the ratios if the arithmetic on their own figures is correct, but loses the marks if inventory was not added to current assets.
(d) This is an evaluative response marked on the quality of the reasoning rather than on the conclusion. Award (A1) for a judgement that is supported by at least two of the calculated figures; (A1) for distinguishing the project from the method of finance, or for an equivalent insight such as recognising the maturity mismatch; (A1) for a developed conclusion -- including the alternative of long-term finance, or a coherent contrary case supported by the figures.
Accept any reasonable conclusion provided it is supported. Do NOT award the final mark for an answer that simply restates that 25.6 per cent is greater than 18.3 per cent and advises proceeding.

Why this question is hard

The item is built so that the arithmetic and the answer pull in different directions, and the gap between them is the whole question. Part (c) produces a return of $25.6\%$ against a current ROCE of $18.3\%$ and a payback of $3.9$ years. Every one of those numbers says yes. Part (c) also produces an acid test ratio of $0.41$, and that number says no. A candidate who stops at the first set has done the calculation and missed the decision.

What makes it a judgement rather than a tie-break is that the conflict is not between two estimates of the same thing -- it is between two different questions. The return and the payback ask whether the expansion is worth doing. The liquidity ratios ask whether it can be survived. Both are legitimate, and neither reduces to the other. The candidate has to hold them together long enough to see that they are not actually in conflict about the same object: the expansion is fine, the overdraft is not, and financing the one with the other is the single decision that is wrong.

That is why the answer rewards the long-term-finance comparison even though it is not asked for. Recomputing the current ratio at $1.76$ under a loan settles the matter in a way no amount of argument can: the project improves liquidity when it is financed properly, so the objection was never to the project. This is the move the item is looking for, and it is a genuinely IB move -- using a second quantitative scenario to test a qualitative conclusion.

There is also a quieter trap in part (b). ROCE rises while both margins fall, and the absolute profit rises while the percentage earned on each dollar of revenue falls. Candidates read 'profit went up' as 'performance improved' and stop. The figures are arranged so that the opposite reading is equally available, and the mark scheme rewards holding both.

This is adapted from the UK A-level Business treatment of ratio analysis, where a two-year comparison is followed by an evaluative judgement on a proposed investment. The A-level version typically asks simply whether the business should proceed. Here the proposal is specified as overdraft-financed and the inventory treatment is made explicit, so that the liquidity effect can be calculated rather than asserted, and the mark scheme credits the alternative financing comparison -- which the source does not require.
