The enrichment that cannot move the energy per fission

Physics HL · PHYS-E.4-001

E.4 Fission (energy released in spontaneous and neutron-induced fission, mass defect and binding energy, chain reactions, the role of control rods and moderators, energy production in a nuclear power plant)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatDetermineCalculateExplain

Question

A nuclear power reactor produces a steady thermal power $P = 3.0\ \mathrm{GW}$ from the neutron-induced fission of uranium-235. A representative fission reaction is
$$^{235}\mathrm{U} + ^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \rightarrow\ ^{141}\mathrm{Ba} + ^{92}\mathrm{Kr} + 3\,^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n}.$$
The core is loaded with $1.0\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{kg}$ of uranium enriched to $3.0\%$ uranium-235 by mass, the remainder being uranium-238. The chain reaction is held steady by control rods, and the fast neutrons released are slowed by a moderator before they can induce further fission.

Data (2025 data booklet): atomic mass of $^{235}\mathrm{U} = 235.0439\ \mathrm{u}$; atomic mass of $^{141}\mathrm{Ba} = 140.9144\ \mathrm{u}$; atomic mass of $^{92}\mathrm{Kr} = 91.9262\ \mathrm{u}$; mass of a neutron $= 1.008665\ \mathrm{u}$; $1\ \mathrm{u} = 1.661\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$; $1\ \mathrm{u} = 931.5\ \mathrm{MeV}\,c^{-2}$; $1\ \mathrm{MeV} = 1.602\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{J}$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that the energy released in this reaction is about $2.8\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J}$, and state the same energy in $\mathrm{MeV}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    The reactor operates at a constant thermal power. Determine the number of fission reactions per second and the mass of uranium-235 that undergoes fission each second.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Calculate the mass of uranium-235 consumed in one day and the mass converted to energy in one day, and explain why these two masses differ by a factor of about $10^{3}$.

    (Calculate)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the total energy that the uranium-235 in the core can release and the operating lifetime of the core at $3.0\ \mathrm{GW}$. Hence explain what would change, and what would not, if the fuel were enriched to $4.5\%$ instead.

    (Explain)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The energy released is the mass defect of the reaction multiplied by $c^{2}$; the incident neutron is balanced by one of the three emitted neutrons, so
$$\Delta m = m_{\mathrm{U}} - m_{\mathrm{Ba}} - m_{\mathrm{Kr}} - 2m_{\mathrm{n}}$$ (M1)
$$= 235.0439 - 140.9144 - 91.9262 - 2(1.008665) = 0.18597\ \mathrm{u}$$ (A1)
$$E = \Delta m\,c^{2} = 0.18597 \times 931.5 = 173.2\ \mathrm{MeV}$$ (A1)
$$E = 173.2 \times 1.602\times10^{-13} = 2.78\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J}$$ (A1)(AG)

(b) The thermal power equals the energy per fission multiplied by the fission rate, $P = R\,E$, so
$$R = \frac{P}{E} = \frac{3.0\times10^{9}}{2.775\times10^{-11}} = 1.08\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$$ (M1)(A1)
Each fission removes one uranium-235 atom of mass
$$m_{1} = 235.0439 \times 1.661\times10^{-27} = 3.904\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$$ (M1)
so the mass consumed each second is
$$R\,m_{1} = (1.081\times10^{20})(3.904\times10^{-25}) = 4.22\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$$ (A1)

(c) In one day the fuel consumed is
$$4.22\times10^{-5} \times 86400 = 3.65\ \mathrm{kg}$$ (A1)
while the mass converted to energy follows from $E = mc^{2}$:
$$m = \frac{P\,t}{c^{2}} = \frac{(3.0\times10^{9})(86400)}{(2.998\times10^{8})^{2}} = 2.88\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg}$$ (M1)(A1)
The ratio is $3.65/(2.88\times10^{-3}) = 1.3\times10^{3}$, close to $235/0.186$: almost all of the fuel mass is carried by nucleons that survive the reaction, and only the mass defect becomes energy. (A1)

(d) The core holds $0.030 \times 1.0\times10^{5} = 3.0\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{kg}$ of uranium-235, or $N = 3.0\times10^{3}/(3.904\times10^{-25}) = 7.7\times10^{27}$ nuclei, so
$$E_{\mathrm{tot}} = N\,E = (7.7\times10^{27})(2.775\times10^{-11}) = 2.1\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{J}$$ (M1)(A1)
At $3.0\ \mathrm{GW}$ the operating lifetime is
$$t = \frac{2.1\times10^{17}}{3.0\times10^{9}} = 7.1\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s} \approx 820\ \mathrm{days}$$ (A1)
Raising the enrichment to $4.5\%$ leaves the energy per fission unchanged, since it is fixed by the mass defect, and leaves the fission rate needed for $3.0\ \mathrm{GW}$ unchanged; it multiplies the number of fissionable nuclei by $1.5$, so the total energy and the lifetime rise by that factor. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) requires the mass defect as reactants minus products, with the three emitted neutrons counted. A candidate who omits them from the products, or counts only two, obtains the wrong $\Delta m$ and forfeits the value marks but may still earn the conversion mark by follow-through. Accept $173\ \mathrm{MeV}$ or $2.8\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J}$; using $1\ \mathrm{MeV} = 1.60\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{J}$ gives $2.77\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J}$, also accepted.

(b) The (M1) is for $P = R\,E$; a candidate who divides $E$ by $P$ has inverted it and loses the value mark. Accept $1.1\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

(c) Follow-through from (b) applies throughout. The final mark is for the explanation, not the number: the fuel consumed is the mass of the uranium nuclei, whereas only the mass defect, roughly $0.08\%$ of it, becomes energy.

(d) The (M1) is for multiplying by the enrichment fraction. Accept lifetimes between $7\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s}$ and $850\ \mathrm{days}$. The final mark needs both halves: the energy per fission is unchanged, and the total energy scales with the number of nuclei. Claiming the reactor becomes more powerful at a fixed control-rod setting confuses the rate with the energy per fission.

Why this question is hard

The question turns on a distinction the word energy conceals. A reactor's power is an energy per second, and it factorises into the energy released per fission and the number of fissions per second. The first factor is a property of the nucleus, fixed by the mass defect of the reaction; for uranium fuel it is close to $2.8\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J}$, and no amount of enrichment or moderation changes it. The second factor is a property of the reactor, set by how many neutrons survive to cause further fission, which is exactly what the moderator, the control rods and the enrichment influence.

A candidate who has learned that enrichment improves the fuel reaches for it to explain the power, and finds that it does nothing to the energy per fission. The correct account is that enrichment changes the number of fissionable nuclei, and therefore the total energy the core can deliver and the length of time over which it can deliver it, while the power is held steady by the control rods. A moderator acts in the same place in the argument: it slows neutrons so that the fission cross-section is larger, changing how readily the chain reaction proceeds, but the energy released in a single fission is untouched.

The mass bookkeeping is the second trap. The mass of uranium consumed is about a thousand times the mass converted to energy, because the mass defect is only about $0.08\%$ of the fuel mass. A candidate who uses the fuel mass rather than the mass defect in $E = mc^{2}$ overestimates the energy by three orders of magnitude. The ratio $235/0.186 \approx 1.3\times10^{3}$ is a useful check on this.

Classic wrong turns: treating the moderator as adding energy; assuming that doubling the enrichment doubles the power at a fixed control-rod setting; and using the fuel mass rather than the mass defect in $E = mc^{2}$.

Metadata

Challenge lever
The energy released in a single fission is fixed by the mass defect of the reaction and is therefore almost completely insensitive to the enrichment of the fuel or the choice of moderator; what enrichment and moderation actually change is the number of fissionable nuclei and the rate at which the chain reaction proceeds, so the quantity the student instinctively reaches for to raise the reactor's output is not the quantity that sets it.
