D.4 Choosing the turns ratio that delivers most power to a load, where stepping the voltage up further reduces the power delivered because the reflected load falls towards the source resistance

Physics HL · PHYS-D.4-301

Question

The figure shows a source of e.m.f. $E = 12\ {\rm V}$ and internal resistance $r = 4.0\ \Omega$ connected to the primary coil of a transformer, whose secondary coil is connected across a resistive load of resistance $R = 100\ \Omega$. The transformer is ideal, so no power is lost in it and no current flows in the secondary when the primary circuit is open. Let
$$k = \frac{N_{s}}{N_{p}}$$
denote the turns ratio of the secondary to the primary. The source is used to drive this load, and the question of interest is how much power actually arrives at the load as $k$ is varied.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the load, as seen from the primary side of the transformer, behaves like a resistance of $R' = R/k^{2}$, and hence show that the primary current is $I_{p} = \dfrac{E}{r + R/k^{2}}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that the power delivered to the load is maximised when $R' = r$, and hence determine the turns ratio $k$ that delivers maximum power to the $100\ \Omega$ load. Determine the value of that maximum power.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine the power delivered to the load when $k = 1$, that is when the load is connected straight across the source with no transformation at all. Hence determine the factor by which the correctly chosen transformer increases the power delivered to the load.

    (Determine)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    At the maximum-power turns ratio found in part (b), determine the primary current, the potential difference across the primary coil, the secondary potential difference and the secondary current, and hence verify that the power entering the primary equals the power leaving the secondary.

    (Determine)
  5. [3 marks](e)

    A student argues that a larger turns ratio always delivers more power, because it always produces a larger secondary voltage. Take $k = 10$ and quantify what the argument costs, then explain why it is wrong in terms of where the missing power goes.

    (Explain)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Let the potential difference across the primary be $V_{p}$ and across the secondary be $V_{s}$. For an ideal transformer $V_{s} = kV_{p}$ (M1). The secondary current is therefore $I_{s} = V_{s}/R = kV_{p}/R$. Since the transformer is ideal, power is conserved across it, so $V_{p}I_{p} = V_{s}I_{s}$, giving
$$I_{p} = \frac{V_{s}I_{s}}{V_{p}} = \frac{(kV_{p})(kV_{p}/R)}{V_{p}} = \frac{k^{2}V_{p}}{R} \;(A1),$$
and hence the effective resistance presented to the primary is
$$R' = \frac{V_{p}}{I_{p}} = \frac{R}{k^{2}} \;(A1).$$
The primary circuit is then a simple series circuit containing $E$, $r$ and $R'$, so $I_{p} = E/(r + R/k^{2})$ as required.
(b) The power dissipated in the reflected load is the power delivered to the real load, since the transformer is lossless, so
$$P = I_{p}^{2}R' = \left(\frac{E}{r + R'}\right)^{2} R' = \frac{E^{2}R'}{(r + R')^{2}} \;(M1).$$
Treating this as a function of $R'$ and differentiating, or equivalently writing $(r+R')^{2}/R' = r^{2}/R' + 2r + R'$ and minimising the bracket with respect to $R'$, gives a stationary point at $R' = r$ (M1); that $r^{2}/R' + R'$ is minimised at $R' = r$ follows from the symmetry of the two terms under $R' \mapsto r^{2}/R'$, and since $P \to 0$ both as $R' \to 0$ and as $R' \to \infty$, this stationary point is a maximum (A1). Hence the optimum satisfies
$$\frac{R}{k^{2}} = r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{100}{k^{2}} = 4.0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k^{2} = 25 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = 5.0 \;(A1),$$
and the maximum power delivered is
$$P_{\max} = \frac{E^{2}}{4r} = \frac{12^{2}}{4 \times 4.0} = \frac{144}{16} = 9.0\ {\rm W} \;(A1).$$
(c) With $k = 1$ there is no transformation and $R' = R = 100\ \Omega$, so
$$I_{p} = \frac{12}{4.0 + 100} = 0.115\ {\rm A} \quad\text{and}\quad P = I_{p}^{2}R = (0.115)^{2} \times 100 = 1.33\ {\rm W} \;(M1)(A1).$$
The factor by which the correctly matched transformer improves matters is
$$\frac{9.0}{1.33} = 6.8 \;(A1),$$
so nearly seven times as much power reaches the same $100\ \Omega$ load, coming from the same source.
(d) At $k = 5.0$ we have $R' = 100/25 = 4.0\ \Omega = r$, so
$$I_{p} = \frac{12}{4.0 + 4.0} = 1.5\ {\rm A}, \qquad V_{p} = I_{p}R' = 1.5 \times 4.0 = 6.0\ {\rm V} \;(M1)(A1).$$
Then $V_{s} = kV_{p} = 5.0 \times 6.0 = 30\ {\rm V}$ and $I_{s} = V_{s}/R = 30/100 = 0.30\ {\rm A}$. Checking power: entering the primary, $V_{p}I_{p} = 6.0 \times 1.5 = 9.0\ {\rm W}$; leaving the secondary, $V_{s}I_{s} = 30 \times 0.30 = 9.0\ {\rm W}$ (A1). They agree, as they must for an ideal transformer, and both equal the $9.0\ {\rm W}$ found in part (b). Note that the total power supplied by the source is $EI_{p} = 12 \times 1.5 = 18\ {\rm W}$, the other $9.0\ {\rm W}$ being dissipated in the internal resistance itself.
(e) At $k = 10$ the reflected load is $R' = 100/100 = 1.0\ \Omega$, so
$$I_{p} = \frac{12}{4.0 + 1.0} = 2.4\ {\rm A}, \qquad P_{\rm load} = I_{p}^{2}R' = (2.4)^{2} \times 1.0 = 5.76\ {\rm W} \;(M1),$$
which is below the $9.0\ {\rm W}$ available at $k = 5.0$; the design therefore costs $9.0 - 5.76 = 3.2\ {\rm W}$, a loss of about $36\%$ of the achievable output (A1). The student's argument fails because it tracks the secondary voltage but not the current that can be sustained through it: the power lost in the internal resistance is $I_{p}^{2}r = (2.4)^{2} \times 4.0 = 23\ {\rm W}$, so of the $EI_{p} = 29\ {\rm W}$ that the source supplies, twenty-three watts is dissipated inside the source itself and only 5.76 W survives to the load. Raising $k$ further concentrates more and more of the source's effort into its own internal resistance (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for quoting $V_{s} = kV_{p}$ for an ideal transformer, (A1) for forming $I_{s}$ and then using $V_{p}I_{p} = V_{s}I_{s}$ to reach an expression for $I_{p}$, (A1) for $R' = R/k^{2}$. Candidates may instead quote $V_{s}I_{s} = V_{p}I_{p}$ and $I_{s} = I_{p}/k$; either route earns the marks. Accept a statement that the impedance transformation follows from combining the two transformer ratios.
(b) (M1) for writing $P = E^{2}R'/(r+R')^{2}$ in any equivalent form, (M1) for identifying the maximum by differentiation or by the symmetric-bracket argument, (A1) for justifying that it is a maximum rather than a minimum, (A1) for $k = 5.0$, (A1) for $9.0\ {\rm W}$. Five fields are listed; credit the strongest four. Note particularly that $P_{\max} = E^{2}/(4r)$ is independent of $R$: a candidate may legitimately observe that the $100\ \Omega$ value fixes $k$ but not the ceiling. Follow-through applies from a wrong $k$ provided part (d) is consistent with it.
(c) (M1) for substituting $R' = 100\ \Omega$ into the series circuit, (A1) for $1.33\ {\rm W}$ (accept $1.3\ {\rm W}$), (A1) for the factor $6.8$. A candidate who computes the ratio upside down, giving $0.15$, loses the final mark; the working should make the direction unambiguous since part (d) uses $k = 5$.
(d) (M1) for $I_{p} = 1.5\ {\rm A}$, (A1) for $V_{p} = 6.0\ {\rm V}$ together with $V_{s} = 30\ {\rm V}$ and $I_{s} = 0.30\ {\rm A}$, (A1) for the two power products agreeing at $9.0\ {\rm W}$. The remark about the source supplying $18\ {\rm W}$ is not required but may be credited where a candidate has otherwise failed to justify why half the power is lost.
(e) (M1) for computing $R' = 1.0\ \Omega$ and $I_{p} = 2.4\ {\rm A}$ at $k = 10$, (A1) for the resulting $5.76\ {\rm W}$ and the quantified shortfall, (A1) for explaining that the missing power is dissipated in $r$ rather than anywhere in the transformer. Candidates who simply assert 'the current gets smaller' without tying it to the reflected resistance lose the final mark; the transformer is ideal and no power is lost inside it, so any explanation invoking losses there is wrong.

Why this question is hard

This item exists because a transformer is usually taught as a device for choosing a voltage, and that framing quietly invites the conclusion that more voltage means more power. It does not, and the reason is that a transformer does not only transform voltage; it transforms impedance, by the square of the turns ratio. Every time a candidate doubled the turns ratio hoping for more output they also quartered the resistance the source sees, and those two effects run in opposite directions.

The clean way to see the answer is to forget the transformer exists for a moment. Reflected back to the primary the circuit is just $E$ in series with $r$ and $R'$, and the maximum-power transfer theorem says the load receives most when it equals the source resistance. Here $r = 4.0\\ \\Omega$ and the real load is $100\\ \\Omega$, so $k^{2} = 25$ and $k = 5$. Everything else follows: half the e.m.f. appears across the internal resistance and half across the load, so $6.0\\ {\\rm V}$ each, and the power is $E^{2}/(4r) = 9.0\\ {\\rm W}$. Notably that ceiling does not depend on $R$ at all -- the value of $R$ only decides which turns ratio achieves it.

Part (c) supplies the comparison that makes the result concrete, and sixteenfold is worth noticing: connecting the same $100\\ \\Omega$ directly to the same source delivers barely $1.33\\ {\\rm W}$ out of a possible $9.0\\ {\\rm W}$. Impedance matching is not a refinement, it is the difference between a design that works and one that wastes six sevenths of what it has.

The $k = 10$ case in part (e) is where the naive rule pays its price. The secondary voltage really is larger, and that is exactly why the argument is seductive: nothing the student says about voltage is false. But the reflected load has dropped to $1.0\\ \\Omega$, the current drawn rises to $2.4\\ {\\rm A}$, and $I^{2}r = 23\\ {\\rm W}$ is dissipated inside the source against only $5.76\\ {\\rm W}$ reaching the load. The transformer is not losing anything -- an ideal one cannot -- and there is nowhere else for the power to go. Tracking one quantity to the exclusion of the other is the whole error.

Finally, it is worth flagging the practical conclusion, because it is the same conclusion in audio amplifiers, antenna feeds and every mains adapter with a ferrite core in it: matching is a compromise between efficiency and delivered power. At maximum power transfer exactly half the source's output is wasted internally, which is why power distribution networks deliberately mismatch in the other direction and accept lower delivered power for far better efficiency. A candidate who reaches that unprompted has grasped why the optimum is interior rather than extreme.

Challenge lever
A transformer appears to be a device that lets you choose a voltage freely, and the naive design conclusion is therefore to step up until the load sees whatever voltage it wants. But the source has internal resistance, and the transformer also transforms impedance: raising $N_{s}/N_{p}$ reflects the load back to the primary reduced by the square of the turns ratio. Step up too hard and the reflected load falls below the internal resistance, most of the e.m.f. is dropped across $r$ instead, and the power actually arriving at the load falls. The optimum is a genuine interior maximum, not an extreme, and the cost of getting it wrong is quantifiable in watts.

Here the two are fused: the turns ratio becomes the design variable, the reflected resistance is derived rather than given, and the candidate must find the interior optimum themselves and then quantify what an over-designed transformer costs in watts. Verification
The reflected-resistance derivation was checked both ways: from Vp Ip = Vs Is with Vs = k Vp and Is = k Vp / R, giving Ip = k^2 Vp / R and hence R-prime = R/k^2.
