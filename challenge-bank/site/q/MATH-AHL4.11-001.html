A defective rate that is not independent of its machine

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.11-001

SL 4.11 (formal definition and use of the probability formulae for conditional probability, independence and the addition rule)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedFindDetermineShow that

Question

Items are produced either by machine $A$, with $P(A)=0.6$, or by machine $B$, with $P(B)=0.4$. The probability that an item is defective is $0.03$ given that it came from $A$ and $0.05$ given that it came from $B$. Let $D$ denote the event that an item is defective.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Find $P(D)$, the probability that a randomly chosen item is defective.

    (Find)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Given that an item is defective, find the probability that it came from machine $B$, giving your answer as an exact fraction.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine, showing your reasoning, whether the events 'from machine $A$' and 'defective' are independent.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    The machines are adjusted so that $P(D\mid A)=P(D\mid B)=0.04$. Show that after the adjustment the events 'from machine $A$' and 'defective' are independent.

    (Show that)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The law of total probability gives $P(D)=P(A)P(D\mid A)+P(B)P(D\mid B)$ (M1) $=0.6\times 0.03+0.4\times 0.05$ (A1) $=0.018+0.020=0.038$ (A1)(A1). The two machine cases are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, so the overall defective rate is the weighted average of the two conditional rates, using the machine shares as weights, which is why the overall rate lies between the two conditional rates.

(b) Bayes' theorem gives $P(B\mid D)=\frac{P(B)P(D\mid B)}{P(D)}$ (M1) $=\frac{0.4\times 0.05}{0.038}$ (A1) $=\frac{0.020}{0.038}=\frac{20}{38}=\frac{10}{19}$ (A1). The conditioning is reversed here, so the numerator is the joint probability of $B$ and $D$, which is why the answer is not simply the given rate $0.05$ but the share of the defects attributable to machine $B$. This reversal of the conditioning is the whole content of Bayes' theorem.

(c) The events are independent exactly when $P(D\mid A)=P(D)$ (M1). Here $P(D\mid A)=0.03$ whereas $P(D)=0.038$ (A1), and these values are not equal (R1). Equivalently $P(A\cap D)=0.6\times 0.03=0.018$ while $P(A)P(D)=0.6\times 0.038=0.0228$ (A1), so the events are not independent when judged by the product of the marginal probabilities. Comparing the conditional rate with the overall rate is decisive because the two differ even though both numbers are small and both conditional rates lie close together.

(d) After the adjustment the overall rate is $P(D)=0.6\times 0.04+0.4\times 0.04=0.04$ (M1)(A1), while the conditional rate is $P(D\mid A)=0.04$ (A1). These are equal (R1)(AG). Because the conditional rate now coincides with the unconditional rate, knowing that an item came from machine $A$ gives no information about whether it is defective, so the two events are independent, and this remains true whatever the machine shares happen to be.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for the law of total probability with both branches. The (A1) is for the substitution, and the two remaining (A1) marks are for the two products $0.018$ and $0.020$ and for their sum $0.038$. A candidate who averages the two rates without weighting them earns only the method mark.

(b) Award (M1) for Bayes' theorem and (A1) for the substitution. The (A1) requires the exact fraction $\frac{10}{19}$; a decimal such as $0.526$ does not earn it unless the fraction is also shown.

(c) The (M1) is for the independence test $P(D\mid A)=P(D)$. The (A1) is for the value $0.038$, and the (R1) for the explicit comparison with $0.03$. The final (A1) is for the alternative product test $0.018$ against $0.0228$ and the conclusion. A bare statement that the events are dependent earns at most two marks.

(d) Award (M1)(A1) for recomputing the overall rate as $0.04$, the (A1) for the conditional rate $0.04$, and the (R1)(AG) for observing that the two are equal. Follow through from an incorrect part (a) is allowed provided the equality is correctly tested.

Why this question is hard

The item contrasts a conditional defective rate with an overall defective rate, and asks whether the machine and the defect are independent. Part (a) applies the law of total probability, giving $0.038$ as the weighted average of $0.03$ and $0.05$. Part (b) reverses the conditioning with Bayes' theorem and produces the exact fraction $\frac{10}{19}$. Both parts are standard, and they supply the numbers that the later parts need.

Part (c) is the lever. The events from machine $A$ and defective are independent exactly when $P(D\mid A)$ equals $P(D)$. Here the conditional rate is $0.03$ while the overall rate is $0.038$, so the events are dependent. Candidates frequently judge independence by looking at the two given conditional rates, $0.03$ and $0.05$, and concluding from their difference that the events must be dependent; this reasoning is not the definition, and it happens to give the wrong reason even when it gives the right verdict.

Part (d) removes the difference by setting both conditional rates to $0.04$. The overall rate then also becomes $0.04$, and the conditional and unconditional probabilities coincide, so the events become independent. The numbers were chosen so that the weighted average $0.038$ differs from both conditional rates and the fraction $\frac{10}{19}$ is exact, and so that the adjusted case gives the clean equality $0.04=0.04$. A candidate who reasons from the visible gap between $0.03$ and $0.05$ may reach the right verdict for the wrong reason, and part (d) is designed to expose that shortcut by removing the gap entirely.
