A.2 A one-dimensional elastic collision whose simultaneous conservation equations factorise, so that one of the two mathematical solutions is the collision that never happened

Physics HL · PHYS-A.2-301

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.2 Forces and momentum: momentum and impulse in one dimension, conservation of momentum in elastic collisions. With A.3 Work, energy and power: conservation of kinetic energy in an elastic collisionP214 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedDetermineShow thatShow thatDetermine

Question

Two gliders rest on a horizontal air track on which friction may be neglected. Glider 1 has mass $m_{1} = 2.0\ {\rm kg}$ and moves to the right at $u_{1} = 4.0\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$. Glider 2 has mass $m_{2} = 1.0\ {\rm kg}$ and is initially at rest. Glider 1 catches up with glider 2 and the two collide. The collision is perfectly elastic, so that the total kinetic energy is conserved as well as the total momentum. Take the positive direction to be to the right, and denote the velocities immediately after the collision by $v_{1}$ and $v_{2}$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Write down the two conservation equations for this collision, solve them simultaneously for $v_{1}$ and $v_{2}$, and hence determine both mathematical solutions. State which solution describes the actual collision and explain in one sentence what the other one describes.

    (Determine)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Now consider general initial velocities $u_{1}$ and $u_{2}$. Show that the two conservation equations together imply
    $$m_{1}(u_{1} - v_{1})\bigl(u_{1} + v_{1} - v_{2} - u_{2}\bigr) = 0 ,$$
    identify what each of the two factors being zero means physically, and hence show that for a genuine elastic collision
    $$u_{1} - u_{2} = -(v_{1} - v_{2}) .$$
    State in words what this last result says about the relative speed.

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Show that the fraction of the initial kinetic energy that is transferred to glider 2, when glider 2 is initially at rest, is
    $$\frac{4m_{1}m_{2}}{(m_{1} + m_{2})^{2}} ,$$
    and verify this result numerically for the data given.

    (Show that)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    Determine the ratio $m_{1} : m_{2}$ that maximises the fraction transferred, and state the maximum value of the fraction. State also what happens to the incident glider in that case.

    (Determine)
  5. [2 marks](e)

    Explain why the conservation equations carry a solution in which nothing changes, and why the physical hypothesis of a collision is what removes it rather than anything in the algebra itself.

    (Explain)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) With $u_{2} = 0$, conservation of momentum gives
$$2.0(4.0) = 2.0v_{1} + 1.0v_{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2v_{1} + v_{2} = 8 \;(M1),$$
and conservation of kinetic energy gives
$$\tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)(4.0)^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)v_{1}^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}(1.0)v_{2}^{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2v_{1}^{2} + v_{2}^{2} = 32 \;(M1).$$
Substituting $v_{2} = 8 - 2v_{1}$ into the second equation,
$$2v_{1}^{2} + (8 - 2v_{1})^{2} = 32 \;\Longrightarrow\; 6v_{1}^{2} - 32v_{1} + 32 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 3v_{1}^{2} - 16v_{1} + 16 = 0 ,$$
which factorises as $(3v_{1} - 4)(v_{1} - 4) = 0$. Hence either $v_{1} = 4.0\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$, giving $v_{2} = 0$, or $v_{1} = \tfrac{4}{3} = 1.33\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$, giving $v_{2} = 8 - \tfrac{8}{3} = \tfrac{16}{3} = 5.33\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$ (A1)(A1). The second pair describes the actual collision. The first pair describes no collision at all: every body continues unchanged, which conserves momentum and energy trivially. Checking the accepted pair: momentum afterwards is $2.0(1.33) + 1.0(5.33) = 8.0\ {\rm kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$, equal to the initial $2.0 \times 4.0 = 8.0$, and the kinetic energy afterwards is $\tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)(1.33)^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}(1.0)(5.33)^{2} = 16\ {\rm J}$, equal to the initial $\tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)(4.0)^{2} = 16\ {\rm J}$.
(b) Conservation of momentum gives $m_{1}(u_{1} - v_{1}) = m_{2}(v_{2} - u_{2})$, call this equation (i). Conservation of kinetic energy gives $m_{1}(u_{1}^{2} - v_{1}^{2}) = m_{2}(v_{2}^{2} - u_{2}^{2})$, which factorises as
$$m_{1}(u_{1} - v_{1})(u_{1} + v_{1}) = m_{2}(v_{2} - u_{2})(v_{2} + u_{2}) \;(M1)(A1),$$
call this (ii). Using (i) to replace $m_{2}(v_{2} - u_{2})$ by $m_{1}(u_{1} - v_{1})$ on the right-hand side of (ii) and bringing everything to one side,
$$m_{1}(u_{1} - v_{1})\bigl[(u_{1} + v_{1}) - (v_{2} + u_{2})\bigr] = 0 \;(A1),$$
as required. The first factor vanishing, $u_{1} = v_{1}$, together with (i) forces $u_{2} = v_{2}$: this is the solution in which neither body changes its velocity, i.e. no interaction took place (A1). For a genuine collision that factor is non-zero, so the second must vanish, giving $u_{1} + v_{1} = v_{2} + u_{2}$, that is
$$u_{1} - u_{2} = -(v_{1} - v_{2}) \;(A1).$$
The quantity $u_{1} - u_{2}$ is the velocity of 1 relative to 2 before the collision, and $v_{1} - v_{2}$ the same relative velocity afterwards; the result states that for a perfectly elastic collision the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation (A1). It says nothing about the speeds individually, only about the rate at which the gap between the two bodies closes and then reopens.
(c) Using the result of part (b) together with conservation of momentum, or solving the two equations with $u_{2} = 0$, the velocity acquired by the struck glider is
$$v_{2} = \frac{2m_{1}}{m_{1} + m_{2}}\,u_{1} \;(M1).$$
The kinetic energy gained by glider 2 is $\tfrac{1}{2}m_{2}v_{2}^{2}$ and the initial kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2}m_{1}u_{1}^{2}$, so the fraction transferred is
$$\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}m_{2}\left(\frac{2m_{1}u_{1}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}\right)^{2}}{\tfrac{1}{2}m_{1}u_{1}^{2}} = \frac{4m_{1}^{2}m_{2}}{(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}m_{1}} = \frac{4m_{1}m_{2}}{(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}} \;(A1)(A1),$$
the factors $u_{1}^{2}$ cancelling, so that the fraction depends on the masses alone and not on the speed. For the data, $4(2.0)(1.0)/(3.0)^{2} = 8/9 = 0.889$, and independently the energy of glider 2 afterwards is $\tfrac{1}{2}(1.0)(5.33)^{2} = 14.2\ {\rm J}$ against an initial $16\ {\rm J}$, giving $14.2/16 = 0.89$ (A1), which agrees.
(d) The fraction $\dfrac{4m_{1}m_{2}}{(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}}$ is maximised when $m_{1} = m_{2}$, that is for the ratio $1 : 1$ (A1); writing $m_{1} = m_{2} = m$ gives $4m^{2}/(2m)^{2} = 1$, so all of the kinetic energy is transferred and the incident glider comes to a complete stop while the struck glider moves off with the velocity the incident one had (A1). This is why a Newton's cradle transmits motion through a line of identical elastic spheres with such apparent tidiness.
(e) Both conservation laws are statements about totals, and totals are conserved equally well by an interaction and by its absence: if no contact occurs, nothing changes and both totals are trivially preserved (A1). Nothing inside the algebra distinguishes the two cases, because the algebra only knows that the sums before and after agree -- it has no way of encoding that a force actually acted. The unwanted solution is removed only by the physical premise that the bodies did collide, which is expressed mathematically by requiring $u_{1} \neq v_{1}$ so that the first factor is non-zero (A1). This is why the relative-speed result of part (b) is a consequence for collisions and not a general property of the two conservation laws.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for each of the two conservation equations written correctly with the data substituted, (A1) for reaching the quadratic and both roots, (A1) for the accepted pair $1.33$ and $5.33\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$ together with the statement that the other pair describes no interaction. Three fields; credit three marks. Candidates who obtain only one root by using the standard formula lose nothing provided they then identify the second pair by inspection, since the item is precisely about the second pair existing.
(b) (M1) for writing both conservation equations in general form, (A1) for factorising the difference of squares, (A1) for substituting (i) into (ii) to reach the stated product, (A1) for identifying the meaning of the vanishing first factor, (A1) for the relative-speed statement and its verbal interpretation. Five fields; cap at four. Accept any equivalent algebraic route, including combining the equations by dividing one by the other, provided the candidate notes that this division is legitimate only when the common factor is non-zero. A candidate who divides without comment loses the interpretation mark.
(c) (M1) for obtaining $v_{2} = 2m_{1}u_{1}/(m_{1}+m_{2})$, (A1) for forming the ratio of the two energies and simplifying, (A1) for reaching the stated expression with the cancellation of $u_{1}$ shown, (A1) for the numerical check. Three fields; credit three. Note that the independence of speed is a substantive observation and should be credited if stated even without the numerical check.
(d) (A1) for the ratio $1:1$, (A1) for maximum fraction $1$ together with the statement that the incident glider stops. Accept 'equal masses' in place of the ratio notation. No credit for stating the answer without any indication of why the fraction peaks there.
(e) (A1) for observing that absent interactions also conserve both totals, (A1) for the point that only the physical premise removes the second solution. Do not accept answers claiming that the second solution is 'unphysical energy gain' or anything similar: nothing is gained or lost in that solution, which is exactly why it sits there.

Why this question is hard

The awkward fact this item is built around is that the two conservation laws do not determine a unique outcome, and that the extra solution is not nonsense. It is the situation where nothing happens. Every candidate has met this before without noticing, in the form of a quadratic they factorised, obtained two roots from, and silently discarded one of because 'velocities can't be negative' or 'it's in the past'. Here the discarded root has a clean physical reading, and noticing that is worth more than the arithmetic.

Why the factorisation matters is that it reframes the whole topic. The usual textbook treatment quotes the result that the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation, either as an experimental fact or as something to be verified against the standard formulas. In fact it falls straight out of the conservation laws one line after writing them in general form: substitute momentum into the factored energy equation and a product of two terms appears, one of which is the inspection solution. Everything else -- the standard velocity formulas, the transferred-energy fraction, the Newton's-cradle behaviour -- follows from that one relation plus momentum, and each route is dramatically shorter than solving quadratics from scratch.

The transferred-energy fraction, $4m_{1}m_{2}/(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}$, is one of those results that repays being looked at rather than used. It is symmetric in the two masses, which is initially surprising: it says the fraction handed over depends only on the product of the masses relative to their sum squared, not on which body was moving. It peaks at unity for equal masses, so a perfectly elastic collision between equals transfers everything and leaves the projectile dead. And it degrades quadratically rather than linearly at the extremes: for $m_{2} = m_{1}/10$ it is about $0.33$, and for $m_{2} = 10m_{1}$ also about $0.33$, whichever body carries the energy. That symmetry explains a great deal -- from why a billiard ball striking a stationary equal one stops dead, to why a neutron losing energy elastically is best moderated by nuclei of comparable mass rather than by heavy ones.

The markscheme deliberately refuses the tempting wrong explanation. Candidates often say the second root 'violates something' or 'is impossible'. It violates nothing: it conserves momentum exactly and energy exactly. What excludes it is not physics inside the equations but the premise in the question -- that the bodies touched. Keeping that distinction clear is the whole conceptual content of part (e), and it transfers directly to every situation where a conservation law is used backwards: conservation constrains, it does not determine.

Challenge lever
Conservation of momentum is linear and conservation of kinetic energy is quadratic, so together they usually produce two solutions, and candidates are trained to expect one. The second solution here is not a spurious physical possibility but the assertion that nothing happened: each body continues at its original velocity, which conserves both quantities trivially. The factoring that isolates it is one line and it is also the shortest route to the answer the source books quote rather than derive -- that the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation. Recognising that the algebra has already offered you both routes is the difference between a page of substitution and three lines.
