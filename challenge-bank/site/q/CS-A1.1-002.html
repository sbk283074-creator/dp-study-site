Where the cycles actually go

Computer science HL · CS-A1.1-002

Theme A: Concepts of computer scienceA1.1 Computer hardware and operation (A1.1.4 the role of cache memory, A1.1.5 the machine instruction cycle, A1.1.6 the effect of clock speed and cache on performance)P115 marksdifficulty 5CalculateCalculateExplainEvaluate

A processor whose clock speed is not the problem

An embedded vision system processes a stream of images. Measurements on the running system give the following. Of all memory accesses, $95\%$ are satisfied by the level-1 cache in $1$ clock cycle. Of the accesses that miss in level 1, $80\%$ are satisfied by the level-2 cache, and the whole level-2 path, including the level-1 miss, takes $12$ clock cycles. An access that misses in both caches goes to main memory, and that path takes $200$ clock cycles in total. The instruction mix is fixed, and apart from memory accesses the processor completes one instruction per clock cycle.

Question

Two upgrade proposals are on the table. Both are advertised with a large number on the box, and neither number is the one the system will actually see.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the average number of clock cycles spent on memory per instruction, showing how each level of the hierarchy contributes.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    A hardware accelerator can execute $40\%$ of the workload five times faster than the processor does now. Calculate the overall speedup of the system, and state the largest speedup that could ever be obtained by accelerating this same $40\%$, however fast the accelerator is made.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    A second proposal is to raise the clock frequency by $25\%$. The memory chips are unchanged, so their latency stays the same number of nanoseconds and therefore becomes a larger number of clock cycles. Calculate the resulting average cycles per instruction and hence the real speedup, and explain why it falls so far short of $25\%$.

    (Explain)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate which of the two proposals is the better use of the engineering budget, referring to your answers to (b) and (c) and to one further consideration that the numbers do not capture.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Work through the hierarchy from the fastest level outwards. An access hits in level 1 with probability $0.95$ and costs $1$ cycle. It misses level 1 with probability $0.05$; of those, $0.80$ are satisfied by level 2 at $12$ cycles and $0.20$ go to main memory at $200$ cycles. (M1)(A1)
$$\text{average cycles} = 0.95(1) + 0.05\bigl[0.80(12) + 0.20(200)\bigr]$$
$$= 0.95 + 0.05\bigl[9.6 + 40\bigr] = 0.95 + 0.05(49.6) = 0.95 + 2.48 = 3.43$$ (A1)(A1)
So the processor spends about $3.43$ clock cycles per instruction, of which only $0.95$ is the level-1 access itself. The tail dominates: level-2 hits add $0.48$ cycles in total, while main-memory accesses add $2.00$ cycles, so the $1\%$ of all accesses that miss in both caches accounts for $2.00$ of the $3.43$, which is more than half the average and more than twice what the level-1 hits contribute.

(b) Amdahl's law gives the speedup as (M1)
$$S = \frac{1}{(1-f) + \dfrac{f}{s}} = \frac{1}{0.60 + \dfrac{0.40}{5}} = \frac{1}{0.60 + 0.08} = \frac{1}{0.68} = 1.47$$ (A1)(A1)
The limiting case is $s \to \infty$, which gives $S_{\max} = 1/(1-0.40) = 1.67$. (A1) However fast the accelerator becomes, the remaining $60\%$ of the workload is untouched, so the system can never be more than $1.67$ times faster by this route.

(c) With the clock $25\%$ faster, a fixed nanosecond latency corresponds to $1.25$ times as many clock cycles, so the level-2 path becomes $12 \times 1.25 = 15$ cycles and the main-memory path becomes $200 \times 1.25 = 250$ cycles. (M1)
$$\text{average cycles} = 0.95(1) + 0.05\bigl[0.80(15) + 0.20(250)\bigr] = 0.95 + 0.05\bigl[12 + 50\bigr] = 0.95 + 3.10 = 4.05$$ (A1)
Execution time is cycles divided by frequency, so with the new frequency $1.25$ times the old one, (M1)
$$\frac{T_{\text{new}}}{T_{\text{old}}} = \frac{4.05/1.25}{3.43/1.00} = \frac{3.24}{3.43} = 0.945,\qquad \text{speedup} = 1.06$$ (A1)
The speedup is about $6\%$ rather than $25\%$ because the extra cycles are being spent waiting, and waiting does not get faster when the clock does. As the clock rises, the proportion of time spent stalled on memory grows, so the gap between the advertised figure and the delivered figure widens.

(d) The accelerator is the better buy on these numbers: $1.47$ against $1.06$, and the accelerator's figure is also closer to its own ceiling of $1.67$, whereas the clock increase delivers under a quarter of what its name promises. (A1)(A1) One thing the numbers do not capture is cost and risk: an accelerator is new hardware, new drivers and a new failure mode in a system that already works, while a clock change is a configuration setting. If the accelerator's development cost is more than a few times the cost of the clock change, the safer option may be worth its smaller return, and a decision that rests only on the two ratios above would be incomplete. (A1)

It is worth stating what the $3.43$ means before using it in later parts: it is an average over the whole instruction stream, so it already includes the instructions that hit in level 1 and cost almost nothing. Any proposal that changes the miss rates or the path latencies changes this average, and that is the only way an upgrade can help.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for the weighted-sum structure and (A1) for each of the three contributions; accept $3.4$ to $3.5$. A candidate who adds $1 + 12 + 200$ weighted by $0.95$, $0.04$ and $0.01$ has understood the structure but has applied the level-2 probability to all accesses rather than to level-1 misses, and should lose one of the accuracy marks. (b) Accept $1.46$ to $1.48$ and $1.67$; the ceiling mark is separate and is for understanding that the limit comes from the unaccelerated fraction. Do not credit a speedup of $5$ or of $1.40$ obtained by multiplying the fraction by the speedup. (c) Accept average cycles $4.0$ to $4.1$ and speedup $1.05$ to $1.07$; the mark is for dividing by $1.25$ as well as for recomputing the cycles, and a candidate who reports $4.05/3.43 = 1.18$ as a slowdown has forgotten the frequency change entirely. (d) Accept either proposal if it is argued from the calculations and names a non-numerical factor; the comparison must be made, not merely asserted. A candidate who computes $3.43$ correctly and then describes the main-memory contribution as small has misread their own arithmetic and should be corrected in feedback, since the tail is the dominant term and the rest of the question depends on noticing that.

Why this question is hard

The point of this item is that the number printed on a component is almost never the number the system experiences, and that the gap between the two is calculable rather than mysterious. Part (a) sets up the arithmetic by showing where the cycles go: a $95\%$ hit rate sounds excellent, and it is, but $5\%$ of accesses still reach the next level and a fifth of those reach main memory, so $1\%$ of all accesses contributes $0.40$ of the $3.43$ cycles. That small tail dominates the average, and it is the first place a student should look when asked to make something faster.

Parts (b) and (c) then apply that observation to two proposals, and they fail in different ways. The accelerator fails because of Amdahl's law: only $40\%$ of the work can be moved, so the ceiling is $1.67$ no matter how good the hardware is, and a fivefold improvement on that fraction buys $1.47$. The clock increase fails for a different and more interesting reason. Raising the clock shortens the cycle, but the memory chips do not know about the clock, so their latency in nanoseconds is unchanged and their latency in cycles grows. The processor therefore spends a larger fraction of each instruction waiting, and the delivered speedup is $1.06$ against an advertised $1.25$. This is the mechanism behind the phrase 'the memory wall', and it is worth naming, because it explains why clock speeds stopped rising long before transistor counts did.

Part (d) is where the item stops being arithmetic. Both numbers are now on the table and the comparison looks decisive, but a real decision also involves risk, development cost, and whether the system can be taken offline. A candidate who says 'the accelerator, because $1.47 > 1.06$' has answered the question they were asked and missed the one that matters; the evaluation mark is reserved for someone who makes the comparison and then says what it leaves out.
