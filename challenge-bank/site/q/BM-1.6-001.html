An offshore saving that mostly evaporates

Business management SL · BM-1.6-001

Unit 1: 1.6 Multinational companies (MNCs) (reasons for locating production abroad, impact on the host country); 3.3 Costs and revenues.P1 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedCalculateCommentRecommendJustify

Halden Bikes

Halden Bikes assembles bicycles for its home market and produces 200 000 units per year. Its total unit cost in the home factory is €42. The board is considering closing the home assembly line and moving production to Country M, where labour costs are considerably lower.

A feasibility study reports the following figures for the Country M option:

  • ex-works production cost of €28 per unit;
  • an import tariff of 18% of the ex-works value, payable on every unit entering the home market;
  • additional freight, insurance and handling of €3.50 per unit;
  • 4% of units fail quality inspection on arrival and require rework costing €6 per faulty unit;
  • a one-off set-up cost of €2.4 million, to be written off over five years at the current volume;
  • no change in the selling price or in the volume sold.

The tariff is set by the home government and is reviewed annually. Analysts consider it likely that the tariff will rise to 25% within two years.

Question

Halden Bikes sells only in its home market. The board must decide whether to move assembly to Country M, and the tariff is outside its control.

  1. [5 marks](a)

    Calculate the total landed cost per unit of the Country M option, and compare it with the current unit cost of €42.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the annual saving at the current volume of 200 000 units, and express it as a percentage of the current total annual production cost.

    (Calculate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Recalculate the landed cost per unit and the annual saving if the tariff rises to 25%, and comment on the significance of the change.

    (Comment)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Recommend whether Halden Bikes should proceed, justifying your answer with reference to your calculations and to one non-financial factor.

    (Recommend)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Build the landed cost per unit from the components:

  • ex-works production: €28.00
  • import tariff at 18% of €28: €5.04
  • freight, insurance and handling: €3.50
  • expected rework cost: $0.04\times 6=€0.24$
  • set-up cost written off: $\dfrac{2\,400\,000}{5\times 200\,000}=€2.40$ (M1)(M1)(M1)

Total landed cost $=28.00+5.04+3.50+0.24+2.40=€39.18$ per unit. (M1)(A1)

This is €2.82 per unit below the current cost of €42.00. The €39.18 is also a best case: it excludes the cost of managing an overseas supplier — incoming quality inspection, extra safety stock for longer lead times, and currency exposure — none of which appears in the current €42.00.

(b) Annual saving $=2.82\times 200\,000=€564\,000$. (M1)(A1)

Current total annual production cost $=42\times 200\,000=€8\,400\,000$, so the saving is $\dfrac{564\,000}{8\,400\,000}\times 100=6.7\%$. (A1) The 6.7% is a saving on operating cost only; it takes no account of the one-off cost of closing the home line, such as redundancy payments and the write-down of plant, which would have to be recovered before the move pays back.

(c) With a 25% tariff the tariff component becomes $0.25\times 28=€7.00$, so the landed cost is $28.00+7.00+3.50+0.24+2.40=€41.14$ per unit. (M1)(A1)

The saving falls to €0.86 per unit, or $0.86\times 200\,000=€172\,000$ per year, which is 2.0% of current costs. (A1)

The headline saving of €14 per unit has been reduced to €2.82, and a plausible tariff change reduces it to €0.86. The decision therefore rests on a margin of less than €1 per unit on a product whose cost is €42, and the company would be committing to closing its home line, losing the skills and the flexibility that come with it, in exchange for a saving smaller than the annual variation in most input costs.

(d) Recommendation: do not proceed. (A1)

At the current tariff the saving is 6.7% of production cost, which is real but modest for a decision that is hard to reverse, and it exists only because the set-up cost is being spread over five years at the full current volume. If volumes fall, the per-unit write-off rises and the saving shrinks further; if the tariff rises to 25% as analysts expect, the saving falls to 2.0% and the case for moving effectively disappears. (A1)

The non-financial factor that matters most is reputational and political. Halden would be moving production out of its home country while continuing to sell only into that market, which exposes it to criticism from customers and employees and makes it a visible target when the tariff is next reviewed. A company that has moved its production abroad and is then faced with a further tariff increase cannot easily reverse the decision. The Country M option does offer lower costs and access to a larger labour supply, so a board that is confident of a large volume increase might reasonably revisit it, but on the figures available the saving is too small and too fragile to justify the commitment. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a): the five cost components each carry a method mark for correct construction, and candidates who omit the set-up write-off or the rework cost cannot reach €39.18. Accept a candidate who computes the set-up contribution over a different period provided the period is stated and used consistently. Do not accept an approach that compares the ex-works cost of €28 with €42 and stops, which ignores the entire point of the question. Part (b): the percentage mark requires the current total cost to be calculated explicitly; a candidate who gives a fraction of the saving without a base does not earn it. Part (c): the method mark is for applying the new tariff to the ex-works value, not to the landed cost; a candidate who applies 25% to €39.18 obtains €48.98 and should be corrected sharply, as this is the most common conceptual error in the question. Part (d): the recommendation mark is independent of the direction chosen, provided the reasoning is consistent with the candidate's own figures. The non-financial mark requires a factor with a stated consequence for Halden, such as reputational exposure, political risk, loss of control over quality or loss of the home workforce's skills; a list of generic MNC advantages earns nothing.

Why this question is hard

The question is built on the gap between the headline number and the landed cost. The ex-works figure of €28 against a domestic cost of €42 suggests a saving of a third, which is large enough that many boards would stop analysing at that point. Building the landed cost removes most of it: the tariff applies to the ex-works value, freight and handling are unavoidable on every unit, a small failure rate carries a real expected cost, and the set-up cost has to be recovered over the volume actually produced. The saving collapses from €14 to €2.82 per unit, and that is before any consideration of what happens when the tariff changes.

The sensitivity part is the substance of the question. The single largest component after the ex-works cost is the tariff, and it is outside the company's control. A rise from 18% to 25% is well within the range that governments make for reasons that have nothing to do with this company, and it reduces the saving to €0.86 per unit. A business that has closed its home line cannot respond to that by moving production back: the decision is effectively irreversible, while the cost that most threatens it can change at a year's notice. That asymmetry between a reversible threat and an irreversible response is the argument against proceeding, and it is a general principle rather than a number.

It is worth noticing that the analysis is not an argument against locating production abroad in principle. Country M does have substantially lower labour costs, and if volumes grew sharply the per-unit set-up write-off would fall and the case would improve. What the figures show is that at the current volume and with the current tariff, the margin is too thin to carry the risk being taken, and that the decision should be revisited if either the volume or the tariff environment changes in the company's favour.
