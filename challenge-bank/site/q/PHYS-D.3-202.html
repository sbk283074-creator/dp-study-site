D.3 A cyclotron, whose exit energy is fixed by the flux density and the radius of the dees and not by the accelerating potential difference, so that raising the voltage changes the number of revolutions rather than the energy

Physics HL · PHYS-D.3-202

Theme D: FieldsTheme D: Fields, D.3 Motion in electromagnetic fields (charged particles in a uniform magnetic field, circular motion, the cyclotron); Theme A: A.3 Work, energy and power (energy gained across a potential difference).P2 · Section B11 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedDeriveDetermineCalculateExplain

A cyclotron at a teaching hospital

A cyclotron produces a beam of protons that is used to make short-lived radioisotopes for a hospital's scanning service. The protons are injected near the centre of two hollow semicircular electrodes, the *dees*, and are accelerated each time they cross the gap between them by an alternating potential difference applied across that gap. A uniform magnetic flux density $B = 1.2\ \mathrm{T}$ acts at right angles to the plane of the dees, and each dee has a radius $R = 0.35\ \mathrm{m}$. The alternating potential difference has a peak value of $50\ \mathrm{kV}$.

A proton has charge $+e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$ and mass $m = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$. Treat the protons as non-relativistic throughout, and take the speed of light as $c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

Question

A cyclotron is to be used to accelerate protons, and its operating frequency and exit energy must be established. Show that the time for a proton to complete one full circular path inside the dees is $T = \dfrac{2\pi m}{qB}$ and that this time does not depend on the proton's speed, and calculate the frequency the alternating potential difference must have. Then determine the maximum speed the protons can reach, calculate the maximum kinetic energy and the number of revolutions a proton makes, and explain what does and does not raise the maximum energy.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the time $T$ taken by a proton to complete one full circular path inside the dees is $T = \dfrac{2\pi m}{qB}$, and that this time is independent of the proton's speed. Calculate $T$ for the fields given, and hence state the frequency at which the alternating potential difference must be applied.

    (Derive)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Determine the maximum speed of a proton as it leaves the cyclotron, and express this speed as a fraction of the speed of light.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the protons in joules and in MeV, and hence calculate the number of revolutions a proton makes before it leaves the cyclotron.

    (Calculate)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain why raising the peak value of the alternating potential difference does not increase the maximum kinetic energy of the protons, and state one change to the apparatus that would increase it.

    (Explain)

Reveal the answer

(a) Inside a dee the only force is the magnetic force, and it acts at right angles to the velocity, so it supplies the centripetal force (D.3):
$$qvB = \frac{mv^{2}}{r} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad r = \frac{mv}{qB}.$$
The circumference of the circular path is $2\pi r$, and the proton covers it at speed $v$, so
$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi}{v} \cdot \frac{mv}{qB} = \frac{2\pi m}{qB} \;\; (M1)(A1).$$
The speed cancels. That is the whole reason the machine works: a faster proton has a larger radius but covers the larger circumference in the same time, so a single fixed frequency accelerates the proton all the way out (A1).

Numerically,
$$T = \frac{2\pi \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.2} = 5.47 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s},$$
so the alternating potential difference must be supplied at
$$f = \frac{1}{T} = 1.83 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{Hz} \approx 18\ \mathrm{MHz}.$$
The proton crosses the gap twice in each revolution, so the potential difference must complete one full cycle in the same time $T$; the required frequency is therefore $f = qB/2\pi m$, about $18\ \mathrm{MHz}$.

(b) The protons leave when the radius of the path has grown to the radius of the dees, $r = R$ (D.3). From $r = mv/qB$,
$$v_{\max} = \frac{qBR}{m} = \frac{1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.2 \times 0.35}{1.67 \times 10^{-27}} = 4.02 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \;\; (M1)(A1).$$
As a fraction of the speed of light this is
$$\frac{v_{\max}}{c} = \frac{4.02 \times 10^{7}}{3.00 \times 10^{8}} = 0.134,$$
about thirteen per cent of $c$. That justifies the non-relativistic treatment, since the mass of a proton at $0.134c$ is only about $0.9\%$ greater than its rest mass.

(c) The maximum kinetic energy follows from the speed in part (b):
$$E_{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{\max}^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27} \times (4.02 \times 10^{7})^{2} = 1.35 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{J} \;\; (A1).$$
Equivalently, and more directly from the geometry,
$$E_{k} = \frac{q^{2}B^{2}R^{2}}{2m} = \frac{(1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.2 \times 0.35)^{2}}{2 \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27}} = 1.35 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{J}.$$
In electronvolts,
$$E_{k} = \frac{1.35 \times 10^{-12}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 8.4 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{eV} = 8.4\ \mathrm{MeV} \;\; (A1).$$

A proton gains energy $qV$ each time it crosses the gap, and it crosses the gap twice per revolution, so it gains $2qV = 2 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 5.0 \times 10^{4} = 1.60 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm{J}$ per revolution. The number of revolutions is therefore
$$N = \frac{1.35 \times 10^{-12}}{1.60 \times 10^{-14}} = 84.5,$$
that is about $85$ revolutions (A1). The protons cross the gap about $169$ times. Since the period does not change as the protons speed up, the total acceleration time is simply $N T = 84.5 \times 5.47 \times 10^{-8} \approx 4.6\ \mu\mathrm{s}$.

(d) The protons leave when the radius has grown to the fixed radius of the dees, and the speed at that moment is fixed by the fields through $v = qBR/m$ (D.3). Because $R$, $B$, $m$ and $q$ are all properties of the apparatus or the particle, the exit speed is fixed too, and so is the exit energy $\tfrac{1}{2}q^{2}B^{2}R^{2}/m$ (A1).

The voltage never appears in that expression. What the voltage sets is the *energy per crossing*, $qV$, and so the number of crossings needed, $E_{k}/qV$. Raising the voltage from $50\ \mathrm{kV}$ to $100\ \mathrm{kV}$ would double the energy gained at each crossing and halve the number of revolutions, from about $85$ to about $42$, leaving the energy the protons leave with unchanged (A1). The product of the two is the same because both are constrained by the same geometry: the protons have to reach the same radius, and reaching it requires the same total energy however it was delivered.

To raise the maximum energy, the ceiling itself has to be moved. Since $E_{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}q^{2}B^{2}R^{2}/m$, a larger flux density $B$ or a larger dee radius $R$ would do it; both appear squared, so a $10\%$ increase in either raises the maximum energy by about $21\%$ (A1). Increasing $B$ is the cheaper change in practice because it does not require a larger magnet, but it runs into the relativistic limit: once $v$ is an appreciable fraction of $c$, the proton's mass increases and its period $T = 2\pi m/qB$ is no longer constant, so the fixed frequency falls out of step with the protons and the acceleration stalls. A cyclotron can only be extended in $B$ or $R$ until that happens, which is why higher-energy machines use a varying frequency.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for equating the magnetic force to the centripetal force, (A1) for cancelling $v$ to obtain $T = 2\pi m/qB$, (A1) for the value $5.47 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s}$ and the frequency $1.83 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ (accept $18\ \mathrm{MHz}$ or $1.8 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{Hz}$). The independence of the speed must be stated, not merely shown by cancellation; a candidate who derives $T$ but does not draw the conclusion forfeits the third mark.

(b) (M1) for the substitution $r = R$ into $r = mv/qB$, (A1) for $4.02 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (accept $4.0 \times 10^{7}$). The fraction $0.134$ is not separately marked but is expected as the justification for the non-relativistic treatment; a candidate who used a relativistic mass without being asked does not lose marks, provided the value is consistent.

(c) (A1) for $1.35 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{J}$ (accept $1.4 \times 10^{-12}$), (A1) for $8.4\ \mathrm{MeV}$, (A1) for $84.5$ or about $85$ revolutions. A candidate who obtains the energy by multiplying $qV$ by a *fixed* number of crossings has made the central error of the item and will not match the energy; if by luck they do match, the third mark is still forfeited because the reasoning is wrong.

(d) (A1) for stating that the exit speed and therefore the energy are fixed by $B$, $R$ and the particle's $q/m$. (A1) for explaining that the voltage controls the energy per crossing and hence the number of revolutions, with the product unchanged. (A1) for naming a change to the apparatus that raises the ceiling, correctly to $B$ or $R$ (not the voltage). A candidate who says only "increase the voltage" scores at most the first mark.

Forfeits: using $r = m v / q B$ with the wrong mass, such as the electron mass; treating $T$ as depending on $v$; using $E_k = qV$ with $V = 50\ \mathrm{kV}$, which gives $8.0 \times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{J}$ and is three orders of magnitude out; giving the frequency as $2 \times 18\ \mathrm{MHz}$ on the argument that the proton crosses the gap twice per revolution, which confuses crossings with cycles. Condonation: energies quoted as $8.4$ or $8.45\ \mathrm{MeV}$ are both accepted; the number of revolutions may be given as $84$, $84.5$ or $85$ provided the method is shown, since the proton leaves part-way through its final revolution.

Why this question is hard

The interesting thing about a cyclotron is not the force arithmetic, which is two lines long, but the fact that the machine works at all. A proton that is moving faster should take longer to go round a bigger circle; the reason it does not is that the radius and the speed grow together, so the period is unchanged. Every cyclotron question that is worth asking is a consequence of that cancellation, and this item asks for it first because the rest of the question only makes sense once it is understood.

Part (a) is the derivation. From $qvB = mv^2/r$ the radius is $mv/qB$, the circumference is proportional to $v$, and the time to cover it does not depend on $v$ at all. That is what allows a single fixed-frequency supply to serve the whole acceleration: the protons come round in step with the voltage no matter how fast they are going. A student who has memorised $T = 2\pi m/qB$ can quote it; the part asks for the cancellation explicitly.

Part (c) is where the difficulty sits. The tempting route is to say that a proton gains $qV$ per crossing and therefore ends up with $qV$ times the number of crossings, and then to invent a number of crossings from the observation that the protons leave after roughly eighty-five turns. That reasoning is circular and gets the answer right only by accident. The honest route is to note that the protons leave when $r = R$, which fixes $v$, which fixes the energy, and then to work out the number of revolutions as a consequence. The two routes give the same number, which is exactly why the error survives: the answer is right and the mechanism is wrong.

Part (d) is the part that separates the two. The voltage is the only thing putting energy in, and yet it cannot change what the protons leave with. What it changes is how many bites at the apple are needed. Doubling the voltage halves the number of revolutions and leaves the exit energy alone. The exit energy is a property of the apparatus -- the magnet and the size of the dees -- and to change it one of those has to change. This is a genuinely useful thing to know about particle accelerators, and it is the reason that the largest cyclotrons were abandoned in favour of machines that vary the frequency or the field.

Challenge lever
The alternating potential difference is the only thing that puts energy into the protons, one gap crossing at a time, so it is entirely natural to conclude that the energy the protons leave with is proportional to it. It is not. The protons leave when their path has grown to the radius of the dees, and that condition fixes the exit speed through $v = qBR/m$ and the exit energy through $ frac{1}{2}q^2B^2R^2/m$, both of which are independent of the voltage. Raising the voltage does make each crossing worth more, and it therefore reduces the number of revolutions needed, but the product of those two quantities is held constant by the geometry. The item also asks for the period first, because the reason the machine works at all is that the period is speed-independent, which is what allows one fixed frequency to serve the whole acceleration.

Verification
The period, frequency, exit speed and exit energy were computed from the stated charge, mass, flux density and radius, and the exit energy was obtained twice, once as half m v squared from the speed and once in closed form as q squared B squared R squared over 2m, the two agreeing to four significant figures.
