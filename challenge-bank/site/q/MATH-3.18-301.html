3.18 Three planes whose normals are coplanar: no two planes are parallel and yet no point lies on all three, so the configuration is a triangular prism

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-3.18-301

Question

Three planes are given by
$$\Pi_{1}:\ x + y + z = 4, \qquad \Pi_{2}:\ 2x - y + z = 5, \qquad \Pi_{3}:\ 3x + 2z = 10.$$
The diagram shows the configuration the three planes form. Note that no two of the planes are parallel.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the normal vector of $\Pi_{3}$ is the sum of the normal vectors of $\Pi_{1}$ and $\Pi_{2}$, and state what follows about the number of points that can lie on all three planes at once.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find a vector equation of the line of intersection of $\Pi_{1}$ and $\Pi_{2}$, and show that this line is parallel to $\Pi_{3}$ but does not lie in it.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find vector equations of the line of intersection of $\Pi_{1}$ and $\Pi_{3}$ and of the line of intersection of $\Pi_{2}$ and $\Pi_{3}$, and show that the three lines of intersection are parallel and distinct.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the shortest distance between the line found in part (b) and the line in which $\Pi_{1}$ meets $\Pi_{3}$. Hence state, with a reason, the number of points that lie on all three planes.

    (Determine)

Reveal the answer

(a) The normal vectors are $\mathbf{n}_{1} = (1,\,1,\,1)$, $\mathbf{n}_{2} = (2,\,-1,\,1)$ and $\mathbf{n}_{3} = (3,\,0,\,2)$, and
$$\mathbf{n}_{1} + \mathbf{n}_{2} = (1 + 2,\; 1 - 1,\; 1 + 1) = (3,\,0,\,2) = \mathbf{n}_{3} \;(M1).$$
The three normals are therefore linearly dependent, so the determinant of the coefficient matrix is zero and the system cannot have a unique solution $(A1)$. That leaves two possibilities only: the planes share a common line, or there is no point lying on all three $(A1)$.\n(b) A direction vector of the line of intersection is $\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{n}_{1} \times \mathbf{n}_{2} = (2,\,1,\,-3)$ $(M1)$, and $\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{1} = 2 + 1 - 3 = 0$, $\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{2} = 4 - 1 - 3 = 0$, so $\mathbf{d}$ is parallel to both planes as required $(A1)$. Putting $z = 0$ in the first two equations gives $x + y = 4$ and $2x - y = 5$, so $x = 3$, $y = 1$, and the line is
$$\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}3\\1\\0\end{pmatrix} + t\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad t \in \mathbb{R} \;(A1).$$
Substituting this into the left-hand side of $\Pi_{3}$: $3(3 + 2t) + 2(-3t) = 9 + 6t - 6t = 9$, which is constant and never equal to $10$, so the line is parallel to $\Pi_{3}$ $(M1)$ and no point of it lies in $\Pi_{3}$ $(A1)$.\n(c) For $\Pi_{1}$ and $\Pi_{3}$: the point $\left(\tfrac{10}{3},\,\tfrac{2}{3},\,0\right)$ satisfies both, so the line is
$$\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{10}{3}\\[2pt]\tfrac{2}{3}\\[2pt]0\end{pmatrix} + s\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-3\end{pmatrix} \;(M1)(A1).$$
For $\Pi_{2}$ and $\Pi_{3}$: the point $\left(\tfrac{10}{3},\,\tfrac{5}{3},\,0\right)$ satisfies both, so the line is
$$\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{10}{3}\\[2pt]\tfrac{5}{3}\\[2pt]0\end{pmatrix} + u\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-3\end{pmatrix} \;(A1).$$
All three lines have direction $(2,\,1,\,-3)$ and are therefore parallel $(A1)$. They are distinct because the three points $(3,1,0)$, $\left(\tfrac{10}{3},\tfrac{2}{3},0\right)$ and $\left(\tfrac{10}{3},\tfrac{5}{3},0\right)$ all have $z = 0$ and differ in $x$ or $y$, while $(2,1,-3)$ has non-zero $z$-component, so the displacement between any two of the points cannot be a multiple of the common direction.\n(d) Take $\mathbf{v} = \left(\tfrac{10}{3} - 3,\;\tfrac{2}{3} - 1,\;0\right) = \left(\tfrac{1}{3},\,-\tfrac{1}{3},\,0\right)$ $(M1)$. Then
$$\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{d} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt]1\\[2pt]1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{d}| = \sqrt{3}, \qquad |\mathbf{d}| = \sqrt{14},$$
so the shortest distance is $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{14}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{3}{14}} = 0.463$ $(M1)(A1)$. The distance is not zero, so the two parallel lines are distinct and no point lies on all three planes; the number of such points is $0$ $(A1)$. This is visible in the equations too: adding $\Pi_{1}$ and $\Pi_{2}$ gives $3x + 2z = 9$, which contradicts $\Pi_{3}$.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for adding the two normals componentwise, (A1) for the conclusion that the determinant is zero so there is no unique point, (A1) for naming the two remaining possibilities. A candidate who says 'the planes are parallel' here has not read the stem and earns nothing: no two normals are multiples of one another.\n(b) (M1) for a cross product of the two normals, (A1) for $(2,1,-3)$ or any multiple, (A1) for a correct point and a correct vector equation, (M1) for substituting the general point of the line into $\Pi_{3}$ and obtaining the constant $9$, (A1) for concluding that the line is parallel to and outside the plane. Award no mark for 'the point $(3,1,0)$ does not satisfy $\Pi_{3}$' on its own: one point proves nothing until the whole line has been substituted.\n(c) (M1) for using the cross product or an equivalent elimination in each case, (A1) for each of the two lines (a correct point and the correct direction in each), (A1) for observing that all three directions are the same and that the three fixed points are different. Accept any correct parametrisation, including one obtained by setting $x$ or $y$ rather than $z$ to zero, provided the resulting line is verified against both of its planes.\n(d) (M1) for a displacement vector between a point on each line, (M1) for the formula $|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{d}|/|\mathbf{d}|$, (A1) for $\sqrt{3/14}$ or $0.463$, (A1) for the conclusion that no point lies on all three planes, supported either by the non-zero distance or by the contradiction $3x + 2z = 9$ against $3x + 2z = 10$. Note that $\sqrt{3/14}$ is exact; a decimal answer stated without working scores at most two.

Why this question is hard

The three-planes question is a determinant question in almost every course, and the determinant here is zero — which is precisely why the item is not about the determinant. A zero determinant is a negative result: it says what does not happen. The interesting question is which of the two degenerate configurations the planes form, and that is decided by the constants, which the determinant ignores entirely.\n\nTwo design choices make the item bite. First, no two planes are parallel, so the candidate cannot fall back on spotting parallel normals; the degeneracy is hidden in $\mathbf{n}_{3} = \mathbf{n}_{1} + \mathbf{n}_{2}$, which is visible only if the normals are written down. Second, the substitution in part (b) is arranged so that the parameter cancels — $3(3 + 2t) + 2(-3t) = 9$ for every $t$ — which demonstrates the stronger fact that the whole line sits at a constant 'distance' from $\Pi_{3}$ rather than merely failing to meet it at one point, and it hands the candidate the number $9$ that explains the inconsistency.\n\nThe distance in part (d) is the payoff for doing it properly. Two of the three pairwise lines are $\sqrt{3/14}$ apart: not zero, so the system is inconsistent, and not large, so the configuration is the one the diagram shows — a triangular prism, in which every pair of planes meets and no three of them do. Writing the three lines out explicitly also removes the usual escape: the conclusion that there is no solution becomes a distance that can be computed and checked rather than a verdict that has to be taken on trust.

Challenge lever
Three planes in three unknowns either meet in a point or they do not, and the usual test is a determinant. Here the determinant is zero, which tells the candidate only that there is no unique point — and the candidate who stops there will assert that the planes share a line, because that is the only degenerate case most questions show. The deciding information is in the constants: the third normal is the sum of the other two, so the direction common to the first two planes is automatically parallel to the third, and whether that parallel line lies in the third plane is settled by 4 + 5 = 9 against 10. The two constraints cannot both hold, and the distance between the resulting parallel lines is what measures how badly they fail to.
