A4 Machine learning

Computer science HL · CS-A4.2-001

A4.1 Machine learning fundamentals, A4.2 Data preprocessing (HL only), A4.3 Machine learning approaches (HL only), A4.4 Ethical considerationsP1 · Section A16 marksdifficulty 4DistinguishDescribeExplainDiscuss

Stimulus

A bank builds a machine learning system to decide whether to approve personal loan applications. The training dataset contains 40 000 previous applications with the outcome (repaid or defaulted) recorded.

The dataset includes the following fields: applicant age, annual income, the postcode district of the applicant's home address (2 800 distinct values), years in current employment, loan amount requested, and existing debt.

Known issues with the data: 12% of the income values are missing; income ranges from 0 to 4 000 000 across the dataset while age ranges from 18 to 75; and the postcode district correlates strongly with the ethnic composition of the neighbourhood.

Question

The bank intends to deploy the model to make automated decisions.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Distinguish between supervised and unsupervised learning. Identify which type this system uses and justify your answer.

    (Distinguish)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    For each of the three data issues identified above, describe one appropriate preprocessing step and justify why it is needed. (HL only)

    (Describe)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    The model is trained on 80% of the data and tested on the remaining 20%. Training accuracy is 98% while test accuracy is 71%. Explain what these two figures tell you and what should be done about it. (HL only)

    (Explain)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    After deployment, the model rejects applications from one postcode district at four times the average rate. Discuss two ethical issues this raises and one action the bank should take.

    (Discuss)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) In supervised learning the training data are *labelled* — each example is paired with the correct output — and the model learns to map inputs to that known target. In unsupervised learning the data are unlabelled and the model must find structure (clusters, associations) on its own. (A1)(A1)

This system is supervised: each of the 40 000 historical applications carries the recorded outcome (repaid or defaulted), which acts as the label, and the task is to predict that outcome for a new applicant. (A1)(A1) (Specifically it is classification, since the output is one of two categories.)

(b)

| Issue | Preprocessing step | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| 12% of income values missing | Impute the missing values — replace them with the median income (or with the median within a relevant subgroup), or drop the rows if 88% is still a large enough sample | Deleting 12% of 40 000 records throws away 4 800 examples and may bias the sample if the missingness is not random; imputing with the *median* rather than the mean avoids distortion by the extreme incomes noted below |
| Income ranges from 0 to 4 000 000 while age ranges 18–75 | Scale / normalise the features — for example min–max scaling to [0, 1] or standardisation (z-scores) | Many algorithms (distance-based methods, gradient descent, regularised models) are dominated by the feature with the largest numeric range; unscaled, income would swamp age and years-in-employment and the model would effectively ignore them |
| Postcode district: 2 800 distinct values | Reduce cardinality — group districts into broader regions, or replace the district with derived features (e.g. average local income from a lookup) rather than using the raw category | 2 800 categories with few examples each makes the model memorise individual districts (overfitting) and makes one-hot encoding produce thousands of sparse columns; it is also the field through which proxy discrimination enters (see part d) |

Award (A1)(A1) for two well-justified steps and (A1) for a third; up to (A1)(A1) overall for the quality of justification. Accept other valid answers: dropping the field entirely, target/mean encoding, or binning income.

(c) The training set is used to fit the model; the test set is data the model has never seen, and it estimates how the model will perform on future applications. (A1)

A training accuracy of 98% with a test accuracy of 71% is a large generalisation gap: the model has fitted the training data extremely closely — including its noise and quirks — but has not learned patterns that transfer. This is overfitting. (A1)(A1)

What to do: reduce model complexity (fewer features or a shallower model), apply regularisation to penalise overly complex fits, gather more training data, use cross-validation to get a more reliable estimate, and — importantly — treat the 71% test figure as the honest estimate of performance rather than the 98%. (A1)

(d) Ethical issues — award two:

  • Indirect (proxy) discrimination. Even if the protected characteristic is not in the dataset, postcode district correlates with ethnic composition, so the model can reproduce discriminatory outcomes while appearing to be neutral. This may be unlawful and is certainly unfair to individual applicants who are judged by their neighbours rather than by their own circumstances. (A1)
  • Lack of transparency / accountability. If the model cannot explain *why* it rejected an application, the bank cannot give a rejected applicant a reason, cannot check whether the decision was lawful, and cannot be held accountable. Automated decisions with significant effects on people require explainability. (A1)
  • Feedback loop. If the model's rejections are fed back into future training data as 'true' outcomes, the bias is reinforced and amplified over time. (A1)
  • Scale of harm. Because decisions are automated, a biased model harms many people consistently and invisibly, rather than as occasional individual lapses. (A1)

Action — award one:
- Audit for disparate impact, test the model's rejection rates across groups, and if the disparity cannot be justified, remove or transform the postcode field and retrain. (A1)
- Keep a human in the loop for borderline or rejected applications, and publish a meaningful reason for each adverse decision. (A1)
- Use fairness-aware techniques: re-weight or re-sample the training data, or constrain the model so that outcomes are independent of the proxy variable. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Both definitions needed for the first two marks; the identification and the justification (the outcome field is the label) for the second two. Naming 'classification' is a bonus, not a requirement.

(b) One mark per correct step, but the *justification* is what discriminates — 'fill in the missing values' without saying why median rather than mean, or 'scale the data' without saying what goes wrong otherwise, is capped at half credit. Candidates who propose deleting all three problem fields outright should be credited for missing values only if they note the loss of 4 800 records.

(c) The key phrase is 'generalisation gap'. Candidates who say only 'the model is bad' earn at most one mark. Credit 'overfitting' as the diagnosis; the remedy mark accepts any two sensible actions.

(d) This is a discuss question: two issues must each be *explained*, not just named. 'It is racist' is not creditworthy; 'the model may be discriminating indirectly because postcode correlates with ethnic composition' is. The action must address the problem identified — 'retrain the model' on its own is not an action.

Additional guidance. In (b) accept mode imputation or a separate 'missing' indicator for income, provided the choice is justified. In (c) credit k-fold cross-validation or a held-out validation set as valid remedies alongside 'get more data'.

Why this question is hard

Machine learning is new enough in the syllabus that most questions about it are definitional. This one is built so that definitions earn the first four marks and the remaining twelve require judgement.

The three data issues in (b) are not arbitrary — each is the classic symptom that has a specific remedy, and the remedy only makes sense once you know what goes wrong without it. Imputation is not about tidiness; it is about not discarding 4 800 examples and not distorting a distribution that has extreme outliers. Scaling is not cosmetic; with income in the millions and age in double digits, an unscaled distance-based or gradient-based model is effectively a model of income alone. Cardinality reduction is not an efficiency trick; 2 800 categories with a handful of examples each guarantees memorisation.

Part (c) is the standard overfitting diagnosis, but the question is phrased so that the student has to say what the *gap* means rather than compute it. The 98%/71% pair is the signature.

The point of the whole question, though, is (d), and it is deliberately connected back to (b). The postcode field is exactly the one whose 'technical' problem — high cardinality — pushes students towards grouping or encoding it, and it is simultaneously the field through which proxy discrimination enters. A student who handles postcode purely as a technical nuisance has missed that the preprocessing decision *is* an ethical decision. That is the transfer this question is trying to test, and it is why the bank's context matters: an unfair loan model does not produce a wrong answer, it produces a life-changing one, automatically, at scale.

Classic wrong turns: calling the system unsupervised because 'the outcome is just recorded'; imputing missing income with 0 (which the model reads as a real value); suggesting more training data as the only cure for overfitting; and proposing 'remove bias from the data' without explaining how bias would be detected.
