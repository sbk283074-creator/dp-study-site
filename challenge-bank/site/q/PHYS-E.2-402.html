Stopping potential against frequency for two metals

Physics HL · PHYS-E.2-402

Theme E: Nuclear and quantum physicsE.2 Quantum physics: the photoelectric effect, the stopping potential, Einstein's photoelectric equation, the threshold frequency and the work function, and the interpretation of a graph of stopping potential against frequencyP210 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedDetermineDetermineExplainCalculate

Question

A student investigates the photoelectric effect using two different metal plates X and Y in the same evacuated tube. For each metal the student illuminates the plate with light of a known frequency f and measures the stopping potential V_s, the reverse potential difference just sufficient to reduce the photocurrent to zero. The figure shows the graph of V_s against f for the two metals. Both sets of readings lie on straight lines, and the two lines are parallel to each other.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Determine the gradient of the lines in the figure, including its unit, and use it to obtain a value for the Planck constant.

    (Determine)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the threshold frequency of each metal from the figure, and hence determine the work function of each metal in electronvolts.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain why the two lines have the same gradient, and why their intercepts on the frequency axis are different.

    (Explain)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    Calculate the stopping potential for metal X when light of frequency 9.00 x 10^14 Hz is incident on it, and state the corresponding value for metal Y.

    (Calculate)

Total: 10 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The graph is a straight line for each metal, so the gradient is the same at every point. Reading from the line for metal X: at $f = 10 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ the stopping potential is about 1.8 V, and the line meets the frequency axis at about $5.5 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ (M1).

$$\text{gradient} = \frac{1.8 - 0}{10 \times 10^{14} - 5.5 \times 10^{14}} = \frac{1.8}{4.5 \times 10^{14}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{V\ s}$$ (A1)

The gradient has the unit of volts per hertz, which is V s. Since $eV_s = hf - \phi$, the gradient is $h/e$, so

$$h = e \times \text{gradient} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.14 \times 10^{-15} = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\ s}$$ (A1)

which agrees with the accepted value.

(b) The threshold frequency is where the line meets the frequency axis, since at that frequency the stopping potential is zero and the photon energy is exactly the work function.

For metal X, $f_0 \approx 5.5 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ (A1). Its work function is

$$\phi_X = h f_0 = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 5.55 \times 10^{14} = 3.68 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J} = \frac{3.68 \times 10^{-19}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 2.3\ \mathrm{eV}$$ (A1)

For metal Y, $f_0 \approx 7.7 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$, giving

$$\phi_Y = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 7.72 \times 10^{14}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.2\ \mathrm{eV}$$ (A1)

(c) The photoelectric equation is $eV_s = hf - \phi$, which rearranges to

$$V_s = \frac{h}{e} f - \frac{\phi}{e}$$

The gradient of the graph is therefore $h/e$ (M1). Both graphs are measurements of the same physical process on different metals, and neither $h$ nor $e$ depends on which metal is used, so the gradient is a constant of nature and must be identical for both lines. This is why the lines are parallel (A1).

The intercept on the frequency axis is the threshold frequency $f_0 = \phi/h$. This does depend on the metal, because the work function is the energy needed to remove an electron from that particular metal, and different metals hold their outer electrons more or less tightly (A1). Metal Y has the higher threshold frequency, so its work function is larger; more energy per photon is needed before any electron can escape. The intercept is therefore a property of the metal while the gradient is a property of nature.

(d) Using $V_s = (h/e)f - \phi/e$ with $f = 9.00 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$:

$$V_s(X) = 4.14 \times 10^{-15} \times 9.00 \times 10^{14} - 2.30 = 3.73 - 2.30 = 1.43\ \mathrm{V}$$ (M1) (A1)

For metal Y the same photon energy is available but the work function is larger by 0.90 eV, so

$$V_s(Y) = 3.73 - 3.20 = 0.53\ \mathrm{V}$$ (A1)

The difference between the two stopping potentials is exactly the difference of the work functions, 0.90 V, which is the vertical separation of the two parallel lines at that frequency.

Markscheme notes

Part (a): M1 for reading two points off a line, A1 for the gradient 4.1 x 10^-15 V s, A1 for the value of h. Accept any gradient from 3.9 to 4.4 x 10^-15 V s and any h from 6.2 to 7.0 x 10^-34 J s, since the graph must be read by eye. The unit must be given; a bare number scores M1 only.

Part (b): A1 for each threshold frequency read from the intercepts, A1 for one correct work function. Accept 5.4 to 5.6 x 10^14 Hz for X and 7.6 to 7.8 x 10^14 Hz for Y, with work functions 2.2 to 2.4 eV and 3.1 to 3.3 eV. A candidate who reads the intercepts from the wrong axis scores nothing here.

Part (c): M1 for identifying the gradient as h/e, A1 for the argument that h and e are constants and so the gradient cannot depend on the metal, A1 for the intercept as phi/h, which does depend on the metal. The second and third marks are independent; a candidate who says only that the lines are parallel because they are parallel scores nothing.

Part (d): M1 for the equation or for using the gradient, A1 for 1.43 V, A1 for 0.53 V. Accept 0.9 V for the difference as an alternative to the second value if both are shown to be consistent.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on the difference between the two parameters of a straight line. The graph of stopping potential against frequency is the standard photoelectric experiment, and the usual question asks for the gradient and stops there, which teaches that the gradient is h/e without ever asking what the intercept is for. Here both parameters are examined, and the item is arranged so that the gradient is deliberately uninformative: the two lines are drawn parallel, so taking the gradient of each separately returns the same number twice and yields nothing new. The information is entirely in the intercepts.

That structure makes part (c) the discriminating part. A candidate who has understood that $V_s = (h/e)f - \phi/e$ can say immediately that the gradient is a ratio of two constants of nature and therefore cannot depend on which metal is illuminated, while the intercept is the work function of that metal divided by e and therefore must differ. A candidate who has memorised the statement that the gradient of a photoelectric graph gives h has nothing to say about why the lines are parallel, because the memorised fact does not explain the parallelism, it merely coexists with it.

The figure is load-bearing in a specific way: the axis is marked every $1 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ so that the intercepts can be read to about one decimal place, and the two lines are generated from $V_s = (h/e)f - \phi/e$ with the work functions 2.30 eV and 3.20 eV, so the intercepts sit exactly at 5.55 and 7.72 in those units and the separation of the lines at any frequency is exactly 0.90 V. A candidate who reads the intercepts carelessly obtains work functions that disagree with the difference in part (d), which is a self-check the item provides without stating it.

Part (d) is deliberately a two-mark calculation that reuses the constant from part (a). It tests whether the candidate can apply the equation rather than only interpret it, and it gives a second route to the work-function difference: the two stopping potentials differ by exactly the difference of the work functions, which is the vertical separation of the parallel lines. A candidate who obtains a difference other than 0.90 V has made an error somewhere, and the item's own numbers expose it.
