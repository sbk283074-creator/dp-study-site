D.1 Total energy of a satellite in circular orbit, and why raising an orbit slows the satellite down

Physics HL · PHYS-D.1-102

Theme D: FieldsD.1 Gravitational fields (gravitational potential energy; escape speed; orbital motion)P215 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatDetermineExplain

Question

A satellite of mass $m = 800\ \mathrm{kg}$ is in a circular orbit of radius
$r = 7.0 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}$ about the Earth. Take
$G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\,m^{2}\,kg^{-2}}$ and
$M = 5.97 \times 10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg}$ for the mass of the Earth.

(a) Show that the kinetic energy of a satellite in a circular orbit of radius $r$ is

$$E_{K} = \frac{GMm}{2r}. \tag{3}$$

(b) Hence show that the total energy of the satellite is

$$E = -\frac{GMm}{2r},$$

and calculate its value. \tag{5}

(c) Determine the energy that must be supplied to move the satellite into a circular orbit of
radius $2r$. \tag{3}

(d) Show that the satellite is moving more slowly in the larger orbit even though energy has
been supplied to it, and determine the ratio of the two speeds. \tag{4}

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the kinetic energy in a circular orbit of radius $r$ is $E_{K} = \dfrac{GMm}{2r}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Hence show that the total energy is $E = -\dfrac{GMm}{2r}$ and calculate its value.

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine the energy that must be supplied to move the satellite into a circular orbit of radius $2r$.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Show that the satellite moves more slowly in the larger orbit, and determine the ratio of the two speeds.

    (Show that)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) For a circular orbit the gravitational force supplies the centripetal force (M1):

$$\frac{GMm}{r^{2}} = \frac{mv^{2}}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v^{2} = \frac{GM}{r} \tag{A1}$$

Hence

$$E_{K} = \frac{1}{2}mv^{2} = \frac{1}{2}m\frac{GM}{r} = \frac{GMm}{2r} \tag{AG}$$

(b) Taking gravitational potential energy as zero at infinity, the potential energy at radius
$r$ is $E_{P} = -\frac{GMm}{r}$ (M1). Adding the kinetic energy from (a) (M1),

$$E = E_{K} + E_{P} = \frac{GMm}{2r} - \frac{GMm}{r} = -\frac{GMm}{2r} \tag{A1}$$

Numerically, $GM = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24} = 3.98 \times 10^{14}\
\mathrm{m^{3}\,s^{-2}}$, so (A1)

$$E = -\frac{3.98 \times 10^{14} \times 800}{2 \times 7.0 \times 10^{6}}
= -\frac{3.19 \times 10^{17}}{1.4 \times 10^{7}} = -2.28 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J} \tag{A1}$$

The total energy is negative, which is what "bound" means: the satellite does not have enough
energy to escape. Note also that $E = -E_{K}$, a relation that holds for every circular orbit
and is often the fastest way to check a result of this kind.

(c) At radius $2r$ the total energy is

$$E' = -\frac{GMm}{2(2r)} = -\frac{GMm}{4r} = \frac{E}{2} = -1.14 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}
\tag{M1}$$

so the energy that must be supplied is (A1)

$$\Delta E = E' - E = -\frac{GMm}{4r} + \frac{GMm}{2r} = \frac{GMm}{4r}
= 1.14 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J} \tag{A1}$$

Note what this is *not*. Using $mg\,\Delta h$ with $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and
$\Delta h = r = 7.0 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}$ gives
$800 \times 9.81 \times 7.0 \times 10^{6} = 5.49 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$, nearly five times
too large, because $g$ is not constant over a distance comparable with the radius of the Earth.

(d) From (a), $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$ (M1), so

$$\frac{v_{2r}}{v_{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM/(2r)}{GM/r}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}} = 0.707 \tag{A1}$$

The speed in the larger orbit is about $71\%$ of the speed in the smaller one, so the satellite
is unambiguously moving more slowly (A1). The kinetic energy correspondingly halves, from
$2.28 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$ to $1.14 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$, while the total energy
rises from $-2.28 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$ to $-1.14 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$. There is no
contradiction: the energy supplied has gone into gravitational potential energy, which
increases by $2.28 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$, while the kinetic energy falls by
$1.14 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$ (A1). Roughly, half the energy supplied raises the orbit and
the other half pays for the satellite's lost kinetic energy.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for equating the gravitational force to the centripetal force. (A1) for
$v^{2} = \frac{GM}{r}$. (AG) for the final expression. No mark is available for quoting
$E_{K} = \frac{GMm}{2r}$ from memory.

(b) (M1) for the potential energy $-\frac{GMm}{r}$ with the zero at infinity. (M1) for adding
the two contributions. (A1) for the combined expression. (A1) for $GM = 3.98 \times 10^{14}$.
(A1) for $-2.28 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$; accept $[-2.3, -2.27] \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$.
The negative sign is essential and its omission loses the final (A1).

(c) (M1) for $E' = -\frac{GMm}{4r}$. (A1) for forming the difference. (A1) for
$1.14 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$. A candidate who computes $mg\,\Delta h$ here earns nothing;
if they also comment that the result exceeds the magnitude of the binding energy, award a
single mark for the observation.

(d) (M1) for $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$. (A1) for the ratio $0.707$. (A1) for concluding that
the speed is lower. (A1) for reconciling the fall in kinetic energy with the rise in total
energy — this is the conceptual mark and a bare "the ratio is $0.707$" does not earn it.

Condonation: allow the ratio to be given as $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ without a decimal.

Why this question is hard

The expression $E = -\frac{GMm}{2r}$ is one of those results that is easy to memorise and
easy to misuse, so the item makes the candidate build it in two steps: the kinetic energy from
the force balance, and then the sum with the potential energy. The building is quick, and it
buys something a memorised formula does not — the relation $E = -E_{K}$, which falls out of the
algebra and is the fastest available check on any circular-orbit calculation.

The interesting part is part (d), and it is genuinely counter-intuitive. Supplying energy to a
satellite makes it go slower. The resolution is that "lifting" and "speeding up" are not the
same thing, and the energy budget keeps separate accounts for them. Moving from $r$ to $2r$
increases the gravitational potential energy by $2.28 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$ and decreases
the kinetic energy by half that, so the net requirement is the difference,
$1.14 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$. A satellite in a higher orbit is higher up and slower, and
both of those are consequences of the same virial relation.

Part (c) contains the trap that is easiest to fall into and hardest to notice: reaching for
$mg\,\Delta h$. It is worth being explicit about why it fails so badly here. The formula
$mg\,\Delta h$ is the *uniform-field* approximation, valid when $\Delta h$ is small compared
with the radius of the Earth. Here $\Delta h = 7.0 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}$ is comparable
with the Earth's radius itself, so the field strength falls from about $8.1\
\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ at the lower orbit to about $2.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ at the upper one, and
using the surface value throughout gives an answer nearly five times too big. The general
lesson: before using a constant-$g$ formula, check that the distance involved is small compared
with the scale over which $g$ changes.

The item also quietly makes the point that a bound orbit has negative total energy, and that
"more bound" means more negative. The satellite in the lower orbit has *less* total energy
($-2.28 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{J}$) than the one in the higher orbit ($-1.14 \times 10^{10}\
\mathrm{J}$), which is why energy has to be supplied to go up, even though the satellite ends
up with less kinetic energy than it started with.
