5.5 A break-even output above the capacity ceiling: a negative margin of safety and the three single levers that would close the gap

Business management SL · BM-5.5-201

Unit 5: Operations management5.5 Break-even analysis (contribution, break-even output, margin of safety, target profit); 3.3 Costs and revenuesP2 · Section A16 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateDetermineShow thatDetermine

Question

Ridgeway Ceramics makes a single product on one kiln line. The selling price is $\pounds 40$ per unit and the variable cost is $\pounds 24$ per unit. Annual fixed costs are $\pounds 200{,}000$. The kiln line can produce at most $10{,}000$ units in a year, and there is no way to add a second line within the year.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the contribution per unit and the break-even output.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the margin of safety in units and as a percentage of capacity, and explain what a negative margin of safety means for this business.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Show that the best result Ridgeway Ceramics can achieve in a year is a loss of $\pounds 40{,}000$.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine the value of the selling price at which the business would exactly break even at its current capacity, and state the corresponding value for the annual fixed costs and for the variable cost per unit, taking each of the three on its own.

    (Determine)
  5. [4 marks](e)

    Evaluate which of the three changes in part (d) the management of Ridgeway Ceramics is most likely to be able to make, and justify your answer.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

[3 marks] (Calculate) (b) Determine the margin of safety in units and as a percentage of capacity, and explain what a negative margin of safety means for this business. [3 marks] (Determine) (c) Show that the best result Ridgeway Ceramics can achieve in a year is a loss of $\pounds 40{,}000$. [3 marks] (Show that) (d) Determine the value of the selling price at which the business would exactly break even at its current capacity, and state the corresponding value for the annual fixed costs and for the variable cost per unit, taking each of the three on its own. [3 marks] (Determine) (e) Evaluate which of the three changes in part (d) the management of Ridgeway Ceramics is most likely to be able to make, and justify your answer. [4 marks] (Evaluate)
Reveal the answer

(a) Contribution per unit is the selling price less the variable cost per unit (M1):

$$\pounds 40 - \pounds 24 = \pounds 16 \tag{A1}$$

Break-even output is fixed costs divided by contribution per unit (A1):

$$\frac{200{,}000}{16} = 12{,}500 \text{ units}$$

(b) The margin of safety is the amount by which the planned output exceeds the break-even output, so with output at the capacity ceiling (M1):

$$10{,}000 - 12{,}500 = -2{,}500 \text{ units}$$

As a percentage of capacity (A1):

$$\frac{-2{,}500}{10{,}000} \times 100 = -25\%$$

A negative margin of safety means the business cannot reach break-even at any output it is able to produce (A1). Every unit it makes sells for $\pounds 16$ more than its variable cost, and that contribution is never enough to cover $\pounds 200{,}000$ of fixed costs, so the business makes a loss in every year in which it operates. The negative figure is not a shortfall to be made up by selling harder; it is a statement that the break-even point lies outside the set of outputs the business can reach.

(c) At full capacity the total contribution is (M1)

$$10{,}000 \times \pounds 16 = \pounds 160{,}000$$

and the loss is the fixed costs less that contribution (A1):

$$\pounds 160{,}000 - \pounds 200{,}000 = -\pounds 40{,}000$$

Because contribution per unit is positive, each additional unit sold reduces the loss by $\pounds 16$, so profit increases with output throughout the feasible range (M1). The largest output the line can produce is therefore the least unprofitable one, and $\pounds 40{,}000$ is a loss at the best point rather than at a poor one: there is no output at which Ridgeway Ceramics makes a profit, so the maximum profit it can achieve in a year is a loss of $\pounds 40{,}000$.

(d) Each change is found by setting the total contribution at capacity equal to the fixed costs.

For the selling price, let it be $p$ (M1):

$$10{,}000(p - 24) = 200{,}000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p - 24 = 20 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = \pounds 44.00 \tag{A1}$$

For the annual fixed costs, with contribution per unit still $\pounds 16$:

$$10{,}000 \times 16 = \pounds 160{,}000 \tag{A1}$$

For the variable cost per unit, let it be $v$:

$$10{,}000(40 - v) = 200{,}000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 40 - v = 20 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \pounds 20.00 \tag{A1}$$

So the three thresholds are a price of $\pounds 44.00$ (a rise of $\pounds 4.00$, or 10%), fixed costs of $\pounds 160{,}000$ (a cut of $\pounds 40{,}000$, or 20%) and a variable cost of $\pounds 20.00$ (a cut of $\pounds 4.00$, or 16.7%). Each figure is a threshold and not a target: at exactly that value the business breaks even at full capacity and makes nothing, so any of the three has to be bettered before a profit appears.

(e) The three changes are not equally available, and the comparison has to be made on two axes: how large the change is, and whether the business controls it.

The price rise is the smallest proportional change and it requires no investment, no renegotiation and no new equipment, which makes it attractive on paper. It is also the least likely to hold. Ridgeway sells a single product into a market it does not control, and a 10% rise has to be accepted by every customer for the arithmetic to work; if it is accepted by fewer, the contribution gain on the remaining units does not compensate for the volume lost, and at a price of $\pounds 44$ the business needs *all* 10 000 units to break even, leaving no room for any loss of demand at all. The remedy that looks cleanest is the one with the narrowest margin for error.

The fixed-cost cut is the largest change in absolute terms, $\pounds 40{,}000$, and it is the one the business controls most directly, since fixed costs are largely internal commitments. But the kiln line is the fixed cost that matters, and it cannot be halved; a 20% reduction in a single-line ceramics business means cutting maintenance, staffing or the second shift, and cutting the second shift is precisely what would reduce capacity and make the position worse. A fixed-cost cut that lowers the ceiling lowers the output the business can sell, and the break-even output would have to be recalculated against the smaller capacity rather than assumed unchanged.

The variable-cost cut is the one most likely to be achievable. $\pounds 4.00$ per unit is a 16.7% reduction in the cost of materials and power, and variable costs are the category a manufacturer can most often attack: renegotiating a clay or glaze contract, reducing kiln waste, buying in larger quantities. It requires no change to the price the customer sees and no reduction in capacity, so it does not trade one problem for another. The difficulty is that a 16.7% cut is substantial for a business whose inputs are commodity-like, and any saving is likely to arrive in stages rather than at once.

Taken together, the most likely single change is the variable-cost reduction, and the strongest reason is not that it is the largest or the smallest but that it is the only one that leaves the capacity ceiling and the selling price untouched. The most reliable answer, however, is not to rely on any single lever: a smaller price rise combined with a smaller variable-cost reduction reaches the same threshold while exposing the business to less risk on either one, and a management that pursues only one of the three is betting the whole recovery on the assumption that is hardest to verify. What the figures cannot show is how the market would respond to a price rise or how far a supplier would move, and those are the questions that decide between the options.

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for the contribution of 16, one for dividing fixed costs by contribution, one for 12 500 units. A candidate who computes 200 000/24 scores no marks, because 24 is the variable cost rather than the contribution.

(b) One mark for subtracting the break-even output from capacity, one for -25%, one for the meaning. The meaning mark requires the candidate to say that break-even is unreachable at any feasible output; "the margin of safety is low" does not earn it, because the sign is the point.

(c) One mark for the total contribution at capacity, one for the loss of 40 000, one for the observation that positive contribution per unit makes the largest output the least unprofitable. A candidate who computes the loss at some other output, or who reports a profit, cannot score the third mark.

(d) One mark for setting total contribution equal to fixed costs in the price case, and one mark for each of the three thresholds: 44.00, 160 000 and 20.00. Follow-through applies: a candidate who used the wrong contribution in (a) but applies a consistent method should be given the method marks.

(e) The evaluation mark is awarded for a judgement that names one change as most likely and supports it with a reason that distinguishes the three. The remaining marks are for engaging with at least two of the three in terms of feasibility rather than arithmetic -- for example that the price rise has the least margin for error because it needs full volume, or that a fixed-cost cut may reduce capacity. A response that lists the three percentage changes without judging between them scores 0 for the evaluation.

Forfeits: computing the margin of safety as 12 500 - 10 000 = 2 500 and reporting it as positive, which reverses the entire item; treating 12 500 units as achievable and reporting a profit at that output. Condonation: a candidate who expresses the fixed-cost threshold as a 20% reduction rather than as 160 000, and who shows the working, should be given full credit.

Why this question is hard

Break-even is the first quantitative tool a business student meets, and it is almost always presented as a question with an answer: how many units must we sell? The formula is short, the answer is a number, and the number is treated as reachable. This item is built on the observation that the formula contains no capacity, so it will happily produce an answer the business cannot act on. Nothing in the calculation is wrong; what is wrong is the assumption, imported from every exercise the student has seen, that the break-even output is within the range of outputs the business can produce.

That assumption is normally harmless, which is what makes it worth attacking. In a well-posed question the break-even point sits below capacity, the margin of safety is positive, and the tool behaves as advertised. Here it sits 2 500 units above capacity, and every conclusion drawn from the standard reading of the result inverts: the business cannot become profitable by selling more, because selling more is already the best it can do and it still loses money. The margin of safety, which students are taught to read as a cushion, becomes a measure of how far outside the achievable range the break-even point lies.

The item also rewards a student who understands what contribution per unit does and does not mean. A positive contribution means each unit reduces the loss, so the loss is smallest at the largest feasible output. A student who has memorised "profit rises with output above break-even" will look for the profitable region and find none, and the honest conclusion -- that the best available outcome is a loss -- is the one the item is testing for. That conclusion is uncomfortable, and reaching it requires the student to report a result that no textbook exercise would ask for.

The final part moves the item from analysis to judgement, which is where the Business Management assessment objectives live. Three thresholds have been calculated and they are arithmetically interchangeable; deciding between them requires knowledge of the business that the numbers do not contain. The item does not have a single right answer, and it is marked as though it does not: what is assessed is whether the student can say why one lever is more available than another, and whether they notice that a fixed-cost cut in a single-line manufacturer may take capacity down with it. That last observation is the one that separates a student who has understood the model from one who has only learned to use it.
