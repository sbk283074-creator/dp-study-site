4.4 Non-response that is not random: recovering the population figure from two segment readings instead of from the pooled sample

Northgate Leisure: the climbing wall survey

Northgate Leisure runs a leisure centre with 400 members. It is deciding whether to build a climbing wall, and posts a questionnaire to all of them asking whether they would use it.

The membership has two segments. There are 100 premium members, who pay a higher monthly fee and visit at least twice a week, and 300 standard members, who pay the basic fee. Of the 100 premium members, 80 return the questionnaire. Of the 300 standard members, 90 return it.

Among the 80 premium respondents, 56 say they would use the climbing wall. Among the 90 standard respondents, 36 say they would use it. The marketing manager writes in her report that a majority of members want the climbing wall, and recommends that the board approve it.

Question

The chart compares the composition of the membership with the composition of the achieved sample. Use the data to answer the following.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the response rate for each segment and for the survey as a whole, and use them to show that the achieved sample is not representative of the membership.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the percentage of all respondents who said they would use the climbing wall, and state the conclusion the marketing manager draws from it.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Using the known size of each segment, determine a weighted estimate of the percentage of all 400 members who would use the wall, and hence estimate how many members that is. Explain why this figure differs from the one in part (b), and why the direction of the difference could have been predicted before any calculation.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Evaluate the marketing manager's claim that a majority of members want the climbing wall, and recommend how Northgate should have run or reported the survey so that the claim could be tested.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) Premium response rate: 80 ÷ 100 = 80%. Standard response rate: 90 ÷ 300 = 30%. Overall: 170 ÷ 400 = 42.5% (M1)(A1). Premium members are a quarter of the membership, 100 ÷ 400 = 25%, but they are 80 of the 170 responses, which is 47.1% of the achieved sample (A1). A sample in which one segment is represented at nearly twice its true weight is not representative, and the cause is visible in the response rates: premium members were more than two and a half times as likely to reply.
(b) Of the 170 responses, 56 + 36 = 92 said they would use the wall, so the pooled figure is 92 ÷ 170 = 54.1% (M1)(A1). The manager concludes that a majority — more than half — of members want the climbing wall, and recommends approval (A1).
(c) Weight each segment's approval rate by its share of the membership. Premium approval is 56 ÷ 80 = 70% and standard approval is 36 ÷ 90 = 40%. The weighted estimate is 0.25 × 70% + 0.75 × 40% = 17.5% + 30% = 47.5% (M1)(A1), which is 0.475 × 400 = 190 of the 400 members (A1). The pooled figure is higher because the sample contains too many premium members, and premium members are the segment that wants the wall most: 70% against 40%, a gap of thirty percentage points. The direction of the error was predictable before any arithmetic, because the segment with the higher response rate is also the segment with the higher approval rate, so self-selection must inflate the pooled answer (A1).
(d) The claim is not supported. The weighted estimate is 47.5%, which is below half, and 190 of 400 members is 10 short of a majority (M1). Two things qualify this. First, 47.5% is itself an estimate resting on the assumption that non-respondents in each segment resemble respondents in that segment — an assumption the data cannot test, and one that is least safe here, since people who did not bother to answer a questionnaire about a climbing wall may be people who do not want a climbing wall (A1). Second, the commercial case may not need a majority: if premium members generate most of the revenue and 70% of them want the wall, the board might rationally build it anyway (A1). The recommendation is to re-run the survey so that the answer can be tested rather than assumed: weight the responses, or use quota sampling with a target of 100 premium and 300 standard members, follow up non-respondents, and report the result by segment with the response rate stated alongside it (M1)(A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for a correct response rate calculation in any of the three forms, (A1) for all three rates correct, (A1) for the comparison of 25% of the membership with 47.1% of the sample. A candidate who says the sample is unrepresentative because only 42.5% replied, without comparing the two segments, has identified a real but different problem: credit it only if the segment comparison is also attempted.
(b) (M1) for 92 of 170, (A1) for 54.1%, (A1) for stating the manager's conclusion. The conclusion must be reported faithfully even though it is wrong; the evaluation comes in (d).
(c) (M1) for weighting by membership shares rather than by response counts, (A1) for 70% and 40%, (A1) for 47.5% and 190 members, (A1) for the direction argument — the segment that responds more also approves more, so the pooled figure must be too high. Credit an equivalent argument carried out in counts rather than percentages.
(d) (M1) for the conclusion that the claim is not supported, with the weighted figure used, (A1) for the point that non-respondents are unobserved and may differ, (A1) for a second developed consideration such as revenue weighting by segment, and (M1)(A1) for a recommendation that would let the claim be tested — quota sampling, follow-up of non-respondents, or reporting response rates and segment breakdowns. Do not credit 'the sample is too small' on its own: 170 responses is a large sample; the problem is which 170.

Why this question is hard

The item attacks the most common error in the market research topic, which is not a calculation error at all. Students are taught that a bigger sample is a better sample, and a sample of 170 from a population of 400 looks excellent: it is 42.5% of every member. The error is invisible in the pooled figures and appears the moment the sample is split by segment.

The design makes the two errors point the same way on purpose. Non-response is only a bias if the people who do not answer differ from those who do, so the item gives the student the means to see that difference (80% against 30%) and a payoff for seeing it (70% against 40%). The population figure is then recoverable from two aggregate readings — the two segment approval rates — combined with the known segment sizes, and it falls on the other side of the majority line the manager claimed. Recovering a whole from weighted parts is the actual skill, and it is the one a manager needs when a survey comes back looking decisive.

Part (d) keeps the item honest. The weighted estimate is still an assumption about people who did not answer, so the correct conclusion is that the manager's claim is unproven rather than false — a distinction that matters more, in practice, than the arithmetic that precedes it.

Challenge lever
The pooled sample of 170 is not 170 members; it is 80 of the 100 people who care most about the centre and 90 of the 300 who do not, so every premium reply stands for one and a quarter members while every standard reply stands for three and a third. Because the two segments disagree by thirty percentage points, the population figure has to be rebuilt from the two segment readings and their known sizes, and it lands on the other side of fifty per cent from the pooled sample.
