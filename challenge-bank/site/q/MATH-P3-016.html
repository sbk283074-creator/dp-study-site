P3 extended problem: counting monotone lattice paths and the Catalan numbers

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-016

Topic 1: Number and algebraAHL 1.10 (counting principles, including permutations and combinations), SL 1.9 (the binomial theorem), AHL 1.6 (simple deductive proof)P315 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatHenceDetermine

Question

A path on a grid starts at $(0,0)$ and ends at $(n,n)$, where $n$ is a positive integer. Each step of the path is either one unit to the right, written $R$, or one unit upwards, written $U$. A path is called admissible if it never passes through a point whose $y$-coordinate exceeds its $x$-coordinate; that is, every point $(x,y)$ on the path satisfies $y \le x$. Let $C_n$ denote the number of admissible paths from $(0,0)$ to $(n,n)$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that the total number of paths from $(0,0)$ to $(n,n)$ is $\dbinom{2n}{n}$, and that the number of paths that pass through at least one point with $y = x + 1$ is $\dbinom{2n}{n-1}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Hence show that $C_n = \dbinom{2n}{n} - \dbinom{2n}{n-1} = \dfrac{1}{n+1}\dbinom{2n}{n}$, and evaluate $C_n$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$.

    (Hence)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Show that $\dfrac{1}{k+1}\dbinom{2k}{k} = \dbinom{2k}{k} - \dbinom{2k}{k-1}$ for every integer $k \ge 1$, and deduce that $C_n$ is an integer for every $n \ge 1$ without evaluating the binomial coefficient.

    (Show that)
  4. [5 marks](d)

    Let $C(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} C_n x^{n}$ with $C_0 = 1$. By considering the first step at which an admissible path returns to the diagonal $y = x$, show that $C(x) = 1 + x\,C(x)^{2}$. Hence determine the closed form of $C(x)$ and verify that the coefficient of $x^{3}$ is $5$.

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) A path from $(0,0)$ to $(n,n)$ consists of exactly $n$ steps $R$ and exactly $n$ steps $U$, so it is determined by choosing which $n$ of the $2n$ positions in the sequence are the $R$ steps; hence the total number of paths is $\dbinom{2n}{n}$ (A1).

For the second count, consider a path that passes through at least one point with $y = x+1$, and let $T$ be the *first* such point. Reflect the portion of the path from $(0,0)$ up to and including $T$ in the line $y = x+1$, leaving the rest of the path unchanged (M1). Under this reflection the point $(0,0)$ maps to $(-1,1)$, each $R$ step before $T$ becomes a $U$ step and each $U$ step becomes an $R$ step, and $T$ itself lies on the mirror line and is fixed. The reflected path therefore runs from $(-1,1)$ to $(n,n)$, and it consists of $(n+1)$ steps right and $(n-1)$ steps up, again a monotone path of $2n$ steps in total (A1).

The correspondence is a bijection, because the first point at which a path from $(-1,1)$ to $(n,n)$ reaches $y = x+1$ is recovered by reflecting back, so the two sets have the same size (A1). The number of monotone paths from $(-1,1)$ to $(n,n)$ is the number of ways of choosing which $n-1$ of the $2n$ steps are the $U$ steps, that is $\dbinom{2n}{n-1}$ (A1).

(b) A path is admissible precisely when it never reaches $y = x+1$, so the admissible paths are exactly the paths counted in part (a) with the forbidden ones removed (M1):
$$C_n = \binom{2n}{n} - \binom{2n}{n-1} = \binom{2n}{n}\left(1 - \frac{n}{n+1}\right) = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}$$ (A1),
using $\dbinom{2n}{n-1} = \dfrac{n}{n+1}\dbinom{2n}{n}$ (A1). Substituting: $C_1 = \tfrac12\dbinom21 = 1$; $C_2 = \tfrac13\dbinom42 = 2$; $C_3 = \tfrac14\dbinom63 = 5$; $C_4 = \tfrac15\dbinom84 = 14$; $C_5 = \tfrac16\dbinom{10}{5} = 42$ (AG).

(c) By the factorial formula,
$$\binom{2k}{k-1} = \frac{(2k)!}{(k-1)!\,(k+1)!} = \frac{(2k)!}{k!\,k!} \cdot \frac{k}{k+1} = \frac{k}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}$$ (M1) (A1).
Therefore
$$\binom{2k}{k} - \binom{2k}{k-1} = \binom{2k}{k}\left(1 - \frac{k}{k+1}\right) = \frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k},$$
which is the required identity (A1). Since $\dbinom{2k}{k}$ and $\dbinom{2k}{k-1}$ are both integers, their difference is an integer; by the identity that difference equals $\dfrac{1}{k+1}\dbinom{2k}{k}$, and by part (b) this is exactly $C_k$ (AG). Hence $C_n$ is an integer for every $n \ge 1$ without the coefficient ever having been evaluated.

(d) Take an admissible path counted by $C_n$ with $n \ge 1$, and let $(j+1, j+1)$ be the first point after the origin at which it returns to the diagonal $y = x$ (M1). The path splits into three parts: the segment from $(0,0)$ to $(j+1, j+1)$, the segment from $(j+1,j+1)$ to $(n,n)$, and the single step that first reaches $(j+1,j+1)$. Every admissible path from $(0,0)$ to $(j+1,j+1)$ that does not return to the diagonal before its endpoint is the first step $R$, followed by an admissible path from $(1,0)$ to $(j+1,j+1)$ that stays above the diagonal; shifting that middle part down and left by one unit gives an arbitrary admissible path from $(0,0)$ to $(j,j)$ (M1). The final step into $(j+1,j+1)$ from $(j+1,j)$ or $(j,j+1)$ contributes one factor $x$ and the tail contributes a further $C(x)$. Summing over $j \ge 0$ and adding the empty path,
$$C(x) = 1 + x\,C(x)\cdot C(x) = 1 + x\,C(x)^{2}$$ (A1) (AG).

Rearranging, $x\,C^{2} - C + 1 = 0$, so by the quadratic formula $C = \dfrac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4x}}{2x}$ (M1). Since $C(x) \to C_0 = 1$ as $x \to 0$ while $\dfrac{1 + \sqrt{1-4x}}{2x} \to +\infty$, the admissible branch is
$$C(x) = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - 4x}}{2x}$$ (A1). Expanding, $C(x) = 1 + x + 2x^{2} + 5x^{3} + 14x^{4} + \cdots$, so the coefficient of $x^{3}$ is $5$, in agreement with $C_3 = 5$ from part (b) (AG).

Markscheme notes

(a) The total count earns (A1). The reflection argument must identify the *first* point on the line $y = x+1$; a candidate who reflects at an arbitrary such point does not obtain a bijection and forfeits (M1). Award (M1) for performing the reflection and (A1) for the bijection, and (A1) for the resulting count $\dbinom{2n}{n-1}$. Do not accept 'by symmetry' as a justification for the count of forbidden paths; the two sets are not of equal size and the argument is not a symmetry argument.

(b) Award (M1) for the subtraction, (A1) for the algebra giving $\dfrac{1}{n+1}\dbinom{2n}{n}$, and (A1) for the identity $\dbinom{2n}{n-1} = \dfrac{n}{n+1}\dbinom{2n}{n}$, which must be shown and not assumed. The five values earn (AG); one arithmetic slip loses the (AG) but not the earlier marks.

(c) The identity must be derived from the factorial definition; a candidate who verifies it for $k = 1, 2, 3$ and asserts it in general forfeits the second (A1). The deduction that $C_n$ is an integer requires the observation that a difference of two integers is an integer -- not the observation that the formula 'looks like' an integer. A candidate who instead proves integrality by a direct divisibility argument on $(n+1) \mid \binom{2n}{n}$ earns full marks if the argument is complete.

(d) The decomposition must be shown to be a bijection, not merely stated. Award (M1) for identifying the first return to the diagonal, (M1) for the shift argument that converts the 'stays above the diagonal' segment into an arbitrary admissible path of one size smaller, (A1) for $C = 1 + xC^{2}$, (M1) for solving the quadratic, (A1) for selecting the correct root using $C(0) = 1$, and (AG) for the coefficient check. The rejection of the positive root must be justified by the behaviour as $x \to 0$; a candidate who rejects it because 'the coefficients would be negative' should be given credit only if they demonstrate the negativity.

Why this question is hard

The difficulty of this item is that the constraint is global. Every step of an admissible path is subject to a condition that depends on the whole history of the path, so the natural approach -- condition on the first step and recurse -- produces a recursion that is not closed: after one step to the right the remaining problem is 'paths from $(1,0)$ to $(n,n)$ staying above the diagonal', which is a new kind of object, and recursing on it produces further new kinds. A student who pursues this route will compute $C_1$ through $C_5$ correctly and be unable to say why the general formula holds.

The way out is the reflection principle, and its character is worth noticing. Rather than count the admissible paths directly, it counts the *forbidden* ones, and it counts them by exhibiting a bijection with a set that has nothing to do with the constraint: all monotone paths from $(-1,1)$ to $(n,n)$. The reflection is a trick in the sense that it is not forced by the problem statement -- nothing in the question suggests reflecting anything -- but once seen, it is completely rigorous, because reflecting at the *first* crossing gives a bijection rather than merely an injection. This is the archetype of a non-obvious tool, and it is why the item's difficulty is not computational.

Part (c) is a small piece of bookkeeping with a satisfying payoff. The difference of two binomial coefficients is manifestly an integer, and the identity shows that this integer is $\dfrac{1}{n+1}\dbinom{2n}{n}$; so the Catalan numbers are integers by an argument that never divides anything. This is worth pausing on, because the formula $\dfrac{1}{n+1}\dbinom{2n}{n}$ gives no indication that the division comes out exactly, and a student who only knows the formula will find that fact surprising.

Part (d) changes the tool again. The recurrence $C = 1 + xC^{2}$ is a statement about the whole sequence at once, obtained by decomposing a path at its first return to the diagonal; it is the standard 'first-return decomposition' and it is the reason the Catalan numbers have a closed generating function. Solving the quadratic is elementary, but selecting the correct root is not: the two roots differ by $\dfrac{\sqrt{1-4x}}{x}$, which is singular at the origin, and only the condition $C(0) = 1$ -- that is, the fact that there is exactly one path of length zero -- chooses between them. Students who solve the quadratic and pick a sign by guesswork will pick the wrong one about half the time.

Topic
Topic 1: Number and algebra — P3 extended problem: counting monotone lattice paths and the Catalan numbers
Challenge lever
The counting problem is stated in a way that invites case analysis: count the paths for small n, look for a pattern, and try to generalise by splitting on the first step. The first step is exactly where the naive attempt stalls, because the constraint couples the two coordinates -- how many steps up you may still take depends on how many steps right you have already taken -- so the obvious recursion is not self-contained. The tool that works, the reflection bijection, is not the obvious one: it counts the forbidden paths by counting something that has nothing to do with the original problem, namely all paths from a reflected starting point. A student who does not find it can still compute the right numbers for small n and will then be unable to justify the general formula, which is exactly the failure the item is built to expose.

Adaptation
The source genre supplies the lattice-path setting, the reflection argument and the first-return decomposition. Three of the permitted changes were made. First, the constraint was fixed as 'never above the diagonal' with the diagonal drawn as $y = x$, which makes the forbidden condition the single line $y = x+1$ and therefore makes the reflection a single explicit map; the source material often states the constraint in a form in which the reflecting line has to be discovered. Second, a part was added asking for the integrality of $C_n$ to be deduced from the identity $\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k} = \binom{2k}{k} - \binom{2k}{k-1}$, which is normally left implicit. Third, the generating-function part was restricted to the quadratic $C = 1 + xC^{2}$ and its solution, and the usual asymptotic via Stirling's approximation was deliberately omitted because Stirling lies outside the IB syllabus; the closed form is instead checked against $C_3 = 5$. The context and all data are original.
Verification
The reflection count was checked arithmetically: for n = 1..8 the number of paths containing a point with y = x+1, obtained as the total minus the admissible count, equals C(2n, n-1) in every case, giving 1, 4, 15, 56, 210, 792, 3003, 11440. The admissible counts were confirmed to be the Catalan numbers 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132, 429, 1430, and the identity C(2n,n) - C(2n,n-1) = C(2n,n)/(n+1) was checked as an exact integer equality for n = 1..8. The identity C(2k,k)/(k+1) = C(2k,k) - C(2k,k-1) was checked as an exact equality for k = 1..7. The closed form was evaluated at x = 0.01 and compared with the series: C(0.01) = 1.0102051443 and 1 + 0.01 C(0.01)^2 = 1.0102051443, agreeing to 2e-15, which confirms the quadratic identity numerically as well as symbolically.
