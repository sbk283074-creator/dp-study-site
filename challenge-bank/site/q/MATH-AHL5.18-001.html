5.18 First order differential equations

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.18-001

AHL 5.18 (first order differential equations; integrating factor), AHL 5.14 (implicit differentiation)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatHenceFindDetermine

Question

A curve $C$ lies in the first quadrant and passes through the point $(1,\,4)$.

The tangent to $C$ at any point $\mathrm{P}(x,\,y)$ meets the $x$-axis at the point $\mathrm{A}$. The area of triangle $\mathrm{OAP}$, where $\mathrm{O}$ is the origin, is equal to $2$ for every position of $\mathrm{P}$ on $C$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $\mathrm{A}$ has coordinates $\left(x-\dfrac{y}{y'},\,0\right)$, where $y'=\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}-\dfrac{x}{y}=-\dfrac{4}{y^{2}}$.

    (Show that)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Hence find an expression for $x$ in terms of $y$.

    (Hence)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine the least possible value of $x$ on $C$, and justify your answer.

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Tangent at $\mathrm{P}$: $Y-y=y'(X-x)$ where $(X,\,Y)$ is a general point on the tangent. (M1)
At $\mathrm{A}$, $Y=0$, so $-y=y'(X-x)$, giving $X=x-\dfrac{y}{y'}$ (valid since $y'\neq0$ on $C$). (A1)
Hence $\mathrm{A}=\left(x-\dfrac{y}{y'},\,0\right)$. (A1)

(b) $\mathrm{OA}=x-\dfrac{y}{y'}$ and the perpendicular height of $\mathrm{P}$ above $\mathrm{OA}$ is $y$, so

$$\text{area}=\tfrac12\left(x-\frac{y}{y'}\right)y=2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \left(x-\frac{y}{y'}\right)y=4.$$ (M1)

Expanding: $xy-\dfrac{y^{2}}{y'}=4$, so $\dfrac{y^{2}}{y'}=xy-4$ and therefore $y'=\dfrac{y^{2}}{xy-4}$. (A1)

Inverting, $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}=\dfrac{xy-4}{y^{2}}=\dfrac{x}{y}-\dfrac{4}{y^{2}}$. (M1)

Hence $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}-\dfrac{x}{y}=-\dfrac{4}{y^{2}}$. (A1)

(c) The left-hand side becomes a single derivative once divided by $y$:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)=\frac{y\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}-x}{y^{2}}=\frac1y\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}-\frac{x}{y}\right)=\frac1y\left(-\frac{4}{y^{2}}\right)=-\frac{4}{y^{3}}.$$ (M1)(A1)

Integrating with respect to $y$: $\dfrac{x}{y}=\displaystyle\int -4y^{-3}\,\mathrm{d}y=\dfrac{2}{y^{2}}+k$. (M1)

So $x=\dfrac{2}{y}+ky$. (A1)

Using $(1,\,4)$: $1=\dfrac24+4k \Rightarrow k=\dfrac18$, hence $\boxed{x=\dfrac{2}{y}+\dfrac{y}{8}},\quad y>0$. (A1)

(Equivalently $y^{2}-8xy+16=0$.)

(d) From (c), $x=\dfrac2y+\dfrac y8$ with $y>0$. By AM–GM,

$$x=\frac2y+\frac y8 \;\ge\; 2\sqrt{\frac2y\cdot\frac y8}=2\sqrt{\frac14}=1,$$ (M1)

with equality when $\dfrac2y=\dfrac y8$, i.e. $y^{2}=16$, so $y=4$ and then $x=1$. (A1)

Since $x\to\infty$ as $y\to0^{+}$ and as $y\to\infty$, this stationary value is the least value: $x_{\min}=1$ at $(1,\,4)$. (R1)

(Equivalently $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}=-\dfrac{2}{y^{2}}+\dfrac18=0 \Rightarrow y=4$, with $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}<0$ for $0<y<4$ and $>0$ for $y>4$.)

Markscheme notes

(a) Allow the tangent equation in any correct form. A student who writes the tangent as $y=mx+c$ and finds the intercept earns (M1) only if the substitution of $m=y'$ is shown.

(b) Award (M1) for $\tfrac12(\text{base})(\text{height})$ with base $=x-\tfrac{y}{y'}$ and height $=y$; condone a missing modulus since $y>0$. The reciprocal step from $y'$ to $\tfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}$ must be seen — a candidate who stays in $\tfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}$ cannot reach (c).

(c) The intended route is the recognition of $\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{y}\right)$. Accept the formal integrating-factor method: IF $=\exp\!\left(\int-\tfrac1y\,\mathrm{d}y\right)=\tfrac1y$, giving $\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{y}\right)=-\tfrac4{y^{3}}$ and the same result. Award full marks for either.

(d) Accept a sign argument on $\tfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}$ in place of AM–GM. The justification that the stationary point is a *least* value is required for the final (R1); do not award it for $y=4$ alone.

Alternative for (c): writing the answer as $y^{2}-8xy+16=0$ and verifying it satisfies the condition in (b).

Why this question is hard

The difficulty is almost entirely in the set-up, not the calculus. Two things have to be noticed before any standard technique applies:

1. The constant-area condition is a statement about the *tangent*, so the first job is to convert a geometric sentence into an equation involving $y'$. Students often try to use the length $\mathrm{AP}$ instead of the perpendicular height $y$, which produces an unworkable expression.
2. The resulting differential equation, $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}=\dfrac{y^{2}}{xy-4}$, is not separable and is not linear in $y$. It *is* linear in $x$. Recognising that $\dfrac1y\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}-\dfrac{x}{y}\right)$ is exactly the derivative of $\dfrac{x}{y}$ is the single step that unlocks the question — this is the same observation that stands behind the integrating-factor formula, but here it has to be made explicitly rather than recalled.

The data were chosen so that the given point $(1,\,4)$ is the leftmost point of the curve, which is why part (d) collapses to the neat value $x=1$; a candidate who sketches the curve will see that the curve is a hyperbola with two branches meeting at $(1,\,4)$.

Classic wrong turns: assuming $\mathrm{A}$ lies between $\mathrm{O}$ and the foot of the perpendicular from $\mathrm{P}$; treating $k$ as zero; and, in (d), differentiating $x=\tfrac2y+\tfrac y8$ with respect to $x$ rather than $y$.
