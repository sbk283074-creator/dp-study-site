A weather station that samples every 16 milliseconds

A weather station records a temperature every 16 ms. Each reading is stamped with the elapsed time in milliseconds since the shift began, so during the first 128 ms the eight keys that are stored are 16, 32, 48, 64, 80, 96, 112 and 128.

The readings are held in a hash table of m buckets. The hash function is h(k) = k mod m, and collisions are resolved by linear probing: a key whose bucket is already occupied is placed in the next bucket that is free, wrapping around to bucket 0 if the end of the table is reached. A lookup follows the same sequence and stops when it finds the key or reaches an empty bucket.

Two table sizes are under consideration, m = 16 and m = 17. A colleague has also suggested doubling the table to m = 32, on the grounds that a larger table must be faster.

Question

Evaluate whether the load factor is a reliable predictor of the cost of a lookup in this table. Work in probes, where one probe examines one bucket. Assume the eight keys are inserted in increasing order, that no key is ever deleted, and that the table is empty before the first insert.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the bucket index that each of the eight keys hashes to when m = 16, and state the number of probes needed to insert each of those keys.

    (Calculate)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Determine the load factor of the table at m = 16 and at m = 17, and determine how many of the m buckets each table can ever reach with these eight keys. Give both reachable shares as percentages to two decimal places.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the number of probes needed to look up the key 128, and the number needed to establish that the key 144 is absent, in each of the m = 16 and m = 17 tables. Explain why the two load factors do not predict the difference you find.

    (Explain)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Your colleague proposes doubling the table from m = 16 to m = 32. Determine the load factor and the reachable-bucket share at m = 32, and justify whether the proposal addresses the problem you identified in part (c).

    (Justify)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

Part (a). Every key is a multiple of 16, because 16 = 16 x 1, 32 = 16 x 2 and so on up to 128 = 16 x 8. So for every one of the eight keys k mod 16 = 0. (M1) All eight keys hash to bucket 0. (A1)

Linear probing then places them in consecutive buckets starting at 0: the first key finds bucket 0 free and takes it, the second finds it occupied and takes bucket 1, and so on. The probes needed are therefore 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8, and the total for the eight inserts is 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 7 + 8 = 36. (A1)

The finished table holds 16 in bucket 0, 32 in bucket 1, 48 in bucket 2, and so on up to 128 in bucket 7. Buckets 8 to 15 are empty.

Part (b). At m = 16 the table holds 8 keys in 16 buckets, so the load factor is 8 / 16 = 0.50. (M1)(A1) At m = 17 it is 8 / 17 = 0.470588..., which is 0.47 to two decimal places. (A1)

The reachable share is a different quantity. At m = 16 only bucket 0 is ever reached, so 1 of the 16 buckets is in play: 1 / 16 = 6.25%. (M1)(A1)

At m = 17 the keys hash to 16, 15, 14, 13, 12, 11, 10 and 9 — eight distinct buckets, because 17 shares no factor with the key step of 16, so each successive key lands in the bucket before the previous one. So 8 of the 17 buckets are reachable: 8 / 17 = 47.06%. (A1)

It is worth noticing that at m = 17 the reachable share and the load factor are the same number, 47.06%, because every key occupies a bucket of its own. That coincidence does not hold at m = 16, where the two quantities are 0.50 and 0.0625 — a factor of eight apart. The two quantities are equal only when no two keys collide, which is exactly the case in which there is nothing to predict.

Part (c). At m = 16 the key 128 was the eighth key inserted, so it sits at the end of a run of eight occupied buckets that begins at bucket 0. A successful search for 128 examines buckets 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7: eight probes. (M1)(A1)

The key 144 is also 0 mod 16, so it too begins at bucket 0. It must examine all eight occupied buckets and then bucket 8, which is empty, before it can conclude that the key is absent: nine probes. (A1)

At m = 17, 128 mod 17 = 9 and bucket 9 holds 128 alone, so the successful search costs one probe. And 144 mod 17 = 8, and bucket 8 is empty, so the unsuccessful search also costs one probe. (M1)(A1)

The two load factors are 0.50 and 0.47, differing by 0.03, or about 6% of either value. Yet the successful search costs eight times as much in one table as in the other, and the unsuccessful search nine times as much. A quantity that differs by 6% cannot account for a cost that differs by 700%. The load factor describes how full the table is; what governs the cost here is how the keys *distribute*, and with a key step of 16 and a table size of 16 the distribution is degenerate — every key competes for one bucket. The load factor is a correct measure of a real property that happens not to be the property that decides this question.

Part (d). At m = 32 the keys 32, 64, 96 and 128 are all 0 mod 32, and 16, 48, 80 and 112 are all 16 mod 32. So the keys occupy exactly two buckets, 0 and 16, each holding four keys. (M1)(A1)

The load factor is 8 / 32 = 0.25, exactly half of 0.50. (A1) The reachable share is 2 / 32 = 1 / 16 = 6.25%, which is unchanged from m = 16. (A1)

So the proposal does reduce the worst-case successful search from eight probes to four, and the unsuccessful search from nine to five, because each run of colliding keys is now half as long. But it does not address the defect found in part (c). The table can still reach only one sixteenth of its buckets, and it has paid for that with twice as much memory. Doubling the table halves the *consequence* of the clustering while leaving its *cause* untouched, and the cause is that the table size shares a factor with the key step. Changing the size to 17 removes that shared factor; doubling it to 32 does not, and doubling it again to 64 would not either. The general rule is that the number of buckets a key set can reach is m divided by the greatest common divisor of m and the key step, capped by the number of keys — 1 at m = 16, 2 at m = 32, 8 at m = 17.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for recognising that every key is a multiple of 16 and therefore 0 mod 16. A1 for all eight keys in bucket 0. A1 for the probe counts 1 to 8, or for the total of 36, or for the finished table. A candidate who writes "k mod 16 = k" has not seen the divisibility and scores nothing in this part.

(b) M1 for the load factor definition, A1 for 0.50. A1 for 0.47 (accept 0.4706, accept 8/17). M1 for the idea that the reachable share is counted in buckets rather than keys. A1 for 6.25% at m = 16. A1 for 47.06% at m = 17. Do not award the last A1 for an answer that reports 100% or 8/8: the denominator is the number of buckets, not the number of keys.

(c) M1 for the correct probe count on one of the two tables, A1 for eight probes to find 128 at m = 16. A1 for nine probes to establish that 144 is absent. M1 for the corresponding m = 17 figures, A1 for both being one probe. The explanation must connect the near-equal load factors to the very different costs; a candidate who reports the numbers but says only that the tables are "different" has not answered the question and should lose the final A1.

(d) M1 for the two occupied buckets, A1 for identifying buckets 0 and 16 with four keys each. A1 for 0.25. A1 for the reachable share staying at 6.25%. The justification must separate the halved worst case from the unchanged reachable share. Accept a conclusion either way provided the two quantities are distinguished; a candidate who claims the reachable share improves has not done part (b) correctly.

Why this question is hard

This item is built on a quantity that is correct, standard and useless for the question asked. The load factor is the first thing a prepared candidate computes when a hash table is described, and here it is genuinely informative about one thing — how full the table is — while being almost silent about the thing that matters, which is how long a lookup takes.

The trap is laid by making the two load factors nearly equal in the two tables whose costs are furthest apart. At m = 16 and m = 17 the load factors are 0.50 and 0.47, a difference of 0.03. A candidate who reasons "the load factors are almost the same, so the tables perform almost the same" has done the arithmetic correctly and drawn the wrong conclusion, and nothing in the arithmetic will warn them. The correct answer needs a second quantity — how many buckets the key set can reach — that is not in the standard vocabulary of load factor and probe length.

The reason the two diverge is structural. The keys are 16, 32, ..., 128, so every key is a multiple of 16, and the number of buckets a key set can reach is m divided by the greatest common divisor of m and the key step. At m = 16 that divisor is 16 and the table has one usable bucket; at m = 17 it is 1 and the table has eight, one per key. The load factor cannot see this because it counts keys and buckets, not residues.

Part (d) is where the item earns its label rather than merely stating it. Doubling the table halves the load factor and halves the worst-case probe count, so a candidate who trusted the load factor will find that it *worked* — and will conclude that the proposal is sound. It is not, because the reachable share does not move at all: 2 of 32 buckets is 6.25%, exactly what 1 of 16 was. The load factor improved, the probe length improved, and the underlying defect is untouched. A single measurement that moves in the right direction is not evidence that the cause has been addressed, and the second measurement — the one nobody thinks to take — is what shows it.
