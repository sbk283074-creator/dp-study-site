Two curves that are reflections, and the range of k that keeps both roots

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL2.9-001

SL 2.9 (exponential functions and their graphs, including the asymptote and the relationship between y = a^x and y = (1/a)^x) and SL 2.10 (solving equations analytically)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedSketchShow thatFindDetermine

Question

Let $f(x)=3^{x}$ and $g(x)=3^{-x}$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Sketch the graphs of $y=f(x)$ and $y=g(x)$ on the same axes, stating the equation of the asymptote and the coordinates of the $y$-intercept of each.

    (Sketch)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that the graph of $y=g(x)$ is the reflection of the graph of $y=f(x)$ in the $y$-axis, and find the coordinates of their point of intersection.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the two transformations that map the graph of $y=f(x)$ onto the graph of $y=3^{x-2}+1$, and state the equation of the asymptote and the coordinates of the $y$-intercept of the image.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the set of values of $k$ for which $3^{2x}-4\cdot3^{x}+k=0$ has two distinct real solutions.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Both curves have the negative $x$-axis as a horizontal asymptote, that is $y=0$ (A1). Since $3^{0}=1$, both pass through $(0,1)$ (A1). The graph of $f$ rises from left to right without bound, while the graph of $g$ falls from left to right, tending to zero (A1)(A1). Both curves lie entirely above the $x$-axis, so neither ever meets it, and the only point they share is the intercept.

(b) Replacing $x$ by $-x$ in $f$ gives $f(-x)=3^{-x}=g(x)$ (M1)(A1), so $g$ is the reflection of $f$ in the $y$-axis. At an intersection $3^{x}=3^{-x}$ (M1), so $3^{2x}=1$ and hence $x=0$ (A1); the point is $(0,1)$. The reflection is a symmetry of this particular pair of curves, and it does not depend on the value of the base.

(c) We have $3^{x-2}+1=f(x-2)+1$. The graph of $f(x-2)$ is obtained by translating $y=f(x)$ by $2$ units in the positive $x$-direction (A1), and adding $1$ translates the result by $1$ unit in the positive $y$-direction (A1). The asymptote $y=0$ moves to $y=1$ (A1), and the $y$-intercept is $3^{0-2}+1=\frac{1}{9}+1=\frac{10}{9}$, that is $\left(0,\frac{10}{9}\right)$ (A1). Because both operations are translations, their order is immaterial, and the image is the same curve either way.

(d) Put $u=3^{x}$, so $u>0$ and the equation becomes $u^{2}-4u+k=0$ (M1). Two distinct real solutions in $x$ require two distinct positive roots $u$ (R1). The discriminant $16-4k$ must be positive, giving $k<4$ (M1)(A1), and the product of the roots, equal to $k$, must be positive, giving $k>0$ (A1). Hence $0<k<4$ (A1). The product condition is the one that is easily overlooked, and it is what removes the negative values of $k$ from the answer.

Markscheme notes

(a) The sketch need not be to scale, but both curves must show the correct shape: one increasing, one decreasing, both passing through $(0,1)$ and both asymptotic to the $x$-axis. Award the asymptote mark for $y=0$ and the intercept mark for $(0,1)$. A candidate who draws only one curve cannot earn more than two of the four marks.

(b) The (M1) is for the substitution $x\mapsto-x$, and the (A1) for identifying it as a reflection in the $y$-axis. The intersection mark is independent: award it for any correct argument, including equating indices.

(c) The order of the two translations does not matter here, because both are translations. Accept a translation of $2$ to the right and $1$ up in either order. The asymptote of the image is $y=1$, not $y=0$. The $y$-intercept must be exact; a decimal such as $1.11$ forfeits the final (A1).

(d) The (R1) is the crucial mark: it is not enough that the quadratic in $u$ has two distinct roots, because $u=3^{x}$ must be positive. A candidate who gives only $k<4$ has forgotten the positivity of $u$. Follow through from a correct discriminant in an otherwise wrong part.

Extension: for $k\le0$ exactly one root is positive, so the original equation has exactly one real solution.

Why this question is hard

The question concerns two exponential curves that are mirror images of each other, but the difficulty is concentrated in the final part, where a hidden quadratic in $3^{x}$ appears. Parts (a) to (c) are standard graph work and are there to establish the vocabulary of asymptote, intercept and transformation before the algebraic twist.

The most common error is in part (d). Candidates set the discriminant of $u^{2}-4u+k=0$ to be positive, obtain $k<4$, and stop. That is necessary but not sufficient: the substitution $u=3^{x}$ restricts $u$ to positive values, so both roots of the quadratic must be positive. For a quadratic with leading coefficient one, the product of the roots equals $k$, so positivity of the product gives $k>0$. The sum of the roots is $4$, which is positive automatically, so no further condition is needed. The answer is therefore the open interval $0<k<4$.

A candidate who reasons only about the discriminant will include negative values of $k$ in the answer. At $k=-1$, for instance, the quadratic $u^{2}-4u-1=0$ has one positive and one negative root, and only the positive root corresponds to a real $x$, so there is exactly one solution rather than two. This is the fastest numerical check.

Other traps include treating the asymptote of $3^{x-2}+1$ as $y=0$; computing the $y$-intercept as $3^{-2}$ and forgetting the $+1$; and in part (b) assuming the curves meet where the graph crosses the axis. The base three is a red herring, since every positive base other than one behaves in the same way and the structure of the answer does not depend on it. The base is arbitrary, so the result is really a statement about all exponential functions whose base is greater than one.
