Two reflections that agree on a set

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL2.16-001

AHL 2.16 (graphs of y = |f(x)|, y = f(|x|) and y = 1/f(x)), AHL 2.15 (solutions of g(x) >= f(x) graphically and analytically)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedSketchSolveWrite down

Question

Let $f(x)=x^{2}-4x+3$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    On the same axes, sketch the graphs of $y=|f(x)|$ and $y=f(|x|)$, stating the coordinates of all intercepts and of every local minimum or maximum.

    (Sketch)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Solve the equation $|f(x)|=f(|x|)$, giving your answer as a union of intervals.

    (Solve)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Solve the inequality $f(|x|)<2$, giving exact values in your answer.

    (Solve)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the range of $g(x)=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}$ and the equations of all three asymptotes of its graph.

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) $f(x)=(x-1)(x-3)$, so the parabola has roots at $x=1$ and $x=3$ and its minimum is $f(2)=-1$.

$y=|f(x)|$: the part of the parabola below the axis is reflected upwards. Intercepts $(1,0)$, $(3,0)$, $(0,3)$; local maxima at $(1,0)$ and $(3,0)$; local minimum $(2,1)$. (M1)(A1)

$y=f(|x|)$: the right-hand half is reflected in the $y$-axis. Intercepts $(-3,0)$, $(-1,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(3,0)$ and $(0,3)$; local minima at $(-2,-1)$ and $(2,-1)$; local maximum $(0,3)$. (M1)(A1)

(b) For $x\geq 0$, $f(|x|)=f(x)$, so the equation becomes $|f(x)|=f(x)$, which holds exactly when $f(x)\geq 0$, i.e. $x\leq 1$ or $x\geq 3$. (M1)(A1) On $[0,\infty)$ this gives $[0,1]\cup[3,\infty)$.

For $x<0$, $f(|x|)=f(-x)=x^{2}+4x+3$ while $|f(x)|=x^{2}-4x+3$, since $f(x)>0$ for all $x<0$. Equating gives $x^{2}+4x+3=x^{2}-4x+3$, so $8x=0$ and $x=0$, which is not negative. (M1) There are therefore no negative solutions, and the solution set is $[0,1]\cup[3,\infty)$. (A1)

(c) With $u=|x|\geq 0$, $f(|x|)<2$ becomes $u^{2}-4u+1<0$. (M1) The roots are $u=2\pm\sqrt{3}$, so $2-\sqrt{3}<u<2+\sqrt{3}$. (A1) Since $u=|x|$, the solution is $x\in\left(-\left(2+\sqrt{3}\right),-\left(2-\sqrt{3}\right)\right)\cup\left(2-\sqrt{3},\ 2+\sqrt{3}\right)$. (A1)

(d) $f$ has minimum value $-1$ at $x=2$ and $f(x)\to\infty$ as $x\to\pm\infty$, so the range of $f$ is $[-1,\infty)$. Therefore the range of $g$ is $(-\infty,-1]\cup(0,\infty)$. (M1)(A1)

The vertical asymptotes are $x=1$ and $x=3$; the horizontal asymptote is $y=0$. (A1)(A1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a): accept sketches that show the correct shape and the stated key points; a candidate who reflects only one branch, or who draws $y=f(|x|)$ with the minimum at $(2,-1)$ only on the right, loses one (M1). Do not require accurate curvature. Part (b): the crucial mark is the split at $x=0$; a candidate who solves only the positive case and writes $[0,1]\cup[3,\infty)$ with no mention of $x<0$ earns (M1)(A1) and loses the remaining two marks. Accept an argument from the sketch that the two graphs coincide precisely where the parabola is above the axis and $x\geq0$, provided the negativity of $x$ on the other side is addressed. Part (c): accept $0.27<|x|<3.73$ as an intermediate, and condone a final answer in the form $|x|\in(2-\sqrt{3},2+\sqrt{3})$ only if the resulting $x$-interval is also given. Part (d): the (A1) for the range depends on identifying the minimum of $f$; a candidate who gives $(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)$ has missed the upper bound of the negative branch entirely and loses both range marks but keeps the three asymptote marks.

Why this question is hard

Two different transformations are applied to the same function, and the whole question turns on noticing that they are not the same transformation. $|f(x)|$ acts on the output and therefore lifts the part of the graph below the axis; $f(|x|)$ acts on the input and therefore discards the left half and mirrors the right half across the $y$-axis. Students who conflate them produce a single curve and then cannot see why part (b) has two cases.

Part (b) is the pivot. Because $f$ is positive for every $x<0$, the modulus on the left is inert there, while the reflection on the right changes the function completely; equating the two branches forces $x=0$, which lies outside the interval being considered. The correct conclusion — that the equation is satisfied exactly where $f(x)\geq0$ and $x\geq0$ — is a genuine piece of reasoning, and the answer is a union of two intervals rather than the single interval most students write.

Part (c) is a routine substitution $u=|x|$ and then a careful unwinding, and it is included because the unwinding is where marks are lost: the interval in $u$ must become two intervals in $x$, and the exact surds should survive to the final answer.

Part (d) is the part of the question most likely to be got wrong by a strong student. The range of $1/f$ is not determined by the asymptotes but by the minimum value of $f$, which is $-1$. Because $f$ attains $-1$, the reciprocal attains $-1$ as its largest negative value, and the range has a closed upper end at $-1$. Students who reason 'as $f\to 0^{+}$, $1/f\to+\infty$, and as $f\to 0^{-}$, $1/f\to-\infty$' and conclude that the range is all real numbers except zero have drawn the right asymptotes and reached the wrong conclusion.

Metadata

Challenge lever
The equation |f(x)| = f(|x|) looks like a single case but splits into two because the two transformations act on different sides of the axis, and the negative branch turns out to have no solutions at all; the final part then requires the minimum value of f to be used as the endpoint of the range of 1/f rather than the obvious asymptote.

Adaptation
Modulus and reciprocal graph questions in the IB bank almost always ask for a sketch or for the asymptotes alone. Here the two different modulus transformations are set against each other in an equation, the reciprocal graph's range depends on a finite minimum rather than an asymptote, and the inequality reuses the transformation from part (a). The function, all four parts and the exact surd answer are original.
Verification
Solved analytically. f(x) = (x-1)(x-3) with minimum -1 at x = 2. On x < 0, f(x) > 0 and f(|x|) = x^2 + 4x + 3, so equality forces 8x = 0. The inequality u^2 - 4u + 1 < 0 has roots 2 -/+ sqrt(3) = 0.2679492 and 3.7320508. The range of 1/f follows from the minimum of f being -1.
