A.3 A vehicle driven at constant power, whose driving force therefore falls as the speed rises, so that the constant-acceleration equations do not apply

Physics HL · PHYS-A.3-201

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.3 Work, energy and power: power as the rate of doing work, applied to a vehicle whose engine delivers constant power, and the relationship between driving force, power and speedP213 marksdifficulty 3technology: permittedCalculateShow thatDetermineExplain

Question

A car of mass $m = 1200\ {\rm kg}$ travels along a straight horizontal road. Its engine delivers a constant mechanical power of $P = 60\ {\rm kW}$ to the wheels, and at the speeds considered the resistive forces may be neglected in parts (a) to (d). At the instant from which times are measured the car is travelling at $10\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$. Take $g = 9.81\ {\rm m\,s^{-2}}$, though note that the road is horizontal so its value is not needed until part (e) at the earliest.
The relationship between the driving force $F$, the power $P$ and the speed $v$ is $P = Fv$.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Calculate the driving force exerted on the car when it is travelling at $10\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$, and again when it is travelling at $20\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Show that, while the power remains constant and resistance is neglected, the acceleration of the car is inversely proportional to its speed, and calculate the acceleration at each of the two speeds in part (a).

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine the time taken for the car to accelerate from $10\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$ to $20\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$, using the fact that the work done by the engine in that interval equals the increase in kinetic energy of the car.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    A student obtains a different answer to part (c) by using the constant-acceleration equation $t = (v - u)/a$ with the acceleration at $10\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$. State the value that route gives, explain why it is wrong, and determine the mean acceleration over the interval for comparison with the two values from part (b).

    (Explain)
  5. [2 marks](e)

    In reality a constant resistive force of $500\ {\rm N}$ opposes the motion. Determine the maximum speed the car can reach on the level, and state what the acceleration is at that speed.

    (Determine)

Total: 13 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Using $P = Fv$ with $P = 6.0 \times 10^{4}\ {\rm W}$:
$$F_{10} = \frac{6.0 \times 10^{4}}{10} = 6.0 \times 10^{3}\ {\rm N}, \qquad F_{20} = \frac{6.0 \times 10^{4}}{20} = 3.0 \times 10^{3}\ {\rm N} \;(M1)(A1).$$
The driving force halves when the speed doubles, even though the power is the same throughout.
(b) With no resistance the net force is the driving force, so $F = ma$ and
$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{P}{mv} \;(M1).$$
Since $P$ and $m$ are both constant, $a \propto 1/v$ as required (A1). Numerically,
$$a_{10} = \frac{6.0 \times 10^{3}}{1200} = 5.0\ {\rm m\,s^{-2}}, \qquad a_{20} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{3}}{1200} = 2.5\ {\rm m\,s^{-2}} \;(A1).$$
(c) The work done by the engine in time $t$ at constant power is $Pt$, and this equals the gain in kinetic energy:
$$Pt = \tfrac{1}{2}m\bigl(v^{2} - u^{2}\bigr) \;(M1)$$
$$t = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}(1200)\bigl(20^{2} - 10^{2}\bigr)}{6.0 \times 10^{4}} = \frac{600 \times 300}{6.0 \times 10^{4}} = \frac{1.80 \times 10^{5}}{6.0 \times 10^{4}} = 3.0\ {\rm s} \;(M1)(A1).$$
(d) Using $t = (v-u)/a$ with $a = 5.0\ {\rm m\,s^{-2}}$ gives
$$t = \frac{20 - 10}{5.0} = 2.0\ {\rm s} \;(A1),$$
which is too short. The constant-acceleration equations require the acceleration to be constant, and part (b) showed that it is not: the driving force is $P/v$, so as the car speeds up the force, and hence the acceleration, falls continuously from $5.0$ to $2.5\ {\rm m\,s^{-2}}$ over the interval (M1)(A1). Because the acceleration is falling, the car spends longer at the slower end of the range than a constant-acceleration model allows, so the true time exceeds $2.0\ {\rm s}$. The mean acceleration over the interval is
$$\bar{a} = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{10}{3.0} = 3.3\ {\rm m\,s^{-2}} \;(A1),$$
which does lie between $2.5$ and $5.0\ {\rm m\,s^{-2}}$ as it must, and is closer to the smaller of the two because the car spends more time near the lower end of the speed range.
(e) At the maximum speed the driving force has fallen to equal the resistive force, so the net force, and hence the acceleration, is zero (M1). With $F = 500\ {\rm N}$:
$$v_{\max} = \frac{P}{F} = \frac{6.0 \times 10^{4}}{500} = 120\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}, \qquad a = 0 \;(A1).$$
(This is about $430\ {\rm km\,h^{-1}}$, which is far faster than any ordinary car; the figures were chosen to make the arithmetic clean rather than realistic.)

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for using $P = Fv$, (A1) for both forces. Accept $6.0\ {\rm kN}$ and $3.0\ {\rm kN}$. A candidate who gives the same force at both speeds has assumed constant force and should be followed through into (b) but loses this mark.
(b) (M1) for combining $F = ma$ with $P = Fv$, (A1) for stating the inverse proportionality explicitly, (A1) for both accelerations. Candidates may instead note that doubling the speed halves the force and therefore halves the acceleration; this is acceptable as the demonstration of proportionality.
(c) (M1) for the energy statement $Pt = \Delta E_{k}$, (M1) for substituting with the squares of the speeds taken correctly, (A1) for $3.0\ {\rm s}$. A candidate who writes $\tfrac{1}{2}m(v-u)^{2}$ instead of $\tfrac{1}{2}m(v^{2}-u^{2})$ should be treated as having made a physics error rather than an arithmetic one and loses the second mark.
(d) (A1) for stating $2.0\ {\rm s}$, (M1) for identifying that the acceleration is not constant, (A1) for explaining the direction of the error in terms of varying acceleration, (A1) for the mean acceleration $3.3\ {\rm m\,s^{-2}}$ and the observation that it lies between the two endpoint values. Four fields; credit three. Do not accept 'the acceleration averages out' without the remark that it is the time spent at each speed that matters.
(e) (M1) for recognising that driving force equals resistive force at the maximum speed, (A1) for $120\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}$ and $a = 0$. Accept the speed in kilometres per hour if the conversion is shown.

Why this question is hard

There is one idea in this item and it is a small one, which is why the item is claimed at difficulty 3 rather than 4 or 5. Constant power is not constant force. Everything else follows from that in a line or two, and nothing in the item asks the candidate to do anything they have not done many times before.

What makes it worth asking at all is how reliably the wrong route is triggered. The stem supplies a mass, an initial speed, a final speed and the word 'accelerates', which is the complete trigger set for the constant-acceleration equations. A candidate who uses them gets $2.0\ {\rm s}$ against the correct $3.0\ {\rm s}$, a fifty per cent error produced by reading one word -- 'power' -- as if it said 'force'.

The correct route is also the one that generalises, and that is the second reason to teach it. Because power is the rate of transfer of energy, the work done over any interval is $Pt$ whatever the force was doing inside that interval, so the energy statement $Pt = \Delta E_{k}$ holds even when nothing about the motion is uniform. The constant-acceleration equations need uniformity; the energy statement does not. Whenever a question gives power rather than force, the energy route is the one that is guaranteed to be available.

The mean acceleration in part (d) is a useful check rather than a required answer. It comes out at $3.3\ {\rm m\,s^{-2}}$, between the endpoint values $5.0$ and $2.5$, and noticeably closer to the lower one. That asymmetry is the signature of a falling acceleration: the car spends more of the interval at lower speeds, where the acceleration is larger but the time spent is longer, and the two effects tilt the mean towards the slower end. A candidate who computes a mean acceleration above $5.0$ or below $2.5$ has made an error that they can now detect without being told.

Finally, part (e) closes the loop back onto force. At the maximum speed the driving force has fallen to meet the resistive force, the net force is zero and so is the acceleration. The same relation $P = Fv$ that made the force fall at low speed is what sets the ceiling at high speed, and seeing both ends of it in one item is worth more than either alone.
