2.12 The number of real roots of x^3 - 3x + k, and the parameter range that gives three

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL2.12-201

Topic 2: FunctionsAHL 2.12 (polynomial functions, their graphs and equations) with SL 2.7 and AHL 5.8 (local maximum and minimum points)P1 · Section B12 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedDetermineDetermineShow thatSketch

Question

The function $f$ is defined by $f(x) = x^{3} - 3x + k$, where $k$ is a real constant.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Determine the coordinates of the two stationary points of the graph of $f$, in terms of $k$.

    (Determine)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the set of values of $k$ for which the equation $f(x) = 0$ has three distinct real roots.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Show that $k = 2$ gives a repeated root, find that root, and factorise $f(x)$ completely for this value of $k$.

    (Show that)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    Sketch the graph of $y = f(x)$ for a value of $k$ between $-2$ and $2$, marking the stationary points and the three roots.

    (Sketch)

Total: 12 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

The function $f$ is defined by $f(x) = x^{3} - 3x + k$, where $k$ is a real constant. (a) Determine the coordinates of the two stationary points of the graph of $f$, in terms of $k$. [3 marks] (Determine) (b) Determine the set of values of $k$ for which the equation $f(x) = 0$ has three distinct real roots. [4 marks] (Determine) (c) Show that $k = 2$ gives a repeated root, find that root, and factorise $f(x)$ completely for this value of $k$. [3 marks] (Show that) (d) Sketch the graph of $y = f(x)$ for a value of $k$ between $-2$ and $2$, marking the stationary points and the three roots. [2 marks] (Sketch)
Reveal the answer

(a) Differentiate (M1):
$$f'(x) = 3x^{2} - 3 = 3(x - 1)(x + 1)$$

So $f'(x) = 0$ at $x = \pm 1$ (A1). Evaluating:
$$f(-1) = -1 + 3 + k = k + 2, \qquad f(1) = 1 - 3 + k = k - 2$$

The stationary points are $(-1,\,k+2)$ and $(1,\,k-2)$ (A1). Since $f''(x) = 6x$, the first is a local maximum and the second a local minimum.

(b) For a cubic with a positive leading coefficient, the graph rises from $-\infty$, turns down at the local maximum, turns up again at the local minimum, and rises to $+\infty$. Three distinct real roots occur exactly when the local maximum is above the axis and the local minimum is below it (M1)(R1):
$$k + 2 > 0 \quad\text{and}\quad k - 2 < 0$$

That is (M1)
$$-2 < k < 2 \tag{A1}$$

The same conclusion follows from the discriminant of $x^{3} + px + q$, which is $-4p^{3} - 27q^{2}$; here $p = -3$ and $q = k$, so the discriminant is $108 - 27k^{2}$, and it is positive precisely when $k^{2} < 4$.

(c) At $k = 2$ the local minimum is at $(1,\,0)$, so $x = 1$ is a root (M1). Dividing $x^{3} - 3x + 2$ by $(x - 1)$:
$$x^{3} - 3x + 2 = (x - 1)(x^{2} + x - 2) = (x - 1)(x - 1)(x + 2) \tag{A1(A1)}$$

So
$$f(x) = (x - 1)^{2}(x + 2)$$

and the roots are $x = 1$ (repeated, because the curve touches the axis there) and $x = -2$. By symmetry, $k = -2$ gives $f(x) = (x + 1)^{2}(x - 2)$.

(d) For example $k = 0$ gives $f(x) = x(x^{2} - 3)$, so the roots are $x = 0$ and $x = \pm\sqrt{3} \approx \pm 1.73$. The sketch should show a cubic rising from the lower left, a local maximum at $(-1,\,2)$, a local minimum at $(1,\,-2)$, and three crossings of the horizontal axis, one between $-2$ and $-1$, one at the origin, and one between $1$ and $2$.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for differentiating, A1 for $x = \pm 1$, A1 for both coordinates in terms of $k$. Accept the stationary points given as $x$-values with the corresponding $y$-values stated separately. A candidate who does not identify which is the maximum and which the minimum should not be penalised here, but the distinction is needed in (b).

(b) M1 for the condition on the two turning points, R1 for stating that the maximum must be positive and the minimum negative (or the equivalent geometric statement), M1 for translating into inequalities in $k$, A1 for $-2 < k < 2$. A candidate who quotes the discriminant $108 - 27k^{2}$ and solves the inequality should be given full credit. Strict inequalities are required: a candidate who writes $-2 \le k \le 2$ has included the repeated-root cases and loses the A1.

(c) M1 for substituting $k = 2$ and identifying $x = 1$ as a root, A1 for the quadratic factor, A1 for the complete factorisation or for both roots with the repetition stated. Accept $f(x) = (x-1)^{2}(x+2)$ or $f(x) = (x-1)(x-1)(x+2)$. The symmetry remark about $k = -2$ is not required.

(d) A1 for the correct shape with the three roots in the right places, A1 for both stationary points marked with the values $k \pm 2$. A sketch with the maximum and minimum interchanged scores 0 on the second mark.

Forfeits: using $f''(x) = 3x$ by differentiating incorrectly; concluding from the discriminant alone without any reference to the turning points, which is accepted in (b) but leaves (d) unsupported. Condonation: a candidate who works throughout with $x^{3} - 3x - k$ and reverses the sign of the final range consistently should be given full credit.

Why this question is hard

The item looks like a routine curve-sketching exercise and is actually about the relationship between a graph and its parameters. The number of real roots of a cubic is not a property of the coefficients in the abstract; it is a statement about where the turning points sit relative to the horizontal axis, and the whole question follows from locating those two points and looking at their signs.

The boundary is where the marks are lost. At $k = \pm 2$ one turning point touches the axis, so two of the three roots coalesce and the equation has a repeated root rather than three distinct ones. The distinction between $< $ and $\le$ is therefore the difference between the right answer and a wrong one, and it is exactly the kind of boundary that a student relying on a memorised discriminant formula may not think to check. Part (c) makes the boundary concrete by factorising the $k = 2$ case completely, so the repeated root can be seen rather than merely asserted.

Part (d) is deliberately placed last and carries only two marks, but it does real work: it forces the student to exhibit a member of the family, and the choice of $k = 0$ makes the roots irrational, which means the sketch cannot be produced by guessing rational roots. A student who understands the structure can sketch the general shape and place the roots by reference to the stationary points, which is the transferable skill. The symmetry between $k = 2$ and $k = -2$ is not required but is worth noticing, because the original cubic is odd apart from the constant.
