The touch that silences the first seven harmonics

Physics HL · PHYS-C.4-001

C.4 Standing waves and resonance (boundary conditions, nodes and antinodes, the harmonic series of a string fixed at both ends, resonance conditions)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedCalculateShow thatDetermineExplain

Question

A string of length $L=0.80\ \mathrm{m}$ is stretched between two rigid supports with a tension $T=24\ \mathrm{N}$. Its linear mass density is $\mu=0.015\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}$, and the tension may be taken as uniform along the whole string. A player lightly touches the string at a point a distance $d$ from the left support, holding that point fixed, and then sets the string vibrating; the touch forces a node at that point but does not otherwise alter the string. Only a standing wave that already has a node at the touched point can be excited.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the speed of transverse waves on the string, and calculate the frequencies of the first three standing-wave modes of the string when it is not touched.

    (Calculate)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    With the string touched at $d=0.30\ \mathrm{m}$, show that a standing wave can be excited only if the mode number $n$ satisfies $d=jL/n$ for some integer $j$, deduce that $n$ must be a multiple of $8$, and hence determine the lowest frequency that the touched string can produce.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the frequencies of the next two modes that the touched string can produce, and determine the total number of nodes, including the two fixed ends, in the lowest of these allowed modes. State how many of the untouched harmonics below that lowest allowed frequency are absent.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    The player now touches the string at its midpoint instead. Explain how the set of allowed modes changes, determine the new lowest frequency, and explain why every odd harmonic is now missing.

    (Explain)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) For a stretched string the wave speed is given by $v=\sqrt{T/\mu}$: (M1)

$$v=\sqrt{\frac{24}{0.015}}=\sqrt{1600}=40\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.$$ (A1)

The standing-wave condition for a string fixed at both ends is $L=n\lambda/2$, so $f_{n}=nv/(2L)$: (M1)

$$f_{n}=\frac{n(40)}{2(0.80)}=25n\ \mathrm{Hz},$$

giving $f_{1}=25\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $f_{2}=50\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $f_{3}=75\ \mathrm{Hz}$. (A1)

(b) A standing wave on the string has zero displacement at every node, and the nodes of the $n$th mode lie at distances $x_{j}=jL/n$ from the left support, with $j=0,1,2,\dots,n$. (M1) The touched point is held fixed, so it must be a node, and therefore $d=jL/n$ for some integer $j$. (A1) Substituting $d=0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ and $L=0.80\ \mathrm{m}$,

$$n=\frac{jL}{d}=\frac{j(0.80)}{0.30}=\frac{8j}{3}.$$ (A1)

For $n$ to be an integer, $j$ must be a multiple of $3$, so $j=3,6,9,\dots$ and the mode number must be $n=8,16,24,\dots$ — a multiple of $8$. (A1) The lowest allowed mode is therefore $n=8$, with frequency

$$f=\frac{8v}{2L}=\frac{8(40)}{1.60}=200\ \mathrm{Hz}.$$ (A1)

(c) The next two allowed modes are $n=16$ and $n=24$: (M1)

$$f_{16}=16\times25=400\ \mathrm{Hz},\qquad f_{24}=24\times25=600\ \mathrm{Hz}.$$ (A1)

In the $n=8$ mode the nodes lie at $x_{j}=j(0.80)/8=0.10j\ \mathrm{m}$ for $j=0,1,\dots,8$, that is at $0.00,0.10,0.20,\dots,0.80\ \mathrm{m}$. Counting the two fixed ends, there are $9$ nodes in all. (A1) The untouched harmonics below $200\ \mathrm{Hz}$ are $25,50,75,100,125,150$ and $175\ \mathrm{Hz}$ — seven of them — and all seven are absent. (A1)

(d) Touching at the midpoint sets $d=L/2$, so $n=jL/d=2j$ and only even mode numbers survive: the allowed frequencies are $50,100,150,\dots\ \mathrm{Hz}$. (M1) The new lowest frequency is therefore the second harmonic,

$$f=\frac{2v}{2L}=\frac{2(40)}{1.60}=50\ \mathrm{Hz}.$$ (A1)

Every odd harmonic has an antinode at the midpoint, so holding the midpoint fixed suppresses all of them; only the even harmonics, which already have a node there, are unaffected. (A1) In general a touch at a distance $L/p$ from one end selects the harmonics whose numbers are multiples of $p$: the midpoint touch ($p=2$) keeps the even harmonics, and the one-third touch ($p=3$) keeps every third.

Markscheme notes

(a) The first (M1) is for $v=\sqrt{T/\mu}$; a candidate who writes $v=T/\mu$ or $v=\sqrt{\mu/T}$ forfeits it. Accept $40\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and the set $25\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $50\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $75\ \mathrm{Hz}$. Follow-through applies: a wrong $v$ propagates consistently through (b) to (d) and those marks remain available.

(b) The essential step is that the touched point is held at zero displacement and is therefore a node. A candidate who instead treats the touch as cutting the string into two independent segments and looks for a frequency common to both will usually reach the same lowest answer here, but that is not the intended reasoning; award the method mark only if the node condition is stated. The (A1) for $n=8j/3$ must come from substituting $d=0.30$ and $L=0.80$. A candidate who assumes that a touch at three-eighths of the length gives the eighth harmonic by analogy with the familiar one-half and one-third cases earns nothing unless the condition is derived.

(c) Accept $400\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $600\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The node count must include the two fixed ends; a candidate who reports $8$ nodes has counted only the interior nodes and loses the mark. Accept the number seven or the explicit list $25$ to $175\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

(d) The (M1) is for $n=2j$, and the new lowest frequency is $50\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The final mark requires the reason to be stated in terms of the midpoint: odd harmonics have an antinode at the centre, while even harmonics already have a node there. 'Because the string is halved' is not sufficient, since the two halves are not independent in this situation.

Why this question is hard

A stretched string fixed at both ends is the standard example of a harmonic series, and the result $f_{n}=nv/(2L)$ is the first thing a candidate reaches for. The question is designed so that this formula is not the answer. Touching the string at a point does not divide it into two independent strings; it forces a node there, and the whole string must still vibrate as a single standing wave. The modes that can be excited are therefore exactly those whose own node pattern already contains the touched point.

For a mode with $n$ half-wavelengths along the string, the nodes lie at $jL/n$. Requiring one of them to coincide with the touch at $d$ gives $d=jL/n$, or $n=jL/d$. With $d=0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ and $L=0.80\ \mathrm{m}$ this is $n=8j/3$, and because $n$ must be an integer the smallest possible mode number is $8$, not $1$ and not $3$. The lowest note the touched string can give is the eighth harmonic, at $200\ \mathrm{Hz}$ — eight times the fundamental.

The arithmetic is a number-theoretic condition rather than an algebraic one: the touch point is three-eighths of the way along, and $8/3$ only becomes an integer when it is multiplied by three. This is why the familiar cases are the easy ones. Touching at the midpoint gives $n=2j$ and the even harmonics; touching at one third of the length gives $n=3j$ and the third, sixth and ninth. The three-eighths position is deliberately not one of these, so a candidate who tries to generalise from the familiar cases without deriving the node condition will fail.

Part (c) also asks for the node count, which tests whether the candidate has understood that the touched point is a node and not a new end: the eighth mode has nine nodes, not eight, because the two fixed ends count.

The classic wrong turns are: quoting $f_{n}=nv/(2L)$ and reporting $25\ \mathrm{Hz}$ as the lowest available note; treating the touch as cutting the string into two separate strings of length $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ and $0.50\ \mathrm{m}$ and searching for their common resonances, which can give the right number for the wrong reason; and forgetting that the fixed ends are nodes when counting. There is also a practical check worth making: on a real instrument the touched harmonic is much weaker than the open string, because the finger damps the other modes and forces the energy into the one pattern that has a node beneath it.
