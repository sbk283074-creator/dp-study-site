A correlation of 0.8 that licenses no claim of cause

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.4-001

SL 4.4 (linear correlation of bivariate data; Pearson's product-moment correlation coefficient r)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: requiredShow thatCalculateInterpretExplain

Question

Ten paired observations $(x,y)$ are summarised by $\sum x=60$, $\sum y=90$, $\sum x^{2}=400$, $\sum y^{2}=900$ and $\sum xy=588$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that $S_{xx}=40$ and $S_{yy}=90$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Calculate $S_{xy}$ and hence the product-moment correlation coefficient $r$.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Interpret $r$ in context, calculate the coefficient of determination, and comment on what it measures.

    (Interpret)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    A student claims that because $r$ is close to $1$, an increase in $x$ causes an increase in $y$; explain why that conclusion does not follow from $r$.

    (Explain)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The corrected sums are $S_{xx}=\sum x^{2}-\frac{(\sum x)^{2}}{n}=400-\frac{60^{2}}{10}$ (M1) $=400-360=40$ (A1). Similarly $S_{yy}=\sum y^{2}-\frac{(\sum y)^{2}}{n}=900-\frac{90^{2}}{10}$ (M1) $=900-810=90$ (A1)(AG). Both subtract the part of the sum of squares explained by the mean, leaving the variation about the mean.

(b) The cross term is $S_{xy}=\sum xy-\frac{(\sum x)(\sum y)}{n}=588-\frac{60\times 90}{10}$ (M1) $=588-540=48$ (A1). Hence $r=\frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}}=\frac{48}{\sqrt{40\times 90}}$ (M1) $=\frac{48}{\sqrt{3600}}=\frac{48}{60}=0.8$ (A1). The denominator is the geometric mean of the two variations, so $r$ measures the shared variation.

(c) The value $r=0.8$ indicates a strong positive linear relationship between $x$ and $y$, meaning that larger values of $x$ tend to accompany larger values of $y$ (A1). The coefficient of determination is $r^{2}=0.64$ (M1)(A1), so about $64$ per cent of the variation in $y$ is accounted for by the linear relationship with $x$. It measures the strength of the linear association only, not the agreement between the variables (R1). A high $r^{2}$ does not show that the linear model is the right one.

(d) A correlation coefficient measures association within the observed data and cannot establish direction or cause (M1). An increase in $y$ could cause an increase in $x$, or a third variable could drive both, so the relationship is symmetric in $x$ and $y$ and the roles can be exchanged without changing $r$ (A1)(A1). The correlation also depends on the range of values sampled and says nothing about values outside that range (R1). Causation requires additional evidence, such as a controlled experiment or a plausible mechanism.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for the corrected-sum-of-squares form and (A1) for $S_{xx}=40$; the second (M1)(A1) pair is for $S_{yy}=90$. Accept the equivalent expressions $\sum(x-\bar{x})^2$. A candidate who uses $n-1$ rather than $n$ in the correction term should be corrected, since the formula uses the mean of the sample.

(b) The (M1) is for $S_{xy}$ in corrected form and the (A1) for $48$. The second (M1) is for the correlation formula, and the (A1) for $0.8$. A candidate who reaches $0.8$ from a correct formula earns full credit even if $S_{xx}$ or $S_{yy}$ were slightly wrong, provided the follow-through is consistent.

(c) The first (A1) is for a contextual interpretation of $r$. The (M1)(A1) pair is for $r^{2}=0.64$, and the (R1) is for stating that $r^{2}$ measures linear association only. A bare percentage without context does not earn the (R1).

(d) The (M1) is for recognising that correlation does not determine direction. The (A1)(A1) marks are for the reverse-direction possibility and for a confounding variable. The (R1) is for a valid statement about the need for further evidence. Reject any answer that simply asserts correlation is not causation without applying it to this data.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on the product-moment correlation coefficient and on what it does not license. The summary statistics are given in raw form, so the first task is to convert them into the corrected sums of squares. The sample size is ten, and the corrections subtract $\frac{60^{2}}{10}=360$ and $\frac{90^{2}}{10}=810$, leaving $S_{xx}=40$ and $S_{yy}=90$. These values are the variation about the mean in each variable, and the cross term $S_{xy}=48$ measures how much of that variation is shared.

The coefficient is then $\frac{48}{\sqrt{3600}}=\frac{48}{60}=0.8$, a value that most candidates will recognise as strong. The square, $0.64$, is the coefficient of determination and is the quantity that carries a percentage interpretation. The trap is the final part, where a student treats a large $r$ as evidence of cause. Correlation is symmetric: exchanging $x$ and $y$ leaves $r$ unchanged, so the data alone cannot tell which variable, if either, drives the other.

The common errors are to use $n-1$ in the correction term, which changes both $S_{xx}$ and $S_{yy}$ and destroys the exact value $0.8$, and to interpret $r^{2}$ as the proportion of points lying on the line. The numbers were chosen so that the denominator is exactly $60$ and the coefficient is exactly $0.8$, and so that the sample size is hidden inside the corrections rather than stated in the summaries. A candidate who notices that $r$ is unchanged when the roles of $x$ and $y$ are exchanged has the cleanest argument against causation.
