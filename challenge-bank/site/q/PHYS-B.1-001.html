Two readings in a vacuum chamber: emissivity and specific heat

Physics HL · PHYS-B.1-001

B.1 Thermal energy transfers (specific heat capacity, latent heat, conduction, convection, radiation, Stefan-Boltzmann law)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedExplainShow thatDetermine

Stimulus

A block of an unknown alloy is suspended by a fine thread inside a chamber that has been evacuated and whose walls are held at a constant temperature. An electric heater inside the block supplies a constant power. The following values are measured.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| mass of the block, $m$ | $0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$ |
| total surface area, $A$ | $0.020\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$ |
| electrical power supplied, $P$ | $15.0\ \mathrm{W}$ |
| wall temperature, $T_{\text{wall}}$ | $300\ \mathrm{K}$ |
| steady temperature of the block, $T_{s}$ | $420\ \mathrm{K}$ |
| initial rate of fall of temperature at $420\ \mathrm{K}$, after the heater is switched off | $0.130\ \mathrm{K\,s^{-1}}$ |
| Stefan-Boltzmann constant, $\sigma$ | $5.67\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}$ |

Question

The chamber is evacuated so that the block loses energy to the walls by radiation only, and conduction along the supporting thread is negligible.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Explain why, with the chamber evacuated, conduction and convection can be neglected and the block loses energy only by radiation, and state the expression for the net power radiated by the block.

    (Explain)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    The block reaches a steady temperature of $420\ \mathrm{K}$. Show that the emissivity of its surface is $\varepsilon=0.575$, and calculate the value of $\varepsilon$.

    (Show that)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    The heater is switched off when the block is at $420\ \mathrm{K}$ and the temperature then falls at an initial rate of $0.130\ \mathrm{K\,s^{-1}}$. Determine the specific heat capacity of the alloy, explaining why the radiated power at this instant is the same as during the steady state.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    When the block has cooled to $360\ \mathrm{K}$, determine the rate of fall of temperature predicted by the Stefan-Boltzmann law and the ratio of this rate to the rate at $420\ \mathrm{K}$. Explain why a model in which the rate is proportional to $(T-T_{\text{wall}})$ predicts a different ratio.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) In an evacuated chamber there is no gas to conduct heat or to carry it by convection, and radiation is the only transfer mechanism that needs no medium. (A1) The block emits $\varepsilon\sigma A T^{4}$ and absorbs $\varepsilon\sigma A T_{\text{wall}}^{4}$ from the walls, (A1) so the net power it loses is

$$P_{\text{rad}}=\varepsilon\sigma A\left(T^{4}-T_{\text{wall}}^{4}\right).$$ (A1)

(b) At steady state the electrical power supplied is exactly balanced by the net radiated power:

$$P=\varepsilon\sigma A\left(T_{s}^{4}-T_{\text{wall}}^{4}\right)$$ (M1)

$$\varepsilon=\frac{P}{\sigma A\left(T_{s}^{4}-T_{\text{wall}}^{4}\right)}$$ (A1)

$$\varepsilon=\frac{15.0}{5.67\times10^{-8}\times 0.020\times\left(3.1117\times10^{10}-8.100\times10^{9}\right)}$$ (A1)

$$=\frac{15.0}{5.67\times10^{-8}\times 0.020\times 2.3017\times10^{10}}=\frac{15.0}{26.10}=0.575$$ (A1)

(c) At the instant the heater is switched off the block is still at $420\ \mathrm{K}$, and the net radiated power depends only on the temperatures of the block and the walls, so it is unchanged at $15.0\ \mathrm{W}$. (M1) The thermal energy of the block now falls at a rate $mc\left|\dfrac{\Delta T}{\Delta t}\right|$, and this must equal the radiated power:

$$mc\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta t}=\varepsilon\sigma A\left(T_{s}^{4}-T_{\text{wall}}^{4}\right)=15.0\ \mathrm{W}$$ (M1)(A1)

$$c=\frac{15.0}{m\,\Delta T/\Delta t}=\frac{15.0}{0.50\times 0.130}=\frac{15.0}{0.065}$$ (A1)

$$=231\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$$ (A1)

(d) At $360\ \mathrm{K}$,

$$P=\varepsilon\sigma A\left(360^{4}-300^{4}\right)=0.5747\times 5.67\times10^{-8}\times 0.020\times\left(1.6796\times10^{10}-8.100\times10^{9}\right)$$ (M1)

$$=0.5747\times 5.67\times10^{-8}\times 0.020\times 8.6962\times10^{9}=5.67\ \mathrm{W}$$ (A1)

$$\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta t}=\frac{P}{mc}=\frac{5.67}{0.50\times 230.8}=0.0491\ \mathrm{K\,s^{-1}}$$ (A1)

so the ratio to the rate at $420\ \mathrm{K}$ is $\dfrac{0.0491}{0.130}=0.378$. A model in which the rate is proportional to $\left(T-T_{\text{wall}}\right)$ would instead give $\dfrac{360-300}{420-300}=\dfrac{60}{120}=0.50$. (A1) The two predictions differ by about 30%, and it is the $T^{4}$ prediction that fits the data, because the radiated power falls with the difference of the fourth powers of the absolute temperatures rather than with their difference.

Markscheme notes

(a) The three (A1)s are for: radiation needs no medium, so an evacuated chamber removes conduction and convection; the surface emits $\varepsilon\sigma A T^{4}$; and the net loss subtracts the absorbed $\varepsilon\sigma A T_{\text{wall}}^{4}$. A candidate who omits the wall term and uses $P=\varepsilon\sigma A T^{4}$ alone has forgotten that the walls also radiate onto the block; this is a common and significant error and it forfeits the third mark.

(b) The (AG) requires the steady-state condition 'electrical power in equals net radiative power out' to be stated. Award the emissivity marks for $\varepsilon$ between $0.570$ and $0.580$. Do not accept a value obtained with the wrong surface area.

(c) The crucial step is recognising that the radiated power at $420\ \mathrm{K}$ is the same $15.0\ \mathrm{W}$ whether the heater is on or off, because it depends only on the block and wall temperatures. A candidate who tries to subtract something from $15.0\ \mathrm{W}$ forfeits the method mark. Accept $c$ in the range $225$–$237\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. Follow-through applies from (b): if the candidate's $\varepsilon$ differs, their $c$ will be consistent with it and should be credited.

(d) Accept $0.047$–$0.051\ \mathrm{K\,s^{-1}}$ for the rate and $0.37$–$0.39$ for the ratio. The final comparison mark requires both numbers to be stated; 'the $T^{4}$ law fits better' without the linear prediction of $0.50$ earns only one mark. Do not penalise a candidate who compares the rates themselves rather than the ratios, provided the comparison is quantitative. A candidate who uses temperatures in degrees Celsius in the fourth-power term loses the two value marks.

Why this question is hard

The question uses a single apparatus to yield two material properties, and the reason it works is worth stating clearly: the radiated power depends only on the temperatures of the block and the walls, so at the moment the heater is switched off — when the block is still at $420\ \mathrm{K}$ — the radiated power is exactly the $15.0\ \mathrm{W}$ the heater was supplying. Nothing about the heating itself has to be modelled; the energy balance does the work.

The first trap is the term that is missing. Students who have met the black-body formula reach for $P=\varepsilon\sigma A T^{4}$ and forget that the walls radiate back. The net loss is $\varepsilon\sigma A\left(T^{4}-T_{\text{wall}}^{4}\right)$. At $420\ \mathrm{K}$ with walls at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ the wall term is 26% of the surface term, so ignoring it changes the emissivity by a quarter — this is not a rounding error.

The second trap is assuming that the cooling rate is proportional to the temperature difference. That is the linear 'Newton's law of cooling' intuition, and it is wrong for radiation. At $360\ \mathrm{K}$ the temperature difference above the walls is half what it was at $420\ \mathrm{K}$, so the linear model predicts the cooling rate to halve. The $T^{4}$ law predicts a ratio of $0.378$, and the difference is easily measurable. The reason is that the radiated power depends on the fourth power of the absolute temperature, so as the block cools the emitted power collapses much faster than the temperature difference does.

The third point is that the two unknowns decouple only because both measurements are made at $420\ \mathrm{K}$. Had the cooling rate been measured at some other temperature, the student would have to carry the $T^{4}$ ratio through the algebra; measuring at the same temperature makes the radiative term cancel, which is exactly the kind of experimental-design decision the question is testing.

Classic wrong turns: using $P=\varepsilon\sigma A T^{4}$ without the wall term; inserting temperatures in degrees Celsius into the fourth-power term; and assuming the cooling curve is exponential when in fact it is not.

Challenge lever
The cooling rate is not set by the temperature difference the student reaches for but by the difference of the fourth powers, and the specific heat capacity is recovered from the instantaneous energy balance at the very temperature at which the emissivity was fixed, so the two unknowns separate only once the student realises the radiated power is the same in both experiments.

Verification
Emissivity from eps = P/(sigma A (Ts^4 - Tw^4)) = 15.0/26.10 = 0.5747; specific heat capacity from c = P/(m dT/dt) = 15.0/0.065 = 230.8 J/kg/K; the 360 K rate recomputed as eps*sigma*A*(360^4-300^4)/(mc) = 5.667/115.4 = 0.0491 K/s, ratio 0.378 against the linear prediction 0.50.
