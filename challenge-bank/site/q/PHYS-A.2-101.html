A.2 Forces and momentum

Physics HL · PHYS-A.2-101

A.2 (Forces and momentum: Newton's laws, momentum and impulse, conservation of momentum)P1 · Section A5 marksdifficulty 5technology: allowedCalculateDetermineState

Question

Five independent multiple-choice questions on forces and momentum. Each one stands alone. Use $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and give each answer to three significant figures.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    A block of mass $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ rests on a rough horizontal surface and is pulled by a force of $15\ \mathrm{N}$ acting at $30^{\circ}$ above the horizontal. The coefficient of dynamic friction is $0.25$. What is the acceleration of the block?

    (Calculate)
  2. [1 mark](b)

    Two trolleys of mass $1.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $2.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ are held together on a frictionless track by a compressed spring. The spring is released and the $1.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ trolley moves off at $3.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. What is the speed of the other trolley?

    (Calculate)
  3. [1 mark](c)

    A force acting on a ball of mass $0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$ rises uniformly from zero to $60\ \mathrm{N}$ in $0.020\ \mathrm{s}$ and then falls uniformly back to zero over the next $0.040\ \mathrm{s}$. What is the change in speed of the ball?

    (Calculate)
  4. [1 mark](d)

    A lift of mass $400\ \mathrm{kg}$ carries a passenger of mass $60\ \mathrm{kg}$ and accelerates upwards at $1.2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. What is the tension in the supporting cable?

    (Calculate)
  5. [1 mark](e)

    A small sphere falling through a viscous liquid reaches a terminal speed $v$. A second sphere of the same material and twice the radius is dropped into the same liquid. The drag force is given by Stokes' law, $F=6\pi\eta r v$. What is the terminal speed of the larger sphere?

    (Determine)

Total: 5 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) A. Resolve the pull: the horizontal component is $15\cos30^{\circ}=12.99\ \mathrm{N}$ and the vertical component is $15\sin30^{\circ}=7.50\ \mathrm{N}$ upwards. The normal reaction is therefore $mg-7.50=19.62-7.50=12.12\ \mathrm{N}$, not $mg$. Friction is $0.25\times12.12=3.03\ \mathrm{N}$, so the net horizontal force is $12.99-3.03=9.96\ \mathrm{N}$ and $a=9.96/2.0=4.98\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. (A1) The whole question turns on the normal reaction; B is what you get by taking it as $mg$.

(b) A. The initial momentum is zero and no external horizontal force acts, so $1.5(3.0)+2.5(-v)=0$ and $v=1.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ in the opposite direction. (A1) D is the interesting wrong answer: conserving kinetic energy gives $\tfrac12(1.5)(9)=\tfrac12(2.5)v^{2}$, so $v=2.32$, but the spring releases stored energy, so kinetic energy is *not* conserved here.

(c) A. The impulse is the area under the force-time graph. The shape is a triangle of base $0.020+0.040=0.060\ \mathrm{s}$ and height $60\ \mathrm{N}$, so the impulse is $\tfrac12(0.060)(60)=1.8\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ and $\Delta v=1.8/0.50=3.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. (A1) B doubles it by using the rectangle, and C forgets to divide by the mass.

(d) A. Treating the lift and passenger as one body of mass $460\ \mathrm{kg}$: $T-460g=460(1.2)$, so $T=460(11.01)=5065\ \mathrm{N}=5.06\ \mathrm{kN}$. (A1) The mark is for including the passenger in the accelerated mass; D, at $4.99\ \mathrm{kN}$, is the result of accelerating only the lift.

(e) A. At terminal speed, net weight equals drag: $(\rho_{s}-\rho_{l})\tfrac43\pi r^{3}g=6\pi\eta r v$, so $v\propto r^{2}$. Doubling $r$ multiplies $v$ by $4$. (A1) The two scalings are the point: the driving force is cubic in $r$ and the resisting force is linear in $r$, so the ratio grows as $r^{2}$.

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking. (a) Accept $4.9$ to $5.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$; a candidate who writes the normal reaction as $12.1\ \mathrm{N}$ has the key idea even if the final figure slips. (b) The direction is not asked for and should not be penalised, but a candidate who says the trolleys move in the same direction has not conserved momentum. (c) Accept $3.6$ or $3.60$; watch for the candidate who computes the area correctly but then multiplies by the mass instead of dividing. (d) Accept $5.05$ to $5.07\ \mathrm{kN}$; the distinction between B, C and D is entirely about which mass is accelerated and in which direction, so an unannotated correct figure earns the mark but a wrong figure earns nothing. (e) This is a scaling question and no numerical value is needed; accept any answer that correctly states that the terminal speed is proportional to the square of the radius. Note that the answer would be different for drag proportional to $v^{2}$, which is the more common regime for large spheres in air.

Why this question is hard

Every item in this cluster hides a quantity that has to be reconstructed before the familiar formula can be applied. In (a) it is the normal reaction, which the angled pull reduces below the weight; in (c) it is the impulse, which is an area and not a force; in (d) it is the accelerated mass, which includes the passenger; in (e) it is the scaling of the driving force against the scaling of the resisting force. A candidate who reaches straight for $F=ma$ or $p=mv$ without building the missing quantity lands on a distractor, and the distractors are placed exactly where those shortcuts lead.

Two of the five contain a second, deeper trap. In (b) the natural instinct is to conserve kinetic energy, because that is what is usually conserved in the problems students meet; here the spring is an internal energy source, so only momentum is conserved, and the $2.32\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ option is there for exactly that error. In (e) the instinct is to say the bigger sphere falls faster by a factor of two or eight, and only a candidate who writes both scalings down finds the factor of four. The cluster therefore discriminates on whether a student checks the applicability of a conservation law and the dimensions of a scaling, rather than on whether they can substitute into a formula.
