B.5 Current and circuits: e.m.f., internal resistance and power

Physics HL · PHYS-B.5-102

Theme B: The particulate nature of matterB.5 (Current and circuits: electromotive force, internal resistance, terminal potential difference, series circuits, power dissipation)P214 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateDetermineStateExplain

Question

The figure shows a cell of electromotive force $E$ and internal resistance $r$ connected in series with two resistors, $R_1$ and $R_2$, and with a voltmeter connected across $R_2$. The cell has $E = 6.0$ V and $r = 0.50$ $\Omega$, and the resistors have $R_1 = 4.0$ $\Omega$ and $R_2 = 6.0$ $\Omega$ as marked on the diagram. The ammeter and the connecting wires have negligible resistance. In parts (a) to (d) the voltmeter may be treated as ideal, and in part (c) it may not. Give all numerical answers to three significant figures.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Calculate the current in the circuit, treating the voltmeter as ideal.

    (Calculate)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Determine the reading on the voltmeter, treating the voltmeter as ideal.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    The voltmeter is now known to have a resistance of 1.0 k$\Omega$ rather than being ideal. State, and explain, how the reading it gives compares with the reading you found in part (b).

    (State)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Calculate the efficiency of the circuit, defined as the fraction of the total power supplied by the cell that is dissipated in $R_1$ and $R_2$.

    (Calculate)
  5. [4 marks](e)

    $R_2$ is now replaced by a variable resistor of resistance $R$. Determine the value of $R$ for which the power dissipated in it is a maximum, and determine that maximum power. Show your reasoning.

    (Determine)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The cell drives current through $R_1$, $R_2$ and its own internal resistance in series, so the total resistance is $4.0 + 6.0 + 0.50 = 10.5$ $\Omega$. (M1) Applying $I = E/R_{total}$ gives $I = 6.0/10.5 = 0.571$ A. (A1)

(b) The voltmeter is connected across $R_2$ only, so it reads the potential difference across that resistor alone. (M1) $V = IR_2 = 0.5714 \times 6.0 = 3.43$ V. (A1)

(c) A real voltmeter of resistance 1.0 k$\Omega$ is in parallel with $R_2$, so the resistance of that branch is slightly less than 6.0 $\Omega$, not more. (M1) The parallel combination is $\frac{(6.0)(1000)}{1006} = 5.964$ $\Omega$, so the total resistance falls to 10.46 $\Omega$, the current rises to 0.5734 A, and the reading becomes $0.5734 \times 5.964 = 3.420$ V. (A1) The reading therefore decreases by about 0.009 V, which is 0.26%, because the voltmeter draws a small current that was not being drawn before. (A1)

(d) The power supplied by the cell is $P_{total} = EI = 6.0 \times 0.5714 = 3.429$ W. (M1) The power dissipated in the two resistors is $I^2(R_1+R_2) = (0.5714)^2(10.0) = 3.265$ W. (M1) The efficiency is therefore $3.265/3.429 = 0.952$, i.e. 95.2%. (A1) Equivalently, the efficiency is $\frac{R_1+R_2}{R_1+R_2+r} = \frac{10.0}{10.5}$, since the common factor $I^2$ cancels.

(e) With $R$ in place of $R_2$ the current is $I = \frac{E}{R + R_1 + r}$, so the power in $R$ is $P = I^2R = \frac{E^2R}{(R + 4.5)^2}$, writing $4.5$ for $R_1 + r$. (M1) Dividing numerator and denominator by $R$, $P = \frac{E^2}{R + 9.0 + 20.25/R}$, and the denominator is least when $R = 20.25/R$, that is $R = 4.5$ $\Omega$. (M1)(A1) The maximum power is $\frac{E^2}{4(R_1+r)} = \frac{36}{18.0} = 2.0$ W. (A1) Equivalently, $I = 6.0/9.0 = 0.667$ A and $P = (0.667)^2(4.5) = 2.0$ W.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for the total resistance including $r$, A1 for 0.571 A. A candidate who omits the internal resistance obtains 0.600 A and scores 0 of 2; this is the single commonest error in the question. (b) M1 for recognising that the voltmeter reads the p.d. across $R_2$ alone and not the terminal p.d., A1 for 3.43 V. Follow through from the candidate's own current. A candidate who gives 5.71 V has calculated the terminal potential difference, which is a different quantity, and scores 0 of 2. (c) M1 for stating that the voltmeter is in parallel with $R_2$ and reduces the resistance of that branch; A1 for a numerical demonstration, for which any of the new current, the new branch resistance or the new reading is acceptable; A1 for the conclusion that the reading decreases, with the direction explicitly stated. Do not award the third mark for an answer that says the reading is unchanged or increases. (d) M1 for the total power supplied, M1 for the useful power, A1 for 95.2%. Accept the ratio $10.0/10.5$ with no intermediate powers. (e) M1 for expressing the power in $R$ as a function of $R$ alone; M1 for identifying the condition for the maximum, whether by calculus, by completing the square, or by quoting the matched-load result; A1 for $R = 4.5$ $\Omega$; A1 for 2.0 W. A candidate who states the standard result that the power in a load is greatest when the load resistance equals the resistance of the rest of the circuit, and applies it correctly with $R_1 + r = 4.5$ $\Omega$, earns full marks.

Why this question is hard

The circuit is deliberately unremarkable: one cell, two resistors, one meter. The difficulty is entirely in keeping three different potential differences apart. The electromotive force of the cell is 6.0 V, the terminal potential difference is the e.m.f. minus the lost volts across the internal resistance, and the voltmeter reads neither of those, because it is connected across $R_2$ alone. With a current of 0.571 A the three are 6.00 V, 5.71 V and 3.43 V respectively, and the distractors in parts (a) and (b) are exactly the values a candidate gets by confusing them.

Part (c) is the part that separates a candidate who has met a real voltmeter from one who has only met an ideal one. Adding a resistance in parallel always lowers the resistance of the branch, so the total resistance falls, the current rises, and the potential difference across the parallel pair falls. The reading therefore decreases slightly. Because the voltmeter resistance of 1.0 k$\Omega$ is nearly two hundred times $R_2$, the change is only 0.26%, which is why an ideal voltmeter is a good approximation here and a poor one if $R_2$ were larger.

Part (d) has a shortcut worth seeing. Efficiency here is the fraction of the power that ends up in the external resistors rather than in the cell, and since every term in both powers carries a factor $I^2$, the current cancels and the efficiency is just the ratio of the external resistance to the total resistance, $10.0/10.5$. That is 95.2%, and it does not depend on the e.m.f. at all.

Part (e) asks for a maximum, not a value, so it cannot be answered by substituting into the equations already set up; the candidate has to decide what is varying. With $R$ as the variable, the power is $E^2R/(R+4.5)^2$, and the maximum falls at $R = 4.5$ $\Omega$, that is at $R_1 + r$. The result generalises: the power transferred to a load is greatest when the load resistance equals the resistance of everything else in the circuit. The maximum is 2.0 W, which is less than the 1.96 W + 1.31 W the two original resistors were dissipating between them, because the load here is a single resistor carrying the whole current.
