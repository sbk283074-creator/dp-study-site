E.5 Two stars of identical apparent brightness whose surface temperatures and radii differ in ways that cancel exactly in the measured quantity

Physics HL · PHYS-E.5-301

Theme E: Nuclear and quantum physicsE.5 Fusion and stars: apparent brightness and luminosity, distance determined from parallax angle, Wien's displacement law and the Stefan-Boltzmann lawP215 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedDetermineCalculateDetermineCalculate

Question

Two stars, X and Y, are observed to have the same apparent brightness $b = 1.00 \times 10^{-9}\ {\rm W\,m^{-2}}$ at the Earth. Measurements of their parallax angles and of the wavelength at which each emits most strongly give the following data.
$\rule{0pt}{1em}$ Star X: parallax angle $p = 20\ {\rm mas}$; peak wavelength $\lambda_{\rm max} = 290\ {\rm nm}$.
$\rule{0pt}{1em}$ Star Y: parallax angle $p = 10\ {\rm mas}$; peak wavelength $\lambda_{\rm max} = 580\ {\rm nm}$.
The distance to a star in parsecs is related to its parallax angle in arcseconds by $d = 1/p$, and $1\ {\rm pc} = 3.09 \times 10^{16}\ {\rm m}$. Wien's displacement law may be written $\lambda_{\rm max}T = 2.90 \times 10^{-3}\ {\rm m\,K}$, the Stefan-Boltzmann constant is $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\ {\rm W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}$, and the luminosity is related to the apparent brightness by $b = L/(4\pi d^{2})$. Each star radiates as a spherical black body.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Determine the distance to each star, in parsecs and in metres, and state which star is the more distant.

    (Determine)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the luminosity of each star, and hence determine the ratio $L_{Y}/L_{X}$. Explain why that ratio takes the value it does without needing the brightness to be substituted.

    (Calculate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine the surface temperature of each star, and hence determine how much greater the emitted power per unit area of X is than that of Y.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Calculate the radius of each star, and hence determine the ratio $R_{Y}/R_{X}$. Show that this ratio is an exact integer and explain where the integer comes from in terms of the two competing effects. State, giving a reason, which star cannot be a main-sequence star. (The radius of the Sun is $6.96 \times 10^{8}\ {\rm m}$.)

    (Calculate)
  5. [2 marks](e)

    Explain why the identical apparent brightnesses give no hint whatever that one star is twenty-four times the radius of the Sun and the other roughly three, and state which additional measurement made the difference visible.

    (Explain)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Converting milliarcseconds to arcseconds, $20\ {\rm mas} = 0.020''$ and $10\ {\rm mas} = 0.010''$, so
$$d_{X} = \frac{1}{0.020} = 50\ {\rm pc}, \qquad d_{Y} = \frac{1}{0.010} = 100\ {\rm pc} \;(M1)(A1).$$
In metres, $d_{X} = 50 \times 3.09 \times 10^{16} = 1.55 \times 10^{18}\ {\rm m}$ and $d_{Y} = 100 \times 3.09 \times 10^{16} = 3.09 \times 10^{18}\ {\rm m}$, so star Y is twice as far away as star X (A1).
(b) Using $L = 4\pi d^{2} b$:
$$L_{X} = 4\pi (1.545 \times 10^{18})^{2}(1.00 \times 10^{-9}) = 3.00 \times 10^{28}\ {\rm W} \;(M1)(A1),$$
$$L_{Y} = 4\pi (3.09 \times 10^{18})^{2}(1.00 \times 10^{-9}) = 1.20 \times 10^{29}\ {\rm W} \;(A1),$$
so $L_{Y}/L_{X} = 4.0$. This ratio is simply $\bigl(d_{Y}/d_{X}\bigr)^{2} = 2^{2} = 4$ (A1): since the two apparent brightnesses are equal, the luminosities must scale as the squares of the distances, and no substitution of $b$ is needed to see it.
(c) From $\lambda_{\rm max} T = 2.90 \times 10^{-3}\ {\rm m\,K}$:
$$T_{X} = \frac{2.90 \times 10^{-3}}{290 \times 10^{-9}} = 1.00 \times 10^{4}\ {\rm K}, \qquad T_{Y} = \frac{2.90 \times 10^{-3}}{580 \times 10^{-9}} = 5.00 \times 10^{3}\ {\rm K} \;(M1)(A1).$$
Emitted power per unit area is $\sigma T^{4}$, so the ratio is
$$\frac{\sigma T_{X}^{4}}{\sigma T_{Y}^{4}} = \left(\frac{1.00 \times 10^{4}}{5.00 \times 10^{3}}\right)^{4} = 2^{4} = 16 \;(A1),$$
and X emits sixteen times as much power per square metre of surface as Y does.
(d) With $L = 4\pi R^{2}\sigma T^{4}$, we have $R = \sqrt{L/(4\pi\sigma T^{4})}$.
$$R_{X} = \sqrt{\frac{3.00 \times 10^{28}}{4\pi(5.67 \times 10^{-8})(1.00 \times 10^{4})^{4}}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.00 \times 10^{28}}{7.13 \times 10^{9}}} = 2.05 \times 10^{9}\ {\rm m} \;(M1)(A1),$$
$$R_{Y} = \sqrt{\frac{1.20 \times 10^{29}}{4\pi(5.67 \times 10^{-8})(5.00 \times 10^{3})^{4}}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.20 \times 10^{29}}{4.45 \times 10^{8}}} = 1.64 \times 10^{10}\ {\rm m} \;(A1),$$
giving $R_{Y}/R_{X} = 8.0$ exactly (A1). The integer arises because the two effects compete multiplicatively: from part (c), Y emits only $1/16$ as much per square metre, so to be $4$ times more luminous overall its area must be $4 \times 16 = 64$ times greater, and hence its radius $\sqrt{64} = 8$ times greater (A1). In solar radii these are $R_{X} = 2.05 \times 10^{9}/6.96 \times 10^{8} = 2.9\,R_{\odot}$ and $R_{Y} = 1.64 \times 10^{10}/6.96 \times 10^{8} = 24\,R_{\odot}$ (A1). Star Y, at twenty-four solar radii yet only half the Sun-like temperature class of X, is enormously too large and too luminous to be a main-sequence star for a temperature of $5000\ {\rm K}$; it is a giant (A1). X, at about three solar radii and $10\,000\ {\rm K}$, is consistent with an A-type main-sequence star.
(e) Apparent brightness depends only on the ratio $L/d^{2}$, and both $L$ and $d$ are free to vary by factors that exactly compensate: Y is sixty-four times larger in area but emits one sixteenth per unit area and sits twice as far away, giving $(64/16)/4 = 1$ (A1). The entire structure is invisible in $b$, and becomes visible only through the parallax measurement, which supplies the absolute scale, together with the spectral peak, which supplies $T$ (A1). Brightness alone would place the two stars in the same class entirely.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for converting mas to arcsec, (A1) for both distances in parsecs, (A1) for both distances in metres and the comparison. Candidates who forget that the formula $d = 1/p$ requires arcseconds and use $d = 1/20$ must not be credited: it gives $0.05\ {\rm pc}$, which contradicts the comparison asked for. Follow-through is available for the metre conversions even if the parsec values are wrong.
(b) (M1) for $L = 4\pi d^{2} b$, (A1) for each luminosity (accept $3.0 \times 10^{28}$ and $1.2 \times 10^{29}\ {\rm W}$ to two significant figures), (A1) for the ratio $4.0$ together with the argument from $\bigl(d_{Y}/d_{X}\bigr)^{2}$. If the ratio is stated without argument, award (A1) only if the two luminosity values are themselves correct. Note that one mark is explicitly available for seeing that equal $b$ makes this a pure distance-squared statement; this is meant to reward candidates who reason before computing.
(c) (M1) for applying Wien's law with each wavelength converted to metres, (A1) for both temperatures, (A1) for the factor $16$ with the fourth power shown. Candidates writing the ratio inverted, giving $1/16$, lose the final mark even if the temperatures are right, since part (d) depends on its direction. Accept the factor stated as 'X emits sixteen times as much per unit area'.
(d) (M1) for the rearranged Stefan-Boltzmann expression, (A1) for each radius (accept $2.1 \times 10^{9}$ and $1.6 \times 10^{10}\ {\rm m}$), (A1) for the ratio $8.0$, (A1) for explaining the integer through the product of the area factor and the per-area factor, (A1) for both radii in solar radii, (A1) for identifying Y as a giant with a reason that mentions size, temperature or luminosity rather than merely asserting it. Seven award fields are listed here so that examiners can spread the credit; cap the part at its stated four marks by awarding the strongest four fields achieved. Do not condone a radius ratio worked out from the luminosity ratio alone, which ignores the temperature difference and gives $2$ rather than $8$.
(e) (A1) for the compensation argument with the three factors identified, (A1) for naming parallax (and spectral peak) as the additional measurements. Accept either order. A candidate who says only 'the distance hid it' earns one mark, not two, because the per-unit-area compensation is a separate effect from the distance.

Why this question is hard

The first thing to notice about this item is the single measurement that is not hidden: apparent brightness. Both stars give the same number, and any candidate who thinks brightness carries information about size has nothing to work with at all. That is the point. Apparent brightness is $L/(4\\pi d^{2})$, and it is invariant under any combined change in luminosity and distance that keeps their ratio fixed -- so two radically different stars can look identical, and the entire hidden structure has to be recovered from measurements that were never designed to reveal it.

The chain itself has three links and each one is a separate inverse-square or fourth-power. Parallax gives distance, and because the brightnesses are equal the luminosity ratio collapses immediately to $(d_Y/d_X)^2 = 4$; that link needs no arithmetic and it rewards a candidate who reasons before substituting. Wien's law then gives the two temperatures, and the fourth power means Y emits only one sixteenth per square metre. Finally Stefan-Boltzmann converts luminosity and temperature into an area, hence a radius.

The integer eight is worth dwelling on. Y is four times more luminous in total while emitting one sixteenth per unit area, so it needs sixty-four times the area, hence eight times the radius. Those three numbers -- $4$, $1/16$ and $64$ -- are the answer written out. A candidate who arrives at $8$ by calculator arithmetic without seeing the factorisation has missed the structure, and a candidate who sees it can predict $8$ before computing either radius and then use that prediction as a check on the whole chain.

Physically this is the difference between a main-sequence star and a giant, and it is a real distinction rather than a textbook label. Star X at about three solar radii and ten thousand kelvin sits comfortably on the main sequence. Star Y at twenty-four solar radii and five thousand kelvin cannot: a star that cool with that much radiating area is a bloated, evolved object whose core no longer burns hydrogen in the way a main-sequence star does. The classification follows from the numbers rather than being imposed on them.

The persistent wrong move is to assume that since the brightnesses are equal, the stars must be similar in scale, perhaps differing only by distance. That reasoning would give $R_Y/R_X = 2$, from the luminosity ratio alone, and it is wrong by a factor of four because it never accounts for temperature at all. Equal brightness constrains one combination of three quantities; it constrains nothing else.

Challenge lever
Apparent brightness is the one number an astronomer actually records, and it depends on luminosity, which depends on radius squared and temperature to the fourth, which in turn depends on distance squared. Two stars can therefore sit sixteeen times apart in emitted power per unit area and sixty-four times apart in surface area and still deliver identical brightness at the telescope, with one sitting twice as far away. The candidate has to run the chain in both directions and notice that the quantity they were given is the one from which all the interesting structure has been cancelled.
