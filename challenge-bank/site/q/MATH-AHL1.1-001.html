A unit slip worth three orders of magnitude

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.1-001

SL 1.1 (operations with numbers in the form a × 10^k) and SL 1.5 (laws of exponents with integer exponents)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedCalculateDetermine

Question

A metallurgy laboratory measures the mass of a single atom of a metal as $3.27 \times 10^{-22}$ g. A prepared sample is found to contain $4.5 \times 10^{19}$ atoms of that metal.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the mass of the sample in grams, giving your answer in the form $a \times 10^{k}$ with $1 \le a < 10$ and $k$ an integer.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the same mass in kilograms, again giving your answer in the form $a \times 10^{k}$ with $1 \le a < 10$ and $k$ an integer.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    A second sample has mass $8.829 \times 10^{-4}$ kg. Determine the ratio of the mass of the second sample to the mass of the first sample, as an exact decimal.

    (Determine)
  4. [5 marks](d)

    A student claims that the second sample contains $2.7 \times 10^{21}$ atoms. Determine by calculation whether this claim is correct, and state the factor by which the answer would be wrong if the mass in kilograms were divided by $3.27 \times 10^{-22}$ g.

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Multiplying the two given values gives $(3.27 \times 10^{-22})(4.5 \times 10^{19})$. (M1) Grouping the coefficients and the powers of ten separately, $3.27 \times 4.5 = 14.715$ and $10^{-22} \times 10^{19} = 10^{-3}$. (M1) The product is therefore $14.715 \times 10^{-3}$ g, which in the required form is $1.4715 \times 10^{-2}$ g. (A1)

(b) Because $1$ kg $= 10^{3}$ g, the mass in kilograms is the answer to part (a) divided by $10^{3}$. (M1) Subtracting the indices gives $1.4715 \times 10^{-2-3} = 1.4715 \times 10^{-5}$ kg. (A1) The coefficient is unchanged because only the power of ten is altered by the conversion, and dividing by a power of ten never changes the leading digits. (A1)

(c) The required ratio is $\frac{8.829 \times 10^{-4}}{1.4715 \times 10^{-5}}$. (M1) Dividing the coefficients gives $8.829 / 1.4715 = 6$, and dividing the powers gives $10^{-4} / 10^{-5} = 10^{1}$. (M1) Multiplying these, $6 \times 10 = 60$, so the ratio of the masses is exactly $60 : 1$. (A1)(AG) The exactness of the result confirms that the two masses were chosen to be in a simple whole-number ratio.

(d) Converting the second mass into grams, $8.829 \times 10^{-4}$ kg $= 0.8829$ g. (M1) Dividing by the atomic mass gives $0.8829 / (3.27 \times 10^{-22}) = 0.27 \times 10^{22} = 2.7 \times 10^{21}$ atoms. (M1)(A1) This equals the student's claim, so the claim is correct. (R1) If instead the mass in kilograms is divided by the atomic mass in grams, the result is $8.829 \times 10^{-4} / (3.27 \times 10^{-22}) = 2.7 \times 10^{18}$. (M1) Comparing $2.7 \times 10^{21}$ with $2.7 \times 10^{18}$ shows that the second value is $10^{3}$, that is a factor of $1000$, too small. (A1)(R1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a) awards the first (M1) for setting up the product of the two numbers in standard form and the second (M1) for separating coefficients from powers; a candidate who reaches $1.4715 \times 10^{-2}$ without that separation still earns both. The (A1) requires the coefficient to satisfy $1 \le a < 10$, so $14.715 \times 10^{-3}$ left unadjusted is not acceptable. In part (b) the (M1) is for recognising the conversion factor $10^{3}$ and the two (A1) marks are for the index and for keeping the coefficient fixed. A candidate who writes $1.4715 \times 10^{-2}$ kg has divided by ten rather than a thousand and loses the second (A1) but keeps the first. In part (c) the (M1) marks are for the two separate divisions and the (A1) for the exact value; the (AG) confirms the given answer. A candidate who rounds $8.829 / 1.4715$ to $6.0$ may still earn credit if $60$ is stated exactly. In part (d) the first (M1)(A1) pair is for the gram conversion and the division, the (R1) for comparing the result with the claim, and the final (M1)(A1)(R1) for the erroneous calculation, its value and the factor of $1000$. Follow-through from an incorrect part (a) is allowed throughout, but the factor comparison must be consistent with the candidate's own values.

Why this question is hard

The stem hides its difficulty in a unit mismatch rather than in any algebraic obstacle. Both numbers are already in standard form, so the multiplication in part (a) is routine once the candidate remembers to handle the coefficients and the powers separately; the trap is the form requirement, because $14.715 \times 10^{-3}$ is not standard form and must be rewritten as $1.4715 \times 10^{-2}$.

Part (b) is deliberately gentle but sets up the whole question. Converting grams to kilograms means dividing by $10^{3}$, which lowers the index by three while leaving the coefficient untouched. Candidates who subtract one power of ten instead of three produce an answer that is a factor of a hundred too large, and this same slip reappears in part (d).

Part (c) is where the numbers were chosen to be exact. The ratio $8.829 \times 10^{-4}$ divided by $1.4715 \times 10^{-5}$ collapses to $6 \times 10^{1}$, and the fact that the result is exactly $60$ signals that the intended method is index arithmetic rather than a decimal calculation on a calculator, which is why the paper forbids technology.

Part (d) turns the unit mismatch into the central decision. The claim is tested correctly by converting the mass to grams first, giving $0.8829$ g and hence $2.7 \times 10^{21}$ atoms, so the claim stands. The second half of the part asks what happens if the same mass in kilograms is divided by an atomic mass in grams: the mixed units give $2.7 \times 10^{18}$, which is exactly three powers of ten, a factor of $1000$, too small. The classic errors are forgetting the gram conversion, subtracting one power of ten rather than three, and reporting the ratio as $6$ or $600$ by misplacing the index. Each arises from the same failure to track the unit attached to every index.
