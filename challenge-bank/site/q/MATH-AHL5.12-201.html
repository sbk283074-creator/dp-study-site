5.12 The integral of x to the power minus p on (0, 1]: convergence only for p < 1, the partial integral (1 - eps^(1-p))/(1 - p), and the failure of the power rule at p = 1 where the antiderivative is not defined at the lower limit

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.12-201

Topic 5: CalculusAHL 5.12 (improper integrals and the convergence of an integral whose integrand is unbounded) with SL 5.10 (the fundamental theorem of calculus and the power rule for integration)P2 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatDetermineDetermineExplain

Question

For a real constant $p$ define
$$I(p) = \int_{0}^{1} x^{-p}\,dx,$$
which for some values of $p$ is an improper integral, because the integrand $x^{-p} \to \infty$ as $x \to 0^{+}$ whenever $p > 0$. The integral is defined as the limit
$$I(p) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^{+}}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1} x^{-p}\,dx$$
whenever that limit exists and is finite, and is said to diverge otherwise. The integrand has no upper bound near the lower limit, so the integral represents an area that is infinitely tall but only finitely wide, and whether that area is finite is the question. Answers should be given exactly, and the limit as $\varepsilon \to 0^{+}$ should be shown at each stage rather than asserted.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that for $p \ne 1$ and $0 < \varepsilon < 1$, $\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{1} x^{-p}\,dx = \frac{1 - \varepsilon^{1-p}}{1 - p}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Hence determine $I\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)$ and $I\!\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)$, showing the limit explicitly in each case.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the set of real values of $p$ for which $I(p)$ converges, and give the value of the integral for those values of $p$. Justify the boundary of the set.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain what is wrong with applying the power rule directly to $p = 2$ and reporting $I(2) = -1$, and describe the behaviour of the partial integral as $\varepsilon \to 0^{+}$ in that case.

    (Explain)

Reveal the answer

(a) For $p \ne 1$ the antiderivative of $x^{-p}$ is $\dfrac{x^{1-p}}{1 - p}$, so
$$\int_{\varepsilon}^{1} x^{-p}\,dx = \left[\frac{x^{1-p}}{1 - p}\right]_{\varepsilon}^{1} = \frac{1}{1 - p} - \frac{\varepsilon^{1-p}}{1 - p} = \frac{1 - \varepsilon^{1-p}}{1 - p} \;(M1)(A1).$$
The calculation is valid for every $p \ne 1$ and every $\varepsilon$ with $0 < \varepsilon < 1$, because the integrand is continuous on the closed interval $[\varepsilon, 1]$ and the fundamental theorem applies there (A1). Nothing has been assumed about the behaviour at $x = 0$; that is deferred to the limit.
(b) For $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ the exponent is $1 - p = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so
$$\int_{\varepsilon}^{1} x^{-1/2}\,dx = \frac{1 - \varepsilon^{1/2}}{1/2} = 2\bigl(1 - \sqrt{\varepsilon}\bigr) \;(M1).$$
As $\varepsilon \to 0^{+}$ the term $\sqrt{\varepsilon} \to 0$, so the limit is $2$ and $I\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = 2$ (A1).
For $p = \tfrac{3}{4}$ the exponent is $1 - p = \tfrac{1}{4}$, so
$$\int_{\varepsilon}^{1} x^{-3/4}\,dx = \frac{1 - \varepsilon^{1/4}}{1/4} = 4\bigl(1 - \varepsilon^{1/4}\bigr) \;(M1),$$
and as $\varepsilon \to 0^{+}$ the term $\varepsilon^{1/4} \to 0$, giving $I\!\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right) = 4$ (A1). In both cases the partial integral rises towards a finite value and stays below it.
(c) From part (a) the limit depends on the behaviour of $\varepsilon^{1-p}$ as $\varepsilon \to 0^{+}$, and there are three cases (M1).
If $p < 1$ then $1 - p > 0$, so $\varepsilon^{1-p} \to 0$ and $\dfrac{1 - \varepsilon^{1-p}}{1 - p} \to \dfrac{1}{1 - p}$. The integral converges and $I(p) = \dfrac{1}{1 - p}$ (A1).
If $p = 1$ the formula does not apply, and the partial integral must be computed separately: $\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{dx}{x} = -\ln\varepsilon$, which tends to $+\infty$ as $\varepsilon \to 0^{+}$ (A1). The integral diverges.
If $p > 1$ then $1 - p < 0$, so $\varepsilon^{1-p} = \varepsilon^{-(p-1)} = 1/\varepsilon^{\,p-1} \to +\infty$ as $\varepsilon \to 0^{+}$. Since $1 - p < 0$ as well, the quotient $\dfrac{1 - \varepsilon^{1-p}}{1 - p}$ tends to $+\infty$ (A1). The integral diverges.
So $I(p)$ converges exactly for $p < 1$, and for those values $I(p) = \dfrac{1}{1 - p}$. The boundary of the set is $p = 1$, where the two behaviours on either side -- a finite limit from below and divergence from above -- are separated.
(d) Applying the power rule to $p = 2$ gives the antiderivative $\dfrac{x^{-1}}{-1} = -\dfrac{1}{x}$, and substituting the limits formally gives $-1 - (-1/0)$, which involves division by zero (M1). The step that is illegitimate is the substitution of $x = 0$: the antiderivative $-\dfrac{1}{x}$ is not defined at the lower limit, so the fundamental theorem of calculus, which requires an antiderivative continuous on the whole interval of integration, does not apply (A1). The correct calculation uses the partial integral
$$\int_{\varepsilon}^{1} x^{-2}\,dx = \frac{1 - \varepsilon^{-1}}{-1} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon} - 1,$$
which grows without bound as $\varepsilon \to 0^{+}$: at $\varepsilon = 0.1$ it is $9$, at $\varepsilon = 0.01$ it is $99$, and at $\varepsilon = 0.001$ it is $999$ (A1). The integral diverges, and the reported value $-1$ is an artefact of evaluating a formula outside the range where it means anything.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for integrating $x^{-p}$ correctly, (A1) for the exact expression, (A1) for noting that the calculation is valid on the closed interval $[\varepsilon, 1]$ because the integrand is continuous there. A candidate who writes $x^{-p+1}/(-p+1)$ with a sign error loses (M1) but may recover the (A1) by follow-through if the expression is consistent.
(b) (M1) for substituting $p = 1/2$ and simplifying, (A1) for the limit $2$, (M1) for substituting $p = 3/4$, (A1) for the limit $4$. The limit must be shown: a candidate who writes $1/(1 - 1/2) = 2$ without the partial integral and the limiting step scores (A1) only, because the part explicitly asks for the limit.
(c) (M1) for separating the three cases according to the sign of $1 - p$, (A1) for $p < 1$ giving $1/(1 - p)$, (A1) for $p = 1$ giving $-\ln\varepsilon$ and divergence, (A1) for $p > 1$ diverging because $\varepsilon^{1-p} \to \infty$. A candidate who answers $p \le 1$ has included the boundary where the logarithm diverges and loses the last mark. A candidate who answers $p < 1$ but justifies it only by saying that $1/(1 - p)$ is finite scores (M1)(A1) and not the two divergence marks, because finiteness of the antiderivative is not the test.
(d) (M1) for identifying the substitution at $x = 0$ as the illegitimate step, (A1) for the reason that the antiderivative is undefined there so the fundamental theorem does not apply, (A1) for the partial integral $1/\varepsilon - 1$ and its growth. A candidate who says only that the answer is negative and an area cannot be negative has identified a symptom rather than the error and scores (M1) only; a candidate who says the integral diverges but does not exhibit the partial integral loses the third mark.
Follow-through: a candidate whose part (a) expression has a sign error but who then takes limits consistently keeps the method marks in (c). Forfeits: writing $\int_{0}^{1} x^{-p}dx = [x^{1-p}/(1-p)]_0^1 = 1/(1-p)$ as if the lower limit could be substituted; claiming convergence for all $p$ because the antiderivative is finite; and confusing the divergence at $p = 1$ with the divergence for $p > 1$, which have different causes.

Why this question is hard

The temptation in this question is to notice that the antiderivative has an elementary form and to treat the improper integral as a substitution problem. It is not one. The whole content of the item is the distinction between an antiderivative that exists on the open interval and an antiderivative that can be evaluated at both endpoints.

For $0 < p < 1$ the substitution works, and the reason it works is not luck. The exponent $1 - p$ is positive, so $\varepsilon^{1-p}$ goes to zero as $\varepsilon$ does: the partial integral approaches its limit from below and the contribution from the neighbourhood of the singularity vanishes. The integrand is unbounded, but it is integrable, and the total area under it is finite. This is the case that makes the infinite spike look harmless.

At $p = 1$ the formula breaks and the break is real. The integral of $1/x$ is a logarithm, and the logarithm diverges at zero. There is no cancellation and no finite limit. This is the boundary: on one side the spike is thin enough to have finite area and on the other it is not, and the dividing case is the one where the antiderivative is logarithmic.

For $p > 1$ the same formula is still algebraically correct as a partial integral, but now the exponent $1 - p$ is negative, so $\varepsilon^{1-p}$ blows up as $\varepsilon$ shrinks. The partial integral is $1/\varepsilon - 1$ for $p = 2$, and it grows without bound: ten, a hundred, a thousand. The formula $1/(1 - p) = -1$ is obtained by setting $\varepsilon = 0$ in an expression where that substitution is exactly what is forbidden. The negative sign is the tell, but it is a symptom; the error is the substitution.

The reason this matters beyond the exercise is that the same confusion appears whenever an integral is evaluated over a region where the integrand is singular. The safe habit is the one the question forces: write the integral from $\varepsilon$, integrate on the closed interval where the theorem applies, and then take the limit and see whether it exists. The formula is the last step, not the first.

Challenge lever
The power rule produces the expression 1/(1 - p) for every value of p except one, and the expression is finite for every p other than one, so a candidate who applies it and substitutes the limits gets a finite number for p = 2, p = 3 and every other exponent above one. The integrand x to the power minus two is unbounded at the lower limit, and the substitution of zero into the antiderivative -1/x is not a legitimate evaluation: the antiderivative is undefined at the endpoint of integration, so the fundamental theorem does not apply. The correct route is the partial integral from eps to one followed by the limit, and it is the limit that decides convergence, not the antiderivative. The value 1/(1 - p) happens to be right for p < 1 and is meaningless for p >= 1, so a single formula covers both regimes and gives a finite answer in a case where the integral diverges. Labelled difficulty 4 rather than 5 on the merits: the trap is located in one step, and the rest of the item is a systematic three-case limit analysis whose marks are earned by carrying out cases that the candidate can see are there.

Verification
The partial integral was computed exactly as (1 - eps^(1-p))/(1 - p) and evaluated at eps = 0.5, 0.1 and 0.01 for p = 0.5 and p = 2; for p = 0.5 it approaches 2 from below and for p = 2 it grows as 1/eps - 1, giving 9, 99 and 999. Naive numerical quadrature was rejected for this item because the singularity at the lower limit makes the quadrature error dominate; the exact partial integral is the reliable route and it is the route the question asks for. The logarithmic case at p = 1 was checked as -ln(eps) for eps = 1e-3, 1e-6 and 1e-9.
