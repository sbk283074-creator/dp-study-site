C.3 One recorded line of a grating spectrum is not a genuine order: the angle is right but the intensity is essentially zero, because the first zero of the single-slit envelope falls exactly on the second-order maximum when d = 2a, and the same ratio predicts the intensity of the third order

Student investigation of a diffraction grating with a sodium lamp

A student is given a transmission diffraction grating labelled by the manufacturer as having 500 lines per millimetre. The slit width is not stated on the label. She mounts the grating on a spectrometer and illuminates it normally with light from a sodium lamp of wavelength 589 nm. She locates each bright line she can see, reading the spectrometer scale to the nearest 0.1 degrees, and estimates the relative brightness of each line by eye on a scale on which the brightest line is 100. The grating is 25 mm wide, so it contains about 12500 slits. The room temperature is 20 degrees Celsius and the wavelength of the sodium line is unaffected by it. The student's readings are given below. She notes that one of the three lines she recorded was very much fainter than the other two, but she assumes at first that all three are orders of the same spectrum.

Question

The student's readings are given in the table. The grating equation for a transmission grating is $n\lambda = d\sin\theta$, where $d$ is the distance between the centres of adjacent slits and $n$ is the order of the maximum. The intensity of each order is modulated by the single-slit envelope of one slit, which has its minima where $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$ for $m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$, with $a$ the width of a single slit. The envelope is not measured directly in this experiment.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the slit separation $d$ of the grating in metres from the manufacturer's label, and the angle at which the second-order maximum would be expected if every order were present.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine whether the line the student recorded at $36.1$ degrees is a genuine second-order maximum of this grating. If it is not, determine the ratio $d/a$ and the slit width $a$, justifying each step.

    (Determine)
  3. [2 marks](c)

    Calculate the relative intensity of a genuine third-order line predicted by the single-slit envelope, taking the first-order line as 100, and compare it with the value the student recorded.

    (Calculate)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate how far the student's readings support the manufacturer's claim of 500 lines per millimetre, referring to the resolution of her scale, to the anomalous intensity, and to any quantity that the experiment does not determine.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 12 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) A grating with 500 lines per millimetre has a slit separation of one millimetre divided by 500 (M1):
$$d = \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-3}}{500} = 2.00 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m} = 2.00\ \mathrm{\mu m} \;(A1).$$
The second order would satisfy $\sin\theta_{2} = 2\lambda/d = \dfrac{2 \times 589 \times 10^{-9}}{2.00 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.589$, giving $\theta_{2} = \arcsin(0.589) = 36.1^{\circ}$ (A1).
(b) The recorded angle of $36.1^{\circ}$ agrees with the expected position of the second order to within the $0.1^{\circ}$ resolution of the scale, so the position alone does not distinguish a genuine order from an artefact (A1). The intensity does: a genuine second order would be comparable with the first, whereas the student records it at 2 against 100 (A1).
The explanation is the single-slit envelope. Its first minimum lies where $a\sin\theta = \lambda$, and the second order lies where $2\lambda = d\sin\theta$. Dividing the second relation by the first eliminates $\sin\theta$ (M1):
$$\frac{d}{a} = 2.$$
So the first envelope minimum falls exactly on the second-order maximum and the second order has zero intensity, which is why the line is missing and only stray light remains. The slit width follows from the same equation, $a\sin\theta_{2} = \lambda$, so
$$a = \frac{\lambda}{\sin\theta_{2}} = \frac{589 \times 10^{-9}}{0.589} = 1.00 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m} = 1.00\ \mathrm{\mu m} \;(A1).$$
This is consistent with $d = 2.00\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, giving $d/a = 2.00$.
(c) The intensity of order $n$ is proportional to the square of the single-slit amplitude at that angle, that is to $[\sin\beta/\beta]^{2}$ with $\beta = \pi a\sin\theta/\lambda$ (M1). At the first order $a\sin\theta_{1}/\lambda = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so $\beta_{1} = \pi/2$ and the factor is $[1/(\pi/2)]^{2} = (2/\pi)^{2}$. At the third order $a\sin\theta_{3}/\lambda = \tfrac{3}{2}$, so $\beta_{3} = 3\pi/2$ and the factor is $[1/(3\pi/2)]^{2} = (2/3\pi)^{2}$. The ratio is
$$\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}} = \left(\frac{\pi/2}{3\pi/2}\right)^{2} = \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{2} = \frac{1}{9} \;(A1),$$
so the third order should be $100/9 = 11$ on the student's scale. She recorded 11, which agrees, and this is the confirmation that the envelope ratio $d/a = 2$ is right rather than a coincidence of the second order alone.
(d) The claim is supported in part and untested in part. The three recorded angles give $d = n\lambda/\sin\theta$ equal to $2.003\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, $1.999\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ and $1.999\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ for the three lines, all consistent with $2.00\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ and hence with 500 lines per millimetre (A1). The agreement is limited by the $0.1^{\circ}$ resolution of the scale: near $62^{\circ}$ an uncertainty of $0.1^{\circ}$ in $\theta$ corresponds to about $0.3\%$ in $\sin\theta$ and hence in $d$, so the experiment confirms the label to a few parts in a thousand rather than exactly (A1).
The anomalous intensity does not weaken the claim; it strengthens it, because a grating with $d/a = 2$ has its second order missing by construction, and the student's record of a faint line at exactly the second-order angle is evidence that the grating is well made rather than badly made (A1). What the experiment does not determine is the slit width $a$ from the label, since the label gives only the line spacing; $a$ had to be inferred from the position of the envelope zero. It also does not determine whether the line spacing is uniform across the 25 mm of the grating, which is the assumption behind treating the envelope ratio as a single number.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for dividing one millimetre by the line density, (A1) for $2.00\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, (A1) for $36.1^{\circ}$. Accept $36.09^{\circ}$ or $36.1^{\circ}$. A candidate who uses $d = 500\ \mathrm{mm}$ or who fails to convert to metres loses (A1) for the separation and may recover the angle mark only if the value is numerically right.
(b) (A1) for noting that the position agrees and therefore does not settle the question, (A1) for identifying the intensity as the anomaly, (M1) for combining $a\sin\theta = \lambda$ with $n\lambda = d\sin\theta$ at $n = 2$, (A1) for $d/a = 2$ and $a = 1.00\ \mathrm{\mu m}$. A candidate who concludes that the line is a genuine second order because the angle is right scores the first (A1) only; the whole point of the part is that the angle is not the test. A candidate who says the order is missing because the grating is faulty scores nothing in this part.
(c) (M1) for the envelope factor and its values at the two orders, (A1) for the ratio $1/9$ and the prediction 11. Condonation: a candidate who obtains the ratio as $1/9$ directly by arguing that the envelope falls as $1/m^{2}$ for odd $m$ scores full marks. A candidate who predicts $1/3$ has used the amplitude rather than the intensity and loses the (A1).
(d) (A1) for the three values of $d$ and the conclusion that they support the label, (A1) for the resolution argument with a numerical consequence, (A1) for the point that the missing order is evidence for a good grating and for naming at least one quantity the experiment does not determine. Forfeits: using the first-order angle to find $a$ without the missing-order argument; treating the residual intensity of 2 as a measurement of the second order; and reporting the slit width as the distance between adjacent slits.

Why this question is hard

This is a data-based item in which the data are internally consistent and the anomaly is in a column the student did not think to question. Every angle is exactly where it should be, and fitting the grating equation to the three angles returns the same slit separation three times. A candidate who checks only the angles will conclude that the grating is as labelled and that the three lines are the first three orders.

The intensity column says otherwise. The intensity of each order is not the same, because the light from one slit is itself spread out into a diffraction pattern with a broad central maximum and zeros at $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$. The whole grating multiplies that envelope by a sharp interference pattern, so an order that falls on an envelope zero has no intensity at all. If the slit width happens to be exactly half the slit spacing, the first envelope zero lands on the second order and the second order disappears. That is what the ratio $d/a = 2$ means, and it is why a grating is often made this way: it removes an order that would otherwise crowd the spectrum.

The prediction for the third order is the check that turns a plausible explanation into a verified one. If the envelope ratio is two, the third order sits at $a\sin\theta/\lambda = 1.5$, where the envelope amplitude is $1/(1.5\pi)$ against $1/(0.5\pi)$ at the first order, so the intensity ratio is $(1/3)^{2} = 1/9$. The student recorded 11 against 100, which is $1/9$ to the precision of an eye estimate. The same number that explains the missing order also predicts the surviving one.

The final part is about the limits of the experiment. Angles give the line spacing, and they give it to a few parts in a thousand, which is as much as a scale reading to $0.1^{\circ}$ can support. They do not give the slit width, which had to be inferred from where the envelope vanishes. And the residual intensity of two, which a careless reading would treat as a measurement of the second order, is stray light: it is the signature of a grating that is almost exactly right, not of a second order that is very faint.

Challenge lever
The angles of all three recorded lines are exactly where the orders should be, so fitting the grating equation to them confirms the stated 500 lines per millimetre and reveals nothing. The anomaly is in the intensity column: the line at 36.1 degrees is two per cent of the first-order line rather than the forty or so per cent a genuine second order would carry. The resolution is that the intensity of each order is set by the single-slit envelope, whose first minimum falls where a sin(theta) = lambda, and if the grating happens to have d = 2a then that minimum sits exactly on the second-order maximum, so the second order is missing rather than faint. The residual two per cent is stray light, not an order. Once the ratio d/a = 2 is known, the same envelope predicts the third order at one ninth of the first, which is what the student recorded.

Verification
The three angles were computed from n lambda = d sin(theta) with d = 2.00 micrometres and lambda = 589 nanometres and round to 17.1, 36.1 and 62.1 degrees, matching the recorded values. The envelope argument was checked numerically: at the second order a sin(theta)/lambda is exactly 1.000, so the envelope factor is zero; at the third order it is 1.500, and the ratio of the envelope factors at the third and first orders is 1/9 exactly, giving 11.1 on a scale where the first order is 100. The fourth order was confirmed to be impossible, since 4 lambda / d exceeds one.
