The particle that stops twice and travels equal legs

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.9-001

SL 5.9 (kinematic problems involving displacement s, velocity v and acceleration a)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatFindDetermine

Question

A particle moves along a line with displacement $s(t)=t^{3}-6t^{2}+9t$ metres at time $t$ seconds, for $t\ge 0$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that the velocity is $v(t)=3(t-1)(t-3)$ and that the acceleration is $a(t)=6t-12$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the times when the particle is at rest.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the displacement of the particle at those times.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the total distance travelled by the particle in the interval $0\le t\le 4$.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Differentiate the displacement with respect to time to get the velocity $v(t)=3t^{2}-12t+9$ (M1), which factors as $3(t^{2}-4t+3)=3(t-1)(t-3)$ (A1)(AG). Differentiate once more to obtain the acceleration $a(t)=6t-12$ (A1). The velocity factorisation is exact, because expanding the bracket restores $3t^{2}-12t+9$, and this factored form makes the rest times immediate to read without solving a general quadratic.

(b) The particle is at rest when $v(t)=0$, so solve $3(t-1)(t-3)=0$ (M1). This gives $t-1=0$ or $t-3=0$, hence the two times $t=1$ and $t=3$ (A1). These are the only moments at which the velocity vanishes, so the direction of travel changes at both of these instants and the journey must be split there.

(c) Substitute each rest time into the displacement. At $t=1$ we have $s(1)=1-6+9=4$ (M1), and at $t=3$ we have $s(3)=27-54+27=0$ (A1). The displacements at the two stopping times are therefore $4$ m and $0$ m respectively, and the particle is momentarily stationary at each of these positions before it sets off again.

(d) Between $t=0$ and $t=4$ the particle moves from $s(0)=0$ out to $s(1)=4$, back to $s(3)=0$, then out again to $s(4)=4$ (M1). The three legs each have length $|4-0|=4$, $|0-4|=4$ and $|4-0|=4$ (A1), so the total distance is $4+4+4=12$ m (A1). Summing the absolute leg lengths is the only correct way to measure the path actually travelled, and the distinction between total distance and net displacement is the central idea tested here, so the total path length is the sum of three separate four-metre legs, which is why it equals $12$ and not the net displacement of $4$.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for the velocity $3t^{2}-12t+9$, (A1)(AG) for the factorised velocity $3(t-1)(t-3)$, and (A1) for the acceleration $a(t)=6t-12$. A candidate who differentiates only once and states the wrong acceleration loses the last mark but keeps the velocity marks if the differentiation is otherwise correct.

(b) Award (M1) for solving $v(t)=0$ and (A1) for both times $t=1$ and $t=3$. Follow through from an incorrect velocity factorisation is allowed if the solving is consistent with it. Both rest times are needed for the distance in part (d).

(c) Award (M1) for substituting into $s(t)$ and (A1) for the pair of displacements $4$ and $0$. The order is important: $s(1)=4$ and $s(3)=0$. A candidate who swaps the two values earns at most one mark.

(d) Award (M1) for identifying the four positional values $0$, $4$, $0$, $4$, (A1) for the three leg lengths each equal to $4$, and (A1) for the total $12$. A candidate who reports the net displacement $4$ instead of the total distance forfeits the final marks. The absolute values are essential to the method.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on a kinematic cubic whose velocity has two distinct roots, so the particle stops and turns around twice within the interval. The displacement is $s(t)=t^{3}-6t^{2}+9t$, whose derivative is $v(t)=3t^{2}-12t+9=3(t-1)(t-3)$, and the acceleration is $a(t)=6t-12$. The rest times are therefore $t=1$ and $t=3$, and the displacements there are $s(1)=4$ and $s(3)=0$. The trap, and the heart of the question, is that total distance is not the net displacement: between $t=0$ and $t=4$ the particle travels out to $4$, returns to $0$, and goes out again to $4$, so each leg is $4$ m and the total is $12$ m, whereas the net displacement from start to finish is only $4$ m. The intended route is to split the journey at the rest times, take the absolute value of each leg, and add them together. Splitting at the rest times is the only reliable way to capture the reversal, because a single displacement difference assumes monotonic motion throughout. The equal leg lengths are a direct consequence of the symmetric roots of the velocity, and they make the total distance an obvious multiple of one leg. The classic error is to compute $s(4)-s(0)=4$ and call that the distance travelled, which ignores the reversal completely. Another slip is to evaluate the acceleration at the rest times, $a(1)=-6$ and $a(3)=6$, and confuse its sign with the direction of motion. The numbers were chosen so the three legs are equal and integer, giving a clean total of $12$, while a candidate who omits the absolute values usually reports $4$ and fails the final part.
