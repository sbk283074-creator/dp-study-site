A circular plan that pays back only at a reuse rate the trial never reached

Business management SL · BM-6.5-001

Unit 6: 6.5 Business plan and 6.8 Circular business models (the SL Toolkit). P1 Section A.P1 · Section A15 marksdifficulty 4ExplainCalculateEvaluate

Dale & Vale Dairy

Dale & Vale Dairy delivers milk and cream to 3,000 households on five rounds. Each household receives five bottles a week, fifty-two weeks a year. The milk is currently sold in single-use plastic bottles that cost EUR 0.12 each; the bottles are not collected.

The founders, Ravi and Meg, want to switch to returnable glass bottles as part of a circular business model and have written a business plan to raise the money. The plan assumes that each glass bottle will be used twenty times before it is broken or lost. Glass bottles cost EUR 0.60 each, and a bottle washer, crates and a chilled store would cost EUR 180,000. Collecting, washing and handling a returned bottle would cost EUR 0.03 each time it is used. The plan presents the switch as a cost saving and shows a payback period the bank will accept.

The bank lends to the dairy only if the investment pays back within four years.

Before committing, Ravi and Meg ran a three-month trial with 200 households. In the trial each glass bottle was used, on average, six times before it was broken or lost. The founders believe the rate would improve once customers were used to the system, because the trial households were told about the scheme only a week before it began. Ravi argues that the plan's figures are the right ones because they describe how the scheme will work once it is established, and that the dairy has a public commitment to remove single-use plastic. Meg is not sure the dairy can afford to be wrong.

Question

The founders of Dale & Vale Dairy must decide whether to commit to the returnable glass scheme in the business plan.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain two ways in which a circular business model such as returnable glass bottles could reduce Dale & Vale's costs, and one way in which it could increase them.

    (Explain)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Using the figures, calculate the cost per delivery and the total annual cost of bottles and washing under plastic, under the plan's assumption of twenty uses, and under the trial's six uses. State the break-even number of uses and the payback period the plan implies.

    (Calculate)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    Evaluate whether the business plan's proposal to switch to returnable glass bottles is sound. Justify your answer with reference to the trial, the bank's lending rule and the founders' objectives.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) A circular business model keeps materials in use rather than disposing of them after one cycle. Two ways it could cut Dale & Vale's costs. First, a glass bottle that is reused replaces many single-use bottles, so the dairy stops buying a new EUR 0.12 plastic bottle for every delivery. Second, it avoids the waste-disposal and packaging charges attached to single-use plastic and reduces exposure to future levies on single-use packaging. (A1)(A1)

One way it increases costs: the model needs reverse logistics. Bottles must be collected from households, returned to the depot, washed and refilled, which adds handling cost and requires the EUR 180,000 washer and store. The dairy also bears the cost of bottles that are broken or never returned. (A1)(A1)

(b) Annual deliveries = 3,000 households x 5 bottles x 52 weeks = 780,000 deliveries. (M1)(A1)

Plastic: cost per delivery EUR 0.12; annual cost = 780,000 x 0.12 = EUR 93,600. (A1)

Plan (20 uses): cost per delivery = 0.60/20 + 0.03 = EUR 0.03 + EUR 0.03 = EUR 0.06; annual cost = 780,000 x 0.06 = EUR 46,800. (M1)(A1)

Trial (6 uses): cost per delivery = 0.60/6 + 0.03 = EUR 0.10 + EUR 0.03 = EUR 0.13; annual cost = 780,000 x 0.13 = EUR 101,400. (A1)

Break-even uses: 0.60/u + 0.03 = 0.12 gives u = 0.60/0.09 = 6.67, so a glass bottle must be used at least seven times to be cheaper than plastic. (A1)

Payback on the plan's figures: annual saving = 93,600 - 46,800 = EUR 46,800; payback = 180,000/46,800 = 3.8 years. (A1)

(c) Indicative evaluation. The plan is internally consistent and looks attractive on its own assumptions. At twenty uses a bottle, glass costs EUR 0.06 a delivery against EUR 0.12 for plastic, a saving of EUR 46,800 a year, and the EUR 180,000 is repaid in 3.8 years, inside the bank's four-year rule. The bank's condition is therefore satisfied on the plan's numbers, and the circular model also delivers the founders' environmental objective.

The trial contradicts the assumption on which the whole plan rests. At six uses, glass costs EUR 0.13 a delivery, more than the plastic it replaces, and the annual cost rises to EUR 101,400, about EUR 7,800 a year worse than doing nothing. The break-even is seven uses, so the trial is not close: it falls below the point at which the investment stops destroying value. On the trial evidence the payback never arrives, and the bank's four-year rule is not met.

The evaluation therefore turns on a judgement about the trial. Ravi's argument has some force, because the trial lasted only three months and customers were told about the scheme a week before it began, so six uses may understate a mature scheme. Meg's argument is that the dairy cannot afford to be wrong, and the shortfall is a recurring EUR 7,800 a year, not a one-off. The strongest answers note that the plan should be rewritten with a range of reuse rates, that the scheme should be re-trialled with proper notice, and that the founders' zero-waste objective could be pursued at a smaller scale before EUR 180,000 is committed. Credit any supported conclusion. (A1)(A1)(A1)(A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for each of the two cost reductions and one for the cost increase, each correctly explained; naming reuse without explaining the saving earns at most one. Credit avoided bottle purchases, disposal charges and future levies. Do not credit the claim that it is good for the environment as a cost reduction.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for the 780,000 deliveries, (A1) for the plastic cost, (M1)(A1) for the plan's cost per delivery and annual cost, (A1) for the trial cost, (A1) for the break-even of seven uses and (A1) for the 3.8-year payback. Follow through on arithmetic slips. A candidate who uses 0.60/20 without adding the EUR 0.03 washing cost cannot reach the evaluation marks, because the omission is the error the question tests.

(c) The discriminating marks are for the trial cost of EUR 0.13 and the recognition that six uses is below the break-even of seven. A candidate who accepts the plan's twenty uses and concludes that the switch is clearly worthwhile cannot earn more than two marks. Credit a conclusion that proceeds with the scheme only if it is justified by the trial's weakness or by a staged rollout, not by the claim that customers will return bottles out of goodwill.

Why this question is hard

The item is built so that the business plan is arithmetically perfect and rests on a single assumption the case has already tested. On twenty uses a bottle, glass costs EUR 0.06 a delivery, saves EUR 46,800 a year and pays back the EUR 180,000 in 3.8 years, just inside the bank's four-year rule. Every figure the plan contains is correct. The mistake is not in the arithmetic but in the reuse rate, which the plan treats as a design choice and the trial reveals as an empirical result.

The lever is that the circular model inverts its own cost logic at a break-even the plan never states. Reuse replaces the repeated purchase of EUR 0.12 plastic bottles, but it adds reverse logistics: collection, washing and handling at EUR 0.03 a delivery, plus the capital cost of the washer. Break-even is 0.60/0.09 = 6.67 uses, so a bottle must survive seven trips before glass is cheaper. The trial managed six. Below that line, the more circular the model, the more it costs per delivery.

The clash of criteria is the second layer. The bank will lend only against a four-year payback, which the plan meets and the trial does not; the founders have a public zero-waste commitment, which the plan advances and the trial postpones. Neither objective can be satisfied at once on the evidence given, so the answer is a decision about which risk to accept, not a calculation. That is why the strongest answers propose rewriting the plan with a range of reuse rates and re-trialling with proper notice, rather than simply rejecting the scheme.

Classic wrong turns: accepting the plan's twenty uses; computing cost per delivery without the EUR 0.03 washing cost; treating the break-even as one use rather than seven; and concluding that the circular model must be cheaper because it is more sustainable.
