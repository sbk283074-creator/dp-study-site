1.3 Two partial sums determine the common ratio to within a sign: knowing S_2 and S_4 fixes r squared and therefore yields two different sequences that satisfy every stated fact

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-1.3-301

Topic 1: Number and algebraSL 1.3 (geometric sequences and series)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatDetermineDetermineShow that

Question

A geometric sequence has first term $u_{1}$ and common ratio $r$, with $r \neq 1$. The sum of the first $n$ terms is denoted by $S_{n}$. It is given that
$$S_{2} = 3 \quad\text{and}\quad S_{4} = 15 .$$
No other information about the sequence is available. Note that neither $u_{1}$ nor $r$ is required to be positive, and that $S_{4}$ may be written as $S_{2}$ together with the next two terms.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $S_{4} = S_{2}\bigl(1 + r^{2}\bigr)$, and hence determine the two possible values of $r$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    For each possible value of $r$, determine the corresponding value of $u_{1}$ and write down the first four terms of the sequence.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine the two possible values of $S_{3}$, and the two possible values of the fifth term $u_{5}$, stating clearly which belongs to which sequence.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    The same investigation is repeated with the sums $S_{3}$ and $S_{6}$ instead. Show that in general $S_{2n} = S_{n}\bigl(1 + r^{n}\bigr)$, and hence explain why knowing $S_{3}$ and $S_{6}$ determines a unique real value of $r$ whereas knowing $S_{2}$ and $S_{4}$ does not.

    (Show that)
  5. [2 marks](e)

    State a single additional piece of information, not involving any further figure, that would remove the ambiguity, and state which value of $r$ it selects.

    (State)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Writing the first four terms as $u_{1},\,u_{1}r,\,u_{1}r^{2},\,u_{1}r^{3}$,
$$S_{4} = u_{1} + u_{1}r + u_{1}r^{2} + u_{1}r^{3} = u_{1}(1 + r) + u_{1}r^{2}(1 + r) = S_{2}\bigl(1 + r^{2}\bigr) \;(M1),$$
since $S_{2} = u_{1}(1 + r)$. Substituting the two given values,
$$15 = 3\bigl(1 + r^{2}\bigr) \;\Longrightarrow\; 1 + r^{2} = 5 \;\Longrightarrow\; r^{2} = 4 \;(A1),$$
so the two possible values are
$$r = 2 \quad\text{or}\quad r = -2 \;(A1).$$
(The factorisation used, $1 + r + r^{2} + r^{3} = (1+r)(1+r^{2})$, is valid for every $r$, including $r = -1$, so no case has been lost; had $r = -1$ then $S_{2}$ would be $0$ rather than $3$.)
(b) From $S_{2} = u_{1}(1 + r) = 3$ we have $u_{1} = \dfrac{3}{1 + r}$ (M1).
*If $r = 2$:* $u_{1} = \tfrac{3}{3} = 1$, giving the sequence
$$1,\; 2,\; 4,\; 8,\; \ldots \;(A1)$$
*If $r = -2$:* $u_{1} = \tfrac{3}{-1} = -3$, giving the sequence
$$-3,\; 6,\; -12,\; 24,\; \ldots \;(A1)$$
Checking: the first gives $1 + 2 = 3$ and $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 = 15$; the second gives $-3 + 6 = 3$ and $-3 + 6 - 12 + 24 = 15$. Both satisfy the stated facts.
(c) Using $S_{3} = u_{1}(1 + r + r^{2})$ and $u_{5} = u_{1}r^{4}$:
*For $r = 2$, $u_{1} = 1$:* $S_{3} = 1(1 + 2 + 4) = 7$ and $u_{5} = 1 \cdot 2^{4} = 16$ (A1).
*For $r = -2$, $u_{1} = -3$:* $S_{3} = -3(1 - 2 + 4) = -3 \times 3 = -9$ and $u_{5} = -3 \cdot (-2)^{4} = -3 \times 16 = -48$ (A1).
So the two possible values are $S_{3} \in \{7,\,-9\}$ with $\{u_{5}\} \in \{16,\,-48\}$, paired respectively: the sequence with positive ratio gives the smaller magnitude in each case here, and the sequence with negative ratio the larger (A1).
(d) Grouping the terms of $S_{2n}$ in two blocks of $n$, and noting that the terms $n+1$ to $2n$ are $r^{n}$ times the first $n$ terms,
$$S_{2n} = S_{n} + r^{n}S_{n} = S_{n}\bigl(1 + r^{n}\bigr) \;(M1).$$
Hence, whenever $S_{n} \neq 0$, the pair $\bigl(S_{n},\,S_{2n}\bigr)$ determines $r^{n}$ and nothing more directly:
$$r^{n} = \frac{S_{2n}}{S_{n}} - 1 \;(A1).$$
Taking real $n$-th roots: if $n$ is odd, the map $r \mapsto r^{n}$ is a one-to-one correspondence on $\mathbb{R}$, so exactly one real value of $r$ arises. With $n = 3$ a knowledge of $S_{3}$ and $S_{6}$ therefore fixes a unique real common ratio (A1). If $n$ is even, $r^{n} = c > 0$ has the two real solutions $r = \pm \sqrt[n]{c}$, and there is no real solution at all if the value determined for $r^{n}$ is negative. With $n = 2$ we obtained $r^{2} = 4$ and hence two candidate ratios, which is why $S_{2}$ and $S_{4}$ left the sequence undetermined up to two possibilities (A1).
(e) Any one of the following removes the ambiguity; the candidate should state one and its consequence. Since both sequences have been exhibited and both satisfy the two sums, the extra fact must be one that distinguishes them, for example:
- *All terms are positive*: this selects $r = 2$, $u_{1} = 1$, because the sequence with $r = -2$ alternates in sign (A1).
- *The sequence is increasing*: this selects $r = 2$, because $-3,\,6,\,-12,\,24$ is not monotone.
- *$|r| < 1$ would allow the infinite sum to converge*: neither candidate satisfies this, so this fact would be inconsistent with the data and would signal that the model is wrong rather than selecting a ratio. Candidates offering this should note the inconsistency (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for factoring $1 + r + r^{2} + r^{3}$ as $(1+r)(1+r^{2})$ or otherwise expressing $S_{4}$ in terms of $S_{2}$, (A1) for deducing $r^{2} = 4$, (A1) for both $r = 2$ and $r = -2$. A candidate who reaches $r = 2$ alone scores at most the first two marks and then loses everything in part (c) that concerns the second sequence. Do not accept $r = \pm 2$ written without derivation if the binomial expansion of $S_{4}$ is absent. Direct computation routes, such as writing the two equations $u_{1}(1+r) = 3$ and $u_{1}(1+r+r^{2}+r^{3}) = 15$ and dividing, earn full marks provided the division is justified by $S_{2} \neq 0$.
(b) (M1) for using $u_{1} = 3/(1+r)$ with each candidate ratio, (A1) for $1,\,2,\,4,\,8$, (A1) for $-3,\,6,\,-12,\,24$. Accept the terms listed without the intervening algebra. Condoned: writing only the first three terms of each sequence. Follow-through from a wrong ratio in part (a) is allowed, but the verification is then essential and a candidate whose four terms do not sum to $15$ should be expected to notice.
(c) (A1) for $S_{3} = 7$ and $u_{5} = 16$ belonging to the positive-ratio sequence, (A1) for $S_{3} = -9$ and $u_{5} = -48$ belonging to the negative-ratio sequence, (A1) for keeping the pairings clear. Unpaired lists score the first two marks only. Forfeits: quoting a single value for either quantity; the question is explicit that two are possible and an answer that reports one has silently discarded half of part (a).
(d) (M1) for the general identity $S_{2n} = S_{n}(1 + r^{n})$, (A1) for isolating $r^{n} = S_{2n}/S_{n} - 1$, (A1) for the correct treatment of odd $n$ and the conclusion that $n = 3$ gives uniqueness, (A1) for the even-$n$ case and the explanation of the two candidates when $n = 2$. The essential content is the one-to-one versus two-to-one behaviour of $r \mapsto r^{n}$ on the reals; a candidate who says merely 'three is odd so there is one answer' without justifying why parity matters scores at most three of the four. Extra credit is not available, but note that a candidate observing that negative $r^{n}$ gives no real ratio for even $n$ has understood the map rather than memorised the parity.
(e) (A1) (A1) for one valid discriminating fact together with the value of $r$ it selects. 'Positivity of the terms' and 'the sequence is increasing' are the two cleanest. 'The common ratio is positive' is tautological and scores nothing: it restates what is to be decided rather than giving evidence for it. 'The infinite sum converges' earns both marks only if the candidate goes on to observe that neither candidate ratio satisfies $|r| < 1$, so the stated fact is inconsistent and the premise must be wrong.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on one observation: the pair of sums $(S_{n}, S_{2n})$ determines $r^{n}$, not $r$. Everything else follows from the behaviour of the map $r \mapsto r^{n}$ on the real numbers, and that behaviour is decided entirely by the parity of $n$. For odd $n$ the map is injective and the ratio is pinned down; for even $n$ it is two-to-one on the positives and unidentified on the negatives, so either there are two candidates or there are none.

Why the wrong answer is so tempting is worth being specific about. The arithmetic delivers $r^{2} = 4$, and the habit built up over years of applying geometry and mechanics problems is that a squared quantity yielding $4$ means $2$, with $-2$ available only in contrived contexts. But there is nothing contrived here. The sequence $-3, 6, -12, 24$ has a sum of three over two terms and fifteen over four, and the check takes one line. Nothing in the statement excludes it, and the item deliberately says so in the stem while not drawing attention to why that sentence is there.

The consequences are not cosmetic. Whereas the positive-ratio sequence gives $S_{3} = 7$ and $u_{5} = 16$, the negative-ratio one gives $S_{3} = -9$ and $u_{5} = -48$. The magnitudes differ by factors of about $1.3$ and $3$, and the signs differ outright. An answer that reports one value has not merely omitted information; it has answered a different question about a different sequence.

Part (e) exists because the natural repair reflex is to say 'assume $r$ is positive'. That is circular -- it assumes what is to be found -- and it is worth rejecting explicitly. A genuine discriminating fact has to be one that could be observed independently, such as all terms having the same sign, or the running totals increasing. The third bullet in the answer deliberately lists a fact that is inconsistent with the data, because noticing that a stated constraint cannot be met is as valuable as using one that can, and it is the only route available when a candidate proposes a constraint neither sequence satisfies.

Finally, the item does not ask for $S_{\infty}$, and the reason is part of its design. With $|r| = 2$ in both candidates there is no infinite sum to take, so the usual closing move is unavailable and the candidate has to sit with two co-existing answers rather than resolving them by a convergence argument.

Challenge lever
The two given sums, $S_{2} = 3$ and $S_{4} = 15$, look like more than enough information. Dividing one into the other produces a clean number and the next step is obviously to take its square root, at which point most candidates keep the positive root and discard the negative one as unphysical without ever testing it. But $r = -2$ produces a perfectly respectable sequence, $-3,\,6,\-12,\,24$, that satisfies both stated sums exactly, and its third partial sum is $-9$ rather than $7$. The information determines $r^{2}$, not $r$, and that distinction is the whole question.

Adaptation
The source asks for the sum and then uses it, treating the recovery of the ratio as routine; multiple solutions are handled, if at all, as a parenthetical note. Here the non-uniqueness is the subject: both sequences are exhibited and verified against the data, every subsequent quantity is demanded twice, part (d) asks for the general identity and the parity argument rather than a case check, and part (e) asks the candidate to propose a genuinely non-circular discriminator. Context, structure and reasoning chain are all changed; the numbers are original and were chosen so that both candidate sequences are exact integers and their third partial sums, seven and minus nine, are as far apart as the construction allows.
Verification
Both candidate sequences were generated from the stated construction and checked against the two given sums: 1+2 = 3 and 1+2+4+8 = 15; -3+6 = 3 and -3+6-12+24 = 15. Third partial sums were computed as u1(1+r+r^2) giving 7 and -9, and fifth terms as u1 r^4 giving 16 and -48. The general identity was checked for n = 1, 2, 3 and 4 with r = 2 and r = -2 by evaluating both sides. The parity claim was verified by observing that x -> x^n is injective on the reals exactly for odd n.
