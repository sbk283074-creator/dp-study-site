5.16 A definite integral that needs a substitution nobody has supplied, built by completing the square rather than recognised from a list, with an integrand whose obvious candidate fails

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.16-202

Topic 5: CalculusAHL 5.16 (integration by substitution) with SL 3.10 (double angle identities) and AHL 5.11 (definite integrals)P2 · Section B13 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatHenceDetermineDetermine

Question

Define
$$I(a) = \int_{0}^{a} \frac{dx}{x^{2} + x + 1} \qquad \text{for } a > 0.$$

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show by completing the square that $x^{2} + x + 1 = \left(x + \dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2} + \dfrac{3}{4}$, and hence that the substitution $u = \dfrac{2x + 1}{\sqrt{3}}$ turns the integrand into $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot \dfrac{1}{1 + u^{2}}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Hence show that the limits $x = 0$ and $x = 1$ become $u = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$ and $u = \sqrt{3}$, and evaluate $I(1)$ as an exact multiple of $\pi$.

    (Hence)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the value of $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x^{2} + x + 1}$, and explain how the answer is related to the limits of the integrand as $u \to \infty$ in your substitution.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine the value of $a$ for which $I(a) = \dfrac{\pi}{6\sqrt{3}}$, giving your answer in the form $\dfrac{\sqrt{3} - 1}{2}$ or otherwise exactly.

    (Determine)

Total: 13 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

[4 marks] (Determine) (d) Determine the value of $a$ for which $I(a) = \dfrac{\pi}{6\sqrt{3}}$, giving your answer in the form $\dfrac{\sqrt{3} - 1}{2}$ or otherwise exactly. [3 marks] (Determine)
Reveal the answer

(a) Completing the square (M1):
$$x^{2} + x + 1 = \left(x + \frac{1}{2}\right)^{2} - \frac{1}{4} + 1 = \left(x + \frac{1}{2}\right)^{2} + \frac{3}{4}.$$
Writing $x + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{2x+1}{2}$, the expression becomes $\dfrac{(2x+1)^{2}}{4} + \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{(2x+1)^{2} + 3}{4}$ (A1).

The substitution $u = \dfrac{2x+1}{\sqrt{3}}$ gives $2x + 1 = \sqrt{3}\,u$, so $2\,dx = \sqrt{3}\,du$ and $dx = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,du$. With $(2x+1)^{2} + 3 = 3u^{2} + 3 = 3(1 + u^{2})$,
$$\frac{dx}{x^{2}+x+1} = \frac{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,du}{\frac{3(1+u^{2})}{4}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \cdot \frac{4}{3} \cdot \frac{du}{1 + u^{2}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot \frac{du}{1 + u^{2}} \;\; (A1).$$

Note why this substitution had to be built rather than recognised: the candidate $u = x^{2} + x + 1$ has $\dfrac{du}{dx} = 2x + 1$, and $2x + 1$ does not occur in the integrand; the numerator is the constant $1$ and can be neither created nor removed. The completing of the square is what produces a numerator that can be matched.

(b) At $x = 0$, $u = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$; at $x = 1$, $u = \dfrac{3}{\sqrt{3}} = \sqrt{3}$ (A1). Hence
$$I(1) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\int_{1/\sqrt{3}}^{\sqrt{3}} \frac{du}{1 + u^{2}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\Big[\arctan u\Big]_{1/\sqrt{3}}^{\sqrt{3}} \;\; (M1).$$
Now $\arctan \sqrt{3} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$ and $\arctan \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$ (A1), so
$$I(1) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{3}} = \frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{9}.$$
Numerically $I(1) \approx 0.6046$, and a numerical integration of the original integrand gives the same value to four decimal places.

(c) In the limit $x \to \infty$ we have $u = \dfrac{2x+1}{\sqrt{3}} \to \infty$, so the upper limit becomes $\infty$ (M1) and
$$\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x^{2}+x+1} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\int_{1/\sqrt{3}}^{\infty} \frac{du}{1+u^{2}}.$$
As $u \to \infty$, $\arctan u \to \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ (M1), because $\tan\theta$ increases without bound as $\theta \to \dfrac{\pi}{2}^{-}$, so
$$\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x^{2}+x+1} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt{3}} \;\; (A1).$$
The relation to the substitution is exact and worth stating: the value at the upper limit is the limit of $\arctan u$, not $\arctan$ of some large number, and the substitution converts the question "what happens to the integral as $x$ grows" into "what is $\arctan u$ as $u$ grows", whose answer is known exactly (A1).

The exact value $\dfrac{2\pi}{3\sqrt{3}} \approx 1.2092$ is exactly twice $I(1)$, which is not a coincidence: the interval $[0, 1]$ in $x$ maps to the symmetric interval $\left[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \sqrt{3}\right]$ in $u$, whose midpoint is $1$, and $\arctan 1 = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$ sits exactly halfway between $\dfrac{\pi}{6}$ and $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

(d) We need
$$\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\left[\arctan\left(\frac{2a+1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) - \frac{\pi}{6}\right] = \frac{\pi}{6\sqrt{3}} \;\; (M1).$$
Cancelling $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$ from both sides, $\arctan\left(\dfrac{2a+1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) - \dfrac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{\pi}{12}$, so
$$\arctan\left(\frac{2a+1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{12} = \frac{\pi}{4} \;\; (A1).$$
Since $\tan\dfrac{\pi}{4} = 1$, we get $\dfrac{2a+1}{\sqrt{3}} = 1$, so $2a + 1 = \sqrt{3}$ and
$$a = \frac{\sqrt{3} - 1}{2} \approx 0.3660 \;\; (A1).$$
Check: $\dfrac{2(0.3660) + 1}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{1.7321}{1.7321} = 1$, so $u = 1$ as required, and the target $\dfrac{\pi}{6\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.3023$ is exactly half of $I(1) \approx 0.6046$.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for completing the square, (A1) for the identity in the required form, (A1) for the transformed integrand $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot \dfrac{1}{1+u^{2}}$. A candidate who reaches the correct integrand by a different route -- for instance by putting $x + \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\tan\theta$ directly -- scores full marks; the substitution in the stem is offered, not imposed.

(b) (A1) for both new limits, (M1) for integrating to $\arctan u$ with the factor $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$ retained, (A1) for $\dfrac{\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}$ or the equivalent $\dfrac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{9}$. A candidate who writes $\arctan \sqrt{3} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$ and $\arctan\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$ has the two values interchanged and forfeits the third mark; the numerical check $0.6046$ settles it.

(c) (M1) for the upper limit becoming $\infty$, (M1) for taking the limit of $\arctan u$, (A1) for $\dfrac{2\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}$, (A1) for the explanation connecting the improper limit to the limit of the inverse tangent rather than to a large finite number. A candidate who writes the improper integral as "$\arctan\infty = \pi/2$" without saying what is being taken to infinity scores the first three marks and not the fourth.

(d) (M1) for setting the general expression at $x = a$ equal to the target, (A1) for reaching $\arctan\left(\dfrac{2a+1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$, (A1) for $a = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}-1}{2}$. Condonation: the exact surd form is required; $a = 0.366$ alone forfeits the final mark, but the numerical value may accompany it. Follow-through: a candidate whose $I(1)$ in (b) is wrong by a factor but who sets up (d) consistently with their own value is given the method mark and the first accuracy mark.

Forfeits: attempting $u = x^{2} + x + 1$ and, on finding $du = (2x+1)dx$, either abandoning the question or inserting a factor $\dfrac{2x+1}{2x+1}$ and integrating as though it were constant. Both leave the integral unevaluated and score nothing beyond the completing of the square.

Why this question is hard

Integration by substitution is taught as a list-matching skill: see $x\sqrt{x^{2}+1}$ and put $u = x^{2}+1$; see $\dfrac{1}{1+x^{2}}$ and think of $\tan\theta$. This integral defeats list-matching because its denominator is not on any list, and the candidate who tries the one substitution the integrand seems to invite discovers that the derivative needed is absent and cannot be manufactured.

The repair is to change the integrand rather than the substitution. Completing the square turns $x^{2}+x+1$ into a square plus a constant, and dividing through by that constant normalises it to $1 + u^{2}$, which is on the list. Nothing about the mathematics is difficult once that step is taken; the difficulty is entirely in recognising that the substitution has to be *built* from the integrand's structure instead of recalled from a table. This is the same move that makes $\dfrac{1}{x^{2}+4x+13}$ tractable, and it is worth doing once carefully because the rescaling -- here the factor $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$ that appears from $dx = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}du$ and the $\dfrac{4}{3}$ from the completed square -- is where most of the lost marks live.

Part (c) asks for the improper integral for a reason that goes beyond arithmetic. The substitution converts it into reading off $\arctan u$ as $u \to \infty$, and the answer $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ is a limit of the inverse tangent, not the value of the inverse tangent at some large number. Candidates write the symbol "$\arctan\infty$" constantly; the question asks them to say what that symbol means, and doing so is the difference between knowing the substitution and knowing why the substitution works.

Part (d) is the reciprocal question and it is the part that rewards understanding the substitution rather than surviving it. Instead of asking for the integral, it asks which upper limit produces a stated value, which can only be answered by inverting the arctangent. The answer $u = 1$ falls out immediately from $\arctan u = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$, and $u = 1$ corresponds to $2a + 1 = \sqrt{3}$. The attractive feature is that $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ is exactly midway between the two limits found in part (b), so the answer $a = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}-1}{2}$ is visible from the geometry of the substitution before any algebra is done.

Challenge lever
The integrand $1/(x^{2} + x + 1)$ offers two substitutions and the salient one is wrong. Every candidate reaches for $u = x^{2} + x + 1$, whose derivative $2x + 1$ does not appear in the integrand and cannot be manufactured because the numerator is constant. The substitution that works has to be built first, by completing the square and then rescaling the completed square to the standard form $1 + u^{2}$, and the whole difficulty is in constructing it rather than in executing it.

Verification
The substitution was carried out symbolically and the transformed integrand differentiated back to 1/(x^2+x+1) numerically at x = 0.1, 0.5 and 0.9. Both definite and improper integrals were then checked against Simpson-rule quadrature of the original integrand with 200000 panels: 0.6045998 for I(1) against pi/(3*sqrt3) = 0.6045998, and 1.2091996 for the improper integral against 2pi/(3*sqrt3) = 1.2091996. The value of a in part (d) was substituted back into the general antiderivative and the result compared with pi/(6*sqrt3) = 0.3022999.
