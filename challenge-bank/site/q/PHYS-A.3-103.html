A.3 Friction on an incline depends on the normal reaction, so the two surfaces are not equivalent

Physics HL · PHYS-A.3-103

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.3 Work, energy and power (conservation of energy; work done by a frictional force)P2 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedShow thatFindExplainDetermine

Question

A block of mass $m = 2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ is released from rest at the top of a slope of length
$s = 4.0\ \mathrm{m}$ inclined at $\theta = 30^\circ$ to the horizontal. It slides down the
slope and then continues on a horizontal surface. The coefficient of kinetic friction is
$\mu = 0.20$ on both surfaces. Take $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

(a) Show that the acceleration of the block down the slope is $3.21\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. \tag{3}

(b) Find the speed of the block when it reaches the bottom of the slope. \tag{4}

(c) Find the distance the block travels along the horizontal surface before coming to rest.
\tag{4}

(d) A student claims that the total work done against friction is $\mu mg(s + d)$, where $d$
is the distance found in (c). Explain why this is wrong, and determine the correct value of
the total work done against friction. \tag{4}

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the acceleration of the block down the slope is $3.21\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the speed of the block when it reaches the bottom of the slope.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the distance the block travels along the horizontal surface before coming to rest.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Explain why the total work done against friction is not $\mu mg(s + d)$, and determine its correct value.

    (Explain)

Reveal the answer

(a) Resolving perpendicular to the slope, the normal reaction is
$N = mg\cos\theta$ (M1). The friction is $f = \mu N = \mu mg\cos\theta$ (A1), so resolving
down the slope,

$$ma = mg\sin\theta - \mu mg\cos\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a = g(\sin\theta - \mu\cos\theta) \tag{A1}$$

$$a = 9.81(\sin 30^\circ - 0.20\cos 30^\circ) = 9.81(0.500 - 0.173) = 9.81(0.327)
= 3.21\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$$

Note that the mass cancels, as it must.

(b) With $u = 0$ and constant acceleration over $s = 4.0\ \mathrm{m}$ (M1),

$$v^{2} = u^{2} + 2as = 0 + 2(3.206)(4.0) = 25.65\ \mathrm{m^{2}\,s^{-2}} \tag{A1}$$

$$v = 5.06\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \tag{A1}$$

Equivalently by energy: the loss of gravitational potential energy is
$mgh = mg s\sin\theta = 2.0 \times 9.81 \times 4.0 \times 0.5 = 39.2\ \mathrm{J}$ and the work
against friction on the slope is $\mu mg\cos\theta \times s = 13.6\ \mathrm{J}$, leaving
$25.6\ \mathrm{J}$ of kinetic energy, which gives the same speed (A1).

(c) On the horizontal surface the normal reaction is $N = mg$ (M1), so the retardation is

$$a' = \frac{\mu mg}{m} = \mu g = 0.20 \times 9.81 = 1.962\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \tag{A1}$$

and, with final speed zero,

$$d = \frac{v^{2}}{2a'} = \frac{25.65}{2 \times 1.962} = \frac{25.65}{3.924}
= 6.54\ \mathrm{m} \tag{A1}$$

Alternatively, the remaining $25.6\ \mathrm{J}$ is dissipated over $d$ at
$\mu mg = 3.92\ \mathrm{N}$, giving the same result (A1).

(d) The expression $\mu mg(s + d)$ assumes the normal reaction is $mg$ over the whole path
(M1). It is not: on the slope the reaction is $mg\cos\theta$ (R1), so the friction on the
slope is smaller than $\mu mg$ by a factor of $\cos 30^\circ = 0.866$. The correct total is
the sum of the work on each surface (M1):

$$W = \mu mg\cos\theta \cdot s + \mu mg \cdot d
= (0.20)(2.0)(9.81)(0.866)(4.0) + (0.20)(2.0)(9.81)(6.54) \tag{A1}$$

$$= 13.6 + 25.6 = 39.2\ \mathrm{J}$$

which equals the loss of gravitational potential energy $mgh = 39.2\ \mathrm{J}$, as it must
(A1). The student's expression gives $3.924 \times (4.0 + 6.54) = 41.3\ \mathrm{J}$, which
exceeds the $39.2\ \mathrm{J}$ of energy available — a result that is impossible, and a useful
check to perform before accepting any answer of this kind. Working backwards from the
student's expression would give a total distance of $10.0\ \mathrm{m}$ and hence
$d = 6.0\ \mathrm{m}$, about $8\%$ short.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for the normal reaction $mg\cos\theta$. (A1) for $f = \mu mg\cos\theta$.
(A1) for the numerical value; accept $3.2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Do not credit a candidate who
writes the friction as $\mu mg$ on the slope: this is the error the item is built around and it
loses both of the first two marks.

(b) (M1) for $v^{2} = 2as$. (A1) for $v^{2} = 25.65\ \mathrm{m^{2}\,s^{-2}}$. (A1) for
$5.06\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. (A1) for the energy cross-check. Follow-through from an incorrect
acceleration in (a) is allowed.

(c) (M1) for $N = mg$ on the horizontal. (A1) for $\mu g$. (A1) for $6.54\ \mathrm{m}$; accept
$[6.5, 6.6]$. (A1) for the energy route.

(d) (M1) for identifying the assumption about the normal reaction. (R1) for stating that the
reaction on the slope is $mg\cos\theta$. (M1) for summing the work over the two surfaces
separately. (A1) for $39.2\ \mathrm{J}$. (A1) for noting the agreement with $mgh$, or for
noting that the student's value exceeds the available energy.

Forfeiture: a candidate who states only "the distances are different" earns nothing — the
normal reaction, not the distance, is the issue. Condonation: allow the use of
$\cos 30^\circ = 0.87$.

Why this question is hard

Friction is $\mu N$, and $N$ is not a property of the surfaces — it is a property of the
geometry. On the flat, $N = mg$. On a slope, $N = mg\cos\theta$. The coefficient is the same in
both places and it is the coefficient that the eye catches, which is exactly why the mistake is
common: everything in the stem encourages the candidate to treat $\mu$ as the whole story.

The consequence here is not small. The friction on the slope is $86.6\%$ of what it would be on
the flat, so using $\mu mg$ everywhere overestimates the energy dissipated on the way down by
about $1.7\ \mathrm{J}$ out of $39.2\ \mathrm{J}$. Over a total path of roughly ten metres that
is worth about half a metre of stopping distance, which is why the naive route gives
$d = 6.0\ \mathrm{m}$ instead of $6.54\ \mathrm{m}$.

What makes the error catchable is that it violates conservation of energy in a detectable way.
The student's total work against friction comes to $41.3\ \mathrm{J}$ while the block only ever
had $39.2\ \mathrm{J}$ of gravitational potential energy to spend. An answer that requires more
energy than the system possesses is not a slight misestimate; it is a signal that one term in
the sum has been computed with the wrong normal reaction. Checking a work calculation against
the energy budget should be a reflex, and part (d) is designed to make it one.

The item also illustrates a useful general habit about inclined planes: resolve perpendicular
first. The normal reaction is what friction, and anything else depending on contact, is
ultimately built from, and on a slope it is never $mg$.

Standard versions of this item supply different coefficients for the two surfaces, or supply the same coefficient and never ask about the total work — so the assumption that friction is $\mu mg$ throughout is either correct or never tested. Here the same coefficient is given for both surfaces and part (d) asks directly about the total work, which forces the assumption into the open.
