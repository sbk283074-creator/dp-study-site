B.5 A potential divider designed from the unloaded ratio: the load appears in parallel with the lower resistor, the output falls by forty per cent, and with the upper resistor fixed the target output is an unattainable limit rather than a mistuning

Physics HL · PHYS-B.5-202

Theme B: The particulate nature of matterB.5 Current and circuits: combinations of resistors in series and parallel, the potential divider, and the effect of connecting a load across the output of a dividerP213 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateCalculateShow thatEvaluate

Question

A student is asked to design a circuit that delivers $4.00\ \mathrm{V}$ to a load of resistance $4.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ from a $12.0\ \mathrm{V}$ supply of negligible internal resistance. She chooses a potential divider with a resistor $R_{1} = 8.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ in series with the supply and a resistor $R_{2} = 4.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ from the output to the negative terminal, and she checks the design with the load disconnected. The supply is then connected, the load is connected across $R_{2}$, and the output is measured. The load is purely resistive and its resistance does not change with temperature.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the potential difference across $R_{2}$ with the load disconnected, and state what fraction of the supply voltage that is.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Calculate the potential difference across the load once it is connected, and the current through $R_{1}$, through $R_{2}$ and through the load, showing that the currents are consistent.

    (Calculate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Show that, with $R_{1}$ held at $8.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$, no finite value of $R_{2}$ gives $4.00\ \mathrm{V}$ across this load, and state the value that the output approaches as $R_{2}$ is increased without limit.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate the design by calculating the fraction of the power drawn from the supply that reaches the load, and recommend one specific change that would allow the circuit to deliver $4.00\ \mathrm{V}$ to this load, supporting your recommendation with a calculation.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 13 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) With no load the two resistors are in series across the supply, so the current is $12.0/(8.0 + 4.0) = 1.00\ \mathrm{mA}$ and the potential difference across $R_{2}$ is (M1)
$$V_{\text{out}} = 12.0 \times \frac{4.0}{8.0 + 4.0} = 12.0 \times \frac{1}{3} = 4.00\ \mathrm{V} \;(A1),$$
which is one third of the supply voltage, in the same ratio as the two resistances (A1).
(b) With the load connected, $R_{2}$ and $R_{L}$ are in parallel (M1):
$$R_{p} = \frac{R_{2}R_{L}}{R_{2} + R_{L}} = \frac{4.0 \times 4.0}{4.0 + 4.0} = 2.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega} \;(A1).$$
The total resistance of the circuit is now $8.0 + 2.0 = 10.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$, so the current through $R_{1}$ is
$$I_{1} = \frac{12.0}{10.0 \times 10^{3}} = 1.20\ \mathrm{mA} \;(A1),$$
and the output falls to
$$V_{\text{out}} = 12.0 \times \frac{2.0}{8.0 + 2.0} = 2.40\ \mathrm{V},$$
a drop of forty per cent from the designed value (A1). The current splits equally between $R_{2}$ and the load, since both are $4.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ at the same potential difference: $I_{2} = I_{L} = 2.40/4.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega} = 0.60\ \mathrm{mA}$, and $0.60 + 0.60 = 1.20\ \mathrm{mA} = I_{1}$, as Kirchhoff's first law requires.
(c) For the output to be $4.00\ \mathrm{V}$ with $R_{1} = 8.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ and a supply of $12.0\ \mathrm{V}$, the divider ratio must be one third, which requires the parallel combination to satisfy (M1)
$$\frac{R_{p}}{8.0 + R_{p}} = \frac{1}{3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 3R_{p} = 8.0 + R_{p} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_{p} = 4.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega} \;(A1).$$
But $R_{p} = \dfrac{R_{2} \times 4.0}{R_{2} + 4.0}$ with $R_{2}$ in kilohms, and setting this equal to $4.0$ gives $4.0R_{2} = 4.0R_{2} + 16$, which is false for every value of $R_{2}$ (A1). The equation has no finite solution because the parallel combination of a $4.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ load with any finite resistance is strictly less than $4.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$: it approaches $4.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ from below as $R_{2} \to \infty$, and reaches it only in that limit. So the output approaches $4.00\ \mathrm{V}$ as $R_{2}$ is made larger and larger, but never attains it; at $R_{2} = 8.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ it is $3.00\ \mathrm{V}$, at $40\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ it is $3.75\ \mathrm{V}$, and at $400\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ it is $3.97\ \mathrm{V}$. The specification is unreachable with this $R_{1}$, not merely mistuned.
(d) The power delivered to the load is $V^{2}/R_{L} = 2.40^{2}/4.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega} = 1.44\ \mathrm{mW}$, and the power drawn from the supply is $\varepsilon I_{1} = 12.0 \times 1.20\ \mathrm{mA} = 14.4\ \mathrm{mW}$ (M1), so the fraction reaching the load is
$$\frac{1.44}{14.4} = 0.10 \;(A1).$$
Nine tenths of the power is dissipated in the two resistors of the divider, which is the penalty for choosing divider resistors comparable with the load. The remedy is to make both divider resistors small compared with the load, so that the load draws only a small fraction of the divider current. Keeping the one-third ratio and choosing $R_{2} = 2.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ gives $R_{p} = 1.33\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$, and the upper resistor must then be $R_{1} = 2R_{p} = 2.67\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ (A1):
$$V_{\text{out}} = 12.0 \times \frac{1.33}{2.67 + 1.33} = 4.00\ \mathrm{V}.$$
The load is then twice $R_{2}$ rather than equal to it, and the loading error is smaller by the same factor. An alternative acceptable answer is to keep the divider and insert a buffer amplifier, or to replace the divider with a voltage regulator; both remove the dependence of the output on the load rather than reducing it.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for the series divider ratio, (A1) for $4.00\ \mathrm{V}$, (A1) for the statement that this is one third of the supply. A candidate who writes $4\ \mathrm{V}$ without units or with only one significant figure loses the second (A1).
(b) (M1) for recognising the parallel combination, (A1) for $2.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$, (A1) for the total current $1.20\ \mathrm{mA}$, (A1) for $2.40\ \mathrm{V}$. The three branch currents are not separately marked but must appear for the fourth mark to be available: a candidate who gives $2.40\ \mathrm{V}$ without any currents scores (M1)(A1) and (A1) for the voltage is not awarded because the part asks for the currents.
(c) (M1) for setting up the condition $R_{p}/(8.0 + R_{p}) = 1/3$, (A1) for $R_{p} = 4.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$, (A1) for showing that $4.0R_{2} = 4.0R_{2} + 16$ is false for all finite $R_{2}$ and that the limit is $4.00\ \mathrm{V}$. A candidate who merely tries two or three values of $R_{2}$ and finds them all too small has given evidence but not a proof, and loses the third mark; the part says \"show that\". A candidate who answers that $R_{2}$ should be made infinite scores the last mark.
(d) (M1) for both powers or for their ratio, (A1) for the fraction $0.10$ or ten per cent, (A1) for a specific change with a supporting calculation. The recommendation must be quantitative: \"use smaller resistors\" without a pair of values, or \"use an amplifier\" without saying what it does, scores at most two of the three marks. Accept any pair $(R_{1}, R_{2})$ for which $R_{1} = 2R_{p}$ and the output is $4.00\ \mathrm{V}$.
Follow-through: a candidate whose parallel combination is wrong but who then divides the supply in the ratio of the resistances consistently keeps the method marks. Forfeits: using $R_{2}$ instead of $R_{p}$ in the divider ratio; treating the load as drawing no current; and concluding that a larger $R_{2}$ will eventually give exactly $4.00\ \mathrm{V}$.

Why this question is hard

The potential divider is the first circuit most students learn to design, and the rule they learn is that the output voltage divides in the ratio of the two resistors. The rule is correct and it is stated in every textbook, and it is also the reason this design fails, because the rule holds only while nothing is connected to the output. As soon as a load draws current, the lower resistor and the load are in parallel, the effective lower resistance falls, and the divider ratio changes. The output does not sag slightly; it falls by forty per cent.

The reason the fall is so large is that the load resistance is equal to the lower resistor. Two equal resistors in parallel give half the resistance, so the lower arm of the divider is halved and the ratio changes from one third to one fifth. If the load were a hundred times the lower resistor, the parallel combination would be within one per cent of the lower resistor and the design would work. The whole question is the ratio of the load to the divider resistors, and the design as drawn ignores it.

Part (c) is the part that turns a numerical error into a structural one. It is tempting to think that a larger lower resistor would fix the sag, and up to a point it does: the output climbs from 2.40 V towards 4.00 V as the lower resistor grows. But it climbs towards a limit it cannot reach, because the parallel combination of the load with any finite resistor is less than the load resistance itself, and the required parallel combination is exactly equal to the load resistance. The equation that expresses the requirement has no solution, and seeing that is different from seeing that a particular value is wrong.

The efficiency calculation is the practical close. Ten per cent of the power reaches the load and ninety per cent is burnt in the divider, which is what happens when the divider's own resistances are of the same order as the load. The fix is not to tune the divider but to change its impedance level, and the calculation in part (d) shows the same one-third ratio realised with resistors that are small compared with the load.

Adaptation
The loaded potential divider is a standard circuit exercise, but the usual version asks for the output voltage once a load is connected and stops. Here the numbers are chosen so that the designed output is exactly one third of the supply, the loaded output falls to exactly 2.40 V, the efficiency is exactly ten per cent, and the specification turns out to be unattainable for any finite lower resistor, so the item asks for the impossibility rather than for a number. The load, the resistors and the supply voltage are chosen here.
Verification
The unloaded output was computed from the series divider ratio and the loaded output from the parallel combination of the lower resistor with the load; the three branch currents were summed and found to equal the supply current. The impossibility argument in part (c) was confirmed by evaluating the output for lower resistances of 4, 6, 8, 16, 40, 400 and 1000000 kilohms, which give 2.400, 2.769, 3.000, 3.429, 3.750, 3.974 and 4.000 volts, and by showing that the required parallel combination satisfies 4.0 R2 = 4.0 R2 + 16, which has no finite solution. The replacement pair was checked to give exactly 4.00 volts.
