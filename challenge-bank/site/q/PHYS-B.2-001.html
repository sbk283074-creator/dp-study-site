The layer that adds a fixed factor to the surface temperature

Physics HL · PHYS-B.2-001

B.2 Greenhouse effect (planetary energy balance, albedo, emissivity, surface temperature, one-layer atmosphere model)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatDetermineEvaluate

Stimulus

A spherical planet of radius $R$ orbits its star. At the planet's distance the solar constant is $S=1.36\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ and the planetary albedo is $\alpha=0.30$. The planet rotates rapidly, so its surface and its atmosphere may each be treated as having a single uniform temperature. Use $\sigma=5.67\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}$.

Question

The planet has no internal heat source, and in the steady state the power it absorbs from its star equals the power it radiates to space.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    By balancing the solar power intercepted by the planet against the power it radiates to space, show that the effective radiating temperature $T_{e}$ satisfies $T_{e}^{4}=\dfrac{S(1-\alpha)}{4\sigma}$, and calculate the value of $T_{e}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    The planet has a single layer of atmosphere that is transparent to sunlight but absorbs all the infrared radiation emitted by the surface, radiating equally upwards and downwards. By considering the energy balance of the layer and of the surface, show that the surface temperature is $T_{s}=2^{1/4}T_{e}$, and calculate the value of $T_{s}$.

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine the surface temperature if the albedo were $0.34$ instead of $0.30$, and determine the ratio $T_{s}/T_{e}$ in each case.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    The one-layer model predicts a surface temperature that is higher than the observed mean surface temperature of the real planet. Evaluate the model, suggesting one reason why it overestimates the warming and one feature of a real atmosphere that a single layer cannot represent.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Sunlight is intercepted over the planet's cross-sectional area $\pi R^{2}$, so the power absorbed is $P_{\text{abs}}=S\pi R^{2}(1-\alpha)$. (M1) The planet radiates from its whole surface, of area $4\pi R^{2}$, at the effective temperature $T_{e}$, so $P_{\text{emit}}=4\pi R^{2}\sigma T_{e}^{4}$. (M1) In equilibrium $P_{\text{abs}}=P_{\text{emit}}$:

$$S\pi R^{2}(1-\alpha)=4\pi R^{2}\sigma T_{e}^{4}\quad\Rightarrow\quad T_{e}^{4}=\frac{S(1-\alpha)}{4\sigma}$$ (M1)(AG)

$$T_{e}^{4}=\frac{1.36\times10^{3}\times 0.70}{4\times 5.67\times10^{-8}}=\frac{952}{2.268\times10^{-7}}=4.20\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{K^{4}}$$

$$T_{e}=255\ \mathrm{K}$$ (A1)

(b) Let $T_{a}$ be the temperature of the layer. The layer absorbs $\sigma T_{s}^{4}$ per unit area from the surface and radiates $\sigma T_{a}^{4}$ upwards and $\sigma T_{a}^{4}$ downwards, so (M1)

$$\sigma T_{s}^{4}=2\sigma T_{a}^{4}\quad\Rightarrow\quad T_{a}^{4}=\frac{T_{s}^{4}}{2}$$ (A1)

At the top of the atmosphere the only power leaving is the upward radiation of the layer, which must equal the absorbed solar power per unit area: (M1)

$$\sigma T_{a}^{4}=\frac{S(1-\alpha)}{4}=\sigma T_{e}^{4}\quad\Rightarrow\quad T_{a}=T_{e}$$

At the surface, the power absorbed is the sunlight $\dfrac{S(1-\alpha)}{4}$ plus the downward radiation from the layer, $\sigma T_{a}^{4}=\sigma T_{e}^{4}$: (M1)

$$\sigma T_{s}^{4}=\sigma T_{e}^{4}+\sigma T_{e}^{4}=2\sigma T_{e}^{4}\quad\Rightarrow\quad T_{s}=2^{1/4}T_{e}$$ (AG)

$$T_{s}=1.189\times 255=303\ \mathrm{K}$$ (A1)

(c) With $\alpha=0.34$,

$$T_{e}^{4}=\frac{1.36\times10^{3}\times 0.66}{4\times 5.67\times10^{-8}}=3.96\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{K^{4}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad T_{e}=251\ \mathrm{K}$$ (M1)

$$T_{s}=2^{1/4}\times 251=298\ \mathrm{K}$$ (A1)

The ratio is $T_{s}/T_{e}=2^{1/4}=1.19$ in both cases. (A1) It is unchanged because the factor $2^{1/4}$ arises from the geometry of the layer, not from the amount of sunlight reaching the planet.

(d) The model gives $303\ \mathrm{K}$ against an observed mean surface temperature of $288\ \mathrm{K}$, so it overestimates the warming above the effective temperature by about $15\ \mathrm{K}$. (A1) A single layer that absorbs all the infrared and is isothermal is an extremely strong absorber: it intercepts every photon leaving the surface and sends half of them back down, which maximises the back-radiation. A real atmosphere absorbs only in certain wavelength bands, so much of the surface radiation escapes directly to space through the atmospheric window, and the effective number of absorbing layers is far smaller than one fully opaque layer. (A1) In addition, a real atmosphere has a temperature that falls with height and is cooled by convection, which carries energy upwards and shortens the path over which the greenhouse effect operates — a feature that a single isothermal layer cannot represent. (A1) The model is nevertheless useful: it reproduces the correct order of magnitude and, more importantly, the correct qualitative result that the surface is warmer than the effective radiating temperature. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The three (M1)/(AG) marks are for the intercepted area $\pi R^{2}$, the emitting area $4\pi R^{2}$ and the equilibrium condition. A candidate who omits the factor of four obtains $T_{e}=360\ \mathrm{K}$; this is the single most common error and it loses the final two marks. The (AG) requires the two areas to be shown explicitly, not merely asserted. Accept $T_{e}=254$–$256\ \mathrm{K}$ and accept $255\ \mathrm{K}$.

(b) The layer's balance must be written as 'absorbs $\sigma T_{s}^{4}$, radiates $2\sigma T_{a}^{4}$'. A candidate who writes the layer balance as $\sigma T_{s}^{4}=\sigma T_{a}^{4}$, radiating upwards only, obtains $T_{s}=T_{e}$ and loses the method marks; the doubling is the entire content of the greenhouse effect. The surface balance must include the absorbed sunlight as well as the back-radiation. Follow-through applies from (a): a candidate whose $T_{e}$ is wrong but whose factor $2^{1/4}$ is correct earns the (AG) and the final (A1) on their own value.

(c) Accept $T_{e}=250$–$252\ \mathrm{K}$ and $T_{s}=297$–$299\ \mathrm{K}$. The key mark is the observation that the ratio is unchanged; a candidate who reports a different ratio has made an arithmetic slip. Do not accept a ratio quoted as $1.19$ with no justification for its constancy.

(d) The evaluation must name a specific deficiency, not merely say 'it is only a model'. Credit any of: partial absorption and the atmospheric window; convection and the lapse rate; clouds; the layer not being isothermal; multiple absorbing layers. Accept the numerical comparison of $303\ \mathrm{K}$ with $288\ \mathrm{K}$. Do not credit 'the model is wrong' without a mechanism.

Why this question is hard

The two-step structure of this question mirrors the physics of the greenhouse effect, and the pay-off is in part (c). The planet intercepts sunlight over a disc of area $\pi R^{2}$ but radiates from a sphere of area $4\pi R^{2}$. That mismatch of areas is the whole reason the effective radiating temperature is not the value you would get from $S(1-\alpha)/\sigma$; it is lower by a factor $4^{1/4}=1.41$. Students who omit the factor of four obtain $360\ \mathrm{K}$ and never notice, because $360\ \mathrm{K}$ is a plausible-looking planetary temperature.

The second step is the greenhouse layer. The layer absorbs all the infrared coming up from the surface and radiates it both ways, so half comes back down. Adding that back-radiation to the sunlight absorbed at the surface raises the surface temperature above the effective temperature, and the factor is exactly $2^{1/4}=1.19$. The derivation requires two separate energy balances — one for the layer, one for the surface — and candidates who try to shortcut by balancing only the top of the atmosphere never see the factor of two appear.

Part (c) is where the lever bites. Changing the albedo from $0.30$ to $0.34$ cools the planet, but it moves $T_{e}$ and $T_{s}$ together: the ratio $T_{s}/T_{e}$ is fixed at $2^{1/4}$ and is completely independent of the albedo and of the solar constant. This is genuinely counter-intuitive, because a student who expects a more reflective planet to have a weaker greenhouse effect will look for the ratio to change. It cannot: in the one-layer model the amplification depends only on the geometry of the layer, not on how much sunlight is available. The only way to change the factor is to change the model — more absorbing layers, partial absorption, or a non-isothermal atmosphere.

Part (d) then asks for judgement rather than calculation. The model predicts $303\ \mathrm{K}$ against an observed $288\ \mathrm{K}$, overestimating the warming by about $15\ \mathrm{K}$. The reasons are physical: real greenhouse gases absorb only in bands, so an atmospheric window lets surface radiation escape; the atmosphere is cooled by convection and has a lapse rate; and clouds and multiple layers complicate the picture. Recognising that a model can be quantitatively wrong and still capture the essential mechanism is the point of the part.
