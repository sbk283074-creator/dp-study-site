The single recording error that moves the mean further than the median

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.1-001

SL 4.1 (concepts of population, sample, random sample, discrete and continuous data)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedExplainCalculateShow thatDetermine

Question

Nine masses, in grams, are recorded as $5$, $6$, $7$, $8$, $9$, $10$, $11$, $12$ and $40$. The values are treated as a sample from a larger population of recorded masses.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain what is meant by a random sample in this context, and give one reason why a random sample is preferred to a convenience sample.

    (Explain)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Calculate the mean and the median of the nine recorded masses, and comment on what the comparison between them suggests about the shape of the distribution.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Show, using the interquartile range, that the value $40$ is an outlier; you should state both quartiles and the boundary you use.

    (Show that)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    The value $40$ is found to be a recording error and is removed; determine the new mean and the new median, and explain why the median moves less than the mean.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) A random sample gives every member of the population an equal chance of selection, independently of the others (M1). The population here is every mass that could have been recorded, and a random sample would select nine items using a random generator (A1). A convenience sample takes the items easiest to reach, so awkward items are excluded and the sample is not representative (R1). That exclusion biases any estimate from the data, whereas random selection keeps the sample representative (A1). Random selection makes the summary trustworthy.

(b) Adding the nine values gives $5+6+7+8+9+10+11+12+40=108$ (M1), so the mean is $\frac{108}{9}=12$ (A1). In order, the fifth value is $9$, so the median is $9$ (A1). The mean lies above the median, suggesting a tail towards the larger values, because one large value lifts the mean without moving the middle (R1). The comparison indicates skew.

(c) With nine ordered values the lower quartile is the mean of the second and third, $\frac{6+7}{2}=6.5$ (M1), and the upper quartile is the mean of the seventh and eighth, $\frac{11+12}{2}=11.5$ (A1). The interquartile range is $11.5-6.5=5$ (A1). The upper boundary is $Q_3+1.5\times IQR=11.5+7.5=19$, and since $40>19$ the value $40$ is an outlier (A1)(AG). The standard $1.5$ IQR rule places $40$ far outside this boundary.

(d) Removing $40$ leaves $n=8$ and a total of $108-40=68$ (M1), so the new mean is $\frac{68}{8}=8.5$ (A1). The new median is the mean of the fourth and fifth values, $\frac{8+9}{2}=8.5$ (A1). The mean falls by $3.5$ while the median falls by only $0.5$, because the mean uses the size of every value and reacts to the outlier, while the median depends only on position (R1). This difference separates the two averages.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for the idea of equal chance of selection. The (A1) requires a concrete mechanism, such as numbering the items and using a random generator, applied to this context. The (R1) is for naming the bias that convenience sampling introduces, and the final (A1) for linking random selection to representativeness. A candidate who only defines a random sample without applying it to masses earns at most two marks.

(b) Award (M1) for the sum $108$, (A1) for the mean $12$, and (A1) for the median $9$. The (R1) requires a statement about skew or tail direction; merely saying the mean is larger earns nothing unless it is linked to the shape.

(c) The (M1) is for either quartile position; the (A1) requires both quartiles correct. The third (A1) is for the interquartile range $5$, and the final (A1)(AG) for the boundary $19$ together with the explicit comparison $40>19$. A candidate who quotes $19$ but never compares it with $40$ forfeits the last mark.

(d) Award (M1) for the reduced total, (A1) for the new mean, and (A1) for the new median. The (R1) is for the positional-versus-magnitude explanation. Follow through from an incorrect outlier decision is allowed, provided the arithmetic is consistent.

Why this question is hard

This item turns on the difference between two measures of centre when one value is wrong. The nine masses are deliberately almost symmetric, running from $5$ to $12$, so that the mean and median would agree at $8.5$ were it not for the final entry. That entry, $40$, is far larger than the rest and lifts the mean to $12$ while leaving the median at $9$. The gap of three grams is produced by one recording.

The intended route is to sum the values, divide by nine, order them and read off the fifth. Part (c) then applies the $1.5$ IQR rule, which is the only reason the value $40$ can be labelled an outlier rather than merely large. Candidates who compute the quartiles by splitting the ordered list into four equal groups often mishandle nine values, since the groups cannot be equal; the convention used here takes the median of each half, giving $6.5$ and $11.5$.

Part (d) turns the comparison into an argument. Removing $40$ gives a mean of $8.5$ and a median of $8.5$, so the two measures now coincide exactly. The reason the mean moved further is that it is computed from every value, so a single extreme observation carries weight, whereas the median is fixed by the position of the middle and only shifts when the count changes. The classic error is to remove the outlier and then recompute the median from the remaining eight values by taking the fourth alone; with an even count the median is the mean of the two central values, here $\frac{8+9}{2}=8.5$. A candidate who treats the outlier as genuine keeps the inflated mean and reaches the opposite conclusion about the shape.
