C.5 An echo from a moving reflector is Doppler shifted twice, not once

Physics HL · PHYS-C.5-102

Theme C: Wave behaviourC.5 Doppler effect (the Doppler effect for sound; moving source and moving observer)P214 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatCalculateExplain

Question

A bat flies towards a wall emitting ultrasound of frequency $f = 40.0\ \mathrm{kHz}$. The wall
moves towards the bat at speed $u = 12.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The speed of sound in air is
$c = 340\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The situation is shown in the diagram.

A student argues that, since the wall is approaching the bat, the echo is Doppler shifted once
and its frequency is $f' = f\dfrac{c + u}{c}$.

(a) Show that the frequency received by the wall is

$$f_{1} = f\frac{c + u}{c},$$

and calculate $f_{1}$. \tag{4}

(b) Hence show that the frequency received back at the bat is

$$f_{2} = f\frac{c + u}{c - u},$$

and calculate $f_{2}$. \tag{5}

(c) Calculate the beat frequency detected by the bat. \tag{2}

(d) Explain why the student's single application of the Doppler formula underestimates the
shift. \tag{3}

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that the frequency received by the wall is $f_{1} = f\dfrac{c + u}{c}$ and calculate it.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Hence show that the frequency received back at the bat is $f_{2} = f\dfrac{c + u}{c - u}$ and calculate it.

    (Show that)
  3. [2 marks](c)

    Calculate the beat frequency detected by the bat.

    (Calculate)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain why a single application of the Doppler formula underestimates the shift.

    (Explain)

Reveal the answer

(a) The source (the bat) is stationary relative to the air, so the wavelength in the air is
$\lambda = \frac{c}{f}$ (M1). The wall moves towards the source, so it meets wavefronts at the
relative speed $c + u$ (M1) and receives

$$f_{1} = \frac{c + u}{\lambda} = \frac{c + u}{c/f} = f\frac{c + u}{c} \tag{A1}$$

$$f_{1} = 40.0 \times \frac{340 + 12.0}{340} = 40.0 \times \frac{352}{340}
= 41.4\ \mathrm{kHz} \tag{A1}$$

(b) The wall now acts as a source of frequency $f_{1}$ — the frequency at which it receives
and re-radiates — and it moves towards the bat, so the wavefronts are compressed (M1). The
wavelength of the reflected sound in the air ahead of the wall is

$$\lambda' = \frac{c - u}{f_{1}} \tag{M1}$$

because the wall advances by $u$ during each period. The bat, stationary in the air, receives

$$f_{2} = \frac{c}{\lambda'} = \frac{c f_{1}}{c - u} = f\frac{c + u}{c - u} \tag{A1}$$

$$f_{2} = 40.0 \times \frac{352}{328} = 40.0 \times 1.0732 = 42.9\ \mathrm{kHz} \tag{A1}$$

Equivalently, $f_{2} = f_{1} \times \frac{340}{328} = 41.41 \times 1.0366 = 42.9\
\mathrm{kHz}$ (A1).

(c) The bat hears both the emitted $40.0\ \mathrm{kHz}$ and the returned $42.9\
\mathrm{kHz}$ simultaneously, so the beat frequency is

$$f_{\text{beat}} = f_{2} - f = 42.93 - 40.0 = 2.93\ \mathrm{kHz} \tag{A1}$$

which is well within a bat's audible range (A1).

(d) The student's $f' = f\frac{c+u}{c}$ is the frequency at the *wall*, not the frequency back
at the bat (A1). The shift happens twice because the wall changes role between the two legs of
the journey: it is a moving observer on the way out and a moving source on the way back (R1).
Each stage multiplies the frequency by a factor greater than one, so the two-stage result
exceeds the one-stage result by the factor $\frac{c}{c-u} = 1.037$, about $3.7\%$ (A1). The
student's value $41.4\ \mathrm{kHz}$ against the true $42.9\ \mathrm{kHz}$ understates the beat
frequency by more than half: $1.41\ \mathrm{kHz}$ instead of $2.93\ \mathrm{kHz}$.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for the wavelength in air, (M1) for the relative speed $c + u$ at which the wall
meets wavefronts, (A1) for the formula, (A1) for $41.4\ \mathrm{kHz}$. Accept $41.41\
\mathrm{kHz}$. A candidate who uses the moving-source formula here has the roles reversed and
loses both (M1)s.

(b) (M1) for recognising the wall as a moving source on the return leg, (M1) for
$\lambda' = \frac{c-u}{f_{1}}$, (A1) for the combined formula, (A1) for $42.9\ \mathrm{kHz}$,
(A1) for the numerical check using $f_{1}$. Accept $42.93\ \mathrm{kHz}$.

Forfeiture: a candidate who writes $f_{2} = f\frac{c+u}{c}$ again — that is, who applies the
same formula twice with the same sign convention — has not noticed the change of role and earns
at most the first (M1).

(c) (A1) for $2.93\ \mathrm{kHz}$. (A1) for noting it is audible to the bat, or for any
correct remark about its size. Follow-through from (b) is allowed.

(d) (A1) for identifying the student's value as the frequency at the wall. (R1) for the change
of role between the two legs. (A1) for the numerical comparison. Do not credit a bare
statement that "it is shifted twice" without identifying what the wall is in each stage.

Why this question is hard

The Doppler effect is usually taught as two separate formulae — one for a moving source
and one for a moving observer — and the usual drill is to decide which one applies and use it.
That drill has no answer here, because the reflector is both, one after the other.

On the outward leg the bat is stationary in the air and the wall moves, so the wall is a moving
observer: it runs into wavefronts at $c + u$ and receives a frequency higher by the factor
$\frac{c+u}{c}$. On the return leg the wall is the source of the reflected sound, and because
it advances during each period the wavefronts it emits are squeezed into a wavelength
$\frac{c-u}{f_{1}}$; the bat, stationary, receives a frequency higher again by the factor
$\frac{c}{c-u}$. The two factors multiply.

The product is not quite the same as applying one of them twice. Because the two formulae have
different denominators — $c$ for the moving observer and $c - u$ for the moving source — the
exact result is $\frac{c+u}{c-u}$, whereas doubling the single-stage shift would give
$1 + \frac{2u}{c}$. At $u = 12\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $c = 340\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ the two
differ by about $3.7\%$, which is small but not negligible, and it is the sort of discrepancy
that shows up when a candidate tries to check an exact expression against a rule of thumb.

The physical reason the shift is applied twice is worth separating from the algebra: an echo is
not a reflection in the mirror sense. The wall does not simply bounce the wave back with the
frequency it arrived with; it re-radiates, and while it re-radiates it is moving. This is why
Doppler ultrasound and radar speed guns both work on the two-stage formula, and why the shift
they measure is roughly double what a single application would predict.

Part (c) is a small but deliberate addition. A bat listening to its own echo is listening to two
frequencies at once, and what it actually detects is the beat between them. Stating the beat
frequency rather than the returned frequency grounds the calculation in what the animal hears.
