4.13 Bayes' theorem with two screening tests that are correlated given the disease status

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.13-102

Topic 4: Statistics and probabilityAHL 4.13 (use of Bayes' theorem for a maximum of three events)P2 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedFindStateCalculateExplain

Question

A rare condition affects $0.8\%$ of a population. Two screening tests, $A$ and $B$, are
available. Test $A$ has sensitivity $0.95$ (that is, $\mathrm{P}(A^{+} \mid D) = 0.95$) and
false positive rate $0.04$ (that is, $\mathrm{P}(A^{+} \mid \overline{D}) = 0.04$), where $D$
denotes having the condition.

Test $B$ probes the same biomarker as test $A$, so the two results are correlated even among
people with the same disease status. Trial data for test $B$ are reported twice: once for the
whole population, and once for the subgroup who tested positive on test $A$.

$$\begin{array}{l|cc}
& \mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid D) & \mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid \overline{D}) \\ \hline
\text{whole population} & 0.86 & 0.18 \\
\text{subgroup with } A^{+} & 0.90 & 0.15
\end{array}$$

A person is selected at random, tests positive on test $A$, and is then given test $B$, on
which they also test positive.

(a) Find the probability that the person has the condition given the positive result on test
$A$ alone. \tag{3}

(b) State, with a reason, which row of the table is the appropriate one to use when combining
the two results. \tag{3}

(c) Calculate the probability that the person has the condition given that both tests are
positive. \tag{5}

(d) Explain why using the other row of the table gives a different answer. \tag{3}

  1. [3 marks](a)

Reveal the answer

(a) By Bayes' theorem,

$$\mathrm{P}(D \mid A^{+}) = \frac{\mathrm{P}(D)\mathrm{P}(A^{+} \mid D)}
{\mathrm{P}(D)\mathrm{P}(A^{+} \mid D) + \mathrm{P}(\overline{D})\mathrm{P}(A^{+} \mid \overline{D})}
\tag{M1}$$

With $\mathrm{P}(D) = 0.008$ and $\mathrm{P}(\overline{D}) = 0.992$,

$$\mathrm{P}(D \mid A^{+}) = \frac{0.008 \times 0.95}{0.008 \times 0.95 + 0.992 \times 0.04}
= \frac{0.0076}{0.0076 + 0.03968} = \frac{0.0076}{0.04728} = 0.1607 \text{ (4 s.f.)} \tag{A1}$$

So a single positive result leaves the probability at about $16.1\%$ (A1) — still far more
likely than not that the person does *not* have the condition, because the condition is rare.

(b) The subgroup row, $\mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid D) = 0.90$ and
$\mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid \overline{D}) = 0.15$, is the appropriate one (A1). The reason is that
the relevant likelihoods are conditional on what is already known, namely that the person
tested positive on $A$ (R1): the quantity needed in part (c) is
$\mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid A^{+} \cap D)$, not $\mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid D)$ (A1). The population
row averages over people who tested negative on $A$, and those people are never given test
$B$.

(c) Applying Bayes' theorem with the two pieces of evidence,

$$\mathrm{P}(D \mid A^{+} \cap B^{+}) =
\frac{\mathrm{P}(D)\mathrm{P}(A^{+} \mid D)\mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid A^{+} \cap D)}
{\mathrm{P}(D)\mathrm{P}(A^{+} \mid D)\mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid A^{+} \cap D)
+ \mathrm{P}(\overline{D})\mathrm{P}(A^{+} \mid \overline{D})\mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid A^{+} \cap \overline{D})}
\tag{M1}$$

Substituting the subgroup row (M1),

$$= \frac{0.008 \times 0.95 \times 0.90}{0.008 \times 0.95 \times 0.90 + 0.992 \times 0.04 \times 0.15}
= \frac{0.00684}{0.00684 + 0.005952} = \frac{0.00684}{0.012792} \tag{A1}$$

$$= 0.535 \text{ (3 s.f.)} \tag{A1}$$

Two positive results therefore raise the probability from about $16\%$ to about $53\%$ — a
substantial improvement, but still short of even odds (A1). Note that the two tests are *not*
treated as unconditionally independent: the subgroup figures already incorporate the
dependence between them.

(d) Using the population row instead gives

$$\frac{0.008 \times 0.95 \times 0.86}{0.008 \times 0.95 \times 0.86 + 0.992 \times 0.04 \times 0.18}
= \frac{0.006536}{0.006536 + 0.0071424} = 0.478 \text{ (3 s.f.)} \tag{A1}$$

which is about $5.7$ percentage points lower than the correct value (A1). The two rows differ
because the tests probe the same biomarker, so their outcomes are correlated even after the
disease status is fixed (R1). The subgroup with $A^{+}$ is not a random sample of the
population: selecting on a positive result from $A$ enriches it for people in whom the
biomarker is easy to detect, and test $B$ performs differently in that group. Using the
population figures tacitly assumes conditional independence of $A$ and $B$ given $D$, and that
assumption is exactly what the stem rules out.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for the correct statement of Bayes' theorem with the total probability expanded in
the denominator. (A1) for the correct substitution of all four numbers. (A1) for $0.1607$;
accept $0.161$ or $16.1\%$. Award follow-through for an arithmetic slip in the denominator.

(b) (A1) for identifying the subgroup row. (R1) for a reason that refers to conditioning on
what is known. (A1) for stating that the required likelihood is
$\mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid A^{+} \cap D)$ rather than $\mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid D)$. A candidate who
picks the correct row but gives no reason earns (A1) only. A candidate who says "because the
person tested positive on $A$" without distinguishing the two conditional probabilities earns
(A1) and (R1) but not the final (A1).

(c) (M1) for the structure of Bayes' theorem with two pieces of evidence. (M1) for using the
subgroup figures identified in (b); this mark is forfeited if the population figures appear
here even if the arithmetic is then correct. (A1) for $0.00684$ and $0.005952$, (A1) for
$0.535$. (A1) for the interpretation. Follow-through from (b) is available: a candidate who
chose the population row in (b) and uses it consistently may earn (M1), both arithmetic (A1)s
and the interpretation mark, losing only the second (M1).

(d) (A1) for $0.478$. (A1) for the size of the discrepancy. (R1) for identifying the
correlation between the tests — or equivalently for explaining that the subgroup is a selected
sample — as the cause. A candidate who merely says "the numbers are different" earns neither
of the last two marks. Condonation: allow "the two rows are different because the tests are
correlated" without a full account of the selection mechanism.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on a distinction that is easy to state and easy to lose track of: the
accuracy of a test measured on a population, and the accuracy of the same test measured on the
subgroup who actually get it.

A candidate who has met two-stage screening before will reach for the pattern
$\mathrm{P}(D)\mathrm{P}(A^{+} \mid D)\mathrm{P}(B^{+} \mid D)$ in the numerator, because that
is what conditional independence looks like. Here that pattern is wrong twice over. It is
wrong as stated, because the likelihood needed is conditional on $A^{+}$ as well as on $D$; and
the number it tempts the candidate to substitute, $0.86$, is a population figure that has
already averaged over the people who never took test $B$.

The prevalence is doing quiet work throughout. Because only $0.8\%$ of the population has the
condition, the $4\%$ false positive rate on test $A$ generates $0.03968$ in the denominator
against $0.0076$ in the numerator — more than five times as much. A single positive test
therefore moves the probability only from $0.8\%$ to $16.1\%$, and even two positive tests
leave it at $53\%$. Candidates routinely find this counter-intuitive, and it is the reason the
item quotes a rare condition rather than a common one: the arithmetic has to fight the
prevalence, and the fight is the point.

Part (d) is where the two rows are reconciled. The reason they differ is that the tests are
correlated conditional on $D$, which is a physical claim about what they measure, not a
statistical technicality. Two tests for the same biomarker will tend to agree with each other
for reasons that have nothing to do with the disease, and that residual agreement means the
second test carries less fresh information than its headline accuracy suggests. Selecting on
$A^{+}$ makes the correlation visible as a difference between the two rows.

The $5.7$ percentage point gap between $0.478$ and $0.535$ is not a rounding artefact, and it
is large enough to matter in the setting the question describes: it is the difference between
a clinician who treats and one who does not.
