D.1 Gravitational fields

Physics HL · PHYS-D.1-001

D.1 Gravitational fields (AHL): orbital speed, total energy of an orbiting satellite; A.1 kinematicsP215 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatCalculateDetermineState

Question

A satellite of mass $m$ is to be moved from a circular low Earth orbit of radius $r_{1}=6.87\times10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$ to a circular geostationary orbit of radius $r_{2}=4.23\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}$.

The manoeuvre is carried out in two burns. The first burn puts the satellite onto an elliptical transfer orbit whose perigee is $r_{1}$ and whose apogee is $r_{2}$. The second burn, at apogee, circularises the orbit.

The total energy of a satellite of mass $m$ in an elliptical orbit of semi-major axis $a$ is $E=-\dfrac{GMm}{2a}$, where $M$ is the mass of Earth.

Data: $G=6.67\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{2}\,kg^{-2}}$, $M=5.97\times10^{24}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the speed of a satellite in a circular orbit of radius $r$ is $v=\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$, and calculate the orbital speeds in the low orbit and in the geostationary orbit.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that the semi-major axis of the transfer orbit is $a=2.46\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}$ and hence, using the expression for $E$ given above, show that the speed of the satellite at a distance $r$ from the centre of Earth on the transfer orbit satisfies $v^{2}=GM\left(\dfrac{2}{r}-\dfrac{1}{a}\right)$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Calculate the speed of the satellite at perigee and at apogee of the transfer orbit. Hence determine the change in speed required at each burn and the total change in speed.

    (Calculate)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the time of flight from perigee to apogee along the transfer orbit. Hence calculate the angle through which a satellite already in the geostationary orbit moves during that time, and state what this implies for the moment at which the first burn must be made.

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) For a circular orbit the gravitational force provides the centripetal force:

$$\frac{GMm}{r^{2}}=\frac{mv^{2}}{r}\ \Rightarrow\ v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}.$$ (M1)

$GM=6.67\times10^{-11}\times5.97\times10^{24}=3.98\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^{3}\,s^{-2}}$.

Low orbit: $v_{1}=\sqrt{\dfrac{3.98\times10^{14}}{6.87\times10^{6}}}=\sqrt{5.80\times10^{7}}=7.61\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (A1)

Geostationary: $v_{2}=\sqrt{\dfrac{3.98\times10^{14}}{4.23\times10^{7}}}=\sqrt{9.41\times10^{6}}=3.07\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (A1)

(b) $a=\dfrac{r_{1}+r_{2}}{2}=\dfrac{6.87\times10^{6}+4.23\times10^{7}}{2}=2.46\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}$ (A1)

On the ellipse, $E=\tfrac12mv^{2}-\dfrac{GMm}{r}$ (M1) (kinetic + gravitational potential energy)

Equating to the given total energy:

$$\tfrac12mv^{2}-\frac{GMm}{r}=-\frac{GMm}{2a}\ \Rightarrow\ \tfrac12v^{2}=\frac{GM}{r}-\frac{GM}{2a}$$ (M1)

$$\Rightarrow\ v^{2}=GM\left(\frac{2}{r}-\frac{1}{a}\right).\ \ \textbf{(A1)}$$ *(AG)*

(c) At perigee, $r=r_{1}=6.87\times10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$:

$$v_{\text{per}}^{2}=3.98\times10^{14}\left(\frac{2}{6.87\times10^{6}}-\frac{1}{2.4585\times10^{7}}\right)=3.98\times10^{14}\left(2.911\times10^{-7}-4.068\times10^{-8}\right)=9.97\times10^{7}$$

$v_{\text{per}}=9.99\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (A1)

$$\Delta v_{1}=9.99-7.61=2.37\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\ \ (2.37\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}})$$ (A1)

At apogee, $r=r_{2}=4.23\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}$:

$$v_{\text{apo}}^{2}=3.98\times10^{14}\left(\frac{2}{4.23\times10^{7}}-\frac{1}{2.4585\times10^{7}}\right)=3.98\times10^{14}\left(4.728\times10^{-8}-4.068\times10^{-8}\right)=2.63\times10^{6}$$

$v_{\text{apo}}=1.62\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (A1)

$$\Delta v_{2}=3.07-1.62=1.45\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad \Delta v_{\text{total}}=2.37+1.45=3.82\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$$ (A1)

(d) Period of the transfer ellipse: $T=2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{a^{3}}{GM}}$

$$T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(2.4585\times10^{7})^{3}}{3.98\times10^{14}}}=2\pi\sqrt{3.73\times10^{7}}=3.84\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{s}$$ (M1)

Time of flight $=\tfrac12T=1.92\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{s}=5.33\,\mathrm{h}$ (A1)

Period of the geostationary orbit: $T_{\text{geo}}=2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{(4.23\times10^{7})^{3}}{3.98\times10^{14}}}=8.66\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{s}\;(=24.1\,\mathrm{h})$

$$\theta=360^{\circ}\times\frac{1.92\times10^{4}}{8.66\times10^{4}}=79.8^{\circ}\approx80^{\circ}$$ (A1)

The spacecraft travels through $180^{\circ}$ around Earth, so the target must be $180^{\circ}-80^{\circ}\approx100^{\circ}$ ahead of the launch position at the instant of the first burn, so that both arrive at the same point at the same time. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The 'show that' requires $\frac{GMm}{r^{2}}=\frac{mv^{2}}{r}$ stated explicitly; the cancellation of $m$ should be seen. Allow 7.6 km s⁻¹ and 3.1 km s⁻¹ to 2 s.f.

(b) Award (M1) for writing $E=\tfrac12mv^{2}-\frac{GMm}{r}$ — a candidate who writes only $\tfrac12mv^{2}$ cannot proceed. The final line is AG, so the algebraic route must be visible; do not award (A1) for quoting the result.

(c) Accept values in the range 9.9–10.0 km s⁻¹ and 1.60–1.64 km s⁻¹ given rounding of $GM$. Follow through on a candidate's $a$. The second $\Delta v$ must be computed as $v_{2}-v_{\text{apo}}$ and not as $v_{\text{apo}}-v_{2}$; a negative answer here is a physics error, not an arithmetic slip, and forfeits the mark.

(d) Allow $T_{\text{geo}}=24\,\mathrm{h}$ if stated from the definition of a geostationary orbit; then $\theta=360^{\circ}\times5.33/24=80^{\circ}$. Accept 99°–101° for the lead angle. The final (A1) is for the lead angle *and* the statement that the two must arrive together; a bare '100°' with no explanation earns no mark.

Why this question is hard

Two ideas have to be held at once, and both are counter-intuitive.

First, the circular-orbit formula $v=\sqrt{GM/r}$ does not give the speed on the transfer orbit. On an ellipse the total energy is fixed by the semi-major axis alone, so the speed follows from $\tfrac12v^{2}-\frac{GM}{r}=-\frac{GM}{2a}$: the satellite's speed at any point is set by where it is *and* by the size of the whole orbit. Using $\sqrt{GM/r_{1}}$ at perigee is the single most common error.

Second, the 'paradox'. The satellite finishes in an orbit whose speed (3.07 km s⁻¹) is far lower than where it started (7.61 km s⁻¹), so it is natural to expect the second burn to slow it down. It does not: arriving at apogee it is crawling at only 1.62 km s⁻¹, well below the 3.07 km s⁻¹ needed to stay there, so the second burn is also a speed-up. Both burns are prograde, and the total $\Delta v$ of 3.82 km s⁻¹ is what the mission actually costs in fuel.

Part (d) supplies the operational point: a transfer is not available on demand. The launch window recurs each time the geometry repeats, which is why interplanetary missions have narrow windows rather than daily departures.

Classic wrong turns: using $r_{1}$ or $r_{2}$ in place of $a$ in the energy expression; treating the flight time as half the period of either circular orbit; and computing the lead angle as 80° instead of 100°.
