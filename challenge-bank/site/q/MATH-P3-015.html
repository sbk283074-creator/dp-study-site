P3 extended problem: expected waiting times for patterns in a biased coin sequence

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-015

Topic 4: Statistics and probabilitySL 4.7 (concept of discrete random variables and their probability distributions; expected value), AHL 1.8 (sum of infinite convergent geometric sequences), SL 4.5 (concepts of trial, outcome, sample space and event)P314 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedShow thatHenceCalculate

Question

A biased coin is tossed repeatedly and independently. Each toss lands heads with probability $p$ and tails with probability $1 - p$, where $0 < p < 1$. For a given sequence of outcomes, the waiting time is the number of tosses needed to see that sequence for the first time. Let $E_{HH}$ and $E_{HT}$ denote the expected waiting times for the sequences $HH$ and $HT$ respectively.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    By considering the two states 'no progress towards $HT$' and 'the last toss was $H$', show that
    $$E_{HT} = \frac{1}{p\,(1-p)}.$$

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    By a similar argument with the two states 'no progress towards $HH$' and 'the last toss was $H$', show that
    $$E_{HH} = \frac{1+p}{p^{2}}.$$

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Hence show that $E_{HH} < E_{HT}$ if and only if $p > \dfrac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2}$.

    (Hence)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Taking $p = \dfrac{2}{3}$, calculate $E_{HH}$ and $E_{HT}$. By comparing the values of $E_{HH}$ and $E_{HT}$ at $p = \dfrac{1}{2}$, comment on the claim that the rarer of two equally long patterns always takes longer to appear.

    (Calculate)

A biased coin is tossed repeatedly and independently. Each toss lands heads with probability $p$ and tails with probability $1 - p$, where $0 < p < 1$. For a given sequence of outcomes, the waiting time is the number of tosses needed to see that sequence for the first time. Let $E_{HH}$ and $E_{HT}$ denote the expected waiting times for the sequences $HH$ and $HT$ respectively. (a) By considering the two states 'no progress towards $HT$' and 'the last toss was $H$', show that $$E_{HT} = \frac{1}{p\,(1-p)}.$$ [4 marks] (Show that) (b) By a similar argument with the two states 'no progress towards $HH$' and 'the last toss was $H$', show that $$E_{HH} = \frac{1+p}{p^{2}}.$$ [4 marks] (Show that) (c) Hence show that $E_{HH} < E_{HT}$ if and only if $p > \dfrac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2}$. [3 marks] (Hence) (d) Taking $p = \dfrac{2}{3}$, calculate $E_{HH}$ and $E_{HT}$. By comparing the values of $E_{HH}$ and $E_{HT}$ at $p = \dfrac{1}{2}$, comment on the claim that the rarer of two equally long patterns always takes longer to appear. [3 marks] (Calculate)
Reveal the answer

(a) Let $A$ be the expected number of further tosses needed from the state in which no progress towards $HT$ has been made, and let $B$ be the expected number of further tosses needed from the state in which the last toss was $H$ (M1).

From state $B$: one toss is made; a tail completes the pattern with probability $1-p$, and a head returns to state $B$ with probability $p$. Hence $B = 1 + p B + (1-p) \cdot 0$ (M1), so $(1-p)B = 1$ and $B = \dfrac{1}{1-p}$ (A1).

From state $A$: one toss is made; a head moves to state $B$ with probability $p$, and a tail leaves us in state $A$ with probability $1-p$. Hence $A = 1 + pB + (1-p)A$ (M1), so $pA = 1 + pB$ and $A = \dfrac{1}{p} + B = \dfrac{1}{p} + \dfrac{1}{1-p} = \dfrac{(1-p) + p}{p(1-p)} = \dfrac{1}{p(1-p)}$ (A1) (AG). Since we begin with no tosses made, $E_{HT} = A = \dfrac{1}{p(1-p)}$.

(b) Let $C$ be the expected number of further tosses from the state in which no progress towards $HH$ has been made, and let $D$ be the expected number of further tosses from the state in which the last toss was $H$ (M1).

From state $D$: one toss is made; a head completes the pattern with probability $p$, and a tail sends us back to state $C$ with probability $1-p$, because a tail destroys all progress. Hence $D = 1 + p \cdot 0 + (1-p)C$ (M1).

From state $C$: one toss is made; a head moves to state $D$ with probability $p$, and a tail leaves us in state $C$ with probability $1-p$. Hence $C = 1 + pD + (1-p)C$ (M1), so $pC = 1 + pD$ and $C = \dfrac{1}{p} + D$.

Substituting $D = 1 + (1-p)C$ into $C = \dfrac{1}{p} + D$ gives $C = \dfrac{1}{p} + 1 + (1-p)C$, so $pC = \dfrac{1}{p} + 1 = \dfrac{1+p}{p}$ and $C = \dfrac{1+p}{p^{2}}$ (A1) (AG). Since we begin with no tosses made, $E_{HH} = C = \dfrac{1+p}{p^{2}}$.

(c) The two expectations are equal when
$$\frac{1+p}{p^{2}} = \frac{1}{p(1-p)} \iff (1+p)(1-p) = p \iff 1 - p^{2} = p \iff p^{2} + p - 1 = 0$$ (M1).
The roots are $p = \dfrac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1+4}}{2} = \dfrac{-1 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}$, and since $0 < p < 1$ only $p = \dfrac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2} \approx 0.6180$ is admissible (A1). To decide the direction, test $p = \dfrac{2}{3} > \dfrac{\sqrt5-1}{2}$: here $E_{HH} = 3.75$ and $E_{HT} = 4.5$, so $E_{HH} < E_{HT}$ (M1). Since the difference $E_{HH} - E_{HT}$ changes sign only at the single value found above, $E_{HH} < E_{HT}$ precisely for $p > \dfrac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}$ (A1) (AG).

(d) At $p = \dfrac{2}{3}$ we have $1 - p = \dfrac{1}{3}$, so
$$E_{HH} = \frac{1 + \frac{2}{3}}{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{2}} = \frac{\frac{5}{3}}{\frac{4}{9}} = \frac{5}{3} \times \frac{9}{4} = \frac{15}{4} = 3.75,$$ (M1)
$$E_{HT} = \frac{1}{\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{1}{3}} = \frac{1}{\frac{2}{9}} = \frac{9}{2} = 4.5$$ (A1).

At $p = \dfrac{1}{2}$ both patterns have probability $\dfrac{1}{4}$, yet $E_{HH} = \dfrac{1.5}{0.25} = 6$ while $E_{HT} = \dfrac{1}{0.25} = 4$ (A1). The claim is therefore false: two patterns of the same length and the same probability can have expected waiting times differing by 50%. What governs the waiting time is not how likely the pattern is but how much of a partial match survives a failure -- a tail destroys all progress towards $HH$, whereas for $HT$ a head simply leaves the state unchanged -- and this overlap structure is not visible in the pattern probabilities at all (AG).

Markscheme notes

(a) The states must be defined before they are used; a solution that writes down the two recurrences without saying what $A$ and $B$ mean forfeits the first (M1). The recurrence for the completed pattern must set the term for success to $0$ and not to $B$ or to $1$; a candidate who writes $B = 1 + pB + (1-p)B$ has not understood the state space and should not be awarded (M1). Award full marks for a correct first-step analysis, which is the equivalent and equally acceptable route.

(b) The decisive difference from part (a) is that a tail from state $D$ returns to state $C$, not to state $D$; candidates who copy the structure of part (a) and write $D = 1 + pD + (1-p)C$ will obtain a wrong answer and should not be given follow-through credit for (b) itself, although they may keep (M1) for a correctly formed equation for $C$. Accept the answer in either the form $(1+p)/p^{2}$ or $\dfrac{1}{p} + \dfrac{1}{p^{2}}$.

(c) The quadratic must be obtained by equating the two expressions, not by guessing the golden ratio. Award (M1) for the equality and its reduction, (A1) for rejecting the negative root, and (M1)(A1) for establishing the direction of the inequality -- a candidate who asserts the direction without a test value forfeits the final (A1). Condone the exact answer left as $\dfrac{\sqrt5-1}{2}$; the decimal $0.618$ is not required.

(d) Award (M1) for substituting $p = \tfrac23$ into either expression, (A1) for both values, and (A1) for the comparison at $p = \tfrac12$ together with a comment that engages with the overlap structure. The comment must do more than restate the numbers: an answer that says only 'the claim is false because 6 is not 4' earns the (A1) for the arithmetic but not the (AG). Any correct statement of the mechanism -- that a failure resets $HH$ completely while a failure from state $B$ leaves $HT$ still requiring only one specific toss -- is acceptable.

Why this question is hard

The reason this problem is a trap rather than an exercise is that the intuition it defeats is a good intuition in almost every other setting. Usually, rarer events do take longer to occur, and here the two patterns have the same length, so it feels as though nothing else could distinguish them. The calculation shows that at $p = 1/2$ the two patterns are exactly equally likely -- probability $1/4$ each -- and yet one takes 6 tosses on average and the other takes 4. So the probability of the pattern is not the variable that governs the waiting time, and the student has to find the variable that does.

That variable is the pattern's self-overlap. When you are waiting for $HH$ and you have just seen one $H$, a tail destroys your progress entirely: you are back to having nothing, and you must see two heads in a row from scratch. When you are waiting for $HT$ and you have just seen one $H$, a head does not destroy your progress at all -- it leaves you in exactly the state you were already in, still needing one tail. That asymmetry is worth 50% of the expected time, and it is completely invisible in the two probabilities. The recurrences make the asymmetry explicit: in part (a) the failure branch returns to $B$, in part (b) it returns to $C$. Candidates who do not notice this will write the same recurrence twice and get the same answer twice.

Once both expressions are in hand, part (c) is a clean piece of algebra that produces the golden ratio. It is worth pausing on why the answer is a golden ratio and not, say, $1/2$: the condition $(1+p)(1-p) = p$ rearranges to $p^{2} + p - 1 = 0$, which is the defining equation of the golden ratio, and it appears here because the same quadratic governs any comparison between a self-overlapping pattern and a non-overlapping one. The comparison is genuinely two-sided: for a coin strongly biased towards heads ($p > 0.618$) the run $HH$ is the faster target, and for a fairer coin ($p < 0.618$) the mixed pattern $HT$ is faster, even though $HH$ is the more probable pattern for every $p > 1/2$.

The final comment is the part that cannot be answered by calculation alone. It is not enough to observe that 6 and 4 are different; the answer has to say what the difference is made of. The pattern probability is a red herring, and recognising which variable in a problem is a red herring -- rather than assuming that the most obvious quantity must be the governing one -- is the transferable skill.

Challenge lever
Both patterns have length two, so the intuition that governs the whole problem -- that a rarer pattern takes longer to appear -- is available and plausible. It is also not what determines the answer. At p = 1/2 the two patterns have exactly the same probability, 1/4, and yet the expected waiting times are 6 and 4: a 50% difference between two patterns that are equally likely. The variable the student reaches for, the probability of the pattern, does not set the waiting time; what sets it is the overlap structure of the pattern with itself, and that is invisible in the probabilities. The item forces the student to derive both expectations from first principles rather than reason from rarity, and then to discover that the ordering of the two is itself a function of p, reversing at the golden ratio. The parts are deliberately scaffolded -- (a) and (b) name the states, so the derivation is forced rather than left to the student's own judgement -- and that scaffolding contains the misconception: a candidate holding it still has to do the arithmetic that refutes it. The trap is real but survivable, which is why this item is graded 4 rather than 5, and the containment is the thing the label records.

The source genre supplies the state-recursion method and the habit of comparing the result across a parameter. Three of the permitted changes were made. First, the source setting -- typically a gambler's ruin or a Markov chain on a small state space -- was replaced by the waiting times of the patterns HH and HT in a biased coin sequence, chosen because the two patterns are equally likely at p = 1/2 yet have expected waiting times in the ratio 3:2, which makes the usual rarity heuristic visibly fail. Second, the pattern-probability red herring was made the explicit subject of part (d), so that the misconception is tested rather than merely avoided. Third, the parameter comparison was sharpened from a numerical comparison to the exact threshold, which turns out to be the golden ratio. Verification
Both expectations were derived twice: once from the closed forms (1+p)/p^2 and 1/(p(1-p)), and independently by solving the state recurrences symbolically for p = 2/3, giving 1/p^2 + 1/p = 3.75 and 1/p + 1/(1-p) = 4.5, which agree. The identity 1/p + 1/(1-p) = 1/(p(1-p)) was checked at p = 0.5, 2/3 and 0.8. The crossover was found by solving (1+p)(1-p) = p, i.e. p^2 + p - 1 = 0, giving p = (sqrt(5)-1)/2 = 0.618033989, at which the difference of the two expectations is -8.9e-16, i.e. zero to machine precision. The ordering was sampled on both sides: at p = 0.5 and p = 0.6 the pair HT is faster, at p = 2/3, 0.7 and 0.8 the pair HH is faster, consistent with the threshold.
