The rearranged line that is not the regression of x on y

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.10-001

SL 4.10 (equation of the regression line of x on y; use of the regression line for prediction)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: requiredShow thatFindExplain

Question

Twelve pairs of data $(x,y)$ satisfy $\sum x=84$, $\sum y=60$, $\sum x^{2}=638$, $\sum y^{2}=380$ and $\sum xy=460$. A calculator is required throughout.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that $S_{xx}=50$ and $S_{xy}=40$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the equation of the regression line of $y$ on $x$, giving it in the form $y=a+bx$.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the equation of the regression line of $x$ on $y$, giving it in the form $x=c+dy$.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    A student rearranges the line from part (b) to make $x$ the subject and claims that this is the regression line of $x$ on $y$. Explain why the student is wrong.

    (Explain)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The mean of $x$ is $\bar{x}=\frac{84}{12}=7$ (M1) and the mean of $y$ is $\bar{y}=\frac{60}{12}=5$ (A1). Then $S_{xx}=638-\frac{84^{2}}{12}=638-588=50$ (M1)(A1) and $S_{xy}=460-\frac{84\times 60}{12}=460-420=40$ (A1)(AG). These centred sums subtract the variation already accounted for by the means, so they measure spread about the mean rather than about the origin, which is why the printed values are correct.

(b) The gradient of the regression line of $y$ on $x$ is $b=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}=\frac{40}{50}=0.8$ (M1)(A1), and the intercept is $a=\bar{y}-b\bar{x}=5-0.8\times 7=-0.6$ (M1)(A1). Hence $y=-0.6+0.8x$ (A1). The line passes through the point of means $(7,5)$, which is what determines the intercept once the gradient is known.

(c) The gradient of the regression line of $x$ on $y$ uses $S_{yy}$, and $S_{yy}=380-\frac{60^{2}}{12}=80$ (M1)(A1). Therefore the gradient is $d=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{yy}}=\frac{40}{80}=0.5$ (A1), and the intercept is $c=\bar{x}-d\bar{y}=7-0.5\times 5=4.5$ (M1), so $x=4.5+0.5y$ (A1). Exchanging the roles of the two variables replaces $S_{xx}$ by $S_{yy}$ in the denominator, which is why this gradient differs from the one in part (b).

(d) Rearranging the line from part (b) gives $x=\frac{y+0.6}{0.8}=1.25y+0.75$ (M1)(A1). This is not the regression line of $x$ on $y$, because that line minimises the squared deviations in $x$ and has gradient $\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{yy}}=0.5$ (M1). The two lines coincide only when the correlation is exactly $\pm 1$, whereas here $r=\frac{40}{\sqrt{50\times 80}}=0.632$ (A1)(R1). Rearranging changes the subject of the formula but not which variable is treated as the response, so the student has reversed the equation without reversing the regression.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for either mean and the (A1) for both. Award (M1) for the centred-sum formula and (A1) for $S_{xx}=50$, then (A1) for $S_{xy}=40$ and the (AG) for the printed results. A candidate who uses $638/12$ without subtracting the mean term earns the method marks only.

(b) The (M1)(A1) pair is for the gradient $\frac{40}{50}=0.8$ and the (M1)(A1) pair for the intercept $5-0.8\times 7$. The final (A1) is for the assembled equation. Accept the line written as $y=0.8x-0.6$.

(c) The (M1)(A1) is for computing $S_{yy}=80$, and the (A1) for the gradient $0.5$. The (M1) is for the intercept using the point of means and the final (A1) for the equation $x=4.5+0.5y$. A candidate who uses $S_{xx}$ as the denominator here earns no credit for the gradient, because that is precisely the error the part is testing.

(d) Award (M1)(A1) for the rearrangement to $x=1.25y+0.75$. The (M1) is for identifying the true gradient $0.5$, the (A1) for the correlation $0.632$ and the (R1) for the explanation that rearranging does not reverse the regression. Follow through from earlier parts is allowed.

Why this question is hard

The item is about the distinction between the regression line of $y$ on $x$ and the regression line of $x$ on $y$. The two lines share the point of means $(7,5)$ but have different gradients, $0.8$ and $0.5$, because the first minimises vertical deviations and the second minimises horizontal deviations. Parts (a) to (c) compute the centred sums and both lines from the summary statistics, which is routine for a candidate who knows the formulae.

Part (d) is the lever. A student rearranges $y=-0.6+0.8x$ to obtain $x=1.25y+0.75$ and claims this is the regression of $x$ on $y$. The claim is plausible because the rearranged line does pass through the point of means, but it is wrong: the correct line has gradient $0.5$, and the two lines coincide only when the correlation is exactly plus or minus one. Here $r=\frac{40}{\sqrt{50\times 80}}=0.632$, so the discrepancy is substantial. The correlation is close to zero point six three, so the two lines are not merely different in principle but visibly separated on any plot of the data.

The classic error is to believe that making $x$ the subject converts one regression into the other. Regression is defined by which variable is treated as the response, and algebra cannot change that. The numbers were chosen so that $S_{xx}=50$, $S_{yy}=80$ and $S_{xy}=40$ give the clean gradients $0.8$ and $0.5$, and so that the reciprocal $1.25$ and the correlation $0.632$ are visibly different from the correct values, making the distinction impossible to miss.
