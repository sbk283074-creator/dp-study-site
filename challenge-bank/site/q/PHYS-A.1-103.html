A.1 Range of a projectile on an inclined plane: the dependence on alpha and beta is through 2alpha - beta

Physics HL · PHYS-A.1-103

A.1 Kinematics (projectile motion, resolution of vectors)P2 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedShow thatHenceFindCalculate

Question

A ball is projected from a point $O$ on a plane inclined at an angle $\beta$ to the horizontal. The initial speed is $u$ and the initial direction makes an angle $\alpha$ with the horizontal, measured in the vertical plane containing the line of greatest slope. The ball moves up the plane. Air resistance is negligible.

Throughout this question take the axis parallel to the plane, pointing up the slope, and the axis perpendicular to the plane, pointing away from it.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Resolve the initial velocity and the acceleration due to gravity parallel and perpendicular to the plane, and hence show that the time of flight is

    $$T = \frac{2u\sin(\alpha - \beta)}{g\cos\beta}.$$(The ball lands when its displacement perpendicular to the plane returns to zero.)

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Hence show that the range $R$ measured up the plane is

    $$R = \frac{u^2}{g\cos^2\beta}\left[\sin(2\alpha - \beta) - \sin\beta\right].$$

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Hence find, in terms of $\beta$, the value of $\alpha$ that maximises $R$, and show that the maximum range is

    $$R_{\max} = \frac{u^2}{g(1 + \sin\beta)}.$$

    (Find)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    The ball is projected with $u = 30.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ up a plane with $\beta = 20.0^\circ$. Taking $g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, calculate the maximum range up the plane and the corresponding value of $\alpha$.

    A student suggests that the maximum range on any plane is obtained with $\alpha = 45^\circ$. Calculate the range this would give, and state by how much it falls short of the maximum.

    (Calculate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Resolving the initial velocity: parallel to the plane $u\cos(\alpha - \beta)$, perpendicular to the plane $u\sin(\alpha - \beta)$ (A1). Resolving the acceleration due to gravity: parallel to the plane $g\sin\beta$ down the slope, perpendicular to the plane $g\cos\beta$ into the plane (A1). The perpendicular displacement returns to zero at landing, so with $s = u_\perp T - \tfrac{1}{2}a_\perp T^2$ (M1):
$$u\sin(\alpha - \beta)\,T - \tfrac{1}{2}g\cos\beta\,T^2 = 0,$$
and rejecting $T = 0$ gives $T = \dfrac{2u\sin(\alpha - \beta)}{g\cos\beta}$ (AG).

(b) Along the plane, $R = u\cos(\alpha - \beta)\,T - \tfrac{1}{2}g\sin\beta\,T^2$ (M1). Substituting $T$ from part (a) and writing the first term over the common denominator $g\cos^2\beta$ (M1):
$$R = \frac{u^2}{g\cos^2\beta}\left[2\sin(\alpha - \beta)\cos(\alpha - \beta)\cos\beta - 2\sin\beta\sin^2(\alpha - \beta)\right].$$
Using $2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin 2\theta$ and $2\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos 2\theta$ with $\theta = \alpha - \beta$ (M1):
$$R = \frac{u^2}{g\cos^2\beta}\left[\sin(2\alpha - 2\beta)\cos\beta - \sin\beta\left(1 - \cos(2\alpha - 2\beta)\right)\right]$$
$$= \frac{u^2}{g\cos^2\beta}\left[\sin(2\alpha - 2\beta)\cos\beta + \sin\beta\cos(2\alpha - 2\beta) - \sin\beta\right].$$
The first two terms are the expansion of $\sin\left((2\alpha - 2\beta) + \beta\right) = \sin(2\alpha - \beta)$ (A1), giving the required result.

(c) The factor $\dfrac{u^2}{g\cos^2\beta}$ and the term $-\sin\beta$ are both independent of $\alpha$, so $R$ is a maximum when $\sin(2\alpha - \beta)$ is a maximum (M1), that is when $\sin(2\alpha - \beta) = 1$ and $2\alpha - \beta = 90^\circ$ (A1). Hence
$$\alpha = 45^\circ + \frac{\beta}{2}.$$
Then $R_{\max} = \dfrac{u^2}{g\cos^2\beta}(1 - \sin\beta)$ (M1), and since $\cos^2\beta = 1 - \sin^2\beta = (1 - \sin\beta)(1 + \sin\beta)$,
$$R_{\max} = \frac{u^2(1 - \sin\beta)}{g(1 - \sin\beta)(1 + \sin\beta)} = \frac{u^2}{g(1 + \sin\beta)} \quad (\mathrm{AG}).$$
As a check, $\beta = 0$ gives $\alpha = 45^\circ$ and $R_{\max} = \dfrac{u^2}{g}$, the familiar flat-ground results.

(d) $\alpha = 45^\circ + \dfrac{20.0^\circ}{2} = 55.0^\circ$ (A1). Then
$$R_{\max} = \frac{30.0^2}{9.81(1 + \sin 20.0^\circ)} = \frac{900}{9.81 \times 1.3420} = 68.4\,\mathrm{m} \quad (\mathrm{A1}).$$
With $\alpha = 45^\circ$ instead, $\sin(2\alpha - \beta) = \sin(90^\circ - 20^\circ) = \sin 70^\circ$, so (M1)
$$R = \frac{900}{9.81\cos^2 20^\circ}\left[\sin 70^\circ - \sin 20^\circ\right] = \frac{900}{8.662}(0.9397 - 0.3420) = 62.1\,\mathrm{m} \quad (\mathrm{A1}),$$
which falls short of the maximum by $68.4 - 62.1 = 6.3\,\mathrm{m}$, about $9\,\%$ of the maximum range (A1). The student's rule is therefore correct only on level ground.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (A1) for each correct resolution, (M1) for applying s = ut - (1/2)at^2 perpendicular to the plane and setting s = 0, and the final mark is (AG) -- the answer is given, so the working must be shown in full and no credit is available for merely stating the result.
(b) Award (M1) for writing the displacement along the plane, (M1) for substituting T and forming a common denominator, (M1) for applying the double-angle identities, (A1) for recognising the sine addition expansion. Candidates may reach the result by a different algebraic route; award marks for equivalent correct steps.
(c) Award (M1) for observing that only sin(2*alpha - beta) depends on alpha, (A1) for 2*alpha - beta = 90 degrees and alpha = 45 + beta/2, (M1) for substituting back, (A1) for the factorisation of cos^2(beta). Allow the check beta = 0 as a substitute for the final (A1) if the candidate states both flat-ground results correctly.
(d) Award (A1) for alpha = 55.0 degrees, (A1) for 68.4 m (accept 68.3-68.5; units required), (M1) for evaluating sin(70) - sin(20) in the correct formula, (A1) for 62.1 m, (A1) for the shortfall of 6.3 m. Follow-through applies: a candidate using an incorrect but stated value of alpha from part (c) may still earn the (M1) and the final two (A1) marks on their own value.

Why this question is hard

Everything hard about this item is in one line of part (b). Before the double-angle identity is applied, the range is a product of $\sin(\alpha - \beta)$ and $\cos(\alpha - \beta)$ plus a second term in $\sin^2(\alpha - \beta)$, and the dependence on $\alpha$ and $\beta$ runs through three separate places. After the identity, it runs through one: the whole of the $\alpha$-dependence is inside $\sin(2\alpha - \beta)$, and the maximum is read off by inspection. The identity is not a simplification for its own sake; it is what makes the optimisation possible at all.

The distractor is the flat-ground result. Every candidate knows that maximum range needs $45^\circ$, and the item is set up so that the obvious move is to carry that over unchanged. Part (d) makes the cost concrete: $6.3$ m out of $68.4$ m, about 9 per cent. That is a large enough gap to feel, and it is the kind of number that makes the general result memorable.

There is also a small structural point worth naming. The perpendicular axis is what determines the time of flight, and the parallel axis is what determines the range; keeping the two separate is what stops candidates from writing down a single SUVAT equation in the horizontal direction and hoping. Candidates who resolve along the horizontal and vertical instead can still reach the answer, but the algebra is considerably longer and the $\cos\beta$ in the denominator of $T$ is much easier to lose.

This is adapted from the Chinese Gaokao treatment of projectile motion on an inclined plane, where the result $\alpha = 45^\circ + \beta/2$ is a standard item in the mechanics repertoire. The Gaokao version typically asks only for the optimal angle. Here the derivation is scaffolded by two 'show that' parts so that the item sits at difficulty 4, and the final part quantifies the error in the flat-ground answer, which the source does not ask for.
