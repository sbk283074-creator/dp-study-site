E.1 The distance of closest approach in Rutherford scattering, the energy at which that distance falls to within the nucleus and the alpha-scattering picture therefore breaks down, and the de Broglie wavelength that decides whether a trajectory picture was ever appropriate

Physics HL · PHYS-E.1-301

Question

An alpha particle of kinetic energy $E = 5.0\ {\rm MeV}$ is fired directly towards a stationary gold nucleus, whose atomic number is $Z = 79$ and whose nucleon number is $A = 197$. The approach is head-on, so the alpha particle travels along the line through the centre of the nucleus. The alpha particle carries charge $+2e$; take $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ {\rm C}$, $k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}} = 8.99 \times 10^{9}\ {\rm N\,m^{2}\,C^{-2}}$, the Planck constant $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ {\rm J\,s}$, and the mass of the alpha particle $m_{\alpha} = 6.64 \times 10^{-27}\ {\rm kg}$. The mass of the nucleus is so much greater than that of the alpha particle that its recoil may be neglected, and the only force between them, until they touch, is electrostatic repulsion obeying the inverse-square law.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Show that the distance of closest approach $d$, for a head-on approach, satisfies $d = \dfrac{2kZe^{2}}{E}$, and state what the velocity of the alpha particle is at that instant.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the distance of closest approach for this alpha particle, giving your answer in metres and in femtometres ($1\ {\rm fm} = 10^{-15}\ {\rm m}$).

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    The radius of a nucleus may be estimated as $R = R_{0}A^{1/3}$ with $R_{0} = 1.2 \times 10^{-15}\ {\rm m}$. Determine the radius of the gold nucleus, and hence determine by what factor the distance of closest approach from part (b) exceeds it. State what this tells you about whether the alpha particle reaches the nuclear surface.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine the kinetic energy, in MeV, at which the distance of closest approach would become equal to the nuclear radius found in part (c). State what physical effect, neglected up to this point, would begin to matter above this energy.

    (Determine)
  5. [3 marks](e)

    Determine the de Broglie wavelength of the $5.0\ {\rm MeV}$ alpha particle, and comment quantitatively on whether treating its approach as a classical trajectory is justified, referring to your answer to part (c).

    (Determine)

Reveal the answer

(a) As the alpha particle approaches, electrostatic repulsion does work against it, converting kinetic energy into electrostatic potential energy. At the distance of closest approach the alpha particle has momentarily stopped, so all of its original kinetic energy has become potential energy (M1):
$$E = \frac{k(2e)(Ze)}{d} = \frac{2kZe^{2}}{d} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d = \frac{2kZe^{2}}{E} \;(A1).$$
At that instant the velocity is zero: it is the turning point of the motion, at which the alpha particle reverses direction.
(b) Converting the energy to joules, $E = 5.0 \times 10^{6} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 8.0 \times 10^{-13}\ {\rm J}$ (M1). Then
$$d = \frac{2(8.99 \times 10^{9})(79)(1.60 \times 10^{-19})^{2}}{8.0 \times 10^{-13}} = \frac{3.64 \times 10^{-26}}{8.0 \times 10^{-13}} = 4.55 \times 10^{-14}\ {\rm m} \;(M1)(A1),$$
which is $45.5\ {\rm fm}$. So even a head-on approach turns the alpha particle around some forty-five femtometres from the centre of the gold nucleus.
(c) The nuclear radius is
$$R = 1.2 \times 10^{-15} \times 197^{1/3} = 1.2 \times 10^{-15} \times 5.82 = 6.98 \times 10^{-15}\ {\rm m} = 6.98\ {\rm fm} \;(M1)(A1),$$
and the ratio is
$$\frac{d}{R} = \frac{4.55 \times 10^{-14}}{6.98 \times 10^{-15}} = 6.5 \;(A1).$$
The alpha particle therefore turns around at about six and a half nuclear radii, comfortably short of the nuclear surface (A1). Its closest approach is not to nuclear matter, but to a point in empty space but empty space: it never touches nuclear matter, and consequently nothing except the inverse-square electrostatic repulsion is responsible for its deflection. This is precisely the condition under which Rutherford's analysis is valid, and it is why the experiment could be interpreted as evidence for a small dense nucleus at all.
(d) Setting $d = R$ in the relation from part (a) and rearranging,
$$E = \frac{2kZe^{2}}{R} = \frac{3.64 \times 10^{-26}}{6.98 \times 10^{-15}} = 5.21 \times 10^{-12}\ {\rm J} \;(M1)(A1),$$
which in electronvolts is
$$\frac{5.21 \times 10^{-12}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.26 \times 10^{7}\ {\rm eV} = 32.6\ {\rm MeV} \;(A1).$$
Above roughly $33\ {\rm MeV}$ the alpha particle reaches the nuclear surface, and the strong nuclear force -- attractive and of range only a few femtometres, so utterly negligible at forty-five femtometres but dominant once contact is made -- begins to act. Scattering angles would then depart from the Rutherford formula, which assumes pure Coulomb repulsion, and the simple picture breaks down.
(e) The momentum follows from $E = p^{2}/(2m_{\alpha})$, so
$$p = \sqrt{2m_{\alpha}E} = \sqrt{2(6.64 \times 10^{-27})(8.0 \times 10^{-13})} = 1.03 \times 10^{-19}\ {\rm kg\,m\,s^{-1}} \;(M1),$$
and the de Broglie wavelength is
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{1.03 \times 10^{-19}} = 6.43 \times 10^{-15}\ {\rm m} = 6.43\ {\rm fm} \;(A1).$$
Comparing this with part (c), the de Broglie wavelength of $6.4\ {\rm fm}$ is very nearly equal to the nuclear radius of $7.0\ {\rm fm}$ (A1). A particle is well described by a classical trajectory only when its wavelength is small compared with the distances that characterise the situation it moves through; here the wavelength is comparable to the very object doing the scattering, though still about seven times smaller than the distance of closest approach of $45\ {\rm fm}$. The classical picture is therefore serviceable but not secure: at higher incident energies both the distance of closest approach and the wavelength shrink together, and the assumptions behind part (a) fail at much the same place.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for equating the initial kinetic energy to the electrostatic potential energy at the turning point, (A1) for the stated relation with both factors of $e$ included via $(2e)(Ze)$. Do not accept an expression missing the factor of two, which arises from the alpha particle's charge being $2e$; this is the commonest error in this calculation. The statement that the velocity is zero at that instant should be credited within these marks.
(b) (M1) for converting MeV to joules, (M1) for substituting correctly into the relation from part (a), (A1) for $4.55 \times 10^{-14}\ {\rm m}$ (accept $4.5 \times 10^{-14}\ {\rm m}$ or $45\ {\rm fm}$ to two significant figures). Condoned: using $E = 8.0 \times 10^{-13}\ {\rm J}$ directly if shown. Follow-through from a missing factor of two in part (a) is allowed, giving $2.3 \times 10^{-14}\ {\rm m}$ in that case.
(c) (M1) for evaluating $A^{1/3}$ and multiplying by $R_{0}$, (A1) for $6.98 \times 10^{-15}\ {\rm m}$ (accept $7.0\ {\rm fm}$), (A1) for the ratio $6.5$, (A1) for the conclusion that the alpha particle does not reach the nuclear surface and that only the Coulomb interaction is involved. A candidate who reaches the ratio but draws no conclusion from it loses this mark: the ratio is what licenses the whole Rutherford interpretation, not a decoration on it.
(d) (M1) for setting $d = R$ and rearranging to $E = 2kZe^{2}/R$, (A1) for $5.21 \times 10^{-12}\ {\rm J}$ or its equivalent, (A1) for $32.6\ {\rm MeV}$ (accept $33\ {\rm MeV}$), together with the identification of the strong nuclear force and its short range. A candidate who identifies the effect but does not note that the range is negligible at forty-five femtometres loses the mark: the whole point of the threshold is that a force present all along becomes decisive only at contact.
(e) (M1) for obtaining $p$ from $E = p^{2}/2m$, (A1) for $6.43 \times 10^{-15}\ {\rm m}$ or $6.4\ {\rm fm}$, (A1) for the quantitative comparison against the nuclear radius and against the distance of closest approach, with a statement about the validity of the trajectory picture. Credit candidates who note both that the wavelength is comparable to the nucleus and that it is still smaller than the turning distance; a judgement of 'borderline but usable' is the most honest reading and deserves full marks, whereas 'classical picture is fine' or 'must be treated as a wave' without qualification does not.

Why this question is hard

This is a calculation every student can perform and very few can assess. The standard item asks for the distance of closest approach and stops, leaving the impression that a large number like forty-five femtometres is the answer. It is an answer, but it is also a claim about three things being true at once: that the inverse-square law holds all the way in, that the nucleus can be treated as a point charge, and that a particle with a de Broglie wavelength has a trajectory at all.

Part (c) tests the second. The nuclear radius from $R = R_{0}A^{1/3}$ is about seven femtometres, so the alpha particle reverses at roughly six and a half nuclear radii and never touches nuclear matter. That is not incidental detail but the reason Rutherford's argument worked: because the alpha never experiences anything but Coulomb repulsion, the observed angular distribution can be read as a measurement of the nuclear charge, and the inference of a tiny dense nucleus follows.

Part (d) asks where that happy accident stops being true, and the answer is a genuine threshold rather than a gradual deterioration. Setting the turning distance equal to the nuclear radius gives about thirty-three MeV, and below it nothing changes, while above it the strong nuclear force -- attractive, and with a range of a few femtometres so completely negligible at forty-five -- becomes dominant the moment contact is made. Real alpha-scattering experiments at energies above this threshold do show angular distributions that deviate from the Rutherford formula, and the deviation is historically how the nuclear radius was first measured directly.

Part (e) is the one nobody expects. The de Broglie wavelength of a 5.0 MeV alpha is 6.4 femtometres, which is essentially the same size as the nucleus it is scattering off, though still about seven times smaller than the distance it turns around at. So the classical picture is serviceable here but rests on a margin of one order of magnitude rather than several, and that margin closes as the energy rises: both the turning distance and the wavelength shrink together. The two derived limits therefore arrive in the same neighbourhood for the same physical reason, and noticing that is worth rather more than either number.

Worth flagging as the common numerical error: the alpha particle carries charge $2e$, so the potential energy is $k(2e)(Ze)/d$ and the factor of two survives into every downstream answer. Candidates who lose it halve every distance and double every threshold, and nothing in the numbers they produce looks obviously wrong.

Challenge lever
The standard calculation gives a distance of closest approach of about forty-five femtometres against a nuclear radius of seven, and the natural reading of that is how close the alpha particle gets. The question the procedure does not ask is what would have to change for the answer to stop being meaningful. Nothing in the derivation flags its own boundary: the inverse-square law is assumed to hold all the way in, the nucleus is assumed to be a point, and whether the alpha particle behaves like a particle at all is never tested. Each of those assumptions has a number attached, and all three numbers are comparable, which is the interesting part.
