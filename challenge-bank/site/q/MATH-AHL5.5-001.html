Rebuilding the function from its derivative and a single point

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.5-001

SL 5.5 (introduction to integration as anti-differentiation of polynomial and exponential functions; the constant of integration)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedFindFindFindShow that

Question

A function $f$ satisfies $f'(x)=6x^{2}-4x$ and $f(1)=5$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Find the general antiderivative of $f'(x)$, including the constant of integration.

    (Find)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the value of the constant of integration, given that $f(1)=5$.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Hence find the exact value of the function when $x=2$.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Show that $\int_{1}^{2} f'(x)\,dx = f(2)-f(1)=8$.

    (Show that)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Integrating $f'(x)=6x^{2}-4x$ term by term gives $f(x)=6\cdot\frac{x^{3}}{3}-4\cdot\frac{x^{2}}{2}+C=2x^{3}-2x^{2}+C$ (M1)(A1). The constant $C$ must be included because integration undoes differentiation only up to an additive constant (A1). The general form is therefore $f(x)=2x^{3}-2x^{2}+C$, representing a whole family of parallel curves (R1). The antiderivative is confirmed with the constant present, since any two antiderivatives differ only by a constant. This family picture is essential: without the condition in part (b) the exact curve cannot be singled out.

(b) Using the condition $f(1)=5$ gives $2(1)^{3}-2(1)^{2}+C=5$, so $2-2+C=5$ and therefore $C=5$ (M1)(A1). The single data point pins down the otherwise free constant uniquely (A1). The particular function is therefore $f(x)=2x^{3}-2x^{2}+5$ (R1), the one member of the family that passes through $(1,5)$.

(c) With $C=5$ fixed, substituting $x=2$ gives $f(2)=2(8)-2(4)+5=16-8+5=13$ (M1)(A1), so $f(2)=13$ (A1). This value is found by substituting into the fully determined function, not by differentiating again (R1). The value is therefore $13$, the height of the curve above the axis at that point.

(d) By the fundamental theorem of calculus, $\int_{1}^{2}f'(x)\,dx=f(2)-f(1)$ (M1), so this equals $13-5=8$ (A1). Direct integration confirms it: $\int_{1}^{2}(6x^{2}-4x)\,dx=[2x^{3}-2x^{2}]_{1}^{2}=(16-8)-(2-2)=8$ (A1), showing the two routes agree (R1). The two methods agree, so the integral is $8$, which is exactly the net change in $f$ across the interval. Both the rebuilt function and the direct integral give the same number, which is the essence of the fundamental theorem.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for integrating each term of $6x^{2}-4x$, (A1) for $2x^{3}-2x^{2}$, (A1) for the constant $C$, and (R1) for noting an antiderivative needs the constant because differentiation erases it. A candidate who omits $C$ loses the constant mark but may earn the others. The constant is what distinguishes the family of solutions.

(b) The (M1) is for substituting $x=1$ into the antiderivative, (A1) for $2-2+C=5$, (A1) for $C=5$, and (R1) for the idea that one point fixes the constant uniquely. Follow through from an incorrect antiderivative is allowed.

(c) Award (M1) for substituting $x=2$, (A1) for $16-8+5$, (A1) for $13$, and (R1) for the interpretation that this is a function value, not a derivative. A candidate using the wrong $C$ forfeits only the final value if the method is sound.

(d) Award (M1) for the fundamental theorem $f(2)-f(1)$, (A1) for $13-5=8$, (A1) for the direct integration giving $8$, and (R1) for confirming the two methods agree. Both routes must be shown for full credit. Partial credit follows through from earlier errors in the antiderivative.

Why this question is hard

This item reverses the usual direction of calculus: instead of differentiating, the candidate must rebuild $f$ from its derivative using anti-differentiation, then use a single known point to fix the constant. The derivative $6x^{2}-4x$ integrates cleanly to $2x^{3}-2x^{2}+C$, and the given condition $f(1)=5$ forces $C=5$ because the $x$-terms happen to cancel at $x=1$, leaving $C$ bare. The intended route is to integrate, substitute the point, evaluate $f(2)=13$, and finally observe that the definite integral of $f'$ from $1$ to $2$ equals the net change $f(2)-f(1)=8$. The classic wrong turn is to forget the constant of integration entirely, which makes part (b) impossible, or to write the antiderivative with the wrong power, such as $2x^{3}-4x^{2}$ by mishandling the $4x$ term. Another error is to evaluate $f(1)$ before integrating and treat it as the constant itself. In part (d) candidates sometimes compute the integral directly but fail to connect it to $f(2)-f(1)$, or they integrate $f$ instead of $f'$. The numbers were chosen so the $x$-terms cancel at $x=1$, isolating $C$, and so that $f(2)$ and the net change are small integers, keeping the focus on the method rather than on heavy arithmetic. The double appearance of the same antiderivative, once as the rebuilt function and once as the value of an integral, is the conceptual heart of the item and is the detail most often missed by candidates who treat the two parts as unrelated. The conceptual link between the rebuilt function and the integral of its derivative is the detail most often missed by strong candidates.
