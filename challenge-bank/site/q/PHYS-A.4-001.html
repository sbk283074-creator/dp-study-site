A flywheel, a hanging mass, and a brake

Physics HL · PHYS-A.4-001

A.4 Rigid body mechanics (AHL): torque, moment of inertia of a disc, torque = I x angular acceleration, rotational kinetic energyP2 · Section A16 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatCalculateDetermineExplain

Question

A uniform solid disc of mass $M=2.4\ \mathrm{kg}$ and radius $R=0.35\ \mathrm{m}$ is mounted on a fixed horizontal axle. A light string is wound round the rim, does not slip, and hangs vertically with a mass $m=0.80\ \mathrm{kg}$ attached to its free end. The disc is released from rest and the mass falls a distance $h=0.90\ \mathrm{m}$ before it reaches the floor.

  1. [5 marks](a)

    Show that the acceleration of the falling mass is $a=\dfrac{mg}{m+M/2}$ and calculate its value.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the tension in the string while the mass is falling, and explain why it is less than the weight of the mass.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the speed of the mass as it reaches the floor, and verify your answer by comparing the loss of gravitational potential energy with the total kinetic energy gained.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    After the mass lands the string goes slack. A brake then applies a constant torque of $0.24\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ about the axle. Calculate the number of complete revolutions the disc makes before it stops.

    (Calculate)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Let $T$ be the string tension and $a$ the acceleration of the mass; the string does not slip, so the angular acceleration of the disc is $\alpha=a/R$. (M1)

For the hanging mass: $mg-T=ma$ (M1)

For the disc, torque $\tau=TR$ and moment of inertia $I=\dfrac{1}{2}MR^{2}$, so $TR=\dfrac{1}{2}MR^{2}\cdot\dfrac{a}{R}=\dfrac{1}{2}MRa$, giving $T=\dfrac{1}{2}Ma$. (M1)(A1)

Substituting: $mg-\dfrac{1}{2}Ma=ma$, so $a=\dfrac{mg}{m+\dfrac{M}{2}}$. (AG)

Numerically $a=\dfrac{0.80\times 9.81}{0.80+1.2}=\dfrac{7.848}{2.0}=3.92\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. (A1)

(b) $T=\dfrac{1}{2}Ma=\dfrac{1}{2}\times 2.4\times 3.924=4.71\ \mathrm{N}$. (M1)(A1)

The weight of the mass is $mg=0.80\times 9.81=7.85\ \mathrm{N}$, so $T<mg$. (A1) The tension is less than the weight because the mass is accelerating downwards: the resultant downward force $mg-T$ is what supplies its acceleration, and if $T$ equalled $mg$ the mass would not accelerate at all. Equivalently, the tension is the torque that must be provided to give the disc its angular acceleration, and that comes at the cost of part of the weight.

(c) $v^{2}=2ah=2\times 3.924\times 0.90=7.063$, so $v=2.66\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. (M1)(A1)

Energy check: loss of potential energy $=mgh=0.80\times 9.81\times 0.90=7.06\ \mathrm{J}$. (M1)

The angular speed of the disc is $\omega=\dfrac{v}{R}=\dfrac{2.6577}{0.35}=7.59\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ and $I=\dfrac{1}{2}(2.4)(0.35)^{2}=0.1470\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{2}}$, so the rotational kinetic energy is $\dfrac{1}{2}I\omega^{2}=\dfrac{1}{2}(0.1470)(57.66)=4.24\ \mathrm{J}$.

Adding the translational kinetic energy of the mass, $\dfrac{1}{2}(0.80)(2.6577)^{2}=2.83\ \mathrm{J}$, gives $7.06\ \mathrm{J}$, which equals the loss of potential energy. (A1)

(d) At the moment the string goes slack the disc has $\omega=7.59\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ and rotational kinetic energy $E_{k}=4.24\ \mathrm{J}$. (M1)

With a constant braking torque, the work done equals the change in rotational kinetic energy: $\tau\theta=E_{k}$, so $\theta=\dfrac{4.238}{0.24}=17.7\ \mathrm{rad}$. (M1)(A1)

$17.7\ \mathrm{rad}=\dfrac{17.66}{2\pi}=2.81$ revolutions, so the disc makes $2$ complete revolutions before stopping. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a): the (AG) requires both equations of motion to be stated before substitution. A candidate who writes $a=g$ — treating the disc as massless or the tension as zero — earns nothing beyond the first (M1). Accept the use of $I=kMR^{2}$ with $k=1/2$ stated, and accept a solution by energy methods that differentiates an energy expression, provided the tension is still identified. Part (b): award the (A1) for the comparison only if a numerical or symbolic value of $mg$ is given. Part (c): the energy verification mark is independent of the kinematic calculation; a candidate whose kinematics slips but whose energy comparison is internally consistent still earns the final (A1). Follow-through applies from (a). Part (d): the critical step is converting the rotational kinetic energy into an angle; a candidate who divides by $\tau$ but then forgets to convert radians to revolutions loses the last (A1) only. A common error is to use $E_{k}=\dfrac{1}{2}I\omega^{2}$ with $\omega$ in revolutions per second; check units explicitly. Do not accept an answer of 2.81 revolutions without the conversion stated, and do not penalise a candidate who truncates to 2 with a correct reason.

Why this question is hard

The question is built around one misconception that survives right up to the final examination: that the falling mass accelerates at $g$. It cannot, because the string tension exerts a torque on a disc that has a finite moment of inertia, and that torque can only be produced if the tension differs from the weight. The disc therefore holds the mass back, and the acceleration depends on the ratio of the two masses. Part (b) makes the point explicit and demands the physical reason rather than the algebra.

The energy check in part (c) is not decoration. A student who computes $v=2.66\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ from kinematics and then, in the energy check, assumes all the potential energy became translational kinetic energy will find $\sqrt{2gh}=4.20\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and a total of $7.06\ \mathrm{J}$ from only $2.83\ \mathrm{J}$ of translational energy. The missing $4.24\ \mathrm{J}$ is the rotational kinetic energy of the flywheel, and locating it is the point of the question: for a solid disc with the string unwinding from the rim, roughly 60% of the energy ends up in the disc.

Part (d) changes regime deliberately. Once the string is slack there is no driving torque and no translational motion, so the problem becomes a rotational work calculation with a constant torque. The two common errors are to keep using the translational kinetic energy, or to use the angular speed in the wrong units. The answer is framed as complete revolutions to force the radians-to-revolutions conversion, which is where the mark is usually lost.

Flywheel-and-falling-mass problems are standard in A-level and first-year mechanics, usually stopping at the acceleration. This version keeps the disc and mass values chosen so that a is exactly half of g' in magnitude, adds an explicit energy verification against the rotational share, and introduces a change of regime with a braking torque that requires rotational work. The assembly, the values M = 2.4 kg, R = 0.35 m, m = 0.80 kg, h = 0.90 m and the 0.24 N m brake are all original; every figure was recomputed numerically.
