P3 extended problem: exponential Diophantine equation

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-007

Topic 1: Number and algebraSL 1.6 (simple deductive proof), AHL 1.15 (proof by mathematical induction), SL 1.5 (laws of exponents)P315 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedDetermineShow thatHence

Question

Determine all pairs of positive integers $(m,\,n)$ satisfying
$$2^{m} + 1 = 3^{n}.$$

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Determine all solutions with $m = 1$ or $m = 2$, and write down the solutions of the equation that you have found so far.

    (Determine)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Suppose $m \ge 3$. By working modulo $8$, show that $n$ must be even.

    (Show that)
  3. [7 marks](c)

    Hence show that the only solution with $m \ge 3$ is $(m,\,n) = (3,\,2)$, and therefore state all solutions of the equation.

    (Hence)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Determine all pairs of positive integers $(m,\,n)$ satisfying $$2^{m} + 1 = 3^{n}.$$ (a) Determine all solutions with $m = 1$ or $m = 2$, and write down the solutions of the equation that you have found so far. [3 marks] (Determine) (b) Suppose $m \ge 3$. By working modulo $8$, show that $n$ must be even. [5 marks] (Show that) (c) Hence show that the only solution with $m \ge 3$ is $(m,\,n) = (3,\,2)$, and therefore state all solutions of the equation. [7 marks] (Hence)
Reveal the answer

(a) If $m=1$ then $2^{1}+1 = 3 = 3^{1}$, giving $(m,n) = (1,1)$. (A1)
If $m=2$ then $2^{2}+1 = 5$, which is not a power of $3$, so there is no solution with $m=2$. (A1)
So far: $(m,n) = (1,1)$. (A1)

(b) Assume $m\ge3$. Then $2^{m}$ is divisible by $8$, so $2^{m}\equiv 0 \pmod 8$ and the equation gives (M1)
$$3^{n} = 2^{m}+1 \equiv 1 \pmod 8.$$
Now compute powers of $3$ modulo $8$: (M1)
$$3^{1}\equiv 3, \quad 3^{2} = 9 \equiv 1, \quad 3^{3} \equiv 3, \quad 3^{4}\equiv 1 \pmod 8.$$
Since $3^{2}\equiv1$, the pattern has period $2$: $3^{n}\equiv 1 \pmod 8$ when $n$ is even and $3^{n}\equiv 3 \pmod 8$ when $n$ is odd. (A1)(A1)
Therefore $n$ must be even. (A1)

(c) Write $n = 2k$ with $k$ a positive integer. Then (M1)
$$2^{m} = 3^{2k} - 1 = \bigl(3^{k}-1\bigr)\bigl(3^{k}+1\bigr).$$
Both factors are positive integers whose product is a power of $2$, so each factor is itself a power of $2$: (M1)(A1)
$$3^{k}-1 = 2^{a}, \qquad 3^{k}+1 = 2^{b}, \qquad b > a \ge 1.$$
Subtracting, (M1)
$$2 = 2^{b} - 2^{a} = 2^{a}\bigl(2^{\,b-a} - 1\bigr).$$
The second factor is odd, so it must divide $2$; being odd and positive it equals $1$, giving $2^{b-a}-1 = 1$, so $b-a = 1$. Then $2^{a}\cdot 1 = 2$, so $a = 1$ and $b = 2$. (A1)
Hence $3^{k}-1 = 2$, so $3^{k} = 3$ and $k = 1$. Then $n = 2$ and $2^{m} = 3^{2}-1 = 8$, so $m = 3$. (A1)

Checking: $2^{3}+1 = 9 = 3^{2}$. Therefore the complete set of solutions in positive integers is (A1)
$$\boxed{(m,\,n) = (1,\,1)\ \text{ and }\ (m,\,n) = (3,\,2).}$$

Markscheme notes

(a) Award the marks for the two substitutions; the conclusion mark is for listing $(1,1)$ only. (b) The (M1)s are for reducing the equation modulo $8$ and for computing the cycle of powers of $3$ modulo $8$. A candidate who uses modulo $4$ instead will find that $3^n\equiv1\pmod4$ also forces $n$ even when $m\ge2$, which is valid and should be credited in full; modulo $3$ gives nothing useful and earns nothing. Award the final (A1) only if the parity conclusion is stated. (c) The difference-of-two-squares step is essential and earns (M1). The inference that each factor is a power of $2$ needs justification: it follows because the only divisors of a power of two are powers of two. The equation $2^{a}(2^{b-a}-1)=2$ is the crux; award generously for any correct treatment of it, including an argument by inspection of small cases provided it is exhaustive. Note that $a\ge1$ because $3^{k}-1\ge2$ for $k\ge1$. A candidate who finds $(3,2)$ but fails to exclude other $k$ has not completed the problem.

Why this question is hard

This is a complete solution of an exponential Diophantine equation, and its difficulty is entirely concentrated in the choice of modulus. Nothing in $2^{m}+1=3^{n}$ suggests working modulo $8$; the student has to try something, and the reason $8$ is the right choice is that it is the first power of $2$ large enough to annihilate $2^{m}$ while still producing a short cycle for $3^{n}$. Modulo $4$ also works, modulo $3$ does nothing, and modulo $5$ leads nowhere. Recognising which modulus carries information is the real skill being tested.

Once $n$ is known to be even, the rest is forced. Writing $n=2k$ converts the equation into a difference of two squares, and the product $(3^{k}-1)(3^{k}+1)$ is a power of $2$. That means each factor is a power of $2$, and the two factors differ by exactly $2$. The only pair of powers of two differing by $2$ is $2$ and $4$, which pins $3^{k}=3$ and hence $k=1$. Every step after the modulus is a consequence of the previous one, which is why the problem is worth doing: there is no search, only deduction.

The structure is characteristic of the written tests used for the Chinese strengthening-basic-disciplines admissions route (强基计划), where a short exponential equation is solved by a modular reduction followed by a factorisation. The adaptation keeps both stages intact but adds the explicit small-case check in part (a), because $m\ge3$ is needed before the modulus argument applies and students routinely apply it without checking. The answer, two isolated solutions rather than a family, is the point: the equation looks as though it should have many solutions and has exactly two.
