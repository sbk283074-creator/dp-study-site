A pay rise that costs more than the turnover it is meant to fix

Business management SL · BM-2.1-001

Unit 2: 2.1 Introduction to human resource management (workforce planning, labour turnover, absenteeism); 2.4 Motivation and demotivation.P1 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedCalculateExplainEvaluate

Bramble Furniture

Bramble Furniture employs 480 production staff at its single factory. During the past year, 96 production staff left the company. The factory operates 240 working days per year for each production employee, and 6912 working days were lost to absence during the year.

With its current 480 staff the factory produces 96 000 units per year. The board plans to raise output to 120 000 units without any change in productivity per employee. Replacing a member of production staff costs the company about €3300 in recruitment advertising, induction and lost output, and each new production employee also requires training costing about €900.

The average annual salary of a production employee is €28 000. In response to the turnover problem the board has just awarded every production employee a 4% pay rise, and expects the departure rate to fall as a result.

Question

Bramble Furniture operates a single factory and intends to increase output by 25% without changing the productivity of each employee.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the labour turnover rate and the absenteeism rate for the year.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Calculate how many production staff Bramble must recruit during the coming year to reach the planned output, and the total recruitment and training cost of doing so.

    (Calculate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain, using one theory of motivation, why the 4% pay rise is unlikely to reduce labour turnover on its own.

    (Explain)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Using your calculations, evaluate whether the 4% pay rise is a good use of the company's money.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Labour turnover $=\dfrac{\text{number of staff leaving}}{\text{average number of staff}}\times 100=\dfrac{96}{480}\times 100=20\%$. (M1)(A1)

Absenteeism $=\dfrac{\text{days lost}}{\text{total possible working days}}\times 100=\dfrac{6912}{480\times 240}\times 100=\dfrac{6912}{115\,200}\times 100=6\%$. (M1)(A1)

(b) Productivity per employee is $\dfrac{96\,000}{480}=200$ units per year. (M1)

To produce 120 000 units at the same productivity, Bramble needs $\dfrac{120\,000}{200}=600$ staff, an increase of 120. (A1)

In addition, the 96 staff who leave in a typical year must be replaced simply to stand still, so the total number of recruits is $120+96=216$. (M1)

At €3300 plus €900 per hire, the cost is $216\times 4200=€907\,200$. (A1) This figure assumes that each new employee reaches the current output of 200 units in the first year; new recruits typically take time to reach full productivity, so the true requirement may exceed 600 staff or the output fall short in the short run.

(c) Herzberg's two-factor theory distinguishes hygiene factors, which prevent dissatisfaction, from motivators, which create satisfaction. Pay is a hygiene factor: if it is too low it causes dissatisfaction, but improving it beyond a level the employees regard as acceptable does not generate motivation. (M1)

The 4% rise moves pay from below the acceptable level to about the acceptable level, or simply raises it where pay was already acceptable, so at best it removes a source of dissatisfaction rather than creating a reason to stay. If the real causes of leaving are the repetitiveness of the work, the absence of promotion opportunities, or poor supervision and working conditions, those are the motivators and they are untouched by the rise. (M1) Employees who are leaving for a better career or for more varied work will continue to do so, because the factor that would change their mind is not the one that has been altered. (A1)

(d) The 4% rise costs $480\times 28\,000\times 0.04=€537\,600$ per year, and it is a permanent addition to the pay bill. (M1)

The turnover it is intended to reduce costs about $96\times 3300=€316\,800$ per year to manage. (A1) Even if the rise eliminated turnover completely, which Herzberg's theory suggests it will not, the company would be spending €537 600 to avoid at most €316 800, so the measure cannot pay for itself on these figures. In any case the company must recruit 216 people this year because 120 of them are needed for the expansion, not because of turnover, so the pay rise would not remove that cost either. A 4% rise is also a permanent, across-the-board commitment, whereas recruitment costs are incurred only when staff actually leave; if the turnover rate were to fall for other reasons, the company would be locked into the higher pay bill with no offsetting saving. The money would be better spent on the motivators identified in part (c), or on reducing the €907 200 recruitment and training bill by improving retention through job design and progression. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a): accept the use of closing staff numbers instead of average staff numbers if the reason is stated, since the average is not given; the method mark should be awarded either way. Part (b): the two component marks are for the expansion requirement and for the replacement requirement; a candidate who reports only 120 has answered half the question and can earn at most two marks. Do not accept a candidate who adds the 96 leavers twice. Part (c): any theory that identifies pay as a hygiene or lower-order need should be credited, including Maslow's hierarchy with pay located among physiological and safety needs. A candidate who states the theory correctly but does not apply it to Bramble's situation earns the first method mark only. Part (d): the comparison between €537 600 and €316 800 is the discriminating mark; a candidate who evaluates the pay rise as 'expensive' without a figure does not reach it. Follow-through applies from parts (a) and (b), so a candidate with a different recruitment number who reasons consistently should still earn the final mark. Do not accept an evaluation that ignores the fact that the pay rise is permanent and recurring.

Why this question is hard

The question is arranged so that the arithmetic quietly condemns the decision. The pay rise looks modest as a percentage, but 4% of a payroll for 480 people is €537 600 a year and it recurs, while the turnover it is meant to cure costs about €316 800 a year. The comparison is never requested directly, which is why it separates candidates who calculate from candidates who use their calculations: the numbers are already on the page and the judgement requires putting them next to each other.

There is a second trap in part (b). The recruitment requirement has two components, and only one of them is caused by turnover. A company that is growing must hire for growth as well as for replacement, and the total — 216 people in a year for a workforce of 480 — is large enough to make the point that retention and recruitment are separate problems with separate solutions. A candidate who reports only the 120 needed for expansion has answered a smaller question than the one asked.

The motivation theory does real work here rather than being decoration. Pay is a hygiene factor in Herzberg's terms, and the theory predicts exactly the observed outcome: the pay rise removes a possible source of dissatisfaction without creating a reason to stay. That is not an argument against paying people properly; it is an argument against expecting money to solve a problem caused by the nature of the work. The same logic explains why the absenteeism figure matters too — 6% of working days lost is a symptom of something in the working environment, and no pay rise addresses it.

A strong answer will notice that the company has two problems, not one. It must grow, which requires recruitment and training at a cost of €907 200 this year, and it must retain, which a permanent pay rise will not achieve on these figures. The €537 600 would be better deployed on the motivators — progression, job design, supervision — or simply on absorbing the recruitment cost of the expansion.

Challenge lever
