4.14 Two unknown values of a discrete random variable recovered from the mean and the variance together, where either reading alone is satisfied by infinitely many pairs

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.14-201

Topic 4: Statistics and probabilityAHL 4.14 (variance of a discrete random variable) with AHL 4.11 (expected value) and SL 4.5 (probability of an event)P2 · Section B12 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatDetermineShow thatExplain

Question

A charity sells raffle tickets. Each ticket wins one of three prizes. The payout $X$, in pounds, is a discrete random variable with
$$P(X = 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}, \qquad P(X = a) = \tfrac{1}{4}, \qquad P(X = b) = \tfrac{1}{4},$$
where $a$ and $b$ are unknown positive integers with $a < b$. The organiser states that
$$E(X) = 3 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = 6.$$

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Show that the stated expected value implies $a + b = 10$, and explain why that single equation does not determine $a$ and $b$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine $a$ and $b$, showing that the variance condition leads to the quadratic $z^{2} - 10z + 21 = 0$ and explaining why its two roots are the required prizes.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    The organiser first stated $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4$. Show that this value corresponds to $a = b$, and explain why the stated form of the distribution therefore rules that value out.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    An auditor is told only that $P(X \le a) = \dfrac{3}{4}$ and that $a < b$. Explain how this single statement determines $a$ without the variance, and state what it leaves undetermined.

    (Explain)

Total: 12 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Using the definition $E(X) = \sum x\,P(X = x)$ (M1):
$$E(X) = 1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + a \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} + b \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} = \frac{2 + a + b}{4} = 3,$$
so $2 + a + b = 12$ and $a + b = 10$ (A1).

One equation in two unknowns has infinitely many solutions in the positive integers, namely $(a, b) = (1, 9), (2, 8), (3, 7), (4, 6)$, and every one of them gives a distribution with mean $3$. The mean therefore fixes the *total* of the prizes and nothing about how that total is split. A second, independent reading of the distribution is needed, and the variance supplies it.

(b) The second moment is
$$E(X^{2}) = 1^{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + a^{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} + b^{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} = \frac{2 + a^{2} + b^{2}}{4} \;\; (M1).$$
Using $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E(X^{2}) - (E(X))^{2}$ with $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 6$ and $E(X) = 3$:
$$E(X^{2}) = 6 + 3^{2} = 15, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \frac{2 + a^{2} + b^{2}}{4} = 15, \qquad a^{2} + b^{2} = 58 \;\; (A1).$$

Now use $a + b = 10$. Squaring, $a^{2} + 2ab + b^{2} = 100$, so
$$2ab = 100 - 58 = 42, \qquad ab = 21 \;\; (M1).$$
The two numbers whose sum is $10$ and whose product is $21$ are the roots of
$$z^{2} - 10z + 21 = 0, \qquad (z - 3)(z - 7) = 0, \qquad z = 3 \text{ or } z = 7 \;\; (A1).$$

Since $a$ and $b$ are interchangeable in both the mean and the variance -- the distribution is symmetric between them -- the two roots are the two prizes, and the condition $a < b$ assigns $a = 3$ and $b = 7$.

Check: $E(X) = \dfrac{2 + 3 + 7}{4} = 3$; $E(X^{2}) = \dfrac{2 + 9 + 49}{4} = 15$; $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 15 - 9 = 6$. Both readings are satisfied.

(c) If $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4$ instead, then $E(X^{2}) = 4 + 9 = 13$, so $\dfrac{2 + a^{2} + b^{2}}{4} = 13$ and $a^{2} + b^{2} = 50$ (M1). With $a + b = 10$ this gives $2ab = 100 - 50 = 50$, so $ab = 25$, and the quadratic is
$$z^{2} - 10z + 25 = 0, \qquad (z - 5)^{2} = 0 \;\; (A1).$$
Its only root is $z = 5$, so $a = b = 5$.

That is not merely a coincidence of the numbers: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4$ is the smallest variance attainable, because for fixed mean the variance is minimised when the distribution puts as much probability as possible at the mean. Here the mean $3$ lies between the value $1$ (probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$) and the equal prizes, and pushing both prizes to $5$ is exactly the point at which the two remaining outcomes coincide.

The stated form of the distribution requires $a < b$, and more fundamentally the question describes three prizes while $a = b$ collapses the distribution to the two values $1$ and $5$ (A1). So $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4$ is inconsistent with the description, and the corrected value $6$ is the one that restores the third prize.

(d) By definition
$$P(X \le a) = P(X = 1) + P(X = a) = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{3}{4} \;\; (M1).$$
So the statement $P(X \le a) = \tfrac{3}{4}$ is not an extra restriction at all: it holds automatically for every choice of $a$ and $b$ with $a < b$, provided only that $a$ is the middle value in order. It does, however, identify $a$ as the upper quartile of the distribution and, since $a < b$, place $a$ strictly between the two larger values (A1).

What it leaves undetermined is the size of $a$, and this is the point worth stating: $P(X \le a) = \tfrac{3}{4}$ is a statement about probabilities, and probabilities are unchanged when the values are relabelled, so it carries no information about the numerical value of $a$ at all (A1). To determine $a$ one still needs one numerical reading of the distribution, and the mean is enough on its own here: given $a$ and $a + b = 10$ we get $b = 10 - a$, so any one of the three prizes, the mean, or the variance determines the rest. The auditor's statement is therefore *necessary but not sufficient*, which is why the organiser's mean and variance were both needed in part (b).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for forming the expectation with the stated probabilities, (A1) for $a + b = 10$ with the observation that one equation in two unknowns leaves a family of solutions. Listing the four admissible pairs is good practice but is not required for the mark.

(b) (M1) for $E(X^{2})$ in terms of $a^{2} + b^{2}$, (A1) for $a^{2} + b^{2} = 58$, (M1) for using $a + b = 10$ and squaring to obtain $ab = 21$, (A1) for $a = 3, b = 7$ from the quadratic. A candidate who guesses $(3,7)$ from the admissible pairs in (a) must still show the variance condition to earn the marks; the guess alone scores the (M1) for forming $E(X^{2})$ and nothing further, because the question asks for the determination, not for the answer.

(c) (M1) for converting $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4$ into $a^{2} + b^{2} = 50$, (A1) for the degenerate quadratic and $a = b = 5$, (A1) for the reason the stated form excludes it. "Because the question says $a < b$" earns the third mark only if it is connected to the collapse from three prizes to two; a bare restatement of the inequality does not, because the deeper reason is that a repeated prize is not a third prize.

(d) (M1) for evaluating $P(X \le a)$ as $\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{4}$, (A1) for identifying the statement as automatic given the ordering, or as the upper-quartile condition, (A1) for explaining that a probability statement carries no information about the numerical value of $a$. A candidate who answers "it means $a = 3$" has confused the automatic inequality with a determination and scores zero on the third mark.

Condonation: in (b), a candidate who uses $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E(X^{2}) - 9$ correctly but who then solves the quadratic and assigns the roots the other way round -- $a = 7, b = 3$ -- loses nothing in (b) but must be consistent in (c); the condition $a < b$ is given in the stem. Follow-through: a candidate whose (a) is wrong and who continues with their own $a + b$ is credited fully in (b) where the method is correct.

Why this question is hard

A discrete distribution is a list of values and a list of probabilities, and the mean and the variance are two numbers extracted from those lists. The interesting question, and the one this item asks, is the reverse: how much of the lists can be recovered from the two numbers, and what does each number actually constrain?

The mean is a weighted average and it constrains the *total* of the values, not their spread. Knowing $E(X) = 3$ for this distribution gives $a + b = 10$ and leaves four admissible pairs. The temptation at that point is to assume the two prizes are equal, and the assumption is not unreasonable -- it is the symmetric choice, and it satisfies the mean perfectly, giving $a = b = 5$. What it does not satisfy is the variance, which comes out at $4$ rather than $6$. That is the trap in its exact form: the naive answer is not an arithmetic slip, it is a correct computation of the wrong thing, and only the second reading of the distribution exposes it.

The variance is the second aggregate and it measures spread, so it constrains the squares. Together with the mean it turns the problem into a quadratic whose roots are the two prizes, which is the standard way two aggregates determine two unknowns. Notice the direction of the logic in part (c): rather than asking for the variance given the prizes, it asks which prize pairs give a stated variance, and the answer $a = b = 5$ shows that the variance of $4$ is not an arbitrary wrong number but the degenerate limit of the family, the smallest spread the distribution can have given where the mean sits.

Part (d) closes the item by exhibiting a statement that looks like information and is not. $P(X \le a) = \tfrac{3}{4}$ holds for every distribution of this form, because the probabilities are fixed by the raffle's rules and the ordering $a < b$ puts $a$ in the middle. It names $a$ as the upper quartile, which is worth knowing, and it says nothing whatever about the size of $a$. Recognising which constraints bite and which are automatic is the skill the whole item is built around, and it is the same skill that separates a candidate who can use the mean and the variance from one who can say what they mean.
