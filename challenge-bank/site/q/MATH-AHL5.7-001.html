The second derivative that changes sign at two points

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.7-001

SL 5.7 (the second derivative; concavity and points of inflection)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatFindDetermine

Question

Consider the function $f(x)=x^{4}-8x^{3}+18x^{2}$. You are to analyse its concavity using the second derivative.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that $f''(x)=12x^{2}-48x+36$ and that this factorises as $12(x-1)(x-3)$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the values of $x$ for which $f''(x)=0$, and state why these are the only candidates for a change of concavity.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine whether the curve is convex or concave on each of the intervals $x<1$, $1<x<3$ and $x>3$.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Show that $x=1$ and $x=3$ are both points of inflection.

    (Show that)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Begin by differentiating the polynomial $f(x)=x^{4}-8x^{3}+18x^{2}$ once with respect to $x$ to obtain $f'(x)=4x^{3}-24x^{2}+36x$ (M1), then differentiate this result a second time to obtain $f''(x)=12x^{2}-48x+36$ (A1). The quadratic $x^{2}-4x+3$ factors as $(x-1)(x-3)$, so $f''(x)=12(x-1)(x-3)$ (A1)(AG). This confirms the given form, because multiplying the brackets back together restores $12x^{2}-48x+36$ exactly, leaving no remainder, and this step underpins every later part.

(b) Concavity can only change where the second derivative vanishes, so solve $12(x-1)(x-3)=0$ (M1). A product of real factors is zero precisely when one factor is zero, giving $x-1=0$ or $x-3=0$, thus $x=1$ and $x=3$ (A1). These two values are the only real roots of the quadratic, so they form the complete set of candidates that must be tested for a sign change before any conclusion is drawn.

(c) Choose one convenient test value inside each of the three intervals. At $x=0$ we have $f''(0)=36>0$, so the curve is convex on $x<1$ (M1). At $x=2$ we have $f''(2)=-12<0$, so it is concave on the middle interval $1<x<3$ (A1). At $x=4$ we have $f''(4)=36>0$, so it is convex again on $x>3$ (A1). The sign of $f''(x)$ alone decides the concavity on every open interval, and the integer test values keep the arithmetic exact and easy to verify.

(d) At $x=1$ the second derivative passes from positive to negative, and at $x=3$ it passes from negative to positive (M1), so each of these points is a point of inflection (A1)(AG). The function values at the two points are $f(1)=11$ and $f(3)=27$. A point of inflection is defined by a genuine change of sign in $f''(x)$, which occurs at both $x=1$ and $x=3$, and this is precisely why the classification is valid.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for the first derivative $4x^{3}-24x^{2}+36x$ and (A1) for the second derivative $12x^{2}-48x+36$. The factorisation (A1)(AG) requires $12(x-1)(x-3)$ to be shown; accept the working written in either order. A candidate who expands the bracket incorrectly loses the final mark but may keep the two derivative marks.

(b) Award (M1) for setting $12(x-1)(x-3)=0$ and (A1) for both roots $x=1$ and $x=3$. The remark that these are the only candidates is part of the reasoning and need not be written, but follow through from a wrong quadratic is allowed provided the solving step is consistent with it.

(c) Award (M1) for a correct sign test giving convex on $x<1$, (A1) for concave on $1<x<3$, and (A1) for convex on $x>3$. The three test values need not be $0$, $2$, $4$ but the computed signs must be correct. A candidate who states the concavity from $f'$ rather than $f''$ forfeits all three marks.

(d) Award (M1) for identifying the sign change at each root and (A1)(AG) for both points of inflection. The function values $11$ and $27$ are optional supporting detail; the decisive fact is the change of sign, and a candidate who states a root without showing the sign change earns at most one mark.

Why this question is hard

The item is built around a quartic whose second derivative is a quadratic with two distinct real roots, so the student cannot treat the curve as having a single bending point. The first task is to differentiate twice and factor, which yields $f''(x)=12(x-1)(x-3)$. This factorisation is the spine of the whole question, because every later step depends on reading the two roots correctly from the brackets rather than from a sketch. The trap is to stop after finding $f''(x)=0$ and assume both roots are inflection points without checking the sign change, since a stationary point of the first derivative is not automatically an inflection. The intended route is to test one value in each of the three intervals $x<1$, $1<x<3$ and $x>3$, using the sign of $f''(x)$ to decide whether the curve is convex or concave. The values $0$, $2$ and $4$ are convenient because they give $36$, $-12$ and $36$, so the curve is convex, then concave, then convex again in that order. A point of inflection requires the second derivative to change sign, which it does at both $x=1$ and $x=3$, so both values qualify as inflection points. The classic error is to confuse a local minimum of $f'$ with an inflection, or to claim concavity from $f'$ instead of $f''$. Another common slip is to expand $12(x-1)(x-3)$ incorrectly and so miss one of the roots. The numbers were chosen so the second derivative is a clean quadratic and the test values are integers, which means a correct method produces exact signs while a mistaken factorisation usually does not.
