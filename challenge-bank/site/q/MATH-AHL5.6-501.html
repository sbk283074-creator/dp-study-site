Question

The graph of the function $f(x) = x^{3} - 3x^{2}$ is shown in the diagram, together with the stationary point $P(2, -4)$. The dashed horizontal line is the tangent to the curve at $P$, the dashed vertical line is the normal to the curve at $P$, and $R$ is the region bounded by the curve and the tangent.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Find $f'(x)$, and hence show that the tangent to the curve at $P$ is horizontal.

    (Find)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Explain why the usual rule for the gradient of a normal cannot be applied at $P$, and hence determine the equation of the normal to the curve at $P$.

    (Explain)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    The normal at $P$ meets the curve again at the point $Q$, marked in the diagram. Find the coordinates of $Q$.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Hence find the area of the shaded region $R$.

    (Hence)

Reveal the answer

(a) Differentiating term by term,

$$f'(x) = 3x^{2} - 6x = 3x(x - 2).$$ (M1)(A1)

At $P$ the input is $x = 2$, so

$$f'(2) = 3(2)^{2} - 6(2) = 12 - 12 = 0.$$ (A1)

The gradient of the tangent at $P$ is therefore zero, which means the tangent is horizontal. (AG)

(b) The normal is perpendicular to the tangent, and its gradient is normally found as the
negative reciprocal of the gradient of the tangent, that is $-1/f'(x)$. At $P$ the gradient of
the tangent is zero, so $-1/0$ is undefined: the rule has no value to work with. (R1)

The correct reading is geometric. A tangent with gradient zero is a horizontal line, and the
line perpendicular to a horizontal line is vertical. Since the normal passes through $P(2, -4)$,
and every point on a vertical line through $P$ has the same $x$-coordinate, the normal is

$$x = 2.$$ (A1)(A1)

Its equation cannot be written in the form $y = mx + c$, because a vertical line has no finite
gradient; writing $x = 2$ is the whole answer.

(c) A point lies on both the normal and the curve when its $x$-coordinate is $2$ and it
satisfies $y = x^{3} - 3x^{2}$. One such point is $P$ itself. To find the others, substitute
$x = 2$ into the curve:

$$y = 2^{3} - 3(2)^{2} = 8 - 12 = -4,$$

so $P(2, -4)$ is one intersection. To find every intersection at once, solve

$$x^{3} - 3x^{2} = -4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^{3} - 3x^{2} + 4 = 0.$$ (M1)

Since $x = 2$ is a root, divide by $(x - 2)$:

$$x^{3} - 3x^{2} + 4 = (x - 2)(x^{2} - x - 2) = (x - 2)(x - 2)(x + 1) = (x - 2)^{2}(x + 1).$$ (A1)

The roots are $x = 2$ (repeated) and $x = -1$. The repeated root is the point of contact $P$,
so the second intersection is at $x = -1$:

$$y = (-1)^{3} - 3(-1)^{2} = -1 - 3 = -4.$$

Hence $Q(-1, -4)$. (A1)

(d) The region $R$ is bounded above by the curve and below by the tangent $y = -4$, between
$x = -1$ and $x = 2$. The curve sits above the tangent throughout this interval, so the area is

$$A = \int_{-1}^{2} \left[ \left( x^{3} - 3x^{2} \right) - (-4) \right] dx
= \int_{-1}^{2} \left( x^{3} - 3x^{2} + 4 \right) dx.$$ (M1)(A1)

An antiderivative is

$$F(x) = \frac{x^{4}}{4} - x^{3} + 4x.$$ (A1)

$$F(2) = \frac{16}{4} - 8 + 8 = 4, \qquad
F(-1) = \frac{1}{4} + 1 - 4 = -\frac{11}{4}.$$

$$A = F(2) - F(-1) = 4 + \frac{11}{4} = \frac{27}{4}.$$ (A1)

The area of $R$ is $\dfrac{27}{4}$ square units, that is $6.75$. A negative value would have
signalled that the two functions had been subtracted in the wrong order; here the integrand
$x^{3} - 3x^{2} + 4$ equals $(x - 2)^{2}(x + 1)$, which is non-negative on $[-1, 2]$, confirming
the curve lies above the tangent throughout.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for differentiating to $3x^{2} - 6x$, (A1) for the correct derivative, (A1) for evaluating $f'(2) = 0$ and stating that the tangent is therefore horizontal, (AG) for the conclusion. Do not award the final mark for merely writing $f'(2) = 0$ without linking it to the tangent. (b) (R1) for recognising that the negative-reciprocal rule requires a non-zero gradient and therefore fails here, (A1) for the vertical line $x = 2$, (A1) for giving it in the form $x = 2$ rather than $y = mx + c$. An answer of $y = -4$ scores (R1)(A0)(A0): that is the tangent, not the normal. (c) (M1) for setting $x^{3} - 3x^{2} + 4 = 0$, (A1) for the factorisation $(x - 2)^{2}(x + 1)$, (A1) for $Q(-1, -4)$. Accept $x = -1$ with the $y$-value obtained from either the curve or the tangent, since $Q$ lies on both. (d) (M1) for the integral of the curve minus the tangent with the correct limits $-1$ and $2$, (A1) for the correct integrand $x^{3} - 3x^{2} + 4$, (A1) for the antiderivative, (A1) for $ rac{27}{4}$ or $6.75$. Award follow-through on the limits only if part (c) gave a consistent $x$-value. Do not accept a negative area.

Why this question is hard

The item is built around a single exceptional case, and the whole of part (b) is
decided by whether the student notices it. The normal at a point is perpendicular to the
tangent there, and in every routine exercise the gradient of the normal is $-1/f'(x)$. The
point $P$ is chosen so that $f'(2) = 0$, which is exactly the input at which that expression is
undefined. A student who applies the formula mechanically reaches $-1/0$ and must then decide
what to do; the correct response is to stop computing and start reading. A tangent of gradient
zero is horizontal, so the perpendicular through the same point is vertical, and a vertical
line has equation $x = 2$.

The trap has a natural wrong answer attached to it. Because the tangent at $P$ is the
horizontal line $y = -4$, and because the diagram draws both lines through the same point, a
student who has lost track of which line is which will offer $y = -4$ as the normal. The two
lines are perpendicular and both pass through $P$, so the confusion is easy to make and the
answer looks entirely plausible. It is also self-consistent with the rest of the work, because
$Q(-1, -4)$ lies on the tangent as well as on the curve.

Part (c) adds the second demand that lifts the item above a single idea. Knowing that the
normal is $x = 2$ does not immediately give $Q$, because the curve meets that line wherever
$x^{3} - 3x^{2} = -4$. The student has to form the cubic $x^{3} - 3x^{2} + 4 = 0$, notice that
$x = 2$ is a root, and then factorise completely. The factorisation is where an incomplete
answer appears: dividing once gives $(x - 2)(x^{2} - x - 2)$, and a student who stops there and
solves the quadratic correctly still reaches $x = -1$, but one who assumes the cubic has only
the one visible root at $x = 2$ concludes that the normal meets the curve only at $P$ and has no
$Q$ to report. The repeated root is the signature of the point of contact; the simple root is
the point the question asks for.

Part (d) then uses the result of (c) rather than repeating it, which is what the command term
"hence" signals. The region is bounded above by the curve and below by the tangent, and the
integration limits are exactly the two $x$-values already found, $-1$ and $2$. The integrand
factorises as $(x - 2)^{2}(x + 1)$, which is non-negative across the interval, so the signed
integral is already the geometric area and no splitting of the region is needed. That check is
worth making explicitly: it is the reason the answer is positive and the reason a student who
subtracts in the wrong order can detect the error without recomputing.

Challenge lever
The normal is defined as the line perpendicular to the tangent, so the first move every student makes is to compute the gradient of the normal as the negative reciprocal of f'(2). At P the curve has a stationary point, so f'(2) = 0 and the negative reciprocal does not exist: the tangent is horizontal and therefore the normal is the vertical line x = 2. The formula breaks precisely at the point the question is about, and the correct response is to abandon the formula and read the geometry. The second demand is the intersection: substituting x = 2 into the curve gives y = -4, so the vertical normal meets the curve at P and at Q(-1, -4), and the student has to factorise x^3 - 3x^2 + 4 fully as (x - 2)^2 (x + 1) rather than stop at the first factor, which is what makes the area in part (d) tractable.
