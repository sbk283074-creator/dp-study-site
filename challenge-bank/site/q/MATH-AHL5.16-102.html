5.16 A substitution that reproduces the integral instead of evaluating it

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.16-102

Topic 5: CalculusAHL 5.16 (integration by substitution) and AHL 5.19 (Maclaurin series context)P3 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 5technology: allowedShow thatHenceProveEvaluate

Question

Consider the definite integral

$$I = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin x}{\sin x + \cos x}\,dx.$$

The integrand has an elementary antiderivative, but obtaining it is lengthy and the result is not obviously the simplest route to the value of $I$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Use the substitution $u = \dfrac{\pi}{2} - x$ to show that

    $$I = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\cos x}{\sin x + \cos x}\,dx.$$

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Hence find the exact value of $I$.

    (Hence)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Let $f$ be a function that is positive and continuous on the interval $(0, 1)$, and let

    $$K = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{f(\sin x)}{f(\sin x) + f(\cos x)}\,dx.$$

    Prove that $K = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

    (Prove)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    (i) Hence evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{1}{1 + \tan^3 x}\,dx$.

    (ii) State, with a reason, whether $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin x}{\sin x + \cos x + 1}\,dx$ is greater than, equal to, or less than $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Consider the definite integral $$I = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin x}{\sin x + \cos x}\,dx.$$ The integrand has an elementary antiderivative, but obtaining it is lengthy and the result is not obviously the simplest route to the value of $I$. (a) Use the substitution $u = \dfrac{\pi}{2} - x$ to show that $$I = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\cos x}{\sin x + \cos x}\,dx.$$ [3 marks] (Show that) (b) Hence find the exact value of $I$. [3 marks] (Hence) (c) Let $f$ be a function that is positive and continuous on the interval $(0, 1)$, and let $$K = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{f(\sin x)}{f(\sin x) + f(\cos x)}\,dx.$$ Prove that $K = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$. [5 marks] (Prove) (d) (i) Hence evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{1}{1 + \tan^3 x}\,dx$. (ii) State, with a reason, whether $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin x}{\sin x + \cos x + 1}\,dx$ is greater than, equal to, or less than $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$. [4 marks] (Evaluate)
Reveal the answer

(a) With $u = \dfrac{\pi}{2} - x$, $dx = -du$; when $x = 0$, $u = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, and when $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, $u = 0$ (M1). Also $\sin x = \sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2} - u\right) = \cos u$ and $\cos x = \cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2} - u\right) = \sin u$ (A1). Hence
$$I = \int_{\pi/2}^{0} \frac{\cos u}{\cos u + \sin u}(-du) = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\cos u}{\cos u + \sin u}\,du \quad (A1),$$
and renaming the dummy variable $u$ as $x$ gives the required result.

(b) Adding the two expressions for $I$ (M1),
$$2I = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin x + \cos x}{\sin x + \cos x}\,dx = \int_0^{\pi/2} 1\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad (A1),$$
so $I = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$ (A1).

(c) Apply the same substitution, $u = \dfrac{\pi}{2} - x$, to $K$ (M1). Since $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are exchanged, and the expression is symmetric in its two arguments in the sense that the denominator is their sum,
$$K = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{f(\cos x)}{f(\cos x) + f(\sin x)}\,dx \quad (A1).$$
Adding the two expressions for $K$ (M1),
$$2K = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{f(\sin x) + f(\cos x)}{f(\sin x) + f(\cos x)}\,dx = \int_0^{\pi/2} 1\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad (A1),$$
the numerator and denominator being identical for every $x$. Hence $K = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$ (A1). The hypotheses matter: positivity of $f$ guarantees that the denominator is never zero, so the integrand is defined throughout the interval, and continuity guarantees that $K$ exists. Nothing about the particular form of $f$ was used, which is why the result holds for every such $f$.

(d)(i) Write $\tan^3 x = \dfrac{\sin^3 x}{\cos^3 x}$, so (M1)
$$\frac{1}{1 + \tan^3 x} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{\sin^3 x}{\cos^3 x}} = \frac{\cos^3 x}{\cos^3 x + \sin^3 x}.$$
This is the integrand of part (c) with $f(t) = t^3$, which is positive and continuous on $(0, 1)$ (A1), so by part (c) the integral equals $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ (A1).

(d)(ii) It is less than $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$. For $0 < x < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ both $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are positive, so adding $1$ to the denominator strictly decreases the integrand: $\dfrac{\sin x}{\sin x + \cos x + 1} < \dfrac{\sin x}{\sin x + \cos x}$ pointwise on the interval of integration (M1), and therefore the integral is strictly less than $I = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$ (A1). The result of part (c) does not apply here, because the denominator is not the sum of $f(\sin x)$ and $f(\cos x)$ for any $f$ -- the extra term $1$ is not of that form -- so the symmetry argument that gives $\pi/4$ is unavailable. Numerically the integral is approximately $0.4388$, against $\dfrac{\pi}{4} \approx 0.7854$.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for the substitution with correctly transformed limits (or for reversing the limits and absorbing the minus sign), (A1) for $\sin x \leftrightarrow \cos u$ and $\cos x \leftrightarrow \sin u$, (A1) for the final integral. Candidates who omit the change of limits but otherwise proceed correctly may earn (M1)(A1) only.
(b) Award (M1) for adding the two integrals -- this is the idea of the question, and no credit is available for attempting the antiderivative. (A1) for the integrand simplifying to 1 and the value $\pi/2$, (A1) for $I = \pi/4$.
(c) Award (M1) for applying the substitution to the general integrand, (A1) for the exchanged form, (M1) for adding, (A1) for the numerator equalling the denominator, (A1) for the conclusion. Credit a correct argument that observes $g(x) + g(\pi/2 - x) = 1$ for the integrand $g$, so that the average value of $g$ over the interval is $1/2$; this is equivalent and should be marked in full. Do not penalise a candidate who fails to restate the role of the hypotheses, but do not award the final (A1) to a candidate who applies the result of part (b) to a particular $f$ and claims a general proof.
(d)(i) Award (M1) for rewriting in terms of sine and cosine, (A1) for identifying $f(t) = t^3$ and checking that it satisfies the hypotheses, (A1) for the value $\pi/4$.
(d)(ii) Award (M1) for a valid comparison of the two integrands, (A1) for the strict inequality and the conclusion 'less than'. A candidate who states 'less than' with the comparison earns both marks; a candidate who states 'equal to $\pi/4$' by misapplying part (c) earns neither.

Why this question is hard

The substitution in part (a) is unusual because it does not help. Every substitution a candidate has met before has been chosen to make an integral simpler; this one leaves the integrand looking exactly as hard as it was, with $\sin$ and $\cos$ swapped. The instinct at that point is to decide the substitution was the wrong one and start again. What has actually happened is that the integral has been paired with a second integral of the same value, and the pair is trivial where the single member was not.

Part (c) is where the item stops being a trick and becomes a result. The argument does not use anything about $f$ beyond positivity and continuity, and the proof is word for word the proof of part (b) with $f$ inserted. A candidate who understands that has generalised; a candidate who has only followed the steps in (a) and (b) will try to prove (c) by choosing a convenient $f$, or by repeating the substitution without noticing that the same argument is running.

Part (d)(ii) is the check on whether the generalisation was understood or merely accepted. The extra $1$ in the denominator is not of the form $f(\cos x)$ for any function of the cosine alone -- or rather, it is $f(t) = 1$ added to the denominator rather than to one of the two terms, and the sum $f(\sin x) + f(\cos x)$ is no longer the denominator. Candidates reach for $\pi/4$ there because that is what the previous part produced, and the discrimination is entirely in the reason: a pointwise comparison of integrands settles it in one line, and it settles it the other way.

This is adapted from the UK A-level treatment of integration by substitution, where the reflection substitution and the resulting 'integral equals its own twin' argument appear as a standard exercise. The A-level version normally stops at part (b). Adding the general proof in (c) and the counter-example in (d)(ii) is what makes the item discriminate, and both are new.

Challenge lever
The substitution $u = \pi/2 - x$ does not simplify the integrand: it exchanges $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ and returns an integral of exactly the same difficulty. The item only closes for a candidate who treats the transformed integral as an object to be combined with the original rather than evaluated, and then recognises that the generalisation is a statement about the substitution rather than about the particular functions involved.
