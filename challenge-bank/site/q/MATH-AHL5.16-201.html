5.16 A definite integral whose value is independent of the exponent, by a symmetry substitution

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.16-201

Topic 5: CalculusAHL 5.16 (integration by substitution) with SL 5.11 (definite integrals)P3 · Section B13 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatShow thatHence

Question

For $n \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$ define $I_{n} = \displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \frac{1}{1 + \tan^{n}x}\,dx$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    By using the substitution $x \mapsto \dfrac{\pi}{2} - x$, show that $I_{1} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Show that $I_{n} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$ for every positive integer $n$, so that the value does not depend on the exponent.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Hence evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin^{5}x}{\sin^{5}x + \cos^{5}x}\,dx$, justifying the identification you make.

    (Hence)

Total: 13 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

(a) By using the substitution $x \mapsto \dfrac{\pi}{2} - x$, show that $I_{1} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$. [4 marks] (Show that) (b) Show that $I_{n} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$ for every positive integer $n$, so that the value does not depend on the exponent. [5 marks] (Show that) (c) Hence evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin^{5}x}{\sin^{5}x + \cos^{5}x}\,dx$, justifying the identification you make. [4 marks] (Hence)
Reveal the answer

(a) Put $u = \dfrac{\pi}{2} - x$, so $du = -dx$. When $x = 0$, $u = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, and when $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, $u = 0$ (M1). Also $\tan\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cot x = \dfrac{1}{\tan x}$ (M1). Therefore
$$I_{1} = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \frac{1}{1 + \tan x}\,dx = \int_{\pi/2}^{0} \frac{1}{1 + \cot u}\,(-du) = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \frac{1}{1 + \cot u}\,du$$

Simplifying the new integrand (M1):
$$\frac{1}{1 + \cot u} = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{\cos u}{\sin u}} = \frac{\sin u}{\sin u + \cos u}$$

Adding the two expressions for $I_{1}$:
$$2I_{1} = \int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\frac{\cos x}{\sin x + \cos x} + \frac{\sin x}{\sin x + \cos x}\right)dx = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} 1\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2} \tag{AG}$$

so $I_{1} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

(b) The same substitution applies to any $n$ (M1). Writing $c$ for $\cos x$ and $s$ for $\sin x$:
$$I_{n} = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \frac{1}{1 + \tan^{n}x}\,dx = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \frac{\cos^{n}x}{\cos^{n}x + \sin^{n}x}\,dx$$

Under $x \mapsto \dfrac{\pi}{2} - x$ the sine and cosine exchange roles, so (M1)
$$I_{n} = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin^{n}x}{\sin^{n}x + \cos^{n}x}\,dx \tag{M1}$$

Adding the two equal expressions for the same number $I_{n}$:
$$2I_{n} = \int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\frac{\cos^{n}x}{\cos^{n}x + \sin^{n}x} + \frac{\sin^{n}x}{\sin^{n}x + \cos^{n}x}\right)dx = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} 1\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2} \tag{A1}$$

Both denominators are the same expression, so the two fractions sum to $1$ at every $x$ in the interval. Hence
$$I_{n} = \frac{\pi}{4} \quad\text{for every } n \in \mathbb{Z}^{+} \tag{AG}$$

(c) Divide numerator and denominator by $\cos^{5}x$, which is non-zero on $\left[0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$ (M1):
$$\frac{\sin^{5}x}{\sin^{5}x + \cos^{5}x} = \frac{\tan^{5}x}{\tan^{5}x + 1} = \frac{1}{1 + \cot^{5}x}$$

That is exactly the integrand of $I_{5}$ after the symmetry substitution, and by part (b) its value is independent of the exponent (M1). So (A1)(A1)
$$\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin^{5}x}{\sin^{5}x + \cos^{5}x}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{4}$$

The identification is legitimate because part (b) established the value for every positive integer exponent, not merely for $n = 5$; the exponent is irrelevant to the result, which is why no antiderivative is ever needed.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for the substitution and the reversal of the limits, M1 for the identity $\tan(\pi/2 - x) = \cot x$, M1 for simplifying the new integrand, AG for $\pi/4$. A candidate who evaluates the integral directly by writing $\tan x = \sin x/\cos x$ and substituting $v = \cos x$ will obtain $\ln$-type expressions that cancel; accept this route if the cancellation is shown, but note that it does not generalise and therefore cannot support (b).

(b) M1 for applying the same substitution to general $n$, M1 for the exchange of $\sin^{n}$ and $\cos^{n}$, M1 for recognising that both denominators are the same expression, A1 for the sum being $1$, AG for $\pi/4$. The critical step is that the two fractions share a denominator; a candidate who does not notice this will not see why the sum is $1$ and cannot score the AG.

(c) M1 for dividing by $\cos^{5}x$, M1 for identifying the integrand as one of the $I_{n}$, A1 for the value, A1 for the justification that the exponent is irrelevant. A candidate who evaluates $I_{5}$ by a direct numerical method and reports $\pi/4$ should be given the value marks but not the justification marks, because the part is about the identification.

Forfeits: assuming the symmetry substitution requires $n$ to be even, or claiming the result only holds for $n = 1$; treating $\tan^{n}$ as undefined at $\pi/2$ and concluding the integral diverges, when the integrand is bounded by $1$ throughout. Condonation: a candidate who writes $\pi/4$ as $0.785$ should be given full credit if the exact form also appears.

Why this question is hard

The item is a proof, and the proof is the point. The integrand has no elementary antiderivative for general $n$, and it gets less tractable as $n$ grows, so a student who begins by looking for one has already taken the wrong route. The way forward is to notice that the interval $[0, \pi/2]$ is symmetric under the reflection that exchanges sine and cosine, and that under that reflection the integrand becomes the complementary fraction with the same denominator.

The single step that makes the argument work is recognising that the reflected integrand shares its denominator with the original. Two fractions with the same denominator and numerators summing to that denominator add to one, so twice the integral is the integral of one, which is $\pi/2$. This is a genuinely different technique from substitution as a way of finding an antiderivative: here the substitution is used to relate the integral to itself, not to simplify the integrand in isolation. A student who has only met substitution as a tool for antidifferentiation may not think of it this way, which is why the item is set as problem-solving rather than as routine integration.

The independence of the exponent is the striking part, and part (b) makes it explicit rather than leaving it as an observation about $n = 1$. Part (c) then tests whether the student can move between the tangent form and the sine-and-cosine form, which is the same identification used in the proof and the practical skill that makes the technique applicable elsewhere. The result generalises further, to any pair of complementary functions, and a student who has followed the argument should be able to see why.
