Emergency department triage board

A hospital emergency department receives about 220 patients a day and the waiting room holds about 18 patients on average. On arrival each patient is assessed by a nurse and assigned a triage category from 1 to 5, where 1 is the most urgent. The triage board software must support four operations: adding a patient on arrival; removing and returning the most urgent patient when a doctor becomes free; escalating the category of a patient who is already waiting because their condition has deteriorated; and removing a patient who has left without being seen. The department has had an incident in which a category 3 patient waited behind a category 2 patient whose condition had in fact improved, and the board is being rewritten.

Question

You are to design the data structure and the classes behind the new triage board. The four required operations and the operating figures are given in the case study.

  1. [5 marks](a)

    Evaluate an unsorted array, an array kept sorted by category, and a binary max-heap as the underlying structure for the waiting list, referring to each of the four required operations in each case.

    (Evaluate)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Construct Python class definitions for the two classes you would use to model a patient and the waiting list. Include the attributes, the constructor, and the signatures and a one-line comment for each of the four required operations.

    (Construct)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    A patient who leaves without being seen cannot be removed from a heap directly, because their record may sit in the middle of the array. Justify whether the system should remove such a patient immediately or mark them as departed and skip them when they reach the front of the queue.

    (Justify)
  4. [5 marks](d)

    Using the operating figures, determine the total number of comparisons needed for one day of insertions under the unsorted-array design and under the binary-heap design, and determine which structure you would recommend for this department.

    (Determine)

Reveal the answer

(a) *Unsorted array.* Adding a patient is $O(1)$, simply appending. (A1) Producing the most urgent patient requires scanning all $n$ entries, so it is $O(n)$; at $n=18$ that is 18 comparisons every time a doctor becomes free. Escalation is $O(n)$ to find the patient and then $O(1)$ to change the category. Removal of a departed patient is $O(n)$ to find and $O(n)$ to close the gap. (A1)

*Sorted array.* Producing the most urgent patient is $O(1)$, taking the head. (A1) Insertion is $O(n)$ because on average $n/2$ entries must be shifted to make room, and escalation is worse still: the patient must be found, removed and reinserted at a new position. The structure optimises exactly the operation that is already cheap and pays for it on the two that are not. (A1)

*Binary max-heap.* Producing the most urgent patient is $O(1)$ at the root and $O(\log n)$ to restore the heap after removal; insertion is $O(\log n)$. (A1) Escalation is $O(\log n)$ if the position of the patient is known, but $O(n)$ if it is not, because a heap offers no search. Removal of a departed patient has the same difficulty. The heap is therefore the best of the three for the two operations that dominate, and its weakness is that it cannot find an arbitrary entry quickly. (R1)

(M1)(A1)(A1) The two classes are related by composition: TriageQueue holds a collection of Patient objects and does not inherit from them. Encapsulation is respected because the heap array and the index map are private to TriageQueue, so no caller can corrupt the invariant that the index map and the array agree. (A1)

(c) Marking and skipping is the right choice. (A1) Immediate removal costs $O(n)$ to locate the patient and then a sift to restore the heap, and it happens eleven times a day, which is not a large cost in absolute terms. (A1) The real argument is not performance but integrity and audit: a patient who has left is still a patient who attended, and the department needs the record for its own statistics and for any later complaint about waiting times. Flagging the record and discarding it when it surfaces at the root removes the patient from clinical consideration immediately, which is what the board is for, while keeping the fact of the attendance. (M1)(A1) The only cost is that the heap retains stale entries, so the occupancy figure shown on the board must be derived by counting non-departed records rather than from the length of the array. (R1)

(d) Under the unsorted-array design each insertion is $O(1)$, so insertion costs essentially nothing; but the design is unusable because producing the most urgent patient costs 18 comparisons each time. (A1) Comparing the insertion work where it differs meaningfully: a sorted array needs on average $\dfrac{n}{2}=9$ shifts per insertion, so $220\times9=1980$ element moves per day. (M1)(A1) A binary heap needs at most $\log_2 18=4.17$ comparisons per insertion, so $220\times4.17\approx917$ comparisons per day, and removals cost at most $2\log_2 18$ each, giving about 1835 comparisons for the day's removals. (A1) I recommend the binary heap with a hash-based index from patient identifier to heap position. The absolute numbers here are small enough that any of the designs would function at this scale, so the recommendation rests on the shape of the growth rather than on today's figures: the heap keeps both insertion and removal logarithmic, and the index reduces escalation from $O(n)$ to $O(\log n)$, which is the operation the department's incident showed it gets wrong. (R1)

Markscheme notes

In (a) require all three structures to be addressed against all four operations; a candidate who discusses only insertion and removal earns at most three of the five marks. In (b) accept Java or pseudocode of equivalent quality, and accept a design with three classes if the third is justified; the marks are for attributes that support the four operations, a constructor, and composition rather than inheritance between the queue and the patient. Do not require working implementations of the heap methods, but do require signatures and a correct complexity comment for each. In (c) credit either answer provided it is justified, but the justification must engage with the audit requirement; a purely performance-based argument earns at most two marks. In (d) allow 900 to 920 comparisons for the heap and 1900 to 2000 for the sorted array, and note that the recommendation mark is awarded for reasoning about growth and about the escalation operation, not merely for repeating the arithmetic. If a candidate writes a complete working heap implementation in (b), credit it but do not award extra marks beyond the five available: the part asks for a design, and a long listing is often a way of avoiding the complexity comments that carry the marks. Conversely, a candidate who gives only signatures with no complexity annotation has answered a different question and should be capped at three.

Why this question is hard

The item is built so that the fashionable answer and the correct answer diverge. A candidate who has memorised that heaps give O(log n) insertion and O(1) access to the maximum will recommend a heap in (a) and stop; the fourth operation, escalation, is the one that discriminates, because a heap has no search and an escalation request therefore costs O(n) unless an auxiliary index is added. Part (d) then removes the usual escape route: at n = 18 all three designs are fast enough, so a recommendation justified only by the arithmetic is worthless, and the candidate has to argue from growth and from which operation the department actually gets wrong. Part (c) is the second discriminator and works in the opposite direction: the performance answer says delete immediately, the engineering answer says flag and skip, because the record still has to exist for audit even though the patient must leave the queue at once. A strong answer notices that the two parts pull against each other and says so. A useful diagnostic while marking is whether the candidate mentions growth at all in (d). At eighteen patients every design is instant, so an answer that recommends a heap purely because 917 is less than 1980 has mistaken the exercise: the department will not shrink, and the incident in the case study was an escalation failure, which is the operation the plain heap handles worst. Candidates who notice that the index is what makes the heap acceptable, rather than the heap itself, have understood the item. Part (b) sits between them and is not decoration: a design that stores the category as a free integer with no tie-break rule will serve two patients with the same category in arrival order only by accident, and the department's incident was precisely about ordering within and between categories.
