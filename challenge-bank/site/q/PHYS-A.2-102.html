A.2 Forces and momentum

Physics HL · PHYS-A.2-102

A.2 (Forces and momentum: impulse, conservation of momentum) and experimental skills (cross-checking two measurements)P1 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 5technology: allowedCalculateDetermineSuggestEvaluate

Two measurements of the same impulse

A trolley of mass $1.20\ \mathrm{kg}$ runs on a level track and rebounds from a rigid barrier fitted with a force sensor. The force sensor was connected to a data logger that recorded the contact force every $10\ \mathrm{ms}$. Two light gates were also used: one measured the speed of the trolley immediately before it reached the barrier and the other measured its speed immediately after the rebound. The uncertainty in each speed measurement is $\pm 0.010\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and the mass of the trolley is known to $\pm 0.01\ \mathrm{kg}$. The force sensor records to the nearest $1\ \mathrm{N}$ and the timing of the data logger is accurate to $1\ \mathrm{ms}$. Neither instrument had been zeroed before the run.

Question

The light gates recorded $u = 0.850\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ towards the barrier and $v = 0.250\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ away from it. Take the direction away from the barrier as positive.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Use the force-time data to calculate the impulse acting on the trolley during the collision. State the method you use to find the area under the graph.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the change in momentum of the trolley from the light-gate readings, and estimate the uncertainty in this value.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine whether the two results agree within the combined uncertainty. Show your working and state your conclusion clearly.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Suggest a specific instrumental fault that would account for the discrepancy, justifying it from a feature of the data other than the area, and state what should have been done before the run to prevent it.

    (Suggest)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The impulse is the area under the force-time graph. With readings every $10\ \mathrm{ms}$ the trapezium rule gives (M1)

$$I = 0.010\left[\frac{1}{2}(1) + 10 + 27 + 38 + 34 + 19 + 8 + 3 + \frac{1}{2}(1)\right]$$

$$= 0.010\left[0.5 + 139 + 0.5\right] = 0.010(140) = 1.40\ \mathrm{N\,s}$$ (A1)(A1)

Counting squares would give the same result to within the resolution of the plot. The impulse acts away from the barrier, so it is positive. (A1)

(b) Taking the direction away from the barrier as positive, $u = -0.850$ and $v = +0.250\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, so (M1)

$$\Delta p = m(v-u) = 1.20\bigl(0.250-(-0.850)\bigr) = 1.20(1.100) = 1.32\ \mathrm{N\,s}$$ (A1)

Each speed contributes $m\,\delta u = 1.20(0.010)=0.012\ \mathrm{N\,s}$, and the two speeds are independent, so adding in quadrature gives $\sqrt{2}\,0.012 = 0.017\ \mathrm{N\,s}$. The mass uncertainty contributes $0.01/1.20 = 0.8\%$ of $1.32$, about $0.011\ \mathrm{N\,s}$, so the combined uncertainty is $\sqrt{0.017^{2}+0.011^{2}} \approx 0.02\ \mathrm{N\,s}$. (A1)

(c) The difference is $1.40-1.32 = 0.08\ \mathrm{N\,s}$. (M1) The uncertainty in the force-sensor result is dominated by the resolution of $1\ \mathrm{N}$ acting over the whole $80\ \mathrm{ms}$, giving about $1\times0.080 = 0.08\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ in the worst case, but the resolution errors partly cancel across the interval and a realistic estimate is about $0.03\ \mathrm{N\,s}$; combining with the $0.02\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ from (b) gives a combined uncertainty near $0.04\ \mathrm{N\,s}$. (A1) The discrepancy of $0.08\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ is therefore about twice the combined uncertainty, so the two results do not agree within it. (A1)

(d) The force sensor had not been zeroed: it reads $1\ \mathrm{N}$ at $t=0$, before the trolley has made contact, and $1\ \mathrm{N}$ again at $t=80\ \mathrm{ms}$, after contact has ended. (M1)(A1) A constant offset of $+1\ \mathrm{N}$ acting for the full $80\ \mathrm{ms}$ adds $1\times0.080 = 0.080\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ to the area, which is exactly the size of the discrepancy. (A1) The sensor should have been zeroed with no load applied immediately before the run, and a short section of data recorded before contact to confirm that the baseline was zero. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Accept the trapezium rule or counting squares; award the method mark for any sensible treatment of the end intervals. Accept $1.38$ to $1.42\ \mathrm{N\,s}$. A candidate who simply multiplies the peak force by the duration earns no marks. (b) Accept $1.32\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ and any uncertainty between $0.01$ and $0.03\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ that is justified. Watch the sign: a candidate who treats both speeds as positive has computed the wrong quantity and should not earn the momentum mark, though follow-through to later parts may still apply. (c) The mark is for comparing the discrepancy with a combined uncertainty, not for asserting agreement. Accept a conclusion of marginal disagreement; a candidate who says the results agree because both are 'about $1.3$' has not made the comparison. (d) The identifying feature is the non-zero baseline at both ends, not the value of the area; a candidate who proposes friction on the track should be asked what it predicts for the baseline, and should not be credited, since friction would not make the sensor read $1\ \mathrm{N}$ before contact.

Why this question is hard

Most impulse questions ask for one quantity by one method. This one asks for the same quantity twice and then asks whether the two answers agree, which is what experimental physics actually looks like and what students find hardest. The impulse-momentum theorem is being used as a consistency check, so the interesting part is not the calculation but the comparison, and the comparison is only meaningful once both uncertainties have been estimated.

The diagnosis in (d) is the real test. The force-time data do not return to zero: the sensor reads $1\ \mathrm{N}$ at the start, before there is any contact at all, and $1\ \mathrm{N}$ at the end, after contact has ceased. That is a hallmark of an instrument that has not been zeroed, and it is visible without computing anything. It is also quantitatively exactly right: a constant offset of $1\ \mathrm{N}$ sustained over the whole $80\ \mathrm{ms}$ contributes $0.080\ \mathrm{N\,s}$, which is the entire discrepancy. A student who only looks at the area sees a mismatch with no explanation; a student who looks at the shape of the data before integrating finds the cause immediately. The question is built so that the careless route, integrating first and investigating afterwards, produces a correct number and no understanding, while the careful route produces both.

There is a second lesson in the numbers, and it is about which of the two results to trust. The force sensor integrates cleanly and its area is reproducible, but it carries an offset that no amount of repeating the run will remove, because an offset is a calibration fault rather than a random one. The light gates are noisier individually but they measure the quantity the syllabus actually defines, and they have no reason to share the sensor's bias. When two methods disagree, the question is never 'which number looks better' but 'which method carries a systematic error the other does not', and that is the habit this question exists to build.
