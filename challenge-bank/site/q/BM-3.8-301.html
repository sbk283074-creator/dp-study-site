3.8 Capital rationing: the constraint binds only in combination, so the project with the highest net present value is not the one to choose

Verrano Marine Ltd: three projects and a capital limit

Verrano Marine Ltd builds small electric boats. The board has approved £120 000 of capital for new equipment this year and has decided that no further funds will be available for twelve months. Three projects have been appraised. Each is indivisible: it must be undertaken in full or not at all, and no project can be started in part.

All three net present values have been calculated using a cost of capital of 10% and the discount factors supplied with the appraisal. The finance director recommends Project A, on the grounds that it has the highest net present value of the three.

Question

The board must choose a combination of projects that fits inside the £120 000 limit. Answer every part, giving money to the nearest pound and ratios to three decimal places.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the net present value earned per £1 invested for each project, and rank the three projects by that measure.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine every combination of projects that fits inside the £120 000 limit, and the total net present value of each.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Identify the combination Verrano should choose, and calculate the net present value forgone if the finance director's recommendation to take Project A is followed. State how much of the £120 000 would be left unspent in each case.

    (Calculate)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Evaluate the decision to select the two smaller projects rather than Project A, considering at least one further financial factor and two non-financial factors.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Net present value per £1 invested: A, £40 000 ÷ £100 000 = £0.400; B, £25 000 ÷ £40 000 = £0.625; C, £34 000 ÷ £80 000 = £0.425 (M1)(A1). Ranked by this measure the order is B, then C, then A — the reverse of the ranking by net present value alone (A1).
(b) The combinations that fit inside £120 000 are: A alone, £100 000, £40 000; B alone, £40 000, £25 000; C alone, £80 000, £34 000; and B together with C, £120 000, £59 000 (M1)(A1). A with B would cost £140 000 and A with C £180 000, and all three would cost £220 000, so none of those is possible; the projects are indivisible, so no part of A can be funded with the money left after B (A1).
(c) The best combination is B together with C, with a total net present value of £59 000, which also uses the whole of the £120 000 (M1). If the finance director's recommendation is followed, Verrano takes A alone and earns £40 000, so the net present value forgone is £59 000 − £40 000 = £19 000 (A1). Taking A leaves £120 000 − £100 000 = £20 000 unspent, which is less than the £40 000 that B needs and less than the £80 000 that C needs, so the balance earns nothing (M1); taking B and C leaves £120 000 − £120 000 = £0 unspent (A1).
(d) The financial case is straightforward but narrower than it looks: the ranking by net present value per £1 is the right ranking only while capital is rationed, and it says B then C, which is also the best feasible combination (M1). A further financial factor is risk over time: B and C pay back in 1.8 and 2.6 years against 3.4 years for A, so the chosen combination returns the cash well inside the twelve months in which no further funds are available, and leaves the business able to re-appraise A next year with real cash rather than with borrowed money (A1). Against that, two non-financial factors pull the other way. Committing the whole £120 000 leaves no contingency: if C overruns, there is no reserve, whereas A leaves £20 000 unused and some flexibility (A1). And A is the hull moulding line, which is the strategic asset — it raises capacity for the whole product range and may be the project that a competitor could not copy, while a test rig and a set of jigs improve quality and fit-out rather than the product itself (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for dividing each net present value by its own investment, (A1) for all three ratios correct to three decimal places, (A1) for the ranking B, C, A. A candidate who ranks by net present value per £1 but then selects on the same basis without checking feasibility has only completed half of the method: the ratios rank projects, they do not by themselves produce a feasible set.
(b) (M1) for testing combinations rather than single projects, (A1) for the four feasible sets with their totals, (A1) for showing why A with B and A with C are excluded — the cost, not a preference. Credit a candidate who lists all seven subsets provided the infeasible ones are identified as such.
(c) (M1) for identifying B and C, (A1) for £19 000, (M1) for the £20 000 balance and the observation that it funds nothing, (A1) for the £0 balance on the chosen combination. Follow-through applies from an arithmetic slip in the ratios but not from a failure to test combinations at all.
(d) Accept any two developed non-financial factors; the ones named in the answer are contingency and strategic fit, and credit should also go to quality, staff skills, flexibility, or the effect on next year's capital budget. Do not credit 'A has the highest net present value' as an argument here: that is the recommendation under evaluation, and repeating it is not an evaluation.

Why this question is hard

Investment appraisal is taught project by project, and the decision rule that survives in students' notes is 'choose the highest net present value'. That rule is correct when capital is unlimited and wrong the moment it is not, and the two settings produce different orderings of the very same three projects. This item exists to make the difference visible and computable.

The structure is deliberately unhelpful to the obvious answer. Project A has the largest net present value, so the director's recommendation looks considered; it also leaves £20 000 idle, and the item is arranged so that the leftover is too small to fund the second-best project, which is what makes the mistake expensive rather than merely inefficient. The student has to enumerate what is feasible before ranking at all, and the answer to (c) — £19 000 forgone — is the price of skipping that step.

Part (d) stops the item from implying that the arithmetic settles everything. The chosen pair uses every pound and has the shortest paybacks, which cuts both ways: there is no contingency left, and the project with the strategic content is the one deferred. A student who can hold both of those at once has done the evaluation the syllabus asks for. Capital rationing is also temporary: if the limit lifts in twelve months the correct statement is that Project A is deferred rather than rejected, and the appraisal should be repeated next year against the cash the chosen projects will by then have returned.

Challenge lever
The capital limit is not breached by any single project, so it looks as though the choice is simply the best project. It binds only in combination: once A is chosen, the £20 000 left over is too small to fund anything, and the two projects that would together earn most of the available net present value are the two the director ranked second and third. The student has to enumerate the combinations before ranking them, because the ranking of single projects and the ranking of feasible combinations are different orderings of the same three items.

Adaptation
The source genre supplies two or three projects and asks which should be chosen, usually with capital available for all of them, so the ranking by net present value is decisive. Here a limit is imposed that no single project breaches, which is exactly the case in which the limit is easy to overlook: the item asks for the feasible combinations explicitly, and the numbers are chosen so that the highest-value single project is not in the best feasible set and leaves a balance too small to fund anything else.
Verification
Every figure was recomputed in Python. All seven non-empty subsets of the three projects were enumerated and their total investment compared with the limit, giving exactly four feasible sets; the best of them was confirmed to be B with C by direct comparison rather than by the ratio ranking, since the ranking by net present value per £1 is only a heuristic under indivisibility. The leftover after A was checked against both remaining investments and is smaller than each. The opportunity cost was confirmed two ways, as £59 000 − £40 000 and as the sum of the two forgone net present values less nothing.
