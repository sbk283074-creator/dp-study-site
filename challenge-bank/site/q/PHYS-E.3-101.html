E.3 Radioactive decay — data-based question

Physics HL · PHYS-E.3-101

Theme E: Nuclear and quantum physicsE.3 (Radioactive decay: detection of radiation, counting statistics) and experimental skillsP1 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 5technology: allowedCalculateSuggestExplainDetermine

Student investigation: count rate and distance from a source

A student places a sealed gamma source on a metre rule and a Geiger-Muller tube connected to a counter at a measured distance $d$ from it. At each distance she records the count over 100 s and divides by 100 to obtain the count rate. The background count rate was measured separately, over 600 s, as 0.40 counts per second, and has already been subtracted from the values in the table. The distances were set with a metre rule graduated in millimetres. One reading in the table is unreliable.

Question

The student wants to find the exponent $n$ in the relationship $C = \dfrac{A}{d^{\,n}}$, where $A$ is a constant.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    The count recorded at $d = 20.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ over 100 s was 10200. Assuming the counts follow Poisson statistics, calculate the percentage uncertainty in this count rate, and state what the uncertainty would have been had the count been recorded over only 10 s.

    (Calculate)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Suggest why the background count rate was measured over 600 s rather than 100 s, and explain why subtracting the background before analysing the data matters more at large distances than at small ones.

    (Suggest)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain how plotting $\lg C$ against $\lg d$ allows the exponent $n$ and the constant $A$ to be found, and state what the gradient and the intercept represent.

    (Explain)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the exponent $n$ and the constant $A$, making clear which point you excluded and giving the unit of $A$.

    (Determine)
  5. [3 marks](e)

    Evaluate whether the data confirm the inverse-square relationship. Identify the anomalous reading, suggest a plausible cause, and state what should have been done about it.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) For a Poisson count, the uncertainty in the number of counts is $\sqrt{N}$, so with $N = 10200$ the uncertainty is $101$ counts and the percentage uncertainty is $\dfrac{101}{10200} \times 100 = 0.99\%$, which is also $1/\sqrt{N}$ expressed as a percentage. (M1)(A1) Over 10 s the same rate would give $N = 1020$ counts, so the percentage uncertainty would be $1/\sqrt{1020} \times 100 = 3.1\%$. (A1) The uncertainty therefore improves only as the square root of the counting time, which is why long counts are needed for precision.

(b) The background rate is small, so it must be known precisely before it can be subtracted; a 600 s count gives $N \approx 240$ counts and hence a percentage uncertainty of about $1/\sqrt{240} = 6\%$, whereas a 100 s count would give only 40 counts and around $16\%$. (A1) Subtracting the background matters far more at large distances because the corrected rate there is small: at $d = 50\ \mathrm{cm}$ the measured rate is 16 per second, so a background uncertainty of even a few per cent is comparable with the quantity being measured, while at $d = 10\ \mathrm{cm}$ the rate of 403 per second makes the same background correction negligible. (A1)

(c) Taking logarithms of $C = A/d^{\,n}$ gives $\lg C = \lg A - n\,\lg d$, which is the equation of a straight line. (M1) The gradient is $-n$, so the exponent is found directly from the slope (A1), and the intercept on the $\lg C$ axis is $\lg A$, from which $A$ follows as $10^{\text{intercept}}$ (A1). This is the only straightforward way to test the value of an exponent, since a direct plot of $C$ against $d$ is a curve whose shape is very hard to judge by eye.

(d) The point at $d = 30.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ sits clearly above the trend and must be excluded from the fit (R1); part (e) gives the reason. Using the remaining six points, a least-squares fit of $\lg C$ against $\lg d$ gives a gradient of $-1.99$ and an intercept of $4.60$. (M1) Hence $n = 1.99 \approx 2.0$ (A1) and $A = 10^{4.60} = 4.0 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}\ cm^{2}}$, with the unit fixed by the requirement that $A/d^2$ has the unit of a count rate. (A1) A hand-drawn line will give $n$ in the range $1.9$ to $2.1$ and $A$ in the range $3.5$ to $4.5 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}\ cm^2}$; all such values should be credited.

(e) With the anomalous point removed, the remaining six points lie on a straight line of gradient $-2.0$ to within the counting uncertainties, so the data do confirm the inverse-square relationship. (A1) The anomalous reading is at $d = 30.0\ \mathrm{cm}$: the fitted relationship predicts $C = 4.0 \times 10^4/30^2 = 44\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, whereas $57\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ was recorded, roughly $28\%$ high and many times the $1.5\%$ counting uncertainty at that rate. (M1) A plausible cause is that the source was not properly seated against the rule for that reading, so the true distance was smaller than 30.0 cm. The student should have rechecked the alignment and repeated the measurement immediately rather than carrying a suspicious value forward into the analysis. (A1)

Markscheme notes

In (a) accept any value from 0.98 to 1.0 per cent for the 100 s count and 3.0 to 3.2 per cent for the 10 s count; the key relationship being tested is that the uncertainty scales as one over the square root of the number of counts. In (b) the first mark is for linking a longer background count to a smaller percentage uncertainty; the second must refer explicitly to the corrected rate being small at large distances, and 'because the count rate is smaller' on its own is not sufficient. In (c) award the first mark for the logarithmic transformation, the second for the gradient being minus n, and the third for the intercept being the logarithm of A. In (d) allow follow-through from a gradient that includes the anomalous point only if the candidate comments on the resulting poor fit; a silent inclusion earns no reasoning mark. The unit of A must be consistent with the unit of d used, and a candidate who works in metres should have A of order 4.0 per second per square metre, which is equally acceptable. In (e) the anomaly must be identified by its distance, and 'the source moved' is acceptable only if the direction of the resulting error is stated correctly.

Why this question is hard

Two ideas make this item hard, and both are examined regularly in Paper 1B. The first is that an exponent cannot be read off a curve: a plot of count rate against distance looks like 'some kind of decay' for any inverse power, and only a logarithmic plot turns the exponent into a number that can be measured and compared with a prediction. The second is that the uncertainty here is not written on an instrument but has to be derived from the counts themselves, which is why part (a) asks for it before any graph is drawn; a candidate who does not realise that precision improves only as the square root of counting time will not see why the 100 s count was worth the trouble. The seeded anomaly at 30 cm is placed where it does the most damage: it is in the middle of the range, so it pulls a hand-drawn line without producing an obviously wild point, and because the counting statistics at that rate are good, the discrepancy cannot be waved away as random scatter. The final mark is reserved for the experimental response — repeating the measurement then and there, rather than deleting a point afterwards — because that is the habit the question exists to reward. It is also worth being explicit about why the background has to be removed before the logarithms are taken. Subtracting a constant from C is not the same operation as subtracting a constant from the logarithm of C, so a candidate who plots the raw count rates and tries to correct the result afterwards obtains a gradient that drifts steadily with distance and will conclude that the exponent itself is changing. The data here are given already corrected, so the log-log plot is clean, and the underlying point is tested directly in part (b) instead, where it can be assessed on its own.
