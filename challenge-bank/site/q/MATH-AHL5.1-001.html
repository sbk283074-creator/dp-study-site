The two-sided limit that disappears because the one-sided limits disagree

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.1-001

SL 5.1 (introduction to the concept of a limit; one-sided limits and limits that do not exist)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedExplainShow thatShow thatDetermine

Question

Consider the function $f(x)=\frac{x^{2}-1}{|x-1|}$ defined for all real $x$ except $x=1$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain why $f(1)$ cannot be evaluated directly, and state what this means for the value of the function at $x=1$.

    (Explain)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    For $x>1$, show that $f(x)$ simplifies to $x+1$ and hence state the right-hand limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $1$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    For $x<1$, show that $f(x)$ simplifies to $-(x+1)$ and hence state the left-hand limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $1$.

    (Show that)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine whether the two-sided limit $\lim_{x\to 1}f(x)$ exists, justifying your answer using the one-sided limits.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Substituting $x=1$ gives $f(1)=\frac{0}{0}$, which is undefined because division by zero is not permitted (M1), so the function has no value at that point (A1) and cannot be evaluated by direct substitution (A1). A zero denominator means the algebraic expression has no numerical value at $x=1$, so the formula breaks down exactly where the limit is investigated (R1). The expression therefore fails precisely because the denominator vanishes there.

(b) For $x>1$ the quantity $x-1$ is strictly positive, so $|x-1|=x-1$ and $f(x)=\frac{x^{2}-1}{x-1}=\frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}=x+1$ (M1)(A1). As $x$ tends to $1$ from above, this linear expression tends to $2$, so the right-hand limit is $2$ (A1)(AG). The factor $x-1$ cancels legitimately because it is never zero on this side of $1$, leaving a well behaved linear function. This is why the one-sided limit exists even though the formula is undefined at the point.

(c) For $x<1$ the quantity $x-1$ is negative, so $|x-1|=-(x-1)$ and $f(x)=\frac{x^{2}-1}{-(x-1)}=-(x+1)$ (M1)(A1). As $x$ tends to $1$ from below, $-(x+1)$ tends to $-2$, so the left-hand limit is $-2$ (A1)(AG). The minus sign is forced by the absolute value on the negative side, because the denominator equals the opposite of the numerator factor. The left-hand limit therefore exists as a finite value distinct from the right-hand one.

(d) A two-sided limit exists only when the two one-sided limits agree (M1). Here the right-hand limit is $2$ and the left-hand limit is $-2$ (A1), and since $2\neq -2$ (A1), the two-sided limit does not exist (R1). The unequal one-sided limits are exactly what prevent the overall limit from existing, because a proper limit must approach the same value from both directions.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for recognising that substituting $x=1$ produces $0/0$ and that division by zero is forbidden, (A1) for stating the expression is undefined, (A1) for noting the function has no value at $x=1$, and (R1) for the conclusion that direct evaluation fails and says nothing about the surrounding limit.

(b) The (M1) is for using $|x-1|=x-1$ when $x>1$ and cancelling the common factor to reach $x+1$; the (A1) is for that simplified linear form. The second (A1) with (AG) is for the right-hand limit $2$, awarded only when the limit is explicitly evaluated as $x\to1^{+}$. Follow through from a different but consistent simplification is allowed.

(c) The (M1) is for the negative absolute value $|x-1|=-(x-1)$ when $x<1$ and the simplification to $-(x+1)$; the (A1) is for that form. The (A1)(AG) pair is for the left-hand limit $-2$. The minus sign is the most common omission and must be present for the mark.

(d) Award (M1) for the criterion that one-sided limits must agree, (A1) for quoting both limits $2$ and $-2$, (A1) for the inequality $2\neq-2$, and (R1) for concluding the two-sided limit does not exist.

Why this question is hard

This item targets the first genuine stumbling block in the limit concept: a two-sided limit requires both sides to approach the same value, and here they do not. The function is built around an absolute value, so its algebraic form genuinely changes at $x=1$, and the candidate must treat the two sides separately rather than substitute once and hope. The intended route is to recognise that $|x-1|$ equals $x-1$ above $1$ and $-(x-1)$ below $1$, factor $x^{2}-1$ as $(x-1)(x+1)$, cancel, and read the two one-sided limits as $2$ and $-2$. The final conceptual step is the key: because the two limits differ, the two-sided limit does not exist, and many students wrongly declare it to be $0$ or to equal the undefined value at the point itself. The classic wrong turn is to cancel the factor $x-1$ without considering the sign of the absolute value, which produces the single value $x+1$ and hides the left-hand limit entirely. Another common error is to evaluate $f(1)$ as something like $0$ and to claim the limit is $0$ because the numerator vanishes. The numbers were chosen so the two one-sided limits are clean integers, $2$ and $-2$, that are obviously unequal, making the non-existence of the two-sided limit unambiguous. The absolute value is the lever: it is what forces the split into two cases, and a candidate who ignores it cannot reach the correct answer no matter how carefully they compute. The whole point is the disagreement of the one-sided limits, and the absolute value is the device that produces it.
