A flow line that is cheaper per bench and dearer per year

Business management SL · BM-5.2-001

Unit 5: 5.2 Operations methods (job, batch and flow production; capital and labour intensity; factors influencing the choice of method). Toolkit: descriptive statistics and decision trees.P2 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4ExplainCalculateEvaluate

Hartswood Garden Furniture

Hartswood Garden Furniture makes wooden garden benches, tables and chairs at a workshop in Somerset. It produces 120 000 units a year across twelve product variants and sells them through garden centres and a mail-order catalogue. All of the products are currently made by batch production.

The best-selling product is a two-seat bench, which accounts for 48 000 units of the annual output. Sales of the bench are highly seasonal: 80% of the annual volume is sold in April, May and June, and the remaining 20% is spread evenly over the other nine months. The company's marketing manager estimates a 0.6 probability that annual bench sales will be 48 000 units next year and a 0.4 probability that they will be 30 000 units, depending on the weather.

A consultant has proposed that Hartswood install a flow production line for the bench. Under the present batch system the bench has annual fixed costs of EUR 180 000 and a variable cost of EUR 18.25 a unit. The proposed flow line would have annual fixed costs of EUR 480 000 and a variable cost of EUR 10 a unit, and would cost EUR 600 000 to install. It would have a capacity of 8 000 benches a month. It could make only the bench; the other eleven products would stay on batch production.

Because the flow line cannot be expanded, any output needed above 8 000 benches in a month would have to be made in advance and stored. Hartswood's warehouse can hold the finished benches, but storing them costs EUR 3 per bench per month, and the consultant expects benches to be held for an average of four months. The finance director has also pointed out that a flow line is difficult to change over, so any design change to the bench would be expensive and slow.

Question

Hartswood Garden Furniture must decide whether to invest EUR 600 000 in a flow production line for its best-selling bench.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain two reasons why the consultant believes flow production would be suitable for the bench, and one reason why the company's other eleven products make flow production difficult.

    (Explain)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Calculate the mean monthly demand for the bench and the demand in a peak month. Calculate the unit cost of the bench under batch production and under flow production at an annual volume of 48 000 units, and comment on the difference.

    (Calculate)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    Using descriptive statistics and a decision tree, evaluate whether Hartswood should invest in the flow production line. Justify your answer with reference to capacity, storage cost and the product range.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The consultant's case for flow production rests on volume and cost. The bench is a single, standardised product made in large numbers - 48 000 units a year - and flow production is designed for exactly this: a continuous sequence of operations with little or no changeover, which spreads a high fixed cost over a large output and gives a low variable cost. Second, demand for the bench is predictable in total, and the consultant argues that a dedicated line would remove the batch changeovers that currently interrupt production. (A1)(A1)

The eleven other products are the problem. Flow production is only economic when a line can run for long periods without being stopped and altered. Hartswood makes twelve variants in total, and a flow line for the bench cannot make tables, chairs or the other bench designs. Those products must stay on batch production, so the investment would improve the cost of one product out of twelve while leaving the rest of the factory unchanged. (A1)(A1)

(b) Mean monthly demand $= 48 000 / 12 = 4 000$ benches. (A1)

Peak demand: 80% of 48 000 $= 38 400$ benches are sold in three months, so peak monthly demand $= 38 400 / 3 = 12 800$ benches. (M1)(A1)

Unit cost at 48 000 units:

Batch: fixed cost per unit $= EUR 180 000 / 48 000 = EUR 3.75$; plus variable cost EUR 18.25, giving EUR 22.00 per bench. (M1)(A1)
Flow: fixed cost per unit $= EUR 480 000 / 48 000 = EUR 10.00$; plus variable cost EUR 10.00, giving EUR 20.00 per bench. (A1)

Comment: at 48 000 units flow production is EUR 2.00 a bench cheaper, a saving of EUR 96 000 a year, and the crossover point at which flow becomes cheaper is $(EUR 480 000 - EUR 180 000) / (EUR 18.25 - EUR 10.00) = 36 364$ benches. Since annual demand is 48 000, the arithmetic appears to favour flow - but this comparison ignores the constraint that the flow line's capacity of 8 000 benches a month imposes on a seasonal product.

(c) Indicative evaluation, using descriptive statistics and a decision tree.

The descriptive statistics show why the annual average is the wrong basis for the decision. Mean monthly demand is 4 000 benches, but peak monthly demand is 12 800 and off-season demand is about 1 067. The range is therefore about 11 733 benches a month. The flow line can make only 8 000 a month, so in each of the three peak months it is short by $12 800 - 8 000 = 4 800$ benches. Over the three months the shortfall is 14 400 benches, all of which must be made in advance and stored. (M1)(A1)

Storage costs EUR 3 a bench a month, and the consultant expects an average holding period of four months: $14 400 \times EUR 3 \times 4 = EUR 172 800$ a year. The EUR 96 000 saving from flow production is therefore more than wiped out, leaving flow production EUR 76 800 a year more expensive than batch at the higher demand level. (A1)

The decision tree makes the same point under uncertainty. Marketing estimates a 0.6 probability that demand is 48 000 and a 0.4 probability that it is 30 000.

Batch production: at 48 000, cost $= EUR 180 000 + EUR 18.25 \times 48 000 = EUR 1 056 000$; at 30 000, cost $= EUR 180 000 + EUR 547 500 = EUR 727 500$. Expected cost $= 0.6 \times EUR 1 056 000 + 0.4 \times EUR 727 500 = EUR 924 600$. (M1)(A1)

Flow production: at 48 000, cost $= EUR 480 000 + EUR 10 \times 48 000 = EUR 960 000$, plus storage of EUR 172 800, giving EUR 1 132 800. At 30 000, peak monthly demand is $0.8 \times 30 000 / 3 = 8 000$, which exactly equals the line's capacity, so no storage is needed and cost $= EUR 480 000 + EUR 300 000 = EUR 780 000$. Expected cost $= 0.6 \times EUR 1 132 800 + 0.4 \times EUR 780 000 = EUR 991 680$. (M1)(A1)

Batch production has the lower expected cost, by EUR 67 080, and the conclusion does not depend on the probabilities: flow is more expensive in the high-demand state and also more expensive in the low-demand state, so no plausible weighting reverses it.

The flow line would also commit EUR 600 000, would be difficult to change over, and could not make the other eleven products. Conclusion: Hartswood should not invest in the flow line. A stronger answer will note that the same analysis would justify revisiting flow production if the company could smooth demand, reduce the product range, or add a second line to raise peak capacity.

Markscheme notes

(a) Two marks for the consultant's reasoning (high volume of a standardised product; low unit cost) and two for the constraint created by the other eleven products. A candidate who says only 'flow production is cheaper' has not explained why it would be suitable and earns one mark.

(b) Award (A1) for the mean and (M1)(A1) for the peak monthly demand. The peak is the discriminating figure: a candidate who divides 48 000 by 12 and uses 4 000 as the monthly requirement has not understood the seasonality. Award (M1)(A1) for the batch unit cost and (A1) for the flow unit cost. The comment must note that flow is cheaper on unit cost alone and identify the crossover, but that the comparison ignores capacity.

(c) Award (M1)(A1) for the pre-build quantity of 14 400 benches and the storage cost of EUR 172 800; a candidate who omits the storage cost cannot earn the evaluation marks, because that omission is exactly the error the question is testing. Award the decision-tree marks for the two expected costs. A candidate who computes the expected costs without the storage cost will obtain EUR 888 000 for flow against EUR 924 600 for batch and conclude, wrongly, that flow is cheaper; award the method marks but not the evaluation marks, and credit the candidate if they notice the capacity constraint in words even without the full calculation. Credit any supported conclusion.

Why this question is hard

The item is built so that the obvious quantitative answer is the wrong one. At 48 000 units a year the flow line costs EUR 20 a bench against EUR 22 for batch, a saving of EUR 96 000, and the crossover point of 36 364 benches is comfortably below the annual volume. On that arithmetic the investment looks clearly worthwhile. The mistake is to compare costs at an annual volume when the constraint binds monthly.

The lever is that the correct operations method is determined by the volume per production run, not by the annual average. The bench sells 48 000 units a year - 4 000 a month on average - but 80% of that volume is sold in three months, so the peak month requires 12 800 benches and the flow line can make 8 000. The shortfall of 4 800 a month must be produced in advance and stored. Storage of 14 400 benches at EUR 3 a bench a month for an average of four months costs EUR 172 800, which is nearly twice the EUR 96 000 saving the flow line creates. The method that is cheapest per unit is not the method that is cheapest to run.

The decision tree reinforces the conclusion and shows that it is robust. Flow is more expensive in both demand states, so the recommendation does not depend on the 0.6 probability. That is a stronger result than a single expected-value calculation would give, and it is worth saying so.

The second constraint - the twelve product variants - is there to show that the arithmetic is not the only obstacle. Flow production is only economic when a line can run without changeover, and Hartswood's range means eleven products would remain on batch. The investment would improve the cost of one product out of twelve while adding EUR 600 000 of fixed cost.

Classic wrong turns: comparing unit costs at the annual volume and ignoring capacity; dividing annual demand by twelve and using 4 000 as the monthly requirement; computing the decision tree without the storage cost; and concluding that flow must be better because 'higher volume means lower unit cost'.
