Withdrawn consent against a model that was trained before the withdrawal, where deleting the records changes the corpus but not the model

Computer science HL · CS-A4.4-301

Theme A: Concepts of computer scienceA4.4 Ethical considerations (A4.4.1 discuss the ethical implications of machine learning in real-world scenarios, including accountability, consent, privacy and transparency, and the challenges posed by biases in training data)P1 · Section A12 marksdifficulty 5technology: not applicableDetermineExplainEvaluateState

Question

A clinic uses a machine-learning model to prioritise referrals. The model has been through two runs. The first trained a model from scratch on the whole corpus as it stood at that date. The second produced the model that is currently deployed by continuing to train the first model on records that had arrived since.

Patients consent to their records being used for research, and consent may be withdrawn at any time. A group of patients has now withdrawn consent, and their records must no longer be used. The clinic has deleted those records from the corpus and has asked for advice on what else, if anything, is required before the deployed model can be described as compliant.

The figure gives the dates of the two runs, the size of the corpus at each point, the number of records withdrawn, and how those withdrawn records are distributed between the two runs. The clinic intends to describe the deployed model as compliant once the deletions are complete.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Using the figure, determine the number of records the corpus holds immediately before the deletions and immediately after them, and determine how many of the withdrawn records were used by the run that trained the first model and how many by the run that produced the deployed model.

    (Determine)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine how many of the withdrawn records were never used in any training run at all, and explain what your answer implies for the two models that have already been produced. Your answer should state what deleting the records changes about those models and what it does not change.

    (Explain)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    The team proposes to continue training the deployed model on the remaining records and then to describe it as compliant. Evaluate that proposal. Your answer should state how many records a model retrained from scratch would be trained on, express the reduction in training data as a percentage of the corpus the deployed model was built from, explain why that percentage is small while the compliance question is not, and reach a judgement on which remedy the withdrawals require.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) Before the deletions the corpus holds the 48 000 records the first run trained on plus the 12 000 that arrived afterwards, which is 60 000 (A1). After the 900 withdrawn records are deleted it holds $60\,000 - 900 = 59\,100$ (A1).

The withdrawn records are not all in the same run. Of the 900, 620 had already been used by the run that trained the first model, because they were part of the 48 000. The remaining 280 arrived with the 12 000 and were used by the run that produced the deployed model (A1).

(b) The two runs between them used $620 + 280 = 900$ of the withdrawn records, so the number that were never used in any training run is $900 - 900 = 0$ (A1). Every withdrawn record contributed to at least one training run.

The implication is that both models were trained on data that must no longer be used. The first model was trained on a corpus that contained 620 of the withdrawn records, so its parameters were computed in part from them (A1). The deployed model was produced by continuing to train that first model, so it inherits everything the first model learned and adds what it learned from the remaining 280; its parameters are therefore a function of all 900 (A1).

Deleting the records changes the corpus and does not change either model. What it changes is what the clinic holds and what any future training run would be able to use. What it does not change is what the models already contain: a trained model is a set of parameter values, and those values were fixed at the moment the run finished. They do not refer back to the corpus, and they are not recomputed when the corpus changes. Removing a row from the corpus is therefore not the same operation as removing that row's influence from the model, and only the first of the two has happened (A1).

(c) A model retrained from scratch would be trained on the 59 100 records that remain, because a run that starts from an untrained model and uses only the current corpus would have no access to the 900 (A1).

The reduction in training data is $900/60\,000 = 0.015$, which is 1.5 per cent (A1). That is a small number, and the reason it does not settle the question is that it measures the wrong thing. It compares the size of the remaining corpus with the size of the old one, and it would only be relevant if the obligation were to avoid training on a large fraction of the data. The obligation is to avoid using the withdrawn records at all, and by that measure the withdrawn records are not 1.5 per cent of anything: all 900 of them were used, so 100 per cent of the withdrawn records are represented in the deployed model. A proportion can be made to look small by choosing a large denominator, and the denominator here is irrelevant to whether the obligation has been met (A1).

The proposal to continue training the deployed model on the remaining records does not meet it. Continuing to train starts from the deployed model's existing parameters, and those parameters already encode the withdrawn records. Further training on the remaining 59 100 records moves the parameters towards what those records imply, but it provides no mechanism that removes the influence the earlier records had on the point it started from. In the limit, training for long enough on the remaining records would reduce the dependence on the withdrawn ones, but the proposal does not say how long, and there is no measurement in the figure or in the proposal that would show when the influence had gone (A1).

The judgement is that the withdrawals require the model to be retrained from scratch on the remaining corpus, or rebuilt by a method that can demonstrate the removal of the withdrawn records' influence and is accepted as doing so (A1). Retraining from scratch is the remedy that follows from the evidence in the figure, because it is the only one of the two proposals whose training data can be stated exactly, and the statement is that it contains none of the 900. It is also the more expensive of the two, and the cost is the reason the proposal in the question was made. The clinic should also record what happened rather than only what was done, since the models were trained on the withdrawn data and any account of the deployed system that omits this is incomplete: accountability here means being able to say which corpus produced the model that is making decisions about patients, and that is a question about the past that deleting records now cannot answer (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) 3 marks. One mark for 60 000 before the deletions. One mark for 59 100 after them. One mark for splitting the 900 into 620 used by the first run and 280 used by the second. A candidate who gives 48 000 and 12 000 as the two corpora without combining them has read the figure but has not answered the question; award the third mark only if the 620 and 280 split is also given.

(b) 4 marks. One mark for zero, that is for establishing that every withdrawn record was used. One mark for stating that the first model was trained on 620 of them. One mark for stating that the deployed model inherits the first model's parameters and adds the 280, so it depends on all 900. One mark for the distinction between deleting records from the corpus and removing their influence from a model whose parameters were fixed when the run finished.

The fourth mark is the one that separates a candidate who has understood the mechanism from one who has understood the vocabulary. A candidate who says only 'the model must be retrained' without explaining why deletion is insufficient earns three marks.

(c) 5 marks. One mark for 59 100 as the corpus for a retrained model. One mark for the reduction expressed as 1.5 per cent, or as 0.015, of 60 000. One mark for the explanation that the percentage measures the size of the corpus rather than the compliance question, together with the observation that 100 per cent of the withdrawn records were used. One mark for explaining why continuing to train from the existing parameters does not remove the earlier records' influence. One mark for a judgement that names retraining from scratch, or an accepted method that can demonstrate removal of the influence, as the remedy the withdrawals require, and that acknowledges the cost or the difficulty of the choice.

Accept a candidate who argues for an approved machine-unlearning procedure, provided the answer states that the removal of influence has to be demonstrated rather than assumed. Do not accept a candidate who argues that deleting the records is sufficient, however the argument is framed, because part (b) has already established that every withdrawn record was used.

Accept either order for the 620 and 280 split. Accept 'retrain' without the phrase 'from scratch' only if the answer makes clear that the starting point is an untrained model and the training set is the remaining corpus.

Why this question is hard

The gap this item is built on is the gap between a dataset and a model trained on it. A dataset is a collection that can be edited: a row can be removed and the collection is then smaller. A trained model is a set of parameter values produced by a process that read the dataset once, and the values do not contain the dataset. They contain whatever the training process made of it. So an obligation that says 'do not use this record' cannot be discharged by editing the collection, because the use has already happened and its result is elsewhere.

The figure is built so that the arithmetic makes this concrete rather than theoretical. The withdrawal is split across the two runs, 620 and 280, so that neither model can be cleared by looking at one of them. The second run is a continuation of the first, so its dependence on the first run's data is not a matter of degree: the deployed model's parameters are a function of everything the first model saw. And the deletion is 1.5 per cent of the corpus, which is small enough that a team looking at the size of the change would conclude it had done something minor and sufficient.

The percentage is the trap. It is computed correctly and it answers a question nobody asked. The obligation is about a set of records, and the fraction of those records that were used is 100 per cent, which is the figure that matters. The item asks the candidate to hold both numbers at once and to say which one bears on compliance, which is the same discipline the guide asks for when it lists transparency and accountability alongside consent as ethical issues for machine learning.

The judgement is deliberately left with two acceptable routes. Retraining from scratch is the route the evidence in the figure supports directly, because the training set of the new model can be stated exactly and it contains none of the 900. An accepted unlearning method is the other route, and it is admitted only with the condition that removal of the influence has to be demonstrated, which keeps the item honest about a methodology that is still developing. What the item does not admit is the third route, which is to continue training and hope, and the reason is that there is no measurement in the problem that would show when the hope had been realised.

Nothing in the item requires knowledge outside the guide. Consent, accountability, transparency and privacy are all named in A4.4.1, and the machine-learning concepts used here, training and continued training, are the ordinary vocabulary of the topic rather than an additional technique.

Challenge lever
Deleting the records is necessary and it is not sufficient, and the two facts look like the same fact. The corpus after the deletion contains none of the withdrawn records, so a check of the corpus passes; the model was trained while they were still there, so a check of the model fails. The team's evidence and the team's obligation are about different objects, and the size of the deletion makes the gap easy to miss.
