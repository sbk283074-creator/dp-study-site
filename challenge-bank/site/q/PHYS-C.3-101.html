Measuring the wavelength of laser light with a double slit

A student directed a laser at a double slit and measured the fringe separation $s$ on a screen $L = 2.000\ \mathrm{m}$ away, for seven different slit separations $d$. For each Separation she used a travelling microscope to measure the distance across ten fringe spacings and then divided by ten. The microscope could be read to the nearest $0.01\ \mathrm{mm}$, and the student quoted each value of $s$ to $0.01\ \mathrm{mm}$ with an estimated uncertainty of $\pm 0.02\ \mathrm{mm}$, which she judged to be dominated by locating the centre of a fringe rather than by reading the scale. The theory predicts $s = \dfrac{\lambda L}{d}$.

Question

The data do not lie on a straight line when $s$ is plotted against $d$, and one point does not lie on the line even after the axes are chosen correctly. Both facts matter, and they have different consequences.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Describe what the student should plot in order to obtain a straight-line graph, state what the gradient of that line represents, and explain why plotting $s$ against $d$ would not be helpful.

    (Describe)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Tabulate values of $1/d$ and plot $s$ against $1/d$. Determine the gradient of the best-fit line and hence determine the wavelength of the laser light. Estimate the uncertainty in the wavelength by drawing the steepest and the shallowest straight lines consistent with the error bars on the plotted points.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine the intercept of your line on the $s$ axis and hence determine the zero error of the travelling microscope, explaining how a zero error on the instrument relates to the intercept of this graph.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Identify the anomalous row, show quantitatively that your suggested cause accounts for the recorded value, state what the student should have done when it occurred, and state whether the zero error from part (c) affects the value of the wavelength.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Since $s = \lambda L/d$, the student should plot $s$ against $1/d$. (M1) The gradient of the resulting straight line is $\lambda L$, so $\lambda$ is the gradient divided by $L$. (A1) Plotting $s$ against $d$ gives a curve of the form $s \propto 1/d$, from which a gradient cannot be read and an intercept cannot be interpreted. (A1)

(b) Tabulating $1/d$ in $\mathrm{mm^{-1}}$: $2.500,\ 2.000,\ 1.667,\ 1.250,\ 1.000,\ 0.800,\ 0.500$. (A1) The first point lies well above the line through the others, so it is excluded from the fit; the remaining six give a gradient of (M1)
$$\text{gradient} = 1.27\ \mathrm{mm^{2}}$$
(A1) Converting, $1.27\ \mathrm{mm^{2}} = 1.27 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$, and (M1)
$$\lambda = \frac{1.27 \times 10^{-6}}{2.000} = 6.33 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m} = 633\ \mathrm{nm}$$
(A1) For the uncertainty, the steepest line consistent with the error bars passes through $(0.500,\,0.66)$ and $(2.000,\,2.60)$, giving a gradient of $1.293\ \mathrm{mm^{2}}$, and the shallowest passes through $(0.500,\,0.70)$ and $(2.000,\,2.56)$, giving $1.240\ \mathrm{mm^{2}}$. (M1) The half-range is $0.027\ \mathrm{mm^{2}}$, so (A1)
$$\delta\lambda = \frac{0.027 \times 10^{-6}}{2.000} = 1.3 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m}$$
and $\lambda = 633 \pm 13\ \mathrm{nm}$. This is about $2\%$, and it is far larger than the scatter of the points about the line, which tells the student that the precision is limited by locating the fringes rather than by random scatter.

(c) The best-fit line through the six good points has an intercept of about $+0.05\ \mathrm{mm}$. (A1) Each value of $s$ was obtained by measuring a distance $D$ across ten spacings and dividing by ten. If the microscope has a zero error $e$, then $D_{\text{meas}} = 10\,s_{\text{true}} + e$, so (M1)
$$s_{\text{meas}} = s_{\text{true}} + \frac{e}{10}$$
The intercept is therefore $e/10$, giving $e = 0.05 \times 10 = +0.50\ \mathrm{mm}$. (A1) The microscope read $0.50\ \mathrm{mm}$ too high on every setting of the crosswire; that is, it did not read zero when the crosswire was at the reference mark.

(d) The anomalous row is $d = 0.40\ \mathrm{mm}$, where $s = 3.53\ \mathrm{mm}$; the line predicts $3.21\ \mathrm{mm}$. (A1) If the student had counted eleven spacings on that row but still divided by ten, then (M1)
$$s = \frac{11 \times 3.164 + 0.50}{10} = \frac{34.80 + 0.50}{10} = 3.53\ \mathrm{mm}$$
which reproduces the recorded value exactly. (A1) She should have re-counted the fringes and re-measured at the time, and recorded how many spacings each measurement covered; crossing out the point afterwards is not acceptable, and leaving it in the fit raises the gradient by about $9\%$ and gives $\lambda \approx 692\ \mathrm{nm}$. (A1) The zero error does not affect the wavelength: it adds the same constant to every value of $s$, so it moves the intercept and leaves the gradient, and hence $\lambda$, unchanged.

Markscheme notes

(a) Accept 'plot s against the reciprocal of d'; the mark for the gradient is for stating $\lambda L$ and not just $\lambda$. (b) Accept gradients from $1.25$ to $1.29\ \mathrm{mm^{2}}$ and wavelengths from $625$ to $645\ \mathrm{nm}$, provided the excluded point is named. A candidate who includes all seven points gets about $692\ \mathrm{nm}$ and should lose the gradient mark but can still earn the conversion mark on follow-through. (c) Accept intercepts from $0.03$ to $0.07\ \mathrm{mm}$ and zero errors from $0.3$ to $0.7\ \mathrm{mm}$; the reasoning that the intercept is one tenth of the zero error is the point, so credit it even if the arithmetic slips. (d) The quantitative check is what distinguishes an answer from a guess: a candidate must show that the eleven-for-ten count reproduces $3.53\ \mathrm{mm}$, and other causes should only be credited if they too are tested against the number. The final statement about the zero error must be a conclusion about the gradient, not an assertion that zero errors never matter. The uncertainty in (b) must come from the extreme-lines construction rather than from a formula the candidate has memorised; accept a half-range anywhere from $0.02$ to $0.04\ \mathrm{mm^{2}}$ and wavelengths quoted as $633 \pm 10$ to $633 \pm 20\ \mathrm{nm}$. The observation at the end, that the error bars give a larger uncertainty than the scatter does, is the point of the exercise and should be credited if offered.

Why this question is hard

The design of this item rests on a distinction that students find genuinely hard: not all experimental faults are the same kind of thing, and they do not all damage the result equally. A zero error on the travelling microscope adds the same amount to every distance reading, so it shifts the whole data set up by a constant and leaves the gradient untouched. Because the wavelength is obtained from the gradient, the wavelength survives the zero error completely intact. Students who have learned 'systematic errors invalidate the experiment' will say the opposite, and part (c) is built to catch exactly that.

The miscounted fringe spacing is the other kind of fault. It multiplies one row by a factor rather than adding a constant to all of them, so it does move the gradient, and it moves it a long way: including the bad point gives $\lambda \approx 692\ \mathrm{nm}$ instead of $633\ \mathrm{nm}$, an error of about $9\%$ from a single row out of seven. That is the more damaging fault, and it is the one that is easy to miss, because the point does not look obviously absurd on its own.

This is also why part (d) asks for a quantitative check rather than a plausible story. Plenty of causes could be invented for a point that sits high, and most of them would be wrong. The eleven-for-ten count does not merely sound plausible: it produces $3.53\ \mathrm{mm}$ to the last quoted digit, and no other simple account does. Requiring the candidate to show that arithmetic turns the item from an exercise in imagination into an exercise in evidence, which is the habit the data-based question exists to test. The final clause, about re-measuring rather than deleting, follows from the same principle: the point is evidence of what went wrong, and the right response to evidence is to go back and check, not to remove it.

Metadata

Challenge lever
Two different faults are seeded and they are not the same kind of fault. A constant zero error on the travelling microscope shifts every point equally and therefore leaves the gradient, and the wavelength, untouched; a miscounted fringe spacing at one slit separation scales a single point and does move the gradient. Distinguishing the two, and saying which one has to be dealt with, is the whole question.

Adaptation
Original item. The dataset was generated in Python from s = lambda*L/d with lambda = 632.8 nm and L = 2.000 m, a constant zero error of +0.50 mm applied to every distance reading (giving an intercept of +0.05 mm on s), and a miscount of eleven spacings for ten seeded into the d = 0.40 mm row; the value 3.53 mm emerges from that model rather than being chosen. The two-fault structure, one additive and one multiplicative, is the reason the pair was seeded together.
Verification
Dataset generated in Python from s = lambda*L/d with the seeded zero error and the seeded miscount; least-squares gradients computed both with and without the anomalous row, and the eleven-for-ten hypothesis checked against the recorded value to the quoted precision. The uncertainty in the gradient was recomputed from the two extreme lines through the +/-0.02 mm error bars at the ends of the plotted range, and compared with the standard error of the least-squares gradient, which is smaller by an order of magnitude.
