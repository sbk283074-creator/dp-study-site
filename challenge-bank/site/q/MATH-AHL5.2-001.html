The cubic whose derivative flips sign twice

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.2-001

SL 5.2 (increasing and decreasing functions; interpretation of f'(x))P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatDetermineFindFind

Question

Consider the cubic function $f(x)=x^{3}-3x^{2}-9x+5$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    By differentiating the cubic term by term, show that $f'(x)=3(x-3)(x+1)$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the intervals on which $f$ is increasing and the intervals on which $f$ is decreasing.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the coordinates of the stationary point at $x=-1$ and state its nature.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Find the coordinates of the stationary point at $x=3$ and state its nature.

    (Find)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Differentiating term by term using the power rule gives $f'(x)=3x^{2}-6x-9$ (M1), which factors as $3(x^{2}-2x-3)$ (A1) and then as $3(x-3)(x+1)$ because $-3$ and $1$ sum to $-2$ and multiply to $-3$ (A1)(AG). Expanding $3(x-3)(x+1)$ returns $3x^{2}-6x-9$, confirming the factorisation is correct (R1). The derivative is therefore confirmed in a fully factored form that exposes its roots directly. This factorised shape is what makes the later sign analysis straightforward.

(b) The derivative is zero at $x=-1$ and $x=3$, which split the real line into three intervals (M1), and its sign is positive outside these roots and negative between them (A1). Hence $f$ is increasing on $x<-1$ and on $x>3$, and decreasing on $-1<x<3$ (A1)(A1). The sign change is read directly from the factored form $3(x-3)(x+1)$ by testing the sign of each linear factor. The function therefore rises, then falls, then rises again.

(c) Substituting $x=-1$ into the original function gives $f(-1)=(-1)^{3}-3(1)-9(-1)+5=-1-3+9+5=10$ (M1)(A1), so the stationary point is $(-1,10)$ (A1). Since the derivative changes from positive to negative as $x$ passes $-1$, the point is a local maximum (R1). The stationary point is therefore a peak on the curve at that coordinate.

(d) Substituting $x=3$ gives $f(3)=27-27-27+5=-22$ (M1)(A1), so the stationary point is $(3,-22)$ (A1). Since the derivative changes from negative to positive as $x$ passes $3$, the point is a local minimum (R1). The stationary point is therefore a trough, and the cubic has completed its rise-fall-rise pattern. The steep drop of 32 units between the two turning points is a direct consequence of the positive leading coefficient and is easily understated.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for the derivative $3x^{2}-6x-9$, (A1) for factoring out $3$ to reach $3(x^{2}-2x-3)$, and (A1)(AG) for the fully factored $3(x-3)(x+1)$. The (R1) is for verifying by expansion or by quoting the sum and product of the roots. A candidate who expands correctly but does not factor earns only the (M1).

(b) The (M1) is for the two roots $x=-1$ and $x=3$. The (A1) is for the correct sign pattern, and the two final (A1) marks are for the increasing intervals $x<-1$ and $x>3$ and the decreasing interval $-1<x<3$. Accept interval notation with or without the endpoints included.

(c) Award (M1) for substituting $x=-1$, (A1) for the $y$-value $10$, (A1) for the point $(-1,10)$, and (R1) for identifying it as a local maximum from the sign change of $f'$. Follow through from an incorrect derivative is allowed.

(d) Award (M1) for substituting $x=3$, (A1) for $-22$, (A1) for the point $(3,-22)$, and (R1) for local minimum. Follow through from an incorrect derivative is allowed provided the nature matches the sign change.

Why this question is hard

This item is a standard but unforgiving exercise in reading the sign of a derivative. The cubic is chosen so that its derivative is a clean quadratic that factorises into $3(x-3)(x+1)$, giving two stationary points at $x=-1$ and $x=3$. The intended route is to differentiate, factor, locate the roots, then test the sign of the derivative in the three resulting intervals to decide where the function rises and falls. The point of the question is the double sign change: because the leading coefficient of $f$ is positive, the cubic eventually rises on both ends, so it must rise, fall and rise again, producing a local maximum followed by a local minimum. The classic wrong turn is to mis-factor the quadratic, for example writing $3(x-1)(x+3)$ with the constant in the wrong place, which moves the stationary points and inverts the intervals. Another error is to test the sign of $f'$ only at one point and assume the whole side behaves the same, missing the second root entirely. Candidates also confuse the nature of the stationary points, calling $x=-1$ a minimum because its $x$-value is smaller, or swapping the two $y$-values when stating the coordinates. The $y$-values $10$ and $-22$ were chosen so that the two stationary points are simple integers, and the factored form makes the sign chart immediate once the roots are found. The lever is the clean double sign change, which rewards a candidate who reasons from the factored derivative rather than from scattered point evaluations, and the steep drop between the two turning points is the feature most often understated.
