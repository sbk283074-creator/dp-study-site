E.4 Fission

Physics HL · PHYS-E.4-102

Theme E: Nuclear and quantum physicsE.4 (Fission)P216 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedCalculateDetermineCalculateExplain

Question

Each fission of a uranium-235 nucleus releases about $200\ \mathrm{MeV}$ of energy and, on average, $2.4$ neutrons. A power station produces $1.0\ \mathrm{GW}$ of electrical power at an overall efficiency of $33\%$. Take $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$, the unified atomic mass unit as $1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$, and the energy density of coal as $30\ \mathrm{MJ\,kg^{-1}}$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the energy released by a single fission in joules, and hence the number of fissions taking place each second in the reactor core.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Hence determine the mass of uranium-235 consumed in the core in one day, and the mass of coal that would have to be burned in one day to produce the same output.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Only about $0.7\%$ of natural uranium is the fissile isotope uranium-235. Determine the mass of natural uranium that must be mined each day to supply the core, assuming the enrichment process extracts all of the uranium-235 and discards the rest.

    (Calculate)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Explain why the uranium in a reactor cannot simply be assembled as a single lump of fissionable material, referring to the average number of neutrons released per fission and to what happens to those neutrons. Your answer should make clear why a moderator is needed in a thermal reactor.

    (Explain)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) One fission releases (M1)
$$200\ \mathrm{MeV} = 200 \times 10^{6} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J} = 3.20 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J}$$
(A1) The electrical output is $1.0\ \mathrm{GW}$, so at $33\%$ efficiency the thermal power of the core is (M1)
$$P_{\text{th}} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{9}}{0.33} = 3.0 \times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{W}$$
and the number of fissions per second is (A1)
$$N = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{9}}{3.20 \times 10^{-11}} = 9.4 \times 10^{19}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$$

(b) Each fission consumes one uranium-235 nucleus of mass $235 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 3.90 \times 10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$. (M1) In one day the number of fissions is $9.4 \times 10^{19} \times 86\,400 = 8.1 \times 10^{24}$, so the mass consumed is (M1)
$$m = 8.1 \times 10^{24} \times 3.90 \times 10^{-25} = 3.2\ \mathrm{kg}$$
(A1) The energy delivered in one day is $3.0 \times 10^{9} \times 86\,400 = 2.6 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{J}$, so the equivalent mass of coal is (M1)
$$m_{\text{coal}} = \frac{2.6 \times 10^{14}}{30 \times 10^{6}} = 8.6 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{kg} \approx 8600\ \mathrm{tonnes}$$
(A1) The ratio of the two masses is $\dfrac{8.6 \times 10^{6}}{3.2} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{6}$, so kilogram for kilogram the nuclear fuel releases something like three million times as much energy as the coal. This is the number that makes the comparison worth doing: it is far too large to be recovered by any plausible improvement in the efficiency of a coal plant.

(c) Natural uranium is $0.7\%$ uranium-235 by mass, so the mass of natural uranium containing $3.2\ \mathrm{kg}$ of the fissile isotope is (M1)
$$m_{\text{nat}} = \frac{3.2}{0.007} = 4.6 \times 10^{2}\ \mathrm{kg} \approx 460\ \mathrm{kg}$$
(A1)(A1) This assumes complete extraction. In practice the enrichment process leaves some uranium-235 in the discarded tailings, so rather more than $460\ \mathrm{kg}$ must be mined; if the tails assay is $0.2\%$ rather than zero, the requirement rises to about $630\ \mathrm{kg}$ per day. (A1)

(d) Each fission releases on average $2.4$ neutrons, so a chain reaction is possible in principle: one fission can initiate more than one further fission. (A1) It does not happen automatically, for two reasons. First, some neutrons escape through the surface of the material, and the fraction escaping is large when the lump is small because the surface-to-volume ratio is large; below the critical mass, too many neutrons are lost for the reaction to sustain itself. (A1) Second, and more importantly, the neutrons emerge from fission moving fast, with energies of the order of $1\ \mathrm{MeV}$, and the fission cross-section of uranium-235 is very small at those energies — a fast neutron is far more likely to be scattered or to be captured without causing fission than to induce one. (A1) A moderator, such as water or graphite, surrounds the fuel and slows the neutrons by elastic collisions with light nuclei until they reach thermal energies of a fraction of an electronvolt, at which the fission cross-section is hundreds of times larger and the chain reaction proceeds. (A1) Control rods, made of a material that absorbs neutrons strongly, are then used to remove exactly the surplus above the one neutron per fission needed to sustain the reaction, so that on average exactly one neutron from each fission causes another.

Markscheme notes

(a) Accept $3.2 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J}$ and $9.3$ to $9.5 \times 10^{19}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The efficiency conversion is the point of the part: a candidate who divides $1.0\ \mathrm{GW}$ by the fission energy without correcting for efficiency has answered a different question and should lose the second mark. (b) Accept $3.1$ to $3.3\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $8.5$ to $8.8 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{kg}$. Note that the mass consumed is the mass actually fissioned, not the mass loaded into the core, which is considerably larger. (c) Accept $450$ to $470\ \mathrm{kg}$; the tails comment is a bonus and should be credited if offered, but candidates are not expected to know the term. (d) Four distinct points are available: the possibility of a chain reaction because more than one neutron is released, the escape of neutrons through the surface and the existence of a critical mass, the low fission cross-section for fast neutrons, and the action of the moderator. Candidates who mention the moderator but not the cross-section have given the mechanism without the reason and should not receive full marks; the word cross-section need not be used, but the idea that fast neutrons do not readily cause fission must be present.

Why this question is hard

The two numbers in part (b) are the reason this item exists. A large power station fissions about three kilograms of uranium a day, and the same output from coal is eight thousand six hundred tonnes. Stated as raw figures they sound like propaganda; derived from $200\ \mathrm{MeV}$ per fission and a chain of unit conversions that the candidate performs themselves, they are simply arithmetic, and the factor of $2.7 \times 10^{6}$ between nuclear and chemical energy density becomes something the student has established rather than been told. Keeping both calculations running from the same fission energy is what makes the comparison trustworthy — there is no separate route to the coal figure that could be quietly adjusted.

Part (c) adds a detail that changes the picture in an interesting direction. Natural uranium is almost entirely uranium-238, which does not fission with thermal neutrons, so the $3.2\ \mathrm{kg}$ actually consumed has to be dug out of about $460\ \mathrm{kg}$ of ore-concentrate per day. That is still small next to eight thousand tonnes of coal, but it is a hundred times larger than the headline figure, and it is the reason enrichment exists as an industry. The assumption that extraction is complete is false and worth flagging, because the discarded material still contains uranium-235 and the tails assay is an economic as much as a physical choice.

Part (d) is the part that cannot be reached by arithmetic, and it is where candidates most often substitute the word 'critical mass' for an explanation. The chain reaction is possible because $2.4 > 1$, but $2.4$ is an average over all neutrons leaving all fissions, and it says nothing about how many of those neutrons go on to cause another fission. Most do not. Some escape through the surface, which is why geometry and size matter and why a critical mass exists at all. More importantly, most of the remainder are moving too fast to be captured efficiently: the fission cross-section of uranium-235 rises by orders of magnitude as the neutron slows, so a reactor without a moderator is not a reactor. That single fact explains why the moderator is there, why the fuel and the moderator have to be interleaved rather than separated, and why a loss of coolant can shut the reaction down rather than run away — a point worth making, since the intuition runs the other way.
