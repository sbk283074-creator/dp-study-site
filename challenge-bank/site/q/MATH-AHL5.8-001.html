The cubic with a maximum and a minimum found by the second derivative

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.8-001

SL 5.8 (local maximum and minimum points; use of f'(x)=0 and the second derivative test)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatFindDetermine

Question

Consider the function $f(x)=(x-1)^{2}(x+2)$, which expands to $f(x)=x^{3}-3x+2$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    By differentiating the expanded cubic form, show that $f'(x)=3(x-1)(x+1)$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the stationary points of $f$, giving their $x$-coordinates.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Use the second derivative $f''(x)=6x$ to classify each stationary point as a maximum or a minimum.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the coordinates of the local maximum and the local minimum.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Differentiate the expanded form $f(x)=x^{3}-3x+2$ to obtain $f'(x)=3x^{2}-3$ (M1). Factoring the result gives $3(x^{2}-1)=3(x-1)(x+1)$ (A1)(AG). This matches the required derivative exactly, because the constant term $2$ disappears when the function is differentiated and the remaining terms factor neatly. The factorised form is the most useful version of the derivative, because it displays the roots directly without any further algebra.

(b) Stationary points occur where the gradient is zero, so solve $3(x-1)(x+1)=0$ (M1). A product is zero when $x-1=0$ or $x+1=0$, giving the two $x$-coordinates $x=-1$ and $x=1$ (A1). These are the only real solutions, so they are the complete list of places where the curve could turn. Both $x$-coordinates must be found before any classification can begin, since the second derivative test is applied separately at each one.

(c) The second derivative here is the simple linear function $f''(x)=6x$ (M1). At $x=-1$ we have $f''(-1)=-6<0$, which means the curve is bending downwards, so the point is a local maximum (A1). At $x=1$ we have $f''(1)=6>0$, which means the curve is bending upwards, so the point is a local minimum (A1). This linear second derivative is unusually convenient, because its sign is fixed simply by the sign of $x$, and the sign of $f''$ alone decides the nature of each stationary point.

(d) Substitute the two $x$-values back into $f$. We have $f(-1)=(-1)^{3}-3(-1)+2=4$, so the local maximum is at $(-1,4)$ (M1)(A1). Also $f(1)=1^{3}-3(1)+2=0$, so the local minimum is at $(1,0)$ (A1). Recording both coordinates completes the description of the turning points and confirms the overall shape of the cubic, which is why the final coordinates are correct.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for the derivative $3x^{2}-3$ and (A1)(AG) for the factorised form $3(x-1)(x+1)$. Accept differentiation of the product $(x-1)^{2}(x+2)$ if it reaches the same result. A candidate who writes $3x^{2}-3$ but fails to factor it earns only the method mark and not the accuracy mark.

(b) Award (M1) for setting the derivative equal to zero and (A1) for both roots $x=-1$ and $x=1$. Follow through from an incorrect derivative is allowed provided the equation is solved consistently with it. Both roots are required for the later classification marks in part (c).

(c) Award (M1) for stating $f''(x)=6x$, (A1) for the local maximum at $x=-1$ using the fact that $f''(-1)<0$, and (A1) for the local minimum at $x=1$ using $f''(1)>0$. The second derivative test must actually be applied; a candidate who classifies from a sketch earns no credit here.

(d) Award (M1) for substituting into $f$ and (A1) for $(-1,4)$, then (A1) for $(1,0)$. A candidate who gives only the $x$-values forfeits the coordinate marks, and the values $4$ and $0$ must be computed, not read from a graph.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on a cubic that is given in both factorised and expanded form, and the whole point is to classify two stationary points using the second derivative test. The derivative of $x^{3}-3x+2$ is $3x^{2}-3$, which factors as $3(x-1)(x+1)$, so the stationary points are at $x=-1$ and $x=1$. The second derivative is the unusually simple $6x$, and its signs at the two roots are opposite: $f''(-1)=-6<0$ marks a local maximum, while $f''(1)=6>0$ marks a local minimum. The trap is to infer the nature of the turning points from the shape of a cubic sketch, which is unreliable and is not what the syllabus asks for here; the second derivative test is the required route. The intended method is to compute $f''(x)$, evaluate it at each stationary point, and read the sign to decide maximum or minimum. The factorised and expanded forms together let a candidate verify their derivative two ways, which is a useful check against sign errors. The classic error is to mix up the two points and call $x=1$ the maximum, or to forget that the squared factor $(x-1)^{2}$ means $x=1$ is a repeated root of the derivative yet remains an ordinary minimum of the cubic. Another slip is to evaluate $f$ at the wrong root and swap the coordinates $4$ and $0$. The numbers were chosen so the second derivative is linear and the function values at the roots are the small integers $4$ and $0$, which means a correct method gives exact coordinates while a careless one usually swaps them.
