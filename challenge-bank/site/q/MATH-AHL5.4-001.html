The tangent and normal to a curve that is not a polynomial

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.4-001

SL 5.4 (tangents and normals at a given point, and their equations)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatFindFindFind

Question

Consider the curve $y=2x^{2}-\frac{3}{x}$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that $\frac{dy}{dx}=4x+\frac{3}{x^{2}}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the coordinates of the point on the curve where $x=1$ and the gradient of the curve there.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the equation of the tangent to the curve at $x=1$.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Find the equation of the normal to the curve at $x=1$.

    (Find)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Writing $\frac{3}{x}=3x^{-1}$, the derivative is $\frac{dy}{dx}=4x-(-3x^{-2})=4x+3x^{-2}=4x+\frac{3}{x^{2}}$ (M1)(A1)(A1)(AG). The reciprocal term contributes $3x^{-2}$ because differentiating $-3x^{-1}$ raises the index from $-1$ to $-2$ and keeps the minus sign from the original term (R1). The derivative is therefore confirmed as a sum of a polynomial part and a reciprocal part. This formula is the foundation for locating both the tangent and the normal.

(b) At $x=1$, the $y$-coordinate is $y=2(1)^{2}-\frac{3}{1}=2-3=-1$ (M1)(A1), so the point on the curve is $(1,-1)$; the gradient there is $\frac{dy}{dx}=4(1)+\frac{3}{1^{2}}=4+3=7$ (A1). The gradient is found by substituting $x=1$ into the derivative just established (R1). The point and the gradient are therefore $(1,-1)$ and $7$, both of which are needed for the two line equations.

(c) Using the point-gradient form $y-y_{1}=m(x-x_{1})$ with $m=7$ and $(1,-1)$ gives $y+1=7(x-1)$, which rearranges to $y=7x-8$ (M1)(A1). The tangent shares the curve's gradient at the point of contact, so its slope is exactly $7$ (A1). The equation is therefore $y=7x-8$, a line that just touches the curve at that single point (R1).

(d) The normal is perpendicular to the tangent, so its gradient is the negative reciprocal $-\frac{1}{7}$ (M1)(A1), and its equation is $y+1=-\frac{1}{7}(x-1)$, which simplifies to $y=-\frac{x}{7}-\frac{6}{7}$ (A1). The normal's gradient is the negative reciprocal of the tangent's gradient, so their product is $-1$ (R1). The equation is therefore $y=-\frac{x}{7}-\frac{6}{7}$, a line that cuts the curve at right angles at the point.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for rewriting $3/x$ as $3x^{-1}$ and differentiating, (A1) for the intermediate $4x+3x^{-2}$, and (A1)(AG) for the final $4x+3/x^{2}$. The (R1) is for explaining the sign of the reciprocal term. The sign must be positive for full marks; a candidate writing $-3/x^{2}$ loses the reasoning mark. The reciprocal contribution is the main trap and must be shown explicitly.

(b) The (M1) is for substituting $x=1$ into $y$, (A1) for $y=-1$, (A1) for the gradient $7$ from the derivative, and (R1) for linking the gradient to the derivative. The point is $(1,-1)$. Both coordinates are required before either line can be written.

(c) Award (M1) for the point-gradient form, (A1) for $y+1=7(x-1)$, (A1) for $y=7x-8$, and (R1) for stating the tangent shares the curve's gradient. Accept equivalent forms such as $7x-y-8=0$.

(d) Award (M1) for the perpendicular gradient $-1/7$, (A1) for using point-gradient, (A1) for $y=-x/7-6/7$, and (R1) for the negative reciprocal property. Follow through from an incorrect gradient in (b) is allowed. Candidates who leave the answer in unsimplified point-gradient form earn the first two marks only.

Why this question is hard

This item practises tangents and normals on a curve that is not a pure polynomial, because the term $-3/x$ forces the candidate to rewrite it as $-3x^{-1}$ before differentiating. The derivative becomes $4x+3/x^{2}$, and the whole problem turns on evaluating this correctly at $x=1$ to get the gradient $7$. The intended route is to differentiate, find the point $(1,-1)$, write the tangent with the same gradient using the point-gradient form, then take the negative reciprocal $-1/7$ for the normal. The trap is the reciprocal: differentiating $-3/x$ by eye tempts candidates to write $-3/x^{2}$ (forgetting the sign flip from the leading minus) or to apply the quotient rule and tangle the algebra. Another error is to confuse tangent and normal, using gradient $7$ for the normal or $-1/7$ for the tangent. The normal equation also needs careful rearrangement: $y+1=(-1/7)(x-1)$ expands to $y=-x/7+1/7-1=-x/7-6/7$, and candidates who stop at $y+1=-(x-1)/7$ without simplifying lose the final mark. The numbers were chosen so that $x=1$ gives tidy integers for the point and the tangent, isolating the reciprocal derivative as the only genuine difficulty; the normal then tests the negative-reciprocal rule on a clean fraction, and the negative $y$-coordinate must be carried carefully through both expansions. The combination of a reciprocal derivative with a fractional normal is what makes this more than a routine differentiation question. The blend of a reciprocal derivative with a fractional normal lifts this above a routine exercise, and the negative $y$-coordinate must be carried carefully through both line equations to avoid a sign slip.
