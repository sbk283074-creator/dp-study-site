The smallest number of trials that pushes a probability past 0.99

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.8-001

SL 4.8 (binomial distribution; mean and variance of the binomial distribution)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: requiredFindDetermineCalculate

Question

A trial has probability of success $p=0.3$ and is repeated $8$ times, with the trials independent of one another. A calculator is required throughout.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Find the probability that exactly $3$ of the $8$ trials succeed, giving your answer correct to six decimal places.

    (Find)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the probability that at least one of the $8$ trials succeeds.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the smallest number of trials for which the probability of at least one success exceeds $0.99$.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Calculate the mean and the standard deviation of the number of successes in $8$ trials.

    (Calculate)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) If $X$ denotes the number of successes then $X\sim B(8,0.3)$ (M1). The number of ways of choosing which three trials succeed is $\binom{8}{3}=56$ (A1), so $P(X=3)=56(0.3)^{3}(0.7)^{5}$ (M1) $=0.254122$ correct to six decimal places (A1). The coefficient counts the orders in which the three successes can appear, while the two powers record the probabilities of the successes and of the failures.

(b) The complement of at least one success is that no trial succeeds (M1), and $P(X=0)=(0.7)^{8}$ (A1). Therefore $P(X\ge 1)=1-(0.7)^{8}$ (A1) $=0.942352$ (A1). The complement rule replaces a sum of nine separate binomial terms by a single power, and it is correct because the events $X=0$ and $X\ge 1$ together exhaust the sample space.

(c) For $n$ trials the probability of at least one success is $1-(0.7)^{n}$ (M1), and this must exceed $0.99$, so $(0.7)^{n}<0.01$ (A1). Taking logarithms gives $n>\frac{\ln 0.01}{\ln 0.7}=12.91\ldots$ (M1), hence the smallest whole number of trials is $n=13$ (A1). The inequality reverses direction because $\ln 0.7$ is negative, and the value must be rounded up rather than to the nearest integer, since the inequality is strict.

(d) For a binomial distribution the mean is $np=8\times 0.3$ (M1) $=2.4$ (A1), and the variance is $np(1-p)=8\times 0.3\times 0.7=1.68$ (M1). The standard deviation is the square root of the variance, so $\sqrt{1.68}=1.296148$ (A1). The mean is an expected count of successes while the standard deviation is measured in the same units as that count, so the two answers are consistent with each other and both describe the same binomial count.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for identifying the binomial model with $n=8$ and $p=0.3$. The (A1) is for the coefficient $56$, and a candidate who writes $\binom{8}{3}$ without evaluating it forfeits this mark. The second (M1) is for the product of the two powers, and the (A1) requires the value to six decimal places; accept $0.254122$.

(b) Award (M1) for using the complement and (A1) for the term $(0.7)^{8}$. The third (A1) is for the correct structure one minus $(0.7)^{8}$, and the final (A1) for the numerical value. A candidate who sums the terms $P(X=1)$ to $P(X=8)$ earns full credit if the total is correct.

(c) The (M1) is for the general expression one minus $(0.7)^{n}$ and the (A1) for the inequality $(0.7)^{n}<0.01$. The (M1) is for taking logarithms, and the (A1) is for $n=13$. A candidate who rounds $12.91$ up to $13$ by inspection still earns the final mark, but one who gives $12$ forfeits it because the probability there is below $0.99$.

(d) Award (M1)(A1) for the mean $2.4$, (M1) for the variance $1.68$ and (A1) for the standard deviation $1.296148$. Accept three significant figures for the standard deviation. Follow through from an incorrect $p$ is allowed.

Why this question is hard

The item is built on the complement rule and on inverting the binomial inequality. Part (a) is a direct binomial probability and rewards candidates who can form the coefficient and the two powers. Part (b) is where the lever first appears: the phrase at least one invites a sum over eight or nine terms, but the complement reduces it to the single power $(0.7)^{8}$, and a candidate who does not see this will waste time or make an arithmetic slip.

Part (c) pushes the same idea further. The condition that the probability of at least one success exceeds $0.99$ becomes the inequality $(0.7)^{n}<0.01$, and solving it requires logarithms. The critical point is that $\ln 0.7$ is negative, so the inequality reverses when the logarithms are taken; a candidate who forgets this will obtain $n<12.91$ and answer $12$. Because $0.99$ is a strict threshold, the answer must be rounded up, and $12.91$ therefore gives $n=13$, not $12$.

Part (d) returns to the standard mean and variance formulae, $np$ and $np(1-p)$, and asks for the standard deviation rather than the variance, which catches candidates who stop one step early. The numbers $p=0.3$ and $n=8$ were chosen so that $(0.7)^{8}=0.942352$ and $(0.7)^{12}=0.986159$ fall just below $0.99$ while $(0.7)^{13}=0.990311$ rises above it, so the threshold genuinely separates consecutive trial counts. A candidate who treats the question as a single calculation, rather than as a search over whole numbers, will report $12.91$ and lose the final mark, because the question asks for a count of trials rather than for a boundary value.
