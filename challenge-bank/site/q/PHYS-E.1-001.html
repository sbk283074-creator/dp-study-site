The work function that cancels in the difference

Physics HL · PHYS-E.1-001

E.1 Structure of the atom and E.2 Quantum physics (AHL): energy levels of a hydrogen-like ion E_n = -A/n^2, spectral lines from transitions between levels, ionisation energy and its scaling with Z^2, the photoelectric effect, the photoelectric equation eV_s = hf - phi, stopping voltage and work functionP2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedExplainShow thatDetermine

Stimulus

A discharge tube contains a gas of a hydrogen-like ion, that is, an ion with a single electron and nuclear charge number $Z$. The energy levels of the ion are $E_{n}=-\dfrac{A}{n^{2}}$, where $A$ is a positive constant and $n$ is the principal quantum number. The light emitted by the tube is passed through a monochromator, and two spectral lines are isolated in turn and directed at the same metal photocathode inside an evacuated photocell. The two lines are identified as the $n=4\to2$ and $n=2\to1$ transitions of the ion, and the stopping voltage is measured for each.

| Spectral line | Stopping voltage |
|---|---|
| $n=4\to2$ | $5.7\ \mathrm{V}$ |
| $n=2\to1$ | $36.3\ \mathrm{V}$ |

The work function of the cathode is $\phi$ and the electron charge is $e=1.60\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$.

Question

Assume that the light from each line is monochromatic and that the photocell is ideal, so that the stopping voltage corresponds to the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons. The values of the Planck constant and the speed of light are not needed.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Explain why the stopping voltage measures the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons, and state the photoelectric equation that relates the stopping voltage, the photon energy and the work function of the cathode.

    (Explain)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that the photon energies of the $n=4\to2$ and $n=2\to1$ transitions of this ion are $\dfrac{3A}{16}$ and $\dfrac{3A}{4}$ respectively, and state which of the two measured stopping voltages corresponds to each transition.

    (Show that)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Using the two measured stopping voltages, determine the value of $A$ and the work function $\phi$ of the cathode, and explain why neither stopping voltage on its own is sufficient to find $\phi$.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the nuclear charge number $Z$ of the ion and its ionisation energy in electronvolts, and explain how the wavelengths of the two spectral lines would change if the gas were replaced by a hydrogen-like ion of larger $Z$.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The emitted photoelectrons have a range of kinetic energies up to a maximum $K_{\max}$. (A1) The stopping voltage is the reverse potential difference that just reduces the photocurrent to zero, so it removes the kinetic energy of the fastest electrons: $eV_{s}=K_{\max}$. (A1) Energy conservation for the absorption of a single photon gives $hf=\phi+K_{\max}$, and therefore

$$eV_{s}=hf-\phi$$ (A1)

(b) The energy of the photon emitted in a transition equals the difference between the two levels involved. (M1) For $n=4\to2$,

$$hf_{1}=E_{4}-E_{2}=-\frac{A}{16}-\left(-\frac{A}{4}\right)=\frac{A}{4}-\frac{A}{16}=\frac{3A}{16}$$ (A1)

and for $n=2\to1$,

$$hf_{2}=E_{2}-E_{1}=-\frac{A}{4}-(-A)=A-\frac{A}{4}=\frac{3A}{4}$$ (A1)

Since $\dfrac{3A}{4}>\dfrac{3A}{16}$, the $n=2\to1$ line delivers the more energetic photons and must give the larger stopping voltage, $36.3\ \mathrm{V}$; the $n=4\to2$ line corresponds to $5.7\ \mathrm{V}$. (A1)

(c) Applying $eV_{s}=E_{\text{photon}}-\phi$ to each line, and working in electronvolts so that the stopping voltage in volts is numerically equal to the kinetic energy in electronvolts:

$$e(5.7)=\frac{3A}{16}-\phi \qquad\text{and}\qquad e(36.3)=\frac{3A}{4}-\phi$$ (M1)

Subtracting the first equation from the second eliminates $\phi$:

$$e(36.3-5.7)=\frac{3A}{4}-\frac{3A}{16}=\frac{12A-3A}{16}=\frac{9A}{16}$$ (M1)(A1)

$$A=\frac{16\times 30.6}{9}=54.4\ \mathrm{eV}$$ (A1)

Substituting this back into the first equation gives the work function:

$$\phi=\frac{3A}{16}-e(5.7)=\frac{3(54.4)}{16}-5.7=10.2-5.7=4.5\ \mathrm{eV}$$ (A1)

Each stopping voltage is the difference between a photon energy and the work function. A single reading is therefore one equation in two unknowns, $A$ and $\phi$, and cannot be solved; only the difference of the two readings removes $\phi$ and fixes $A$, after which $\phi$ follows from either line. (A1)

(d) For a hydrogen-like ion the energy-level constant is

$$A=13.6Z^{2}\ \mathrm{eV}$$ (M1)

so

$$Z^{2}=\frac{A}{13.6}=\frac{54.4}{13.6}=4.0\quad\Rightarrow\quad Z=2$$ (A1)

The ion is therefore $\mathrm{He}^{+}$, and its ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove the electron from $n=1$ to $E=0$, namely $A=54.4\ \mathrm{eV}$. (A1) Because $A\propto Z^{2}$, a larger $Z$ increases every transition energy. Since $E=hc/\lambda$, each wavelength would be shorter, so both lines would shift to shorter wavelengths, giving more energetic photons and larger stopping voltages. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The first (A1) is for the statement that the photoelectrons have a maximum kinetic energy; the second for $eV_{s}=K_{\max}$; the third for the full energy balance $eV_{s}=hf-\phi$. A candidate who writes $eV_{s}=hf+\phi$ has the sign of the work function wrong and loses the final mark. Accept 'stopping potential' for 'stopping voltage'.

(b) The (M1) is for the principle that a photon's energy equals the difference between the two levels involved. The two (A1)s are for the algebraic results $3A/16$ and $3A/4$. The final (A1) requires both the identification and a reason: because $3A/4>3A/16$, the $n=2\to1$ line gives the larger stopping voltage, $36.3\ \mathrm{V}$. A candidate who reverses the correspondence has made a physics error rather than an arithmetic slip and loses that mark.

(c) The first (M1) is for writing the two photoelectric equations, one for each line. The second (M1) is for subtracting them so that $\phi$ cancels; a candidate who substitutes one equation into the other will still reach the answer but should be given the method mark only if the algebra is shown. Accept $A$ between $54$ and $54.5\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $\phi$ between $4.4$ and $4.6\ \mathrm{eV}$. Follow-through applies: a candidate whose $A$ is slightly off but whose $\phi$ is consistent with it keeps the final mark.

(d) The (M1) is for quoting or deriving $A=13.6Z^{2}\ \mathrm{eV}$. Accept $Z=2$ with the identification of the ion as $\mathrm{He}^{+}$. The final mark requires the direction of the shift: shorter wavelengths, larger photon energies. 'The lines would move' with no direction earns nothing.

Why this question is hard

The difficulty here is not the photoelectric equation, which most candidates can quote, but the fact that a single stopping voltage is an aggregate. It is the photon energy minus the work function, and both are unknown, so one reading gives one equation in two unknowns and is unsolvable. Candidates who have practised only single-line problems will look for a way to pin the work function directly and will not find one; the question is designed so that the only route is to take a difference.

The difference works because the work function appears in both equations with the same sign and the same magnitude. Subtracting removes it exactly, leaving the difference of the two photon energies, which is a pure property of the ion. This is the same trick used in the Millikan experiment to extract the Planck constant from the slope of a stopping-voltage against frequency graph: the intercept carries the work function and the slope carries $h/e$, so a single point cannot separate them. Here the two transitions play the role of two frequencies.

The ion is deliberately left unnamed. The student must recognise that the level spacing of a hydrogen-like ion scales as $Z^{2}$, so recovering $A$ is equivalent to recovering $Z$, and $A=54.4\ \mathrm{eV}$ identifies $\mathrm{He}^{+}$. The two chosen lines, $n=4\to2$ and $n=2\to1$, were picked so that their photon-energy difference is exactly $9A/16$, which makes the arithmetic clean; the ratio of their photon energies, $4:1$, is also a useful check.

Classic wrong turns: assuming that the stopping voltage equals the photon energy, which ignores the work function entirely; trying to find the two wavelengths from the same two equations, which is circular because the wavelengths are what the transition energies already determine; and treating the ion as hydrogen, so that $A=13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$, which is inconsistent with the measured stopping voltages. The last error is easy to make, because the $n=2\to1$ line of hydrogen would give a stopping voltage near $9.1\ \mathrm{V}$, far from the $36.3\ \mathrm{V}$ measured, and a quick numerical check catches it.

Verification
Photon energies from the level differences 3A/16 and 3A/4; the two photoelectric equations subtracted to give A = 16(36.3-5.7)/9 = 54.4 eV, then phi = 3A/16 - 5.7 = 4.5 eV; the reverse substitution 3A/4 - phi = 36.3 V was checked; Z from A = 13.6 Z^2 gives Z = 2 and the ionisation energy equals A = 54.4 eV.
