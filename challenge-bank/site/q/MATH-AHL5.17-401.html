5.17 The area between a sideways parabola and a chord, integrated with respect to y

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.17-401

Question

The region $R$ is enclosed by the curve $x = y^{2}$ and the straight line $x = 3 - 2y$, as shown in the figure. The two boundary curves meet at $(9,\,-3)$ and at $(1,\,1)$.

(a) Find the coordinates of the two points at which the curve and the line meet, by forming and solving an equation in $y$.

(b) Show that the area of $R$ is given by the single integral $\displaystyle\int_{-3}^{1}\left(3 - 2y - y^{2}\right)dy$ and evaluate it.

(c) Explain why integrating with respect to $x$ instead would require the region to be split into two parts, and state the value of $x$ at which the split would occur.

Reveal the answer

(a) A point on both curves satisfies $y^{2} = 3 - 2y$, so $y^{2} + 2y - 3 = 0$ (M1). Factorising, $(y + 3)(y - 1) = 0$, so $y = -3$ or $y = 1$. Substituting back gives $x = y^{2} = 9$ when $y = -3$ and $x = 1$ when $y = 1$, so the two points are $(9,\,-3)$ and $(1,\,1)$ (A1).

(b) For every $y$ between $-3$ and $1$ the line lies to the right of the curve, because $3 - 2y - y^{2} = -(y + 3)(y - 1)$ is positive on that interval (M1). A horizontal strip at height $y$ therefore has width $(3 - 2y) - y^{2}$ and height $dy$, and the area of $R$ is the sum of those strips:

$$A = \int_{-3}^{1}\left(3 - 2y - y^{2}\right)dy = \left[3y - y^{2} - \tfrac{1}{3}y^{3}\right]_{-3}^{1} \;(M1).$$

At the upper limit, $3 - 1 - \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{5}{3}$. At the lower limit, $-9 - 9 + 9 = -9$. Subtracting,

$$A = \tfrac{5}{3} - (-9) = \tfrac{32}{3} \approx 10.67 \;(A1).$$

So the enclosed area is $\tfrac{32}{3}$ square units, which is a little over ten and a half. Notice that the antiderivative is a cubic whose value at the lower limit is negative: the region straddles the $x$-axis, and the integral handles that without any adjustment because the strips are measured horizontally and each one keeps its own sign of $x$, not of $y$.

(c) Integrating with respect to $x$ means slicing the region vertically, and the top of a vertical slice is not the same curve all the way across (M1). To the left of the point where the two boundaries cross, the slice runs from the lower branch of the parabola to the upper branch, so its height is $2\sqrt{x}$; to the right of that point the upper branch of the parabola lies above the line, so the height becomes $\dfrac{3 - x}{2} + \sqrt{x}$ and the two curves have exchanged roles as the upper boundary. The change happens where $y^{2} = 3 - 2y$ has its positive root, that is at $y = 1$, and there $x = 1$; this is the dashed vertical line drawn in the figure, and it is the only vertical slice for which the two descriptions agree, since it runs from $y = -1$ to $y = 1$ (A1). A candidate working in $x$ must therefore write

$$A = \int_{0}^{1} 2\sqrt{x}\,dx + \int_{1}^{9}\left(\frac{3 - x}{2} + \sqrt{x}\right)dx = \tfrac{4}{3} + \tfrac{28}{3} = \tfrac{32}{3},$$

which is the same area at roughly twice the work: two integrals, two antiderivatives, and a split point that has to be found before either integral can be set up. Investing in the coordinate that matches the shape of the region is what removes the split.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for forming $y^{2} = 3 - 2y$ or the equivalent and reducing it to $y^{2} + 2y - 3 = 0$; (A1) for both points $(9,\,-3)$ and $(1,\,1)$ with the substitution shown. Accept a candidate who solves for $x$ first, provided both coordinates of both points are stated. A candidate who gives the roots $y = -3$ and $y = 1$ but never forms the coordinates scores the first mark only.

(b) (M1) for setting up the integral with the line minus the curve over $y$ from $-3$ to $1$, with the sign of $3 - 2y - y^{2}$ justified or evidently checked; (M1) for the correct antiderivative $3y - y^{2} - \tfrac{1}{3}y^{3}$; (A1) for $\tfrac{32}{3}$ or $10.7$ to three significant figures. Three fields; credit two, and the displayed integral already earns the first. Do not accept an area given as negative, and do not accept $\tfrac{27}{3}$ or $9$ from substituting the limits in the wrong order.

(c) (M1) for the observation that the upper boundary changes from the upper branch of the parabola to the straight line; (A1) for $x = 1$, correctly located as the point where the interval of $y$ is the largest. Award the second mark for $x = 1$ alone, but only if the change of upper boundary has been stated. A candidate who says the region must be split 'because it is curved' has not identified what changes and scores nothing.

Why this question is hard

The mathematics here is a single quadratic and a single cubic. What the item tests is whether the candidate chooses the variable that the shape of the region is built for, and it is written so that the wrong choice is also the habitual one. Curves are almost always presented as $y$ equal to something in $x$, and the area between two of them is almost always computed with vertical strips, so the reflex is to make $y$ the subject and integrate in $x$ -- which here means solving $x = y^{2}$ for $y$, meeting the square root, and then discovering that the upper boundary is a different curve over part of the range.

Reading the figure instead of the algebra gives the answer immediately. The region is tall and narrow towards its right-hand end and pinches to a point at its left-hand end, and both of its boundaries are naturally described with $y$ as the running variable: one is $x$ as a function of $y$ and the other is $x$ as a function of $y$. Horizontal strips therefore run from one boundary to the other over the whole height of the region, from $y = -3$ to $y = 1$, and one integral does the whole job. The split at $x = 1$ is not a complication of the region; it is a complication created by the choice of coordinate.

It is worth noticing what the single integral does not have to do. There is no square root in it, because the curve is already written as $x$ in terms of $y$. There is no sign correction, even though the region lies mostly below the $x$-axis, because horizontal strips measure differences in $x$ and the question of whether a strip is above or below the axis never arises. And there is no need to find the maximum of anything, because the widest part of the region is at $y = -1$ and no part of the work depends on it.

The general lesson is the one the syllabus states and the exercises rarely enforce: the area of a region can be found by integrating with respect to either variable, and the useful skill is deciding which one keeps the region in a single piece. A region bounded on one side by a curve written as $x$ equal to a function of $y$ is asking to be cut into horizontal strips, and taking the hint is not a trick, it is reading the question.
