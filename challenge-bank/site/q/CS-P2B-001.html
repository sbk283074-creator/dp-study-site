Four jobs, one machine, and the order that looks efficient

Computer science HL · CS-P2B-001

Theme B: Computational thinking and problem-solvingB1.1 (Approaches to computational thinking: decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction and algorithm design)P216 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedDetermineApplyExplainJustify

One machine, four jobs, and a promise about the worst one

A workshop has a single paint booth and four cars waiting. Only one car can be in the booth at a time, and once a car is started it must be finished. Each car has a painting time in hours and a promised collection time, counted in hours from now. The workshop does not care about the average wait. It cares about the worst case, because the manager is judged on the single most disappointed customer of the week.

The lateness of a job is the time it finishes minus its promised collection time, so a negative lateness means the job finished early. The objective is to make the largest lateness as small as possible, and a negative value is a success rather than a problem.

The four cars are: A takes 5 hours and is promised at hour 8; B takes 2 hours and is promised at hour 6; C takes 4 hours and is promised at hour 20; D takes 7 hours and is promised at hour 16. The booth starts empty at hour 0 and is never idle while jobs remain.

Question

You are asked to choose the order in which the four cars enter the booth. Work in whole hours, and remember that a job's lateness is measured against its own promise and can be negative.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    The cars are currently processed in the order they were booked: A, then B, then C, then D. Determine the finishing time of each car and the largest lateness of that schedule.

    (Determine)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Apply the shortest-job-first rule, that is, process the cars in increasing order of painting time, breaking any tie by the earlier promise. Give the resulting order, each finishing time, and the largest lateness of that schedule.

    (Apply)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Explain why the schedule that puts the car with the earliest promise first can never produce a larger worst-case lateness than the schedule you found in part (b), however the remaining cars are then arranged. Your explanation should identify the property of the worst-case objective that makes the exchange argument work.

    (Explain)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    A fifth car, E, is added. E takes 3 hours and is promised at hour 9. Justify whether the earliest-promise-first order still achieves the smallest possible worst-case lateness for all five cars, and state the value it achieves.

    (Justify)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

Part (a). The cars run back to back from hour 0, so each finishing time is the running total of the painting times.

A finishes at 0 + 5 = 5. B finishes at 5 + 2 = 7. C finishes at 7 + 4 = 11. D finishes at 11 + 7 = 18. (M1) for the running total, (A1) for all four finishing times.

The lateness of each car is its finishing time minus its own promise. A: 5 - 8 = -3. B: 7 - 6 = +1. C: 11 - 20 = -9. D: 18 - 16 = +2. (A1) The largest lateness is therefore +2, set by car D.

Part (b). The painting times are A 5, B 2, C 4, D 7, so increasing order is B (2), C (4), A (5), D (7). No ties arise, so the tie-break is not needed. (M1)

Finishing times: B at 2, C at 2 + 4 = 6, A at 6 + 5 = 11, D at 11 + 7 = 18. (A1)

Lateness: B is 2 - 6 = -4; C is 6 - 20 = -14; A is 11 - 8 = +3; D is 18 - 16 = +2. (A1) The largest lateness is +3, set by car A. (A1) So shortest-job-first is worse than the booking order, which is the surprising result the question is built on: it moved the worst lateness from +2 up to +3.

Part (c). The objective depends only on the largest lateness, so it is decided by a single job. Consider any schedule and suppose that, at some point, the job with the earliest promise among those not yet started is not the one that runs next. (M1) Let that job be X and let the job that actually runs be Y, which therefore has a promise no earlier than X's. Swap X and Y so that X runs in Y's slot and Y runs immediately after. (M1)

The jobs before the swap are untouched, and the jobs after the two swapped ones still finish at the same times, because the pair still occupies the same total length of time. So only X and Y can have their lateness changed. (A1) After the swap X finishes earlier than Y did, and its promise was no later than Y's, so X's lateness does not increase. Y finishes exactly when X used to finish, which is at most as late as Y's old finishing time, so Y's lateness does not increase either. (M1)

Every swap of this kind therefore leaves the largest lateness no larger than it was, and repeated swapping sorts the schedule into earliest-promise-first order without ever making the worst case worse. Since we started from an arbitrary schedule, no schedule can beat the earliest-promise-first order on this objective. (R1) This is exactly why the reasoning fails for the *average* lateness, where a short job delays fewer jobs and the trade is genuinely different.

Part (d). With E added, the promises are B 6, A 8, E 9, D 16, C 20, so the earliest-promise-first order is B, A, E, D, C. (M1)

Finishing times: B 2, A 7, E 10, D 17, C 21. Lateness: B -4, A -1, E 10 - 9 = +1, D 17 - 16 = +1, C 21 - 20 = +1. The largest lateness is +1. (A1)

The exchange argument of part (c) never used the number of jobs, only the fact that swapping an earlier-promise job forward cannot worsen either swapped job, so it applies unchanged to five jobs. (M1) The achieved value +1 is therefore the smallest possible worst-case lateness; a complete search of all 120 orders confirms that no order does better. (A1) Notice that adding E has turned a schedule that was comfortably early everywhere into one that is late on three jobs, which is a property of the job set rather than of the rule.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for treating finishing times as a running total; A1 for all four of 5, 7, 11, 18; A1 for the maximum lateness of +2. A candidate who reports the largest lateness as 2 without the sign is still credited, since the sign is a convention and the value is what is asked for; a candidate who writes -9 or -14 as the largest has compared magnitudes and forfeits the final A1.

(b) M1 for the order B, C, A, D. Award A1 for the finishing times 2, 6, 11, 18, A1 for the lateness values, and A1 for the maximum of +3. Follow-through is allowed: a candidate who produces a different order in (b) but reads a maximum lateness consistently off their own finishing times keeps the final A1. A candidate who gives the average lateness instead of the maximum forfeits that A1 and scores at most 3 of 4.

(c) M1 for identifying that the objective is decided by one job. M1 for the swap construction. A1 for the observation that the jobs after the pair are unaffected. M1 for showing that neither swapped job's lateness rises. R1 for the conclusion that repetition yields the earliest-promise-first order and that this proves optimality. Do not credit an answer that asserts optimality from the rule's name, or that argues only about the first job; the whole force of the argument is that the swap is safe for both jobs involved.

(d) M1 for the order B, A, E, D, C. A1 for the finishing times and the maximum lateness of +1. M1 for explicitly extending the exchange argument rather than re-asserting it. A1 for the justification that +1 is optimal. A candidate who claims the answer changes because the exchange argument depended on there being four jobs scores the M1s but not the A1s.

Across the question, accept any equivalent formulation of lateness provided the candidate is consistent about the sign.

Why this question is hard

The item is a trap built out of two heuristics that are both taught, in different contexts, without the objective they belong to.

Shortest-job-first is the correct answer to a different question. It minimises the *average* time a job spends in the system, and it is genuinely optimal for that. Students meet it as the scheduling rule and reach for it here. But the workshop is not judged on the average; it is judged on the single worst customer, and those two objectives order the jobs differently. On this job set shortest-job-first produces a worst lateness of +3 while the booking order produces +2 and the earliest-promise order produces -1. The rule that is right for the average is the *worst* of the three candidates for the maximum.

The second trap is the sign. Because lateness is defined as finish minus promise, a good schedule has a *negative* maximum. A student who is looking for the largest number and who discards signs will report -14 from part (b), or +3 when the answer is -1, and will have inverted the objective without noticing. This is why the stimulus states the convention explicitly and why part (a) is deliberately a schedule that is early on three cars and late on one.

The reasoning in part (c) is an exchange argument, and the point worth seeing is that the argument is *tight*: it works because the two swapped jobs are adjacent, so nothing after them moves, and because the job moved forward had the earlier promise. Change either condition and it collapses. Part (d) tests whether the student understood the argument or merely memorised its conclusion, by changing the instance in a way that makes the answer worse without changing the rule.

The classic wrong turn in part (c) is to argue from the first job only, or to claim that the rule is obviously optimal because it is named after the deadline. Neither is a proof: the first job being on time says nothing about the last one, and the whole content of the result is that the swap does not harm the job it displaces.
