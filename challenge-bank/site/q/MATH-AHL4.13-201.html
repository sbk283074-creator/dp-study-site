4.13 The posterior probability of a disease after a positive screening test

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.13-201

Topic 4: Statistics and probabilityAHL 4.13 (Bayes' theorem for up to three events) with SL 4.6 (tree diagrams and conditional probability)P2 · Section B12 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedCalculateDetermineCommentDetermine

Question

A screening test is applied to a population in which 1 person in 1000 has a particular disease. For a person who has the disease the test is positive with probability 0.99. For a person who does not have the disease the test is positive with probability 0.05. A person is selected at random from the population and tested. Let $D$ denote the event that the person has the disease and $T^{+}$ the event that the test is positive.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate $P(T^{+})$, the probability that a randomly selected person tests positive.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine $P(D \mid T^{+})$, the probability that a person who tests positive actually has the disease.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Comment on the difference between your answer to (b) and the sensitivity of the test, explaining where the difference comes from.

    (Comment)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    Determine $P(D \mid T^{-})$, the probability that a person who tests negative nevertheless has the disease.

    (Determine)

Reveal the answer

(a) A positive result can arise in two mutually exclusive ways, so apply the law of total probability (M1):
$$P(T^{+}) = P(T^{+} \mid D)P(D) + P(T^{+} \mid D')P(D')$$

Substituting (M1):
$$P(T^{+}) = (0.99)(0.001) + (0.05)(0.999) = 0.00099 + 0.04995 = 0.05094 \tag{A1}$$

So about $5.1\ \%$ of the population tests positive.

(b) By Bayes' theorem (M1):
$$P(D \mid T^{+}) = \frac{P(T^{+} \mid D)P(D)}{P(T^{+})} = \frac{0.00099}{0.05094}$$

$$= 0.019435\ldots \approx 0.0194 \tag{A1(A1)}$$

So only about $1.9\ \%$ of those who test positive actually have the disease (A1). Equivalently, of 100000 people tested, about 100 have the disease and 99 of them test positive, while about 99900 are healthy and 4995 of them also test positive; the proportion is $99/(99 + 4995) = 0.0194$.

(c) The sensitivity $P(T^{+} \mid D) = 0.99$ answers a question about people who have the disease. The quantity calculated in (b), $P(D \mid T^{+})$, answers the reverse question, about people who test positive, and the two are connected by Bayes' theorem rather than by being equal. The difference is driven by the base rate: because only one person in a thousand has the disease, the large healthy group generates far more false positives in absolute terms than the small diseased group generates true positives, even though the false-positive rate is only $5\ \%$. A test can therefore be highly sensitive and still leave a positive result far more likely to be a false alarm than a true one, and the rarer the disease the stronger this effect becomes.

(d) A negative result likewise has two sources. First $P(T^{-}) = 1 - 0.05094 = 0.94906$, then (M1)
$$P(D \mid T^{-}) = \frac{P(T^{-} \mid D)P(D)}{P(T^{-})} = \frac{(0.01)(0.001)}{0.94906} = 1.05 \times 10^{-5} \tag{A1}$$

about $0.001\ \%$. A negative result is very strong evidence of health, which is the mirror image of the weak evidence a positive result provides.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for the law of total probability with both branches, M1 for substituting the three given numbers, A1 for $0.05094$. Accept $0.051$ or $5.1\ \%$.

(b) M1 for Bayes' theorem with the correct numerator $0.99 \times 0.001$, A1 for the value, A1 for the interpretation as a percentage. The numerator is the part most often got wrong: a candidate who uses $0.99$ alone, or who divides by $P(D)$ instead of $P(T^{+})$, scores 0. Follow-through applies from an incorrect $P(T^{+})$ in (a) provided the ratio is formed correctly.

(c) The marks are for identifying that (b) and the sensitivity are different conditional probabilities, and for attributing the gap to the base rate. A statement that the answer is "surprising" earns 0. Accept a reference to the absolute numbers of true and false positives, or to the small prior.

(d) M1 for using $P(T^{-} \mid D) = 1 - 0.99$ and the correct denominator, A1 for the value. Accept $1.05 \times 10^{-5}$ or $0.001\ \%$.

Forfeits: using $0.05$ where $0.95$ is required (confusing the false-positive rate with the specificity); treating the sensitivity and the posterior as the same quantity. Condonation: a candidate who works with 100000 people and counts rather than using the formula should be given full credit, because that is a legitimate and often clearer route; a candidate who writes $0.0194$ as $1.94\ \%$ should not lose a mark for the representation.

Why this question is hard

This is the standard base-rate item, and its value is that the arithmetic is easy while the conclusion is counter-intuitive. A test that detects the disease in 99 per cent of cases sounds almost conclusive, and yet a positive result raises the probability of disease only from $0.1\ \%$ to $1.9\ \%$. The gap between those two numbers is the whole content of the question.

The reason is worth stating precisely, because it is a place where intuition reliably fails. The posterior is a ratio of two frequencies, and the denominator is dominated by the healthy group. With a prevalence of one in a thousand, there are roughly a thousand times as many healthy people as diseased ones, so a false-positive rate of five per cent applied to the healthy majority produces about fifty times as many false positives as the one per cent miss rate applied to the diseased minority produces false negatives. The sensitivity is a property of the test; the posterior is a property of the test and the population, and the population term dominates.

Part (c) asks for the explanation in words, which is the part that cannot be done by formula. Part (d) then completes the picture with the mirror calculation: a negative result is enormously informative, which is what one would expect from a test with a five per cent false-positive rate applied to a rare disease. Seeing that the two results are not symmetric, and that the asymmetry comes from the same base rate, is the transferable understanding, and it is also the practical reason why screening programmes for rare conditions are so often followed by a second, different test.
