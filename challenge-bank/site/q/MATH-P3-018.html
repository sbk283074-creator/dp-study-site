P3 extended problem: the product of the two segments of a chord through an interior point

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-018

Topic 3: Geometry and trigonometrySL 3.1 (the distance between two points), AHL 2.12 (polynomial functions, their graphs and equations), AHL 3.12 (concept of a vector; displacement vectors)P315 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatDeduceDetermine

Question

The circle $\Gamma$ has equation $x^{2} + y^{2} = 25$, so its centre is $O = (0,0)$ and its radius is $5$. The point $P = (3,0)$ lies inside $\Gamma$. For a real constant $m$, let $L_m$ be the line through $P$ with gradient $m$, and let $A$ and $B$ be the two points where $L_m$ meets $\Gamma$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that $L_m$ meets $\Gamma$ in two distinct points for every real value of $m$, and state what happens when the line through $P$ is vertical.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Parametrise $L_m$ as $(x,y) = (3,0) + \dfrac{t}{s}(1, m)$ where $s = \sqrt{1 + m^{2}}$, so that $|t|$ is the distance from $P$ to the point with parameter $t$. Show that the parameters of $A$ and $B$ are the two roots of
    $$t^{2} + \frac{6}{s}\,t - 16 = 0,$$
    and deduce that $PA \times PB = 16$ for every real value of $m$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Now let $P$ be any point inside a circle of radius $r$ whose centre is $O$, and let $d = OP$ with $d < r$. Show that for any line through $P$ meeting the circle at $A$ and $B$, $PA \times PB = r^{2} - d^{2}$.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    For $P = (3,0)$ in the circle $x^{2} + y^{2} = 25$, determine the chord $AB$ for which $PA = PB$, and verify that your answer is consistent with part (b).

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Points of $L_m$ satisfy $y = m(x-3)$. Substituting into $x^{2} + y^{2} = 25$,
$$x^{2} + m^{2}(x-3)^{2} = 25 \iff \left(1 + m^{2}\right)x^{2} - 6m^{2}x + \left(9m^{2} - 25\right) = 0$$ (M1).
The discriminant is
$$\Delta = \left(-6m^{2}\right)^{2} - 4\left(1+m^{2}\right)\left(9m^{2}-25\right) = 36m^{4} - 36m^{4} + 64m^{2} + 100 = 64m^{2} + 100$$ (M1) (A1).
Since $m^{2} \ge 0$ we have $\Delta \ge 100 > 0$ for every real $m$, so the quadratic has two distinct real roots and $L_m$ meets $\Gamma$ in two distinct points (A1). When the line through $P$ is vertical its gradient is undefined and it is not one of the $L_m$; that line is $x = 3$, which meets $\Gamma$ where $9 + y^{2} = 25$, that is at $(3,4)$ and $(3,-4)$, again two distinct points (AG).

(b) Write the point of $L_m$ with parameter $t$ as $\left(3 + \dfrac{t}{s},\; \dfrac{mt}{s}\right)$, where $s^{2} = 1+m^{2}$; the direction vector $\dfrac{1}{s}(1,m)$ has unit length, so $|t|$ is indeed the distance from $P$ (M1). Substituting,
$$\left(3 + \frac{t}{s}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{mt}{s}\right)^{2} = 25 \iff 9 + \frac{6t}{s} + \frac{t^{2}}{s^{2}}\left(1 + m^{2}\right) = 25$$ (M1),
and since $1 + m^{2} = s^{2}$ the last term is simply $t^{2}$, giving
$$t^{2} + \frac{6}{s}\,t - 16 = 0$$ (A1).
By Vieta's formulas the product of the two roots is the constant term, $-16$, so $t_A t_B = -16$ (A1). The two parameters have opposite signs, which is consistent with $P$ lying between $A$ and $B$, and $|t_A| = PA$, $|t_B| = PB$, so
$$PA \times PB = |t_A t_B| = 16$$ (A1) (AG),
independently of $m$.

(c) Place the origin at $O$ and the $x$-axis along $OP$, so that $P = (d, 0)$; this loses no generality because a rotation about $O$ preserves distances and the circle (M1). Let $\mathbf{u} = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ be a unit vector along the line, and write a general point of the line as $P + t\mathbf{u} = \left(d + t\cos\theta,\; t\sin\theta\right)$, so that $|t|$ is the distance from $P$ (M1). Substituting into $x^{2} + y^{2} = r^{2}$,
$$\left(d + t\cos\theta\right)^{2} + \left(t\sin\theta\right)^{2} = r^{2} \iff t^{2} + 2d\cos\theta\,t + \left(d^{2} - r^{2}\right) = 0$$ (A1).
The two intersection points correspond to the two roots, and by Vieta their product is the constant term $d^{2} - r^{2}$. Since $d < r$ this is negative, so the roots have opposite signs -- that is, $P$ lies between $A$ and $B$ -- and therefore
$$PA \times PB = |t_A t_B| = r^{2} - d^{2}$$ (A1) (AG),
which does not involve $\theta$: the product is the same for every chord through $P$. For part (b) we have $r^{2} - d^{2} = 25 - 9 = 16$, in agreement (AG).

(d) Since $PA = PB$ means that $P$ is the midpoint of $AB$, and the perpendicular from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects that chord, the chord with midpoint $P$ is the one perpendicular to $OP$ (M1). Here $OP$ lies along the $x$-axis, so $AB$ is the vertical line $x = 3$ (A1). Then $y^{2} = 25 - 9 = 16$, so $A = (3,4)$ and $B = (3,-4)$, and $PA = PB = 4$, giving $PA \times PB = 16$, which agrees with the value $r^{2} - d^{2} = 25 - 9 = 16$ obtained in parts (b) and (c) (A1) (AG). In terms of part (b) this chord corresponds to the limiting case $s \to \infty$: the sum of the two parameters is $t_A + t_B = -\dfrac{6}{s} \to 0$, so $t_A = -t_B$ and $|t_A| = |t_B| = 4$ (AG).

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for the substitution and (M1)(A1) for the discriminant simplified to $64m^{2}+100$. The final (A1) requires the observation that $64m^{2} + 100 \ge 100 > 0$; a candidate who stops at $\Delta = 64m^{2}+100$ without drawing the conclusion forfeits it. The vertical line must be addressed separately and must be identified as *not* being one of the $L_m$; a candidate who writes 'the case $m = \infty$' is condoning a notational abuse that should be pointed out but not penalised, provided the vertical line is correctly identified as $x = 3$ with two intersection points. Accept a geometric argument in place of the discriminant, provided it establishes that $P$ is strictly inside the circle, since every line through an interior point meets the circle twice; this is a fully valid alternative earning full marks.

(b) The parametrisation must be shown to be by arc length: award (M1) for that observation explicitly, since without it $|t|$ is not the distance and the whole part collapses. Award (M1) for the substitution, (A1) for the simplified quadratic, and (A1) for the Vieta step. The final (A1) requires both the product of the roots and the conversion from $|t_A t_B|$ to $PA \times PB$; a candidate who obtains $t_A t_B = -16$ and stops has not answered the question. Note that $PA \times PB = 16$ is the absolute value, since the roots are of opposite sign; a candidate who reports $-16$ forfeits the final (A1).

(c) The rotation argument must be stated; a candidate who places $P$ on the $x$-axis without saying that this is without loss of generality earns (M1) but should be reminded of it. The key marks are (M1) for the unit-vector parametrisation, (A1) for the quadratic in $t$, and (A1) for the Vieta step together with the observation that $d < r$ makes the product negative and hence $P$ interior to the chord. A candidate who argues from the intersecting-chords theorem in reverse -- computing $r^{2}-d^{2}$ as the product for the diameter and asserting it is the same for all chords -- has assumed what is to be proved and earns at most the (M1) for the diameter case.

(d) Award (M1) for the perpendicular-from-centre argument or an equivalent statement that $P$ is the midpoint, (A1) for the chord $x = 3$ with the two endpoints, and (A1) for the verification. The observation that this is the limit $s \to \infty$ of part (b) earns the final (AG); a candidate who instead sets $m = 0$ has confused the horizontal with the vertical chord and should be checked carefully.

Why this question is hard

The item is built around a quantity that does not depend on the thing everyone expects it to depend on. The product $PA \times PB$ is computed along a chord, the chord has a direction, and the direction is the only free parameter in the problem -- so the overwhelming prior is that the answer is a function of the direction. It is not. The product is $r^{2} - d^{2}$, which mentions the radius and the position of $P$ and nothing else.

Part (b) is designed so that a student who does not see the structure can still get the answer, by brute force, and then be unable to explain it. Parameterising by $x$ and substituting produces a quadratic whose roots are the $x$-coordinates of $A$ and $B$, and the distances require square roots of expressions in those roots; the product of the two distances is a messy ratio of surds in $m$ which does, in fact, simplify to $16$, but the simplification is unpleasant and gives no insight. Parameterising by arc length changes the problem: because the direction vector is normalised, the parameter $t$ *is* the signed distance, the two distances become the two roots of a quadratic, and their product is just the constant term. The constant term is $-16$, and it never mentioned $m$ at all.

Part (c) is the generalisation, and it is worth seeing how little changes. Replacing $9$ by $d^{2}$ and $25$ by $r^{2}$, the same substitution gives $t^{2} + 2d\cos\theta\,t + (d^{2}-r^{2}) = 0$. The direction enters only through the coefficient of $t$ -- that is, through the *sum* of the roots -- and never through the constant term. So the invariance is structural: the product of the two segments is the constant term, and the constant term is fixed by where $P$ is and how big the circle is. This is the theorem usually called the power of a point, and the reason it is worth deriving rather than quoting is that the derivation shows exactly which part of the quadratic carries the direction information.

Part (d) closes the loop by asking for the one chord that the invariance does not distinguish: the chord bisected at $P$. Since the perpendicular from the centre bisects a chord, that chord is perpendicular to $OP$; in the parameterisation of part (b) it is the limit in which the sum of the roots vanishes, $t_A = -t_B$, and the two segments are equal at $4$. A student who has understood that the direction affects only the sum of the roots will find this immediate, and a student who has only brute-forced part (b) will have to compute it from scratch.

Challenge lever
The student is asked about the product PA x PB for a chord through a fixed interior point, and the natural response is to parameterise the chord by its gradient, find the two intersection points, compute the two distances, and multiply. This is entirely doable and produces an expression in the gradient that looks irreducibly messy -- a ratio of surds -- and the student must then discover, by simplifying or by evaluating, that the gradient cancels completely. The variable the student reaches for, the direction of the chord, is not what sets the answer. The efficient route makes this structural rather than miraculous: parametrising by arc length along the chord turns the two distances into the two roots of a quadratic, and their product into the constant term, which never mentions the direction at all.

Inspiration
Chinese 高考 / 强基计划 genre on 解析几何 (analytic geometry) with an invariance: a chord through a fixed point of a conic is parameterised by its direction, and a product or sum of the resulting segment lengths turns out not to depend on the direction -- the classical power-of-a-point relation, usually posed for a circle and asked as a proof of invariance.
Adaptation
The source genre supplies the parametrisation and the invariance. Three of the permitted changes were made. First, the circle and the point were fixed at $x^{2}+y^{2}=25$ and $P=(3,0)$, chosen so that $r^{2}-d^{2}=16$ is a perfect square; this makes the bisected chord of part (d) have segments of length exactly $4$ and therefore makes the invariance checkable by hand, whereas a generic point would give a surd and hide the arithmetic. Second, the parametrisation by arc length was made explicit in the statement of part (b) rather than left for the student to invent, because the Chinese sources typically parameterise by $x$-coordinate and then simplify a surd ratio, which tests algebra rather than structure. Third, a part was added -- part (d) -- asking for the chord bisected at $P$, which is the one chord the invariance cannot distinguish and which is therefore the natural place to test whether the student has understood which quantity carries the direction. The context and all data are original.
Verification
The discriminant of the line-circle intersection was simplified symbolically to 64m^2 + 100 and checked to be positive for m = -50..50. The chord product was computed two ways. First, by brute force: for m = 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10 the two intersection points were found from the x-quadratic and the product of the two distances was computed directly, giving 16 to ten decimal places in every case. Second, by the arc-length parametrisation: the quadratic in t was derived symbolically as t^2 + (6/s)t - 16 = 0, whose constant term is -16 for every m. The vertical chord x = 3 was checked separately, giving endpoints (3, +/-4) and a product of 16. For m = 1 the exact algebra gives distances (sqrt(41)-3)/2 and (sqrt(41)+3)/2, whose product is exactly 8, so the chord product is 2 x 8 = 16 exactly. The general result r^2 - d^2 was checked at d = 0 (giving 25) and d = 3 (giving 16).
