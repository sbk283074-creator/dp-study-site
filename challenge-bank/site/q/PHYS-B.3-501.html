B.3 Gas laws: the temperature along a straight-line change on a pressure-volume diagram

Physics HL · PHYS-B.3-501

Theme B: The particulate nature of matterB.3 (Gas laws: the ideal gas equation, the pressure-volume, pressure-temperature and volume-temperature relationships for a fixed mass of gas, absolute temperature, and the reading of pressure-volume graphs)P1 · Section A5 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedCalculateDetermine

Question

The figure is a pressure-volume diagram for a fixed amount of an ideal gas taken through three successive changes. At state A the pressure is $1.0 \times 10^{5}$ Pa, the volume is $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ m$^{3}$ and the temperature is 300 K. In the first change the gas expands at constant pressure to state B, where the volume is $6.0 \times 10^{-3}$ m$^{3}$. In the second change it is compressed along the straight line shown to state C, where the pressure is $3.0 \times 10^{5}$ Pa and the volume is $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ m$^{3}$. In the third change it is cooled at constant volume back to state A. The gas behaves ideally throughout, and the molar gas constant is $R = 8.31$ J K$^{-1}$ mol$^{-1}$. Questions (a) to (e) all refer to this gas. Each is worth one mark and numerical answers should be given to three significant figures.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    Calculate the temperature of the gas when it is at state B.

    (Calculate)
  2. [1 mark](b)

    Calculate the temperature of the gas when it is at state C.

    (Calculate)
  3. [1 mark](c)

    Calculate the amount of substance of gas, in moles, that is present in the container.

    (Calculate)
  4. [1 mark](d)

    Determine the greatest temperature reached by the gas at any point during the three changes.

    (Determine)
  5. [1 mark](e)

    Calculate the temperature of the gas at the point on the change from B to C at which its pressure is $2.50 \times 10^{5}$ Pa.

    (Calculate)

Reveal the answer

(a) C. For a fixed amount of gas at constant pressure, Charles' law gives
$V \propto T$, so $T_{B} = 300 \times (6.0\times10^{-3})/(2.0\times10^{-3}) = 900$ K.
Note that the ratio has to be taken between absolute temperatures and volumes; no
Celsius conversion is involved because both are already absolute. (A1)

(b) A. The three gas laws combine into $\tfrac{pV}{T} = \text{constant}$, so it
is the product $pV$ that settles the temperature and not either variable alone. At C,
$pV = (3.0\times10^{5})(2.0\times10^{-3}) = 600$ Pa m$^{3}$, and at A,
$pV = 200$ Pa m$^{3}$. The temperature at C is therefore
$300 \times 600/200 = 900$ K. (A1)

(c) D. Rearranging $pV = nRT$ at A,
$n = (1.0\times10^{5})(2.0\times10^{-3})/(8.31 \times 300) = 200/2493 = 0.0802$ mol.
State A is the only state whose temperature is stated, so it is the only one the
equation can be applied to directly. (A1)

(d) B. The second change is a straight line from B, at
$(V,\ p) = (6.0\times10^{-3},\ 1.0\times10^{5})$, to C, at
$(2.0\times10^{-3},\ 3.0\times10^{5})$. Its gradient is
$(3.0 - 1.0)\times10^{5}/(2.0 - 6.0)\times10^{-3} = -5.0\times10^{7}$ Pa m$^{-3}$, so
$p = 4.0\times10^{5} - 5.0\times10^{7}V$. The temperature follows $pV$, and
$pV = 4.0\times10^{5}V - 5.0\times10^{7}V^{2}$ is a downward-opening quadratic whose
stationary point satisfies $4.0\times10^{5} = 1.0\times10^{8}V$, giving
$V = 4.0\times10^{-3}$ m$^{3}$ — exactly midway along the segment. There
$p = 2.0\times10^{5}$ Pa, $pV = 800$ Pa m$^{3}$ and
$T = 300 \times 800/200 = 1200$ K. Both endpoints give only 600 Pa m$^{3}$. (A1)

(e) C. Substituting $p = 2.50\times10^{5}$ Pa into the same line gives
$V = (4.0\times10^{5} - 2.50\times10^{5})/(5.0\times10^{7}) = 3.0\times10^{-3}$
m$^{3}$, so $pV = 750$ Pa m$^{3}$ and $T = 300 \times 750/200 = 1125$ K, which is
1130 K to three significant figures. (A1)

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking, and no method marks: the key is the answer.

(a) Accept 900 K. An answer of 100 K has used the volume ratio the wrong way round;
300 K treats the change as isothermal and 1800 K applies the volume ratio twice.

(b) Accept 900 K. Do not accept 2700 K, which applies the pressure ratio and the
volume ratio separately — the single product $pV$ fixes the temperature. An answer
of 600 K has read the product as a temperature. Note for the record that B and C are
at the same temperature, which is why neither of them can be the maximum.

(c) Accept 0.0802 mol. Allow answers in the range 0.0800 to 0.0804. An answer of
0.0267 mol shows the 900 K of part (a) carried into the denominator, and 0.2407 mol
shows the volume of state B carried into the numerator; 0.0401 mol is a factor of two.

(d) Accept 1200 K and no other value. This is the discriminating question of the
cluster, and a candidate who answers 900 K has simply read off the hottest labelled
state. An answer of 800 K has found the correct maximum value of $pV$ and then failed
to scale it, which is a near miss worth crediting in discussion even though it scores
nothing here. Allow 1200 K whether it is reached by differentiating the quadratic, by
completing the square or by tabulating $pV$ along the line.

(e) Accept 1130 K; accept 1125 K. An answer of 750 K has read $pV$ as a temperature,
and 1200 K is the maximum rather than the value at the stated pressure.

Why this question is hard

The single idea behind this cluster is that the temperature of an ideal gas is
proportional to the product $pV$, and that a straight line drawn on a $p$–$V$
diagram is therefore not a line of constant anything.

An isotherm of an ideal gas is a rectangular hyperbola, $pV = \text{constant}$. A
straight segment cuts across a whole family of those hyperbolae. It starts on one,
passes through higher ones, and comes back down to another — so if the two endpoints
happen to lie on the same isotherm, as B and C do here at $pV = 600$ Pa m³, then the
line must have climbed through every isotherm above 600 in between and returned. The
maximum is unavoidable; only its position has to be found.

Finding it is a one-line calculation once the line is written down. With
$p = a + bV$ the product is $pV = aV + bV^{2}$, a parabola, and because the segment
runs downhill $b$ is negative, so the parabola opens downwards and has a genuine
interior maximum at $V = -a/2b$. For these numbers that is $4.0\times10^{-3}$ m³,
which happens to be the midpoint of the segment — but it is the stationary point of
the quadratic that makes it the answer, not any symmetry of the diagram. The
temperature there is 1200 K, a third above the 900 K at either end, and no labelled
point on the figure comes close to marking it.

Two habits make candidates miss this. The first is reading a graph by its vertices:
the instinct that the extreme value of anything must occur at a corner is sound for
a polytope and wrong for a curve. The second is treating the two variables
separately — "the pressure went up by three and the volume by three, so ..." — when
it is only their product that the gas law knows about. Part (b) is built to catch
exactly that, because C has both a larger pressure and a different volume from A, and
only $pV$ gives the right factor of three.

Part (e) is the same computation at a point the candidate chooses, which is why it
sits last: by then the line equation has already been written for part (d), and the
question is whether it can now be used in the other direction.
