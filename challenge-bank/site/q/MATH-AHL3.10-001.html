A compound-angle equation whose squared version invents solutions

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL3.10-001

AHL 3.10 (compound angle identities), AHL 3.11 (relationships between trigonometric functions)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatHenceExplainDetermine

Question

Consider the equation $$\cos 2x=\sqrt{2}\cos\left(x+\frac{\pi}{4}\right).$$

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $\sqrt{2}\cos\left(x+\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)=\cos x-\sin x$ and that $\cos 2x=(\cos x-\sin x)(\cos x+\sin x)$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Hence solve the equation $\cos 2x=\sqrt{2}\cos\left(x+\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)$ for $0\le x<2\pi$, giving each answer as a multiple of $\pi$.

    (Hence)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    A student solves the equation by squaring both sides. Explain how this leads to $\sin 2x(\sin 2x-1)=0$, and identify the extraneous solutions that the squaring introduces.

    (Explain)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the general solution of the equation, and hence the number of solutions that lie in the interval $-2\pi\le x\le2\pi$.

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) By the compound-angle formula, $$\sqrt{2}\cos\left(x+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\sqrt2\left(\cos x\cos\frac{\pi}{4}-\sin x\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\sqrt2\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}(\cos x-\sin x)=\cos x-\sin x.$$ (M1)(A1) Also, by the double-angle formula and the difference of two squares, $$\cos 2x=\cos^{2}x-\sin^{2}x=(\cos x-\sin x)(\cos x+\sin x).$$ (A1) *(AG)*

(b) Substituting the results of (a), $$(\cos x-\sin x)(\cos x+\sin x)=\cos x-\sin x,$$ so $$(\cos x-\sin x)\bigl(\cos x+\sin x-1\bigr)=0.$$ (M1)

Case 1: $\cos x=\sin x$, that is $\tan x=1$, giving $x=\frac{\pi}{4}$ or $x=\frac{5\pi}{4}$ in $[0,2\pi)$. (A1)

Case 2: $\cos x+\sin x=1$. Writing the left side as $\sqrt2\cos\left(x-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$ gives $\cos\left(x-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, so $x-\frac{\pi}{4}=\pm\frac{\pi}{4}+2k\pi$. (M1) Hence $x=0$ or $x=\frac{\pi}{2}$ in $[0,2\pi)$. (A1)

The solutions are $x=0$, $x=\frac{\pi}{4}$, $x=\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $x=\frac{5\pi}{4}$.

(c) Squaring both sides gives $\cos^{2}2x=2\cos^{2}\left(x+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$. (M1) Using $\cos^{2}2x=1-\sin^{2}2x$ and $2\cos^{2}\theta=1+\cos2\theta$ with $\theta=x+\frac{\pi}{4}$, the right-hand side is $$1+\cos\left(2x+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=1-\sin2x.$$ (A1) Hence $1-\sin^{2}2x=1-\sin2x$, that is $\sin^{2}2x=\sin2x$, or $$\sin2x\,(\sin2x-1)=0.$$ (A1)

The squared equation therefore admits $x=0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2}$ from $\sin2x=0$ and $x=\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{5\pi}{4}$ from $\sin2x=1$. (A1) Of these, $x=\pi$ and $x=\frac{3\pi}{2}$ do not solve the original equation: at $x=\pi$ the left side is $1$ and the right side is $-1$, while at $x=\frac{3\pi}{2}$ the left side is $-1$ and the right side is $1$. They are extraneous and must be rejected.

(d) From (b), $\cos x=\sin x$ gives $x=\frac{\pi}{4}+k\pi$ for $k\in\mathbb{Z}$. (M1) Also $\cos x+\sin x=1$ gives $x=2k\pi$ or $x=\frac{\pi}{2}+2k\pi$ for $k\in\mathbb{Z}$. (A1)

In $[-2\pi,2\pi]$ the family $\frac{\pi}{4}+k\pi$ contributes $k=-2,-1,0,1$, that is $-\frac{7\pi}{4},-\frac{3\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{5\pi}{4}$, four values; the family $2k\pi$ contributes $-2\pi,0,2\pi$, three values; and the family $\frac{\pi}{2}+2k\pi$ contributes $-\frac{3\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}$, two values. (M1) These nine values are all distinct, so there are $9$ solutions. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for the compound-angle expansion and the (A1) for evaluating $\cos\frac{\pi}{4}=\sin\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$; the second (A1) is for the double-angle factorisation. A candidate who quotes $\cos2x=1-2\sin^{2}x$ instead of the factorised form has not produced the factor needed in (b), so the second (A1) should not be given unless the factorisation is also shown.

(b) The (M1) is for setting the two expressions equal and factorising. A candidate who divides by $(\cos x-\sin x)$ and so loses $x=\frac{\pi}{4}$ and $x=\frac{5\pi}{4}$ forfeits the first (A1). The second case needs the compound-angle rewrite $\cos x+\sin x=\sqrt2\cos\left(x-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$; award (M1) for this and (A1) for the two roots. Accept any order of the four solutions.

(c) All four marks depend on the algebra being shown. The (A1) for $2\cos^{2}\left(x+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=1-\sin2x$ requires the double-angle identity applied with the correct angle; a common slip is to write $\cos\left(2x+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$ instead of $\cos\left(2x+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$. The final (A1) requires the two extraneous values to be named and tested against the original equation; merely saying that squaring can introduce roots earns nothing.

(d) The general solutions must carry the $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ parameter; accept $\tan x=1$ written as $x=\frac{\pi}{4}+k\pi$. The count of $9$ is earned only if the three families are enumerated over the closed interval. A candidate who takes the four solutions of (b) and doubles them, or who omits the endpoints $x=-2\pi$ and $x=2\pi$, loses the final (A1).

Why this question is hard

The equation is dressed in a compound angle, and the first move is to expand $\sqrt2\cos\left(x+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$ with the compound-angle identity. That expansion is not cosmetic: it produces $\cos x-\sin x$, which is exactly the factor that $\cos2x$ contains by the difference of two squares. The whole equation therefore becomes $(\cos x-\sin x)(\cos x+\sin x)=(\cos x-\sin x)$, and factorising reduces it to two simple cases. This is the structural reason that $\sqrt2$ and $\frac{\pi}{4}$ appear together: they are chosen so that the compound-angle expansion matches the double-angle factorisation.

The first trap is the common factor $\cos x-\sin x$. Dividing by it discards the two solutions where it vanishes, $x=\frac{\pi}{4}$ and $x=\frac{5\pi}{4}$. Factorising keeps them. This is the standard error in this family of equations, and part (b) is arranged so that a careless cancellation still yields two of the four answers, which makes the mistake easy to miss.

Part (c) exposes a second trap, squaring. Squaring both sides is superficially attractive because both $\cos2x$ and $\cos\left(x+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$ invite a double-angle identity. It does give a solvable equation, $\sin2x(\sin2x-1)=0$, but squaring is not reversible and enlarges the solution set. The two values $x=\pi$ and $x=\frac{3\pi}{2}$ solve the squared equation because the two sides have equal magnitude there but opposite signs, so the residual is $\pm2$ rather than $0$. Identifying and testing these is the point of the part.

Part (d) asks for the general solution and then counts over a closed interval of length $4\pi$. The three families $\frac{\pi}{4}+k\pi$, $2k\pi$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}+2k\pi$ must each be enumerated; both endpoints $x=\pm2\pi$ belong to the second family, so omitting either loses a solution. The classic wrong turns are cancelling the common factor, accepting the roots introduced by squaring, and forgetting either the parameter $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ or the closed endpoints.

Challenge lever
The equation must be rewritten with the compound-angle identity and then factorised, but the tempting route of squaring both sides produces the extra equation $\sin 2x(\sin 2x-1)=0$, so two roots that solve the squared equation but have the wrong sign must be identified and rejected.
