5.11 Two unbounded regions, one of which encloses a finite area, decided by the exponent

Question

The figure shows the graphs of $y = \dfrac{1}{x^{2}}$ and $y = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ for $x \geq 1$. In each panel the region between the curve and the $x$-axis, from $x = 1$ onwards, is shaded. Both shaded regions extend without limit to the right.

(a) Show that the area of the region under $y = \dfrac{1}{x^{2}}$ between $x = 1$ and $x = t$ is $1 - \dfrac{1}{t}$, and hence state the area of the shaded region in the left-hand panel.

(b) Show that the area of the region under $y = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ between $x = 1$ and $x = t$ is $2\sqrt{t} - 2$, and explain what this shows about the shaded region in the right-hand panel.

(c) Both shaded regions are unbounded in extent. Explain, using the two antiderivatives you have found, which property of the integrand decides whether such a region has a finite area.

Reveal the answer

(a) Writing $x^{-2}$ and integrating, $$\int_{1}^{t} x^{-2}\,dx = \left[-x^{-1}\right]_{1}^{t} = -\frac{1}{t} + 1 = 1 - \frac{1}{t} \;(M1)(A1).$$

As $t$ increases without bound the term $\dfrac{1}{t}$ tends to $0$, so the partial area increases towards the value $1$. The shaded region is unbounded in extent but encloses exactly one square unit: to reach an area of $0.99$ the region must be extended to $t = 100$, to reach $0.999$ to $t = 1000$, and no finite $t$ ever achieves the full unit (A1). Each doubling of the extent adds less than half of what the previous doubling added, and the total therefore settles.

(b) Writing $x^{-1/2}$ and integrating, $$\int_{1}^{t} x^{-1/2}\,dx = \left[2x^{1/2}\right]_{1}^{t} = 2\sqrt{t} - 2 \;(M1)(A1).$$

Here the partial area increases without limit as $t$ increases, because $2\sqrt{t} - 2$ does: extending the region to $t = 100$ gives an area of $18$, to $t = 10\,000$ an area of $198$, and to pass an area of $200$ the region must be taken out to $t = 10\,201$. There is no number that the area approaches from below, so the shaded region in the right-hand panel has no finite area (A1). The distinction between the two panels is therefore not that one region is longer than the other -- both are equally long -- but that one of them has a measurable area and the other does not.

(c) Both integrands are powers, $x^{-p}$, and both antiderivatives are powers, $x^{1-p}$ divided by $1-p$ (M1). What decides the outcome is the sign of the exponent $1 - p$ in the antiderivative. When $p > 1$ that exponent is negative, so the antiderivative is a negative power of $t$ and it tends to $0$ as $t$ grows; the area then settles at the finite value $\dfrac{1}{p-1}$, which for $p = 2$ is $1$, and for $p = 1.5$ would be $2$. When $p \leq 1$ the exponent is positive or zero, so the antiderivative grows with $t$ and there is no finite limit; for $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ it grows like $\sqrt{t}$ (A1). The property that decides, then, is how fast the integrand decays: a finite tail requires the integrand to fall away faster than $\dfrac{1}{x}$, and the two panels differ only in that their exponents are $2$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}$, one above the threshold and one below it.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for integrating $x^{-2}$ rather than $x^{2}$, or for any correct antiderivative of $x^{-2}$; (A1) for the evaluation $1 - \dfrac{1}{t}$ with both limits used correctly; (A1) for the limit $1$, with the behaviour of $\dfrac{1}{t}$ stated. Three fields; credit two, and a candidate who writes down $1 - \dfrac{1}{t}$ and then states the area as $1$ without a word about the limit earns two of the three.

(b) (M1) for integrating $x^{-1/2}$; (A1) for $2\sqrt{t} - 2$; (A1) for the conclusion that the area is unbounded, with the reason that the expression grows without limit rather than settling. The conclusion is the mark that matters: an answer of '$\infty$' with no statement about the growth is worth the first two fields only.

(c) (M1) for comparing the antiderivatives of $x^{-p}$ and identifying the sign of $1 - p$ as the deciding feature; (A1) for expressing the criterion as $p > 1$, equivalently that the integrand must decay faster than $\dfrac{1}{x}$, and for observing that the two panels sit on opposite sides of that threshold. Do not accept a criterion stated in terms of the curves 'getting closer to the axis', since both of them do.

Why this question is hard

Two curves that both fall towards a horizontal axis, two regions that both run for ever, one area of exactly one and one area of nothing. The pair is the standard way into improper integrals and it is worth meeting as a picture before it is met as a definition, because the picture makes the surprise legible: nothing visible in the right-hand panel announces that its area is infinite, and nothing visible in the left-hand panel announces that its area is one.

The decision is made entirely by the antiderivative, and the antiderivative is made entirely by the exponent. Integrating $x^{-p}$ raises the power by one, so the behaviour at the upper limit is governed by whether $1 - p$ is negative or positive, which is the same question as whether $p$ is greater than one. This is why $\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}$ is the standard example of a convergent tail and $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is the standard example of a divergent one: one sits just above the threshold and the other exactly on it. The right-hand panel here uses $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ rather than $p = 1$ on purpose, because $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ is visibly a different curve from $\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}$ while $\dfrac{1}{x}$ would have invited the comparison with the logarithm, which is a separate story.

There is a temptation to reason from the width of the region, and it has to be resisted explicitly. Both regions are infinitely wide, so width cannot distinguish them; what distinguishes them is how quickly the height vanishes. A region of infinite width can have a finite area provided its height falls fast enough, and 'fast enough' has a sharp boundary in this family, at the inverse square root of nothing less convenient than the index one. The correct question is not how far the region extends but whether the area accumulated from $x = 1$ to $x = t$ approaches a limit as $t$ grows, and that question is answered by looking at the antiderivative rather than at the graph.

The same criterion recurs wherever a sum or an integral is taken over an infinite range, and the name for the case that fails is the same in each setting. Recognising $p > 1$ as the condition, and being able to produce the antiderivative that shows it, is the whole of the technique.

Challenge lever
The two panels look like the same kind of object. Both curves fall towards the axis, both regions run to infinity to the right, and nothing in the picture announces that one of them settles at an area of exactly 1 while the other has no area at all. The question is settled by the antiderivative and by the sign of its exponent: integrating x to the power minus p gives x to the power one minus p, so the behaviour at the upper limit depends on whether that exponent is negative or positive, which is the same as asking whether p exceeds 1. A candidate who reasons from the picture, or who measures the width of the region instead of the height of the curve, expects both tails to be infinite and is surprised by one of them. The second panel is made only four times flatter than the first in the sense that matters, so the difference has to be traced to the exponent rather than to any gross feature of the graphs.
