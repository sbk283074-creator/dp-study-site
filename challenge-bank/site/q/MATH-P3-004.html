A sequence defined by a definite integral; squeezing and series of integrals

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-004

Topic 5: CalculusAHL 5.11 (definite integrals, analytical approach), AHL 5.13 (limits), AHL 1.8 (sum of infinite convergent geometric sequences)P319 marksdifficulty 5technology: allowedShow thatDetermineFind

Question

A sequence is defined by

$$a_n=\int_0^1\frac{x^n}{1+x}\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad n\ge 0.$$

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $a_n+a_{n-1}=\dfrac{1}{n}$ for every integer $n\ge 1$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine $a_0$ and $a_1$ exactly. Hence determine $a_2$ and $a_3$ exactly.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Show that $(a_n)$ is decreasing, and hence show that $\dfrac{1}{2(n+1)}<a_n<\dfrac{1}{2n}$ for every $n\ge 1$.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} n\,a_n$, justifying your answer by squeezing $n\,a_n$ between two expressions that tend to the same limit.

    (Determine)
  5. [5 marks](e)

    Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}a_n=\ln 2-\frac{1}{2}$.

    (Show that)

Total: 19 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

A sequence is defined by $$a_n=\int_0^1\frac{x^n}{1+x}\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad n\ge 0.$$ (a) Show that $a_n+a_{n-1}=\dfrac{1}{n}$ for every integer $n\ge 1$. [3 marks] (Show that) (b) Determine $a_0$ and $a_1$ exactly. Hence determine $a_2$ and $a_3$ exactly. [4 marks] (Determine) (c) Show that $(a_n)$ is decreasing, and hence show that $\dfrac{1}{2(n+1)}<a_n<\dfrac{1}{2n}$ for every $n\ge 1$. [4 marks] (Show that) (d) Determine $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} n\,a_n$, justifying your answer by squeezing $n\,a_n$ between two expressions that tend to the same limit. [3 marks] (Determine) (e) Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}a_n=\ln 2-\frac{1}{2}$. [5 marks] (Show that)
Reveal the answer

(a) Adding the two integrands,

$$\frac{x^n}{1+x}+\frac{x^{n-1}}{1+x}=\frac{x^{n-1}(x+1)}{1+x}=x^{n-1},$$

so $a_n+a_{n-1}=\displaystyle\int_0^1 x^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}x=\left[\frac{x^n}{n}\right]_0^1=\frac1n$. (M1)(A1)(AG)

(b) $a_0=\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{1+x}=\big[\ln(1+x)\big]_0^1=\ln2$. (A1) By (a) with $n=1$, $a_1=1-a_0=1-\ln2$. (M1)(A1) Then $a_2=\tfrac12-a_1=\tfrac12-(1-\ln2)=\ln2-\tfrac12$ and $a_3=\tfrac13-a_2=\tfrac13-\ln2+\tfrac12=\tfrac56-\ln2$. (M1)(A1)

(c) For $0\le x\le1$ we have $x^n\le x^{n-1}$, so $\dfrac{x^n}{1+x}\le\dfrac{x^{n-1}}{1+x}$ and therefore $a_n\le a_{n-1}$; the inequality is strict on $(0,1)$, so $(a_n)$ is strictly decreasing. (M1)(A1) From $a_n+a_{n-1}=\tfrac1n$ and $a_n<a_{n-1}$ we get $2a_n<\tfrac1n$, so $a_n<\tfrac{1}{2n}$. (M1) Likewise $2a_{n-1}>\tfrac1n$, so $a_{n-1}>\tfrac{1}{2n}$, and replacing $n$ by $n+1$ gives $a_n>\tfrac{1}{2(n+1)}$. (A1)(AG)

(d) Multiplying the bounds in (c) by $n$,

$$\frac{n}{2(n+1)}<n\,a_n<\frac12.$$

(M1) As $n\to\infty$ the left-hand side tends to $\tfrac12$, so by the squeeze theorem $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n\,a_n=\tfrac12$. (A1)(R1)

(e) Let $S_N=\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{n-1}a_n$. Interchanging the finite sum and the integral,

$$S_N=\int_0^1\frac{1}{1+x}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{n-1}x^n\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_0^1\frac{x\left(1-(-x)^N\right)}{(1+x)^2}\,\mathrm{d}x,$$

using the finite geometric sum. (M1)(A1) Hence

$$S_N=\int_0^1\frac{x}{(1+x)^2}\,\mathrm{d}x-(-1)^N\int_0^1\frac{x^{N+1}}{(1+x)^2}\,\mathrm{d}x.$$

The first integral is, with $u=1+x$,

$$\int_1^2\frac{u-1}{u^2}\,\mathrm{d}u=\left[\ln u+\frac1u\right]_1^2=\ln2+\frac12-1=\ln2-\frac12.$$

(M1)(A1) The remainder satisfies $\displaystyle\left|\int_0^1\frac{x^{N+1}}{(1+x)^2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\le\int_0^1 x^{N+1}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{N+2}\to0$, so $S_N\to\ln2-\tfrac12$. (M1)(R1)

Markscheme notes

In (a) the mark is for spotting that the sum of the two integrands collapses; a candidate who tries to integrate either term separately will not get there. In (b) allow $a_1$ found directly as $\int_0^1\left(1-\frac{1}{1+x}\right)\mathrm{d}x=1-\ln2$. In (c) the decreasing property must be justified from the integrand, not asserted. A candidate who obtains only one of the two bounds earns two of the four marks. In (d) the squeeze must be stated; simply noting that $a_n$ behaves like $1/(2n)$ is not sufficient. In (e) the interchange is legitimate only because the sum is finite, and the mark should be withheld from a candidate who writes an infinite sum under the integral without addressing convergence; the remainder estimate is required, and a solution that evaluates $\int_0^1\frac{x}{(1+x)^2}\mathrm{d}x$ alone without bounding the tail earns at most three of the five marks. In (e) an alternative rigorous route is to write the partial sum as an integral of $\frac{x(1-(-x)^N)}{(1+x)^2}$ and then let $N\to\infty$ under a dominated-convergence style bound; this is beyond the syllabus but should not be penalised if the remainder is controlled. Candidates who attempt to justify $\int\sum=\sum\int$ for the infinite series from first principles will not finish and should be advised in the report to use the finite-sum route shown here.

Why this question is hard

The sequence has no closed form for general n — $\int_0^1\frac{x^n}{1+x}\mathrm{d}x$ alternates between expressions involving $\ln2$ and rational corrections, and no candidate can compute $a_{20}$ directly. Everything therefore has to come from the recurrence in (a), and recognising that the two integrands sum to a monomial is the whole key. Part (c) is the interesting move: the recurrence alone does not pin the sequence down, but combining it with monotonicity brackets each term between $1/(2(n+1))$ and $1/(2n)$, which is exactly enough for (d) and is a genuine squeeze rather than an asymptotic hand-wave. Part (e) is the hardest step and deliberately so: it asks the student to sum an infinite series of integrals by summing a finite geometric series first and then controlling the remainder, which is the only rigorous route available at this level. The answer $\ln2-\tfrac12\approx0.1931$ is strikingly close to $a_2$ itself, so a candidate who guesses rather than derives gets no reward. It is worth being explicit about why no closed form is available. Integrating $\frac{x^n}{1+x}$ by division gives an alternating polynomial plus a remainder of $\pm\ln2$, so the exact values really do alternate in form between $p-q\ln2$ and $p\ln2-q$; that irregularity is precisely why the recurrence, and not direct integration, has to carry the argument. A candidate who tries to evaluate $a_{20}$ directly will spend the whole item on it and produce nothing for (d) or (e), which is the intended discrimination: the question rewards the student who looks for structure before computing.
