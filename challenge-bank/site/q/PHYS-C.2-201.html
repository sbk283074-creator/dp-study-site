C.2 Intensity and intensity level from a point source, and adding a second source

Physics HL · PHYS-C.2-201

Theme C: Wave behaviourC.2 Wave model (intensity of a point source; intensity level in decibels; superposition of incoherent sources)P212 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateCalculateDetermineDetermine

Question

A small loudspeaker radiates sound uniformly in all directions and may be treated as a point source emitting a total acoustic power of $P = 0.50\ \mathrm{W}$. Take the threshold of hearing as $I_{0} = 1.0 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ and the intensity level as $L = 10\log_{10}(I/I_{0})$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the intensity of the sound at a distance of $2.0\ \mathrm{m}$ from the loudspeaker.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the intensity level at that distance, in decibels.

    (Calculate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    A second, identical loudspeaker is placed at the same position and emits the same power. The two sources are incoherent. Determine the new intensity level, and state the increase in decibels.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine the distance from the single loudspeaker at which the intensity level is $90\ \mathrm{dB}$.

    (Determine)

Total: 12 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

(a) Calculate the intensity of the sound at a distance of $2.0\ \mathrm{m}$ from the loudspeaker. [3 marks] (Calculate) (b) Calculate the intensity level at that distance, in decibels. [3 marks] (Calculate) (c) A second, identical loudspeaker is placed at the same position and emits the same power. The two sources are incoherent. Determine the new intensity level, and state the increase in decibels. [3 marks] (Determine) (d) Determine the distance from the single loudspeaker at which the intensity level is $90\ \mathrm{dB}$. [3 marks] (Determine)
Reveal the answer

(a) A point source spreads its power over a sphere of area $4\pi r^{2}$ (M1), so
$$I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^{2}} = \frac{0.50}{4\pi (2.0)^{2}} = \frac{0.50}{50.3} = 9.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} \tag{A1}$$

The area is $4\pi(2.0)^{2} = 50.3\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$ (A1).

(b) The ratio to the threshold is
$$\frac{I}{I_{0}} = \frac{9.947 \times 10^{-3}}{1.0 \times 10^{-12}} = 9.9 \times 10^{9} \quad\text{(M1)}$$

$$L = 10\log_{10}(9.9 \times 10^{9}) = 10 \times 9.997 = 100\ \mathrm{dB} \tag{A1(A1)}$$

so $L \approx 1.0 \times 10^{2}\ \mathrm{dB}$ to two significant figures.

(c) Intensities of incoherent sources add, so the new intensity is $2I$ (M1):
$$I' = 2 \times 9.947 \times 10^{-3} = 1.99 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$$

$$L' = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{2I}{I_{0}}\right) = 10\log_{10}2 + 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{I}{I_{0}}\right) = 3.01 + 99.98 = 103\ \mathrm{dB} \tag{A1}$$

The increase is therefore
$$\Delta L = 10\log_{10}2 = 3.0\ \mathrm{dB} \tag{A1}$$

Doubling the acoustic power adds a fixed $3.0\ \mathrm{dB}$, whatever the original level.

(d) The level falls by $20\log_{10}(r/r_{1})$ when the distance increases from $r_{1}$ to $r$, because the intensity falls as $1/r^{2}$ and the logarithm brings down a factor of two (M1). Requiring $L = 90\ \mathrm{dB}$:
$$99.98 - 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{r}{2.0}\right) = 90 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{r}{2.0}\right) = 9.98$$

$$\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{r}{2.0}\right) = 0.499 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{r}{2.0} = 3.16 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = 6.3\ \mathrm{m} \tag{A1(A1)}$$

Check: at $6.3\ \mathrm{m}$, $I = 0.50/(4\pi \times 39.7) = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, which is $10^{9}I_{0}$ and gives exactly $90\ \mathrm{dB}$.

Two results from this question are worth carrying away. Doubling the power of an incoherent source adds exactly three decibels whatever the starting level, because what doubles is the intensity and the level is logarithmic in intensity. And the level falls by about six decibels for every doubling of the distance from a point source, because the intensity falls by a factor of four and ten times the logarithm of four is six. Those two numbers between them cover most of the practical reasoning about sound levels, and neither can be reached without going through the intensity.

Markscheme notes

(a) M1 for the $4\pi r^{2}$ sphere, A1 for $9.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, A1 for the area $50.3\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$. A candidate who uses $\pi r^{2}$ has treated the source as a disc and scores 0.

(b) M1 for forming the ratio $I/I_{0}$, A1 for the logarithm, A1 for $100\ \mathrm{dB}$. Accept $99.98$, $100$ or $1.0 \times 10^{2}$.

(c) The mark is for adding intensities, not levels. A candidate who writes $100 + 100 = 200\ \mathrm{dB}$ scores 0 for the part; a candidate who writes $100 + 3 = 103\ \mathrm{dB}$ without justification scores A1 only. The general result $\Delta L = 10\log_{10}2$ should be credited even if the numerical level is wrong by follow-through.

(d) M1 for the $20\log_{10}$ distance relation (not $10\log_{10}$), A1 for the ratio $3.16$, A1 for $6.3\ \mathrm{m}$. A candidate who uses $10\log_{10}(r/r_{1})$ will get $r = 2.0 \times 10^{0.998} = 20\ \mathrm{m}$ and should be given the M1 only.

Forfeits: using $\log$ base $e$ instead of base $10$; omitting the factor $10$ in the definition of the level. Condonation: an answer of $103.0\ \mathrm{dB}$ with the increase quoted as $3.0\ \mathrm{dB}$ and the level quoted as $100\ \mathrm{dB}$ should not be penalised for the small rounding inconsistency.

Why this question is hard

Two separate traps are laid here, and they fail in opposite directions. The first is that sound levels do not add: two identical sources at $100\ \mathrm{dB}$ produce $103\ \mathrm{dB}$, not $200\ \mathrm{dB}$, because the decibel scale is logarithmic and what doubles is the intensity. This is the single most commonly misremembered fact in the whole topic, and the number $200$ is so obviously wrong to anyone who has stood next to two loudspeakers that it is a useful self-check.

The second trap is the factor in front of the logarithm. Intensity level is defined with a factor of $10$, so an intensity ratio of $10^{9}$ is $90\ \mathrm{dB}$. Distance, however, enters through $20\log_{10}(r)$, not $10\log_{10}(r)$, because the intensity depends on $r^{2}$ and the logarithm of a square is twice the logarithm. A student who carries the factor $10$ across from the level definition gets $20\ \mathrm{m}$ instead of $6.3\ \mathrm{m}$ in the final part, an error of a factor of three that is easy to make and easy to miss.

The question is sequenced so that the student has to move in both directions across the logarithmic bridge: forward in (b), where an intensity becomes a level, and backward in (d), where a level becomes a distance. Part (c) sits between them and tests the one rule that cannot be applied by pattern: that superposition of intensities is linear but superposition of levels is not.

The self-check built into the last line of part (d) is deliberate. A student who finds a distance of about six metres can put it back into the inverse-square law and recover ninety decibels exactly, which confirms the factor of twenty without having to trust it. That is the habit the question is trying to install: a logarithmic result is easy to get wrong by a constant factor, and the constant factor is always visible if the answer is substituted back.
