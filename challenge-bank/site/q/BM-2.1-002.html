2.1 Labour turnover as a weighted aggregate: recovering a missing department and targeting the right one

Business management SL · BM-2.1-002

Unit 2: Human resource management2.1 Introduction to human resource management (workforce planning, labour turnover, absenteeism); 6.7 Descriptive statisticsP2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateShow thatDetermineEvaluate

Question

Haldane Logistics employs $250$ staff in three departments. The workforce plan records
the number of staff who left during the year, but the entry for department B has been lost.

| Department | Number employed | Number leaving during the year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 120 | 9 |
| B | 80 | not recorded |
| C | 50 | 11 |

The company's overall labour turnover for the year is known to have been $14.4\%$. Each leaver
costs the business $\pounds 3{,}500$ to replace, covering recruitment, training and lost output.

Labour turnover is calculated as

$$\text{labour turnover} = \frac{\text{number of staff leaving during the year}}
{\text{number of staff employed}} \times 100.$$

(a) Calculate the number of staff who left department B during the year. \tag{3}

(b) Calculate the labour turnover rate for each department and confirm the overall rate of
$14.4\%$. \tag{5}

(c) Show that the overall rate is not the average of the three departmental rates, and explain why
the difference arises. \tag{4}

(d) The human resources director proposes an overall target of $10\%$ and must choose between two
interventions: reducing department A's turnover to $5\%$, or reducing department C's turnover to
$10\%$. Evaluate which should be prioritised. \tag{4}

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the number of staff who left department B during the year.

    (Calculate)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Calculate the labour turnover rate for each department and confirm the overall rate of $14.4$ per cent.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Show that the overall rate is not the average of the three departmental rates, and explain why the difference arises.

    (Show that)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    The human resources director proposes an overall target of $10$ per cent and must choose between reducing department A turnover to $5$ per cent and reducing department C turnover to $10$ per cent. Evaluate which should be prioritised.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) Overall turnover of $14.4\%$ on $250$ staff gives a total of

$$0.144 \times 250 = 36 \text{ leavers} \tag{M1}$$

of which $9$ came from A and $11$ from C, so department B accounts for (M1)

$$36 - 9 - 11 = 16 \text{ leavers} \tag{A1}$$

(b) Applying the formula to each department in turn (M1):

$$\text{A}: \frac{9}{120} \times 100 = 7.5\%, \qquad
\text{B}: \frac{16}{80} \times 100 = 20.0\%, \qquad
\text{C}: \frac{11}{50} \times 100 = 22.0\% \tag{A1A1A1}$$

The overall rate is the total leaving divided by the total employed, not the average of the three
percentages:

$$\frac{9 + 16 + 11}{120 + 80 + 50} \times 100 = \frac{36}{250} \times 100 = 14.4\% \tag{M1A1}$$

which confirms the figure given.

(c) The simple average of the three departmental rates is

$$\frac{7.5 + 20.0 + 22.0}{3} = \frac{49.5}{3} = 16.5\% \tag{M1A1}$$

against a true overall rate of $14.4\%$, so the average overstates turnover by $2.1$ percentage
points (A1). The reason is that the departments are not the same size. The average gives each
department's rate a weight of one third, but department A actually employs $48\%$ of the workforce
and department C only $20\%$ (A1). The overall rate is a weighted mean in which each department's
rate is weighted by its headcount:

$$\frac{120(7.5) + 80(20.0) + 50(22.0)}{250}
= \frac{900 + 1{,}600 + 1{,}100}{250} = \frac{3{,}600}{250} = 14.4\%$$

The $7.5\%$ earned by the largest department pulls the figure down more than the $22\%$ earned by
the smallest pulls it up, which is exactly the effect the unweighted average discards.

(d) Reducing department A to $5\%$ means $0.05 \times 120 = 6$ leavers, a saving of $3$ (M1). Total
leavers fall to $33$ and the overall rate becomes

$$\frac{33}{250} \times 100 = 13.2\% \tag{A1}$$

an improvement of $1.2$ percentage points, at a saving of $3 \times 3{,}500 = \pounds 10{,}500$.

Reducing department C to $10\%$ means $0.10 \times 50 = 5$ leavers, a saving of $6$ (M1). Total
leavers fall to $30$ and the overall rate becomes

$$\frac{30}{250} \times 100 = 12.0\% \tag{A1}$$

an improvement of $2.4$ percentage points, at a saving of $6 \times 3{,}500 = \pounds 21{,}000$.

Department C should be prioritised. It delivers twice the reduction in the overall rate and twice
the cost saving, from $20\%$ of the workforce rather than $48\%$ (A1). The reason is that the
improvement depends on two things multiplied together — the size of the department and the size of
the cut — and department C's cut is $12$ percentage points against department A's $2.5$.

Two qualifications belong in a full answer. Neither intervention reaches the $10\%$ target on its
own: both together give $12.0\%$, and hitting $10\%$ would require $25$ leavers in total, so a
further five must come from somewhere. And the arithmetic is silent on why people leave. Department
C's $22\%$ may reflect working conditions, pay or management, and if the underlying cause is not
addressed the reduction may prove temporary; conversely, if department A's low turnover reflects a
genuinely well-managed operation, cutting it further may be the cheapest of all. The numbers say
where the leverage is, not what to do about it (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for converting 14.4% of 250 to 36 leavers, one for subtracting the known leavers, one for the figure 16. (b) One mark for applying the formula, one for each of the three departmental rates, one for dividing total leavers by total employed. Do not award a rate calculated as an average of the others. Accept rates to one decimal place. (c) One mark for the average of 16.5%, one for the comparison with 14.4%, one for explaining that the departments differ in size, one for the weighted-mean calculation or an equivalent explanation in words. A candidate who says only 'the average is wrong' without saying why should be held at two marks. (d) One mark for the 3 leavers saved in A, one for 13.2%, one for the 6 leavers saved in C, one for 12.0%. The evaluation mark is awarded for a supported choice of C; allow A only if a cost or feasibility argument is given. Credit the 10,500 and 21,000 figures even if the candidate backs the other option. Follow-through: a candidate who finds 16 in (a) by a different route should not be penalised again in (b), provided the method is consistent.

Why this question is hard

Labour turnover is a ratio, and ratios built from parts do not behave like the parts.
Every student knows this in principle and almost nobody applies it under pressure, because the
unweighted average is quicker to compute and feels like a fair summary. This item makes the shortcut
cost something: the average gives $16.5\%$ against a true $14.4\%$, and a manager working from the
higher figure would set targets against a rate the business never had.

The naive path in part (d) is to read the three rates, see that department C is worst at $22\%$, and
then notice that department A is four times its size and conclude that A must be where the numbers
are. That is a plausible argument and it is wrong, because the gain from an intervention is the
product of two quantities rather than either one alone. Department A offers a cut of $2.5$
percentage points on $120$ staff, worth three leavers. Department C offers a cut of $12$ points on
$50$ staff, worth six. The smaller department wins by a factor of two, and it wins on cost as well
as on the headline rate, because the replacement cost attaches to leavers rather than to
percentages.

Part (a) is doing more work than it appears to. Recovering a missing component of an aggregate is
not a routine operation in a business context, and it forces the candidate to hold the aggregate and
its parts in mind at the same time — which is precisely the mental posture the rest of the item
needs. It also makes the weighted structure unavoidable in part (b), because the three departmental
rates have to reconcile with a total that was supplied before any of them was known.

The final part asks for an evaluation rather than a calculation, which is deliberate. The arithmetic
points one way and it points clearly, but it rests on an assumption — that turnover can be reduced by
a stated number of points in a chosen department — that the data does nothing to support. A candidate
who recommends C, gives the figures, and then observes that the cause of C's turnover is unknown and
that neither intervention reaches the target has done the whole question. One who produces only
$12.0\%$ and $13.2\%$ has done the arithmetic and stopped, and that is the difference between a
business answer and a numeracy answer.

The item is adapted from a UK A-Level human-resource data-response that supplies a table of
departmental turnover and asks which department the business should be most concerned about. Making
one entry missing so that the aggregate has to be recovered, making the smallest department the
highest-turnover one so that the rates and the contributions point in opposite directions, and
requiring the two interventions to be compared in both percentage and money terms are all new here.
The source asks for an identification; this asks for a decision that the identification would get
wrong.

Unit 2: Human resource management — 2.1 Labour turnover as a weighted aggregate: recovering a missing department and targeting the right one
Challenge lever
Three departments report turnover, and the overall rate is not the average of them. It is a headcount-weighted aggregate, and the weighting is what carries every conclusion in the item. The smallest department has the highest rate and the second-smallest has the second-highest, so the department a manager would target on the rates alone is the one that contributes least to the headline figure. Cutting the largest department from $7.5\%$ to $5\%$ moves the overall rate by $1.2$ points; cutting the smallest from $22\%$ to $10\%$ moves it by $2.4$ — twice as much, from $42\%$ of the headcount.
