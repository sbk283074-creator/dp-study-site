An area that fixes the sine but not the angle

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL3.3-001

SL 3.3 (applications of right and non-right angled trigonometry: the sine rule, the cosine rule and the area of a triangle)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatFindDetermineExplain

Question

In triangle $PQR$, the side $PQ$ has length $7$ cm, the side $QR$ has length $15$ cm, and the area of the triangle is $42$ cm$^{2}$. A calculator is permitted throughout this question.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that $\sin Q=0.8$, and write down the two possible values of angle $PQR$ correct to one decimal place.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Find the two possible values of $PR$, giving one of them as an exact surd and one as an integer.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the perimeter of each triangle, and state which of the two is obtuse-angled.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain why the area alone does not determine the triangle, and state one further measurement that would.

    (Explain)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The area of a triangle is $\frac{1}{2}(PQ)(QR)\sin Q$ (M1), so $\frac{1}{2}(7)(15)\sin Q=42$ (A1). Since $\frac{1}{2}(7)(15)=52.5$, this gives $\sin Q=\frac{42}{52.5}=0.8$ (A1)(AG). Hence angle $PQR=53.1^{\circ}$ or $126.9^{\circ}$, correct to one decimal place (A1). The area formula is the only relation that uses the given quantity directly, and it is the reason the sine of the angle, rather than the angle itself, is what the data determine.

(b) By the cosine rule, $PR^{2}=PQ^{2}+QR^{2}-2(PQ)(QR)\cos Q$ (M1) $=7^{2}+15^{2}-210\cos Q=274-210\cos Q$ (A1). For $Q=53.1^{\circ}$ we have $\cos Q=0.6$, so $PR^{2}=274-126=148$ and $PR=2\sqrt{37}$ cm (M1)(A1). For $Q=126.9^{\circ}$ we have $\cos Q=-0.6$, so $PR^{2}=274+126=400$ and $PR=20$ cm (A1). The cosine rule is applied twice, once with the positive cosine and once with the negative, because the two cases differ only in the sign of $\cos Q$.

(c) The first perimeter is $7+15+2\sqrt{37}=34.2$ cm to three significant figures (M1)(A1). The second perimeter is $7+15+20=42$ cm (A1). The second triangle is obtuse-angled, because angle $PQR=126.9^{\circ}$ is greater than $90^{\circ}$ (R1). The perimeters differ by almost eight centimetres, which is a useful reminder that equal areas do not imply congruent triangles.

(d) The area fixes only $\sin Q$, and $\sin Q=\sin(180^{\circ}-Q)$, so an acute angle and its supplement produce the same area while giving two different triangles (M1)(A1). One further measurement would remove the ambiguity: whether angle $PQR$ is acute or obtuse, the length of $PR$, or the size of either of the remaining angles (A1). The ambiguity is a feature of the sine function rather than of the triangle, and it disappears as soon as any measurement that distinguishes an angle from its supplement is supplied.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for the area formula with two sides and the included angle; the (A1) for the correct substitution. The (A1) requires the exact value $0.8$, and the final (A1) both angles to one decimal place. A candidate who writes only $53.1^{\circ}$ earns three of the four marks, because the supplement is the point of the question.

(b) Award (M1) for the cosine rule and (A1) for the reduced expression $274-210\cos Q$. The (M1)(A1) pair is for the acute case, including the exact surd $2\sqrt{37}$; a decimal such as $12.2$ does not earn the surd mark. The final (A1) is for $PR=20$ in the obtuse case. Accept the two answers in either order if they are correctly matched to their angles.

(c) The (M1) is for adding the three sides; the first (A1) is for the perimeter $34.2$ cm to three significant figures, and the second for $42$ cm. The (R1) requires an explicit comparison of $126.9^{\circ}$ with $90^{\circ}$; stating only that the second triangle is larger does not earn it.

(d) This is a reasoning part. Award (M1) for the identity $\sin Q=\sin(180^{\circ}-Q)$ or an equivalent statement about the area, (A1) for linking it to the two triangles, and the final (A1) for any one valid extra measurement. Follow through from part (a) is allowed.

Why this question is hard

The item is built around a quantity that is not determined by the information given. Two sides and the area fix $\sin Q$, but $\sin Q$ is the same for an acute angle and its supplement, so the data are consistent with two triangles of equal area. The cosine rule then separates them, because $\cos Q$ changes sign between the two cases: one triangle has $PR=2\sqrt{37}$, and the other has $PR=20$.

Candidates who use the cosine rule without thinking about the sign of $\cos Q$ will obtain only one value. If they assume the acute angle, they compute $274-126=148$; if they assume the obtuse angle, they compute $274+126=400$. Both are correct, but each belongs to a different triangle. The trap is to treat the second as an error rather than as the second branch.

Part (c) makes the distinction concrete through the perimeters, and the obtuse triangle is identified by the size of angle $Q$ rather than by any feature of the sides alone. Part (d) steps back and asks why the ambiguity arises in the first place; the answer is the symmetry $\sin Q=\sin(180^{\circ}-Q)$, which is also the reason the sine rule has an ambiguous case. The numbers $7$, $15$ and $42$ were chosen so that $\sin Q=0.8$ exactly and so that the two squared lengths are $148$ and $400$, the first reducing to $2\sqrt{37}$ and the second to a whole number. A candidate who checks $\cos^{2}Q+\sin^{2}Q=0.36+0.64=1$ will confirm that both branches correspond to genuine angles. The two triangles share the same two sides and the same area, so no measurement of area can ever separate them; only a measurement sensitive to the size of the angle can. It is the same ambiguity that appears in the ambiguous case of the sine rule.
