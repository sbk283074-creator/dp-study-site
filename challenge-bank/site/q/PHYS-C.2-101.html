C.2 Wave model

Physics HL · PHYS-C.2-101

Theme C: Wave behaviourC.2 (Wave model: reflection, refraction, superposition, interference, polarisation)P1 · Section A5 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedCalculateDetermineState

Question

Five independent multiple-choice questions on the wave model. Each one stands alone, so you can attempt any of them without the others. Unless a question says otherwise, the frequency of a wave is fixed by its source and does not change when the wave crosses a boundary between two media.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    A transverse pulse travels along a stretched string whose far end is rigidly clamped. What happens to the pulse when it reaches the clamped end?

    (State)
  2. [1 mark](b)

    A water wave of frequency $2.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$ travels from deep water, where its speed is $0.80\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, into shallow water, where its speed is $0.60\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. What happens to its wavelength?

    (Calculate)
  3. [1 mark](c)

    Two coherent sources emit in phase. At a point P the difference in the distances from the two sources to P is $1.5\lambda$, where $\lambda$ is the wavelength. What is observed at P?

    (Determine)
  4. [1 mark](d)

    Two coherent waves of amplitudes $3A$ and $A$ superpose in phase. What is the ratio of the intensity of the resultant wave to the intensity of the larger wave on its own?

    (Calculate)
  5. [1 mark](e)

    Unpolarised light of intensity $I_{0}$ is incident on a polariser. A second polariser, whose transmission axis is at $60^{\circ}$ to the first, is placed after it. What is the intensity of the light emerging from the second polariser?

    (Calculate)

Total: 5 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) A. A clamped end is a fixed boundary: the displacement there must be zero at every instant, so the reflected pulse must cancel the incident one at that point, which means it is inverted. A free end gives an uninverted reflection instead. (A1) The distinction is the boundary condition, not the size of the pulse.

(b) A. Frequency is set by the source and is unchanged on crossing the boundary. With $v=f\lambda$, the deep-water wavelength is $0.80/2.0=0.40\ \mathrm{m}$ and the shallow-water wavelength is $0.60/2.0=0.30\ \mathrm{m}$. (A1) C is the common error: it is the frequency that is invariant, not the wavelength.

(c) A. A path difference of $1.5\lambda$ equals three half-wavelengths, an odd number, so the phase difference is $3\pi$ radians, equivalent to $\pi$, and the superposing arrivals cancel. (A1) C is the trap worth noting: it names the correct phase difference and then draws the opposite conclusion.

(d) A. Amplitudes add before squaring: the resultant is $3A+A=4A$, so the resultant intensity is proportional to $16A^{2}$ while the larger wave alone is proportional to $9A^{2}$, giving $16/9=1.78$. (A1) B omits the squaring and C treats the waves as incoherent, which would give $(9+1)/9=1.11$ and would be right only if the phase relationship were random.

(e) A. The first polariser transmits half the unpolarised intensity, giving $I_{0}/2$ of plane-polarised light. Malus' law then multiplies by $\cos^{2}60^{\circ}=0.25$, so the final intensity is $I_{0}/2\times0.25=0.125\,I_{0}$. (A1) B, at $0.25\,I_{0}$, is Malus' law applied without the initial halving.

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking. (a) Accept any statement that the pulse is reflected and inverted; a candidate who adds that the reflection at a free end is not inverted shows the contrasting case and should not be penalised for the extra material. (b) Accept $0.40$ and $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$; the mark is for holding the frequency constant, so a candidate who changes the frequency and gets both wavelengths wrong earns nothing. (c) Accept any justification in terms of an odd number of half-wavelengths; note that $1.5\lambda$ and $2.5\lambda$ give the same verdict, so the answer does not depend on which odd multiple was chosen. (d) Accept $1.7$ to $1.8$, or the exact fraction $16/9$; a candidate who writes $16$ has done the hard part and then omitted the division, and should be given the benefit of the doubt only if the ratio was clearly intended. (e) Accept $0.125$ or $I_{0}/8$; the two-stage structure is the point, so a single-stage answer earns nothing.

Why this question is hard

This cluster tests the wave model through a series of distinctions that are easy to state and easy to forget under pressure. In (a) the boundary condition decides the reflection, and a fixed end is not a free end. In (b) exactly one of frequency and wavelength is invariant across a boundary, and it is the one students are least inclined to hold constant because they can see the wavelength change. In (c) the classification depends on whether the path difference is an odd or an even number of half-wavelengths, and the $1.5\lambda$ value is chosen so that the wrong counting gives a confident wrong answer.

Items (d) and (e) are where the cluster earns its difficulty, because both hinge on an operation that must be done before the comparison. In (d) amplitudes must be added first and only then squared, because intensity is proportional to the square of the resultant amplitude; adding the intensities instead throws away the cross term, which is the entire interference effect and is larger than one of the two intensities on its own. In (e) there are two stages and the first is the one that gets forgotten: unpolarised light loses half its intensity at the first polariser, and Malus' law applies only to the polarised beam that emerges. Both items reward a candidate who writes down what is being combined before reaching for a formula, and both punish one who pattern-matches the surface features of the question.
