A.2 An impulse obtained as the area under a force-time record whose sampling interval changed part-way through, so that the area depends on reading the time column rather than assuming a constant step

Physics HL · PHYS-A.2-202

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.2 Forces and momentum, with A.1 KinematicsP1 · Section B10 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedStateDetermineDetermineSuggest

A collision recorded by a force sensor

A trolley of mass $0.850 \pm 0.001\ \mathrm{kg}$ was pushed towards a force sensor mounted on the end of a track and bounced off it. A light gate placed just before the sensor measured the trolley's speed before the collision as $0.450 \pm 0.005\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The sensor's output was logged, and the logger was left on its automatic sampling setting. The record begins when the trolley first touched the sensor. The manufacturer states a force resolution of $\pm 0.2\ \mathrm{N}$ and a timing uncertainty of $\pm 0.2\ \mathrm{ms}$ on each sample; the logger's activity log shows that its sampling interval changed during the record. No uncertainty is quoted for the impact duration itself.

Question

The force sensor's record of the collision, the trolley's mass and the light gate's reading of the approach speed are given in the stimulus and its table. Answer all parts using those readings.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    State the two different sampling intervals present in the record and the number of intervals of each kind, and explain why a trapezium rule applied to this table must use the time differences as given.

    (State)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the impulse delivered to the trolley, using the trapezium rule on the recorded pairs of values and stating the unit of your answer.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine the trolley's speed immediately after the collision, and determine the value a candidate who assumed a constant $10\ \mathrm{ms}$ interval would obtain for the impulse and for that speed.

    (Determine)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    Suggest what should have been recorded at the time to make this record analysable without ambiguity, and state how the uncertainty in the impulse could best be estimated from the data available.

    (Suggest)

Total: 10 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Reading the time column: the first four intervals are $10\ \mathrm{ms}$ each, the intervals $30 \to 35 \to 40$ are $5\ \mathrm{ms}$ each, and the last two are $10\ \mathrm{ms}$ (A1). So there are four intervals of $10\ \mathrm{ms}$ ($0$-$10$, $10$-$20$, $20$-$30$, $40$-$50$, $50$-$60$ is five) -- counting them explicitly: $0$-$10$, $10$-$20$, $20$-$30$, $40$-$50$ and $50$-$60$ are five intervals of $10\ \mathrm{ms}$, and $30$-$35$, $35$-$40$ are two intervals of $5\ \mathrm{ms}$ (A1). Total record length $60\ \mathrm{ms}$.

The trapezium rule approximates $\int F\,dt$ by $\sum \tfrac{1}{2}(F_{i} + F_{i+1})\Delta t_{i}$, so each pair of forces must be weighted by the time difference that actually separates them. The force is not a function of sample number; it is a function of time, and the samples are not equally spaced in time. Using a single step $\Delta t = 10\ \mathrm{ms}$ for all seven intervals would assign the same weight to a $5\ \mathrm{ms}$ interval as to a $10\ \mathrm{ms}$ one, which inflates the area near the peak where the sampling was fastest.

(b) Apply the trapezium rule interval by interval (M1):
$$\begin{aligned}
0\text{-}10: &\ \tfrac{1}{2}(0.0 + 4.2)(10) = 21.0 \\
10\text{-}20: &\ \tfrac{1}{2}(4.2 + 9.8)(10) = 70.0 \\
20\text{-}30: &\ \tfrac{1}{2}(9.8 + 15.6)(10) = 127.0 \\
30\text{-}35: &\ \tfrac{1}{2}(15.6 + 19.4)(5) = 87.5 \\
35\text{-}40: &\ \tfrac{1}{2}(19.4 + 18.2)(5) = 94.0 \\
40\text{-}50: &\ \tfrac{1}{2}(18.2 + 11.0)(10) = 146.0 \\
50\text{-}60: &\ \tfrac{1}{2}(11.0 + 3.4)(10) = 72.0
\end{aligned}$$
The sum is in $\mathrm{N\,ms}$, so
$$\sum = 21.0 + 70.0 + 127.0 + 87.5 + 94.0 + 146.0 + 72.0 = 617.5\ \mathrm{N\,ms} \;\; (A1).$$
$$J = 617.5 \times 10^{-3} = 0.6175\ \mathrm{N\,s} \approx 0.62\ \mathrm{N\,s} \;\; (A1).$$
The unit is the newton second, $\mathrm{N\,s}$, equivalently $\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$.

(c) The impulse equals the change in momentum, $J = \Delta p = m(v - u)$ with $u = +0.450\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (M1):
$$v = u + \frac{J}{m} = 0.450 + \frac{0.6175}{0.850} = 0.450 + 0.7265 = 1.18\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \;\; (A1).$$

A candidate who assumes a constant $10\ \mathrm{ms}$ interval weights the two short intervals as though they were twice as long:
$$\sum = 21.0 + 70.0 + 127.0 + 175.0 + 188.0 + 146.0 + 72.0 = 799.0\ \mathrm{N\,ms},$$
so $J = 0.799\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ and $v = 0.450 + \dfrac{0.799}{0.850} = 1.39\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (A1).

The error is $\dfrac{799.0 - 617.5}{617.5} = 29\%$ in the impulse and $($1.39 - 1.18$)/$1.18$ = 18\%$ in the speed. It arises entirely from the two $5\ \mathrm{ms}$ intervals at the peak, where the force is largest and where doubling the assumed width adds the most area.

(d) The sampling interval should have been fixed by the operator and recorded with the data, together with a note of any change; on an automatic rate the interval is a property of the logger rather than of the experiment, and a record whose abscissa is not documented cannot be integrated unambiguously. A second clock channel, or a fixed rate with the interval written in the log, would settle it (A1).

Given the record as it stands, the honest estimate of the uncertainty in the impulse comes from the force resolution: each sample carries $\pm 0.2\ \mathrm{N}$, and the trapezium rule weights each sample by half the sum of the two intervals that touch it, namely $\tfrac{1}{2}(10 + 10) = 10\ \mathrm{ms}$ for an interior sample at a $10\ \mathrm{ms}$ step and $\tfrac{1}{2}(10 + 5) = 7.5\ \mathrm{ms}$ for one at the junction. Adding the contributions in quadrature gives a few tenths of a per cent of the total, so the resolution is not the limiting factor -- the sampling interval is (A1). The duration itself has no quoted uncertainty, so a defensible statement is that the impulse is known to about $1\%$ from the record and to whatever the undocumented interval change contributes on top.

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for both intervals $10\ \mathrm{ms}$ and $5\ \mathrm{ms}$ located in the right part of the record, (A1) for the reason the actual differences must be used. A candidate who says only "the intervals are not constant" scores the first mark if the two values are named.

(b) (M1) for the trapezium rule applied interval by interval with the correct widths, (A1) for $617.5\ \mathrm{N\,ms}$, (A1) for $0.6175\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ or $0.62\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ with the unit. Working left in $\mathrm{N\,ms}$ and converted at the end is the expected route; a candidate who converts each term to $\mathrm{N\,s}$ first and reaches $0.6175$ scores the same. A candidate who obtains $799\ \mathrm{N\,ms}$ scores the method mark only, and this is the discriminating case for part (c).

(c) (M1) for $J = m\Delta v$ with the correct sign convention and $u = 0.450\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, (A1) for $1.18\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, (A1) for the constant-interval value $0.799\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ and $1.39\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. A candidate who adds the impulse to the initial momentum in the wrong direction -- obtaining $0.450 - 0.7265$ -- forfeits the second mark; the trolley bounces off the sensor and its speed increases, which the data support since the force is always positive.

(d) (A1) for a concrete suggestion about recording the interval, (A1) for an uncertainty estimate that names the resolution and the weights. A candidate who computes a percentage from the resolution alone, without noticing that the interval is the dominant unknown, scores the first mark only.

Condonation: a candidate who writes the answer to (b) as $0.618\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ or $0.62\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ scores full marks; two or three significant figures are both acceptable here, since the forced resolution is $0.2\ \mathrm{N}$. Follow-through: a candidate whose (b) is $799\ \mathrm{N\,ms}$ and who then obtains $1.39\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ in (c) is fully credited the (M1) and (A1) for the method and the arithmetic, and is expected to identify $1.18$ in the second half of (c).

Forfeits: treating the force-time record as a triangle and using $\tfrac{1}{2}F_{\max}t$; taking the sum of the forces as the impulse, which omits the time dimension entirely.

Why this question is hard

This question is about a collision and it is not really about collisions. The physics is one line -- impulse is the area under a force-time graph and equals the change in momentum -- and the work is in noticing what the data actually are.

The record has eight samples and seven intervals, and the intervals are not equal: two of them are $5\ \mathrm{ms}$ where the rest are $10\ \mathrm{ms}$. This is not a hypothetical hazard. Loggers with an automatic rate setting really do change interval mid-record, usually to capture detail where the signal is changing fastest, which is exactly where the force is largest and where the area is most sensitive to the width assumed. The two short intervals carry a total area of $181.5\ \mathrm{N\,ms}$; treating them as $10\ \mathrm{ms}$ each makes that $350\ \mathrm{N\,ms}$, and the impulse comes out $29\%$ too large.

The reason this is a trap worth setting is that nothing about the numbers looks wrong. The forces are plausible and rise and fall smoothly, the times are in increasing order, and the table is not missing any rows. A candidate who reads the force column and takes the step for granted is doing arithmetic on an assumed dataset rather than the one in front of them, and the mark scheme is arranged so that this candidate scores the method mark in (b) and then sees, in (c), exactly what the assumption cost. Showing the wrong value inside the question rather than merely penalising it is deliberate: the point is that $1.39$ and $1.18$ are both "reasonable" numbers for a trolley to rebound with, and only the time column distinguishes them.

Part (d) is where the item finishes rather than where it tails off. The uncertainty in the impulse is *not* dominated by the sensor's resolution -- that contributes a few tenths of a per cent -- but by whether the abscissa can be trusted at all, which is a recording decision made before the collision happened. A candidate who produces a careful quadrature sum from the resolution and reports a comfortable $0.2\%$ has answered a question that the data do not support, and saying so is the judgement this part rewards.
