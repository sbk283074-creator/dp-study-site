An irrigation gateway choosing how to share its processor

Computer science HL · CS-A1.3-001

A1.3 Operating systems and control systems (A1.3.2 functions of an operating system, A1.3.3 comparison of scheduling approaches, A1.3.4 evaluation of polling and interrupt handling, A1.3.6 open-loop and closed-loop control)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedDescribeCalculateEvaluateExplain

A field irrigation gateway

A solar-powered gateway controller sits in a field and manages irrigation for sixteen plots. It runs a small operating system on a single-core processor and executes four periodic tasks, described as A, B, C and D. Each task becomes ready at the same moment when the controller wakes from sleep, and their processor requirements are 5 ms, 3 ms, 8 ms and 2 ms respectively. The controller must also handle two inputs: a soil-moisture probe that is read on a fixed schedule every 30 seconds, and a rain sensor that triggers a shutdown and must be acted on within 5 milliseconds of the first drop reaching it. The device is battery-backed and mains-charged, and is connected to the farm network so that readings can be uploaded.

Question

The four tasks A, B, C and D all become ready simultaneously when the controller wakes. Their processor requirements are 5 ms, 3 ms, 8 ms and 2 ms.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Describe how a closed-loop control system would maintain soil moisture at a target level, identifying the input, the process, the output and the feedback in your answer.

    (Describe)
  2. [6 marks](b)

    Calculate the average waiting time for the four tasks under first-come-first-served scheduling and under round-robin scheduling with a time quantum of 3 ms, then recalculate each total if every context switch costs 0.5 ms and the tasks are executed in the order A, B, C, D.

    (Calculate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain why the average waiting time alone is a misleading basis for choosing between the two policies, using your figures, and state which policy you would deploy.

    (Explain)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate the use of polling for the soil-moisture probe against the use of interrupts for the rain sensor, and explain one additional operating system function that the network connection makes necessary.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) In a closed-loop system the controller measures the actual soil moisture, compares it with the target value, and uses the difference to decide how much water to release; the measurement is then repeated so that the effect of the action is observed and corrected. (M1)

Input: the reading from the soil-moisture probe, converted to a digital value. Process: the controller compares this reading with the target and computes the valve opening and the duration of watering. Output: the signal that opens the irrigation valve, and the water delivered to the plot. Feedback: the next reading from the same probe, which shows whether the moisture has reached the target and allows the next decision to be adjusted. (M1)(A1)(A1)

The feedback path is what makes the system closed-loop and is what distinguishes it from an open-loop system, which would water for a fixed time regardless of the resulting moisture level.

(b) First-come-first-served, executing in the order A, B, C, D:

| Task | Starts | Runs | Completes | Waiting time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 5 | 5 | 0 |
| B | 5 | 3 | 8 | 5 |
| C | 8 | 8 | 16 | 8 |
| D | 16 | 2 | 18 | 16 |

Average waiting time $=(0+5+8+16)/4=7.25$ ms. (M1)(A1)

Round robin with a quantum of 3 ms, queue order A, B, C, D:

| Slice | Task | Runs | Remaining |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A | 3 | 2 |
| 2 | B | 3 | 0 completes at 6 ms |
| 3 | C | 3 | 5 |
| 4 | D | 2 | 0 completes at 11 ms |
| 5 | A | 2 | 0 completes at 13 ms |
| 6 | C | 3 | 2 |
| 7 | C | 2 | 0 completes at 18 ms |

Waiting times are A 8 ms, B 3 ms, C 10 ms and D 9 ms, so the average is $(8+3+10+9)/4=7.50$ ms. (M1)(A1)

Both schedules take 18 ms of processing. First-come-first-served needs 3 context switches, adding 1.5 ms, for a total of 19.5 ms. Round robin needs 7 switches, adding 3.5 ms, for a total of 21.5 ms. (M1)(A1)

(c) Under round robin the average waiting time is 0.25 ms worse and the total time is 2 ms worse, so on the average the two policies are almost indistinguishable and the familiar claim that round robin is fairer is not visible. (M1)

What the average hides is the distribution. Under first-come-first-served the shortest task waits 16 ms, eight times its own running time, and a task that became ready just after a long one would wait longer still. Round robin bounds the wait for any task that is ready: with four tasks and a 3 ms quantum, no task is ever bypassed for more than three slices, so the worst case is 9 ms of other work. (A1)

For a controller whose main job is to keep the valves responding, the bounded worst case is worth more than a marginally better average, so round robin is the defensible choice, with the caveat that the context-switch cost should be reduced before the quantum is made smaller. (A1)

(d) Polling the moisture probe every 30 seconds is cheap and predictable. The probe changes slowly, the event rate is two checks per minute, and the processor overhead of reading a value on a timer is negligible; an interrupt-driven design would add hardware complexity for no benefit. (M1)(A1)

Polling the rain sensor is not acceptable. Drops arrive unpredictably and the required response is within 5 ms, so a polling loop would need a period shorter than 5 ms, consuming processor time continuously and still risking a missed event between samples. An interrupt lets the processor sleep until a drop arrives, which also suits a battery-backed device, and it guarantees the latency requirement rather than merely making it likely. (A1)

The network connection makes authentication and access control necessary: the gateway must be able to accept uploads and possibly commands from the farm network, so the operating system must identify who is allowed to talk to it and protect its memory from a malicious or faulty remote message.

Markscheme notes

Part (a): the four components must be identified in the context of this device, not defined generically; a candidate who writes 'input: a sensor' without saying which quantity is being sensed earns at most two of the four marks. Part (b): accept a correctly drawn Gantt chart as a substitute for the table. The trace marks are for the completion times and the waiting times respectively; a candidate with correct completion times but who reports waiting time as completion time minus start time should be corrected and given the trace mark only. Do not require the context-switch calculation to be presented in the same form as here, but do require an explicit count of switches; a candidate who counts 15 switches for round robin rather than 7 has counted slices rather than switches and loses the last (A1). Part (c): the essential argument is the difference between an average and a worst case; a candidate who merely repeats the average figures without discussing the shortest task's wait does not reach the explanation mark. Part (d): award the two marks for the moisture probe for the low event rate and predictability, and the mark for the rain sensor for the latency guarantee; a candidate who argues only on cost does not address the requirement. The final mark is for any plausible security function tied to the network connection, such as authentication, encryption of the upload, or memory protection from a malformed network packet.

Why this question is hard

The scheduling part of this question is arranged so that the standard intuition fails. Round robin is usually described as fairer, and it is, in the sense that no task is starved. But on this specific workload the average waiting time is very slightly worse and the total time is worse once the context-switch cost is included, because the longest task is interrupted and restarted and the switching overhead is not free. A student who answers 'round robin is fairer' without computing anything has not answered the question, and a student who computes only the averages is forced to choose round robin for a reason the numbers do not support.

The way out is to look at the distribution rather than the mean. Under first-come-first-served the two-millisecond task waits sixteen milliseconds; under round robin nothing waits more than about nine milliseconds. For a device whose purpose is to keep responding to field conditions, the bound matters more than the mean, and that is a genuine engineering judgement rather than a textbook answer.

The second half of the question is about matching a mechanism to an event's statistics. Polling and interrupts are not better or worse in themselves: polling is simpler, has no hardware setup, and is entirely adequate when the event rate is low and the latency requirement is loose, which is exactly the situation of a moisture probe read twice a minute. Interrupts become necessary when the event is rare but must be handled quickly, because a polling loop fast enough to guarantee five-millisecond latency would run continuously and defeat the low-power design. The fact that the two sensors sit on the same device and require opposite answers is the point of the question, and it is the reason a blanket rule such as 'interrupts are more efficient' is unhelpful.
