P3 extended problem: binomial identities by coefficient extraction

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-009

Topic 1: Number and algebraSL 1.9 (the binomial theorem), AHL 1.10 (counting principles), SL 1.6 (simple deductive proof)P316 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatHenceDetermine

Question

Throughout, $n$ is a positive integer and $\dbinom{n}{k}$ denotes the binomial coefficient. The three parts below are linked: each is obtained by comparing two different ways of writing the coefficient of $x^{n}$ in a product or power of binomials.

  1. [5 marks](a)

    By considering the coefficient of $x^{n}$ in $(1+x)^{2n}$ written as $(1+x)^{n}(1+x)^{n}$, show that
    $$\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k}^{2} = \binom{2n}{n}.$$

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Hence, by using the symmetry $\dbinom{n}{k} = \dbinom{n}{n-k}$, determine $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n} k\binom{n}{k}^{2}$.

    (Hence)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    Determine $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}^{2}$, giving separate answers according to whether $n$ is odd or even.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Throughout, $n$ is a positive integer and $\dbinom{n}{k}$ denotes the binomial coefficient. The three parts below are linked: each is obtained by comparing two different ways of writing the coefficient of $x^{n}$ in a product or power of binomials. (a) By considering the coefficient of $x^{n}$ in $(1+x)^{2n}$ written as $(1+x)^{n}(1+x)^{n}$, show that $$\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k}^{2} = \binom{2n}{n}.$$ [5 marks] (Show that) (b) Hence, by using the symmetry $\dbinom{n}{k} = \dbinom{n}{n-k}$, determine $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n} k\binom{n}{k}^{2}$. [5 marks] (Hence) (c) Determine $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}^{2}$, giving separate answers according to whether $n$ is odd or even. [6 marks] (Determine)
Reveal the answer

(a) Consider $(1+x)^{2n}$. By the binomial theorem the coefficient of $x^{n}$ is $\dbinom{2n}{n}$. (M1)(A1)

On the other hand $(1+x)^{2n} = (1+x)^{n}(1+x)^{n}$, and expanding each factor separately, (M1)
$$(1+x)^{n}(1+x)^{n} = \left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}x^{i}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}x^{j}\right).$$
The coefficient of $x^{n}$ in the product comes from all pairs with $i+j=n$, so $j = n-i$ and (M1)
$$[x^{n}] = \sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\binom{n}{n-i}.$$
Using $\dbinom{n}{n-i} = \dbinom{n}{i}$, this is $\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}^{2}$. (A1)

The two expressions are coefficients of $x^{n}$ in the same polynomial, so they are equal:
$$\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^{2} = \binom{2n}{n}.$$ (AG)

(b) Let $S = \displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n} k\binom{n}{k}^{2}$. (M1)
Substitute $k \mapsto n-k$; as $k$ runs from $0$ to $n$ so does $n-k$, and $\dbinom{n}{n-k} = \dbinom{n}{k}$, so (M1)(A1)
$$S = \sum_{k=0}^{n}(n-k)\binom{n}{n-k}^{2} = \sum_{k=0}^{n}(n-k)\binom{n}{k}^{2}.$$
Adding the two expressions for $S$: (M1)
$$2S = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\bigl(k + n - k\bigr)\binom{n}{k}^{2} = n\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^{2} = n\binom{2n}{n},$$
using part (a). Hence (A1)
$$S = \frac{n}{2}\binom{2n}{n}.$$

(c) Consider $(1-x)^{n}(1+x)^{n} = (1-x^{2})^{n}$. (M1)
Expanding the two factors separately and extracting the coefficient of $x^{n}$, exactly as in (a): (M1)
$$[x^{n}]\,(1-x)^{n}(1+x)^{n} = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\left[(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}\right]\binom{n}{n-k} = \sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}^{2},$$
which is the required sum. (A1)

On the other hand, $(1-x^{2})^{n} = \displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}(-1)^{j}x^{2j}$, which contains only even powers of $x$. (M1)

If $n$ is odd, the coefficient of $x^{n}$ is therefore $0$. (A1)

If $n$ is even, say $n = 2m$, the coefficient of $x^{2m}$ is $(-1)^{m}\dbinom{n}{m} = (-1)^{n/2}\dbinom{n}{n/2}$. (A1)

Hence
$$\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}^{2} = \begin{cases} 0, & n \text{ odd},\[4pt] (-1)^{n/2}\dbinom{n}{n/2}, & n \text{ even}.\end{cases}$$

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1)s are for writing the single power as a product of two expansions and for identifying the pairs with $i+j=n$; the symmetry $\binom{n}{n-i}=\binom{n}{i}$ must be stated. A candidate who tries induction will find the inductive step intractable and should be credited only for the base case. (b) The substitution $k\mapsto n-k$ is the whole idea and earns (M1); the second (M1) is for adding the two forms. Award the final (A1) only if part (a) is used, since the question says 'hence'. (c) The recognition that $(1-x)^n(1+x)^n=(1-x^2)^n$ earns (M1) and is the crux. The parity split must be explicit: a candidate who gives only the even case has lost half the marks. Check the sign of the even case carefully, since $(-1)^{n/2}$ is positive when $n/2$ is even and negative when $n/2$ is odd; a candidate who writes $(-1)^n$ has the wrong period. Candidates may verify small cases, and doing so is encouraged. Numerical checking is a legitimate route to the answer in (c) but never to the proof: for $n=2$ the sum is $1-4+1=-2$, which is $-\binom{2}{1}$, and for $n=3$ it is $1-9+9-1=0$; a candidate who spots the parity from these two cases but gives no argument for general $n$ has guessed, not shown. In (b) the final value is $\frac{n}{2}\binom{2n}{n}$, which is an integer, and a candidate who reports $\frac{n}{2}$ times the part (a) sum without simplifying has not used 'hence' as instructed. Do not penalise index notation differences between $i$, $j$ and $k$.

Why this question is hard

This problem is about one idea used three times: if two expressions are the same polynomial, then corresponding coefficients are equal. Nothing more than the binomial theorem is required, and yet each part produces a result that looks, on the page, as though it ought to need heavy machinery. That gap between the simplicity of the method and the unobviousness of the results is exactly what makes coefficient extraction worth examining.

Part (a) is the template. Writing $(1+x)^{2n}$ as $(1+x)^{n}(1+x)^{n}$ and extracting $x^{n}$ two ways gives $\sum\binom{n}{k}^{2}=\binom{2n}{n}$ immediately. Part (b) then adds a twist that is not coefficient extraction at all: the substitution $k\mapsto n-k$ shows that the weighted sum equals itself with $k$ replaced by $n-k$, and adding the two forms makes the weight collapse to $n$. It is a symmetrisation argument, and it is the standard trick for summing $k$ times a symmetric summand.

Part (c) is the most demanding, because the student has to see that changing the sign in one factor turns the product into $(1-x^{2})^{n}$, which contains only even powers. That single observation disposes of the whole problem: for odd $n$ the sum is zero, and for even $n$ it is one signed binomial coefficient. It is worth dwelling on why this is surprising, because the sum $\sum(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}^{2}$ has $n+1$ terms of rapidly increasing size that cancel almost completely, and no amount of numerical checking would lead a student to guess that the total is zero for every odd $n$. The method explains a fact that computation cannot.
