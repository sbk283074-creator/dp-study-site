A.3 Work done by a varying force, found from the area under a force-extension graph

Physics HL · PHYS-A.3-101

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.3 (Work, energy and power)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedStateDetermineCalculateSuggest

Student investigation: a non-linear spring

A student investigates a short steel spring using a force sensor and a metre ruler. The spring is stretched horizontally and the force needed to hold it at a given extension is recorded. The force sensor reads to the nearest 0.05 N and the ruler to the nearest millimetre. The readings are given below. The student intends to use the spring to launch a 0.250 kg trolley along a horizontal track.

Question

The readings are shown in the table. Answer all parts with reference to the data.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    State, using evidence from the table, whether the spring obeys Hooke's law over this range.

    (State)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the work done in extending the spring from zero to $0.120$ m by dividing the area under the force-extension graph into trapezia.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    A student assumes Hooke's law and uses $W=\tfrac12 kx^2$ with $k$ taken from the reading at $0.120$ m. Calculate the value this gives, and the percentage difference from your answer to (b).

    (Calculate)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Hence calculate the speed at which the trolley would leave the spring, assuming all of the stored energy is transferred to the trolley, and estimate the percentage uncertainty in this speed from the resolution of the force sensor.

    (Calculate)
  5. [3 marks](e)

    Evaluate whether the trapezium method overestimates or underestimates the true work done, and state why.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) It does not. (A1) The first differences of the force column are $1.60,\,1.70,\,1.80,\,1.90,\,2.10,\,2.30$ N for equal extension steps of $0.020$ m; they are not constant, and they increase, so the ratio $F/x$ is not constant either and Hooke's law does not hold. (A1)

(b) Dividing the range into six trapezia of width $0.020$ m,

$$W=0.020\left[\frac{0.00+11.40}{2}+1.60+3.30+5.10+7.00+9.10\right]$$

$$=0.020\left[5.70+26.10\right]=0.020\times31.80=0.636\text{ J}.$$

(M1)(A1)(A1) Award the unit mark separately. (A1)

(c) $k=\dfrac{11.40}{0.120}=95.0$ N per metre, so $W=\tfrac12\times95.0\times0.120^{2}=0.684$ J. (M1)(A1) The difference from (b) is $0.684-0.636=0.048$ J, which is $\dfrac{0.048}{0.636}\times100=7.5\%$. (A1)

(d) $\tfrac12\times0.250\,v^{2}=0.636$, so $v=\sqrt{\dfrac{2\times0.636}{0.250}}=2.26$ m per second. (M1)(A1) Each of the six interior force readings carries an uncertainty of $0.05$ N, so the worst-case uncertainty in the sum is $0.020\times6\times0.05=0.006$ J, which is $0.9\%$ of $0.636$ J. (M1) Since $v$ depends on the square root of the energy, the percentage uncertainty in the speed is half of this, about $0.5\%$. (A1)

(e) It overestimates. (A1) The force-extension graph is curving upwards, so each straight-line chord lies above the curve between the two readings, and the trapezia include a little area that is not under the curve at all. (A1) Two refinements are worth recording. The trapezium estimate could be improved by halving the interval width, which would require readings at 0.010 m intervals and would roughly quarter the method error, since the error of the composite rule falls as the square of the step; a candidate who suggests this has understood that the limitation is the sampling and not the arithmetic. Alternatively Simpson's rule would be exact here for a quadratic force law and would remove the curvature bias altogether, though it needs an odd number of intervals and so cannot be applied to this table without an extra reading. The reason the simple approach is adequate is that the method error is far below the uncertainty contributed by the force sensor, so refining the integration before improving the sensor would be wasted effort.

Markscheme notes

In (a) the evidence must be numerical; a statement that the graph is not a straight line through the origin earns the mark only if a pair of ratios or differences is quoted. In (b) allow $0.63$ to $0.64$ J and accept an equivalent count of squares if the working is shown; the method mark is for the trapezium sum, not for the answer. In (c) allow $7.4\%$ to $7.6\%$ and allow the difference to be expressed against either value provided it is stated which. In (d) allow $2.25$ to $2.27$ m per second, and note that the halving of the percentage uncertainty is the point of the part: a candidate who quotes $0.9\%$ for the speed has not applied the square root. Allow follow-through from (b). In (e) the reason must refer to the curvature of the graph; 'the trapezia are not exact' is not sufficient. Note also that the first and last ordinates carry half weight in the trapezium sum, and candidates frequently give them full weight; that error alone changes the answer by about five per cent and should be looked for specifically. Where a candidate uses a counting-squares method instead, credit it on the same terms, but require the scale of one square to be stated in joules before the count is accepted. In (e) accept an answer based on a sketch if the chord and the curve are both drawn and the area between them is identified.

Why this question is hard

The item exists because the obvious formula is the wrong one and the data hide that fact. Every textbook spring question uses $\tfrac12kx^{2}$, and the table has been constructed so that this gives a plausible-looking $0.684$ J against the correct $0.636$ J — a $7.5\%$ error, large enough to matter and small enough that no candidate will spot it by eye. The only way to see it is to look at the first differences, which is why (a) is worth two marks and comes first: it forces the test before the method is chosen. Part (b) then requires numerical integration, which is a skill the examined practical work demands and which most candidates have met only in passing. Part (d) contains a second, quieter trap: the percentage uncertainty in the speed is half that in the energy, because of the square root, and a candidate who propagates it directly has missed the relationship between the two. Part (e) closes the loop by asking about the sign of the method error, and the answer follows from the same curvature that made Hooke's law fail in the first place. The item also carries a lesson about when to stop refining a measurement. The method error in the trapezium rule here is a fraction of a per cent while the sensor contributes about one per cent, so the sensible next investment is a better force sensor, not a finer integration rule; candidates rarely say this, and it is worth drawing out because the same judgement recurs in every experiment they will meet.
