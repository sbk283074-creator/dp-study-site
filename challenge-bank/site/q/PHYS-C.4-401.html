C.4 Harmonics that cannot be excited because the plucking point is one of their nodes

Physics HL · PHYS-C.4-401

Question

A string of length $L$ is fixed at both ends and is plucked at a point one third of the way along it, that is at a distance $\tfrac{L}{3}$ from one end, as shown in the figure. The resulting vibration is a superposition of normal modes of the string.

(a) Show that the third harmonic has nodes at $x = \tfrac{L}{3}$ and $x = \tfrac{2L}{3}$, where $x$ is measured from one end.

(b) Hence determine which harmonics are absent from the vibration, giving your answer as a condition on the harmonic number $n$.

(c) Explain why a mode that has a node at the plucking point cannot be excited at all, and state which harmonics would be absent if the string were plucked at $\tfrac{L}{4}$ instead.

Reveal the answer

(a) The displacement of the $n$th harmonic has the form $y \propto \sin\!\left(\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$, which is zero when $\tfrac{n\pi x}{L} = k\pi$, so the nodes are at $x = \tfrac{kL}{n}$ for $k = 0,\,1,\,\ldots,\,n$ (M1). For $n = 3$ these are $x = 0$, $\tfrac{L}{3}$, $\tfrac{2L}{3}$ and $L$, so there are nodes at $\tfrac{L}{3}$ and $\tfrac{2L}{3}$ (A1).

(b) A node of the $n$th harmonic falls at $x = \tfrac{L}{3}$ when $\tfrac{kL}{n} = \tfrac{L}{3}$ for some integer $k$, that is when $n = 3k$ (M1). The absent harmonics are therefore those whose number is a multiple of $3$: the third, sixth, ninth and so on (A1).

(c) The amplitude of each mode in the superposition is set by how far the string is pulled aside at the plucking point in that mode (M1). If the pluck point is a node of the mode, the displacement there is zero for that mode at all times, so the pluck does no work on it and it receives no energy: the mode is absent entirely, not merely quiet. Plucking at $\tfrac{L}{4}$ instead would remove the harmonics whose number is a multiple of $4$ -- the fourth, eighth, twelfth and so on (A1), since $\tfrac{kL}{n} = \tfrac{L}{4}$ gives $n = 4k$.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for the node positions of the nth harmonic, or directly for the third harmonic from a sketch of three half-wavelengths; (A1) for both L/3 and 2L/3. A candidate who draws the mode and reads the nodes off their sketch is fully creditable -- the figure invites exactly that.

(b) (M1) for setting kL/n equal to L/3 and rearranging to n = 3k; (A1) for the condition stated in words or as a list. A list that stops at the third harmonic earns the method mark only, because the part asks for a condition on n.

(c) (M1) for the energy or amplitude argument; (A1) for the multiples of 4. Do not credit 'the string cannot move there, so that mode is damped': the mode is never started, which is a different claim, and the distinction is what separates an explanation from a restatement. Also accept a candidate who answers in terms of symmetry: a mode whose node sits at the pluck point is antisymmetric about that point while the initial displacement is not.

Why this question is hard

The pluck point is a constraint on the motion, and the interesting thing about it is that it constrains *shape* rather than frequency. A string fixed at both ends admits every harmonic; a string plucked at L/3 admits every harmonic except those that are already still at L/3. Nothing about the allowed frequencies changes -- the third harmonic is still a perfectly good mode of the string -- but it is not excited, and so it is not heard.

This is why a plucked string sounds different depending on where it is plucked, and why guitarists exploit it: plucking near the bridge produces a bright, thin tone because the low harmonics, whose nodes are widely spaced, are the ones most likely to have a node near the bridge and are thus the ones suppressed. The item is the simplest case of that, with a pluck point at a simple fraction of the length so that the condition on n comes out as divisibility.

The general statement is worth carrying away: for a pluck at L/m, the absent harmonics are the multiples of m. Part (c) asks for m = 4 so that the candidate can check the rule they have just derived against a second case and see that it was a rule and not an accident of the number three.

Challenge lever
The constraint is invisible until it is looked for. A string fixed at both ends can vibrate in any of its normal modes, and the usual question asks which frequencies are possible -- all of them. Plucking the string at a particular point adds a second condition which is not a condition on frequency at all: any mode that has a node at that point receives no energy, whatever its frequency. The candidate's task is to notice that the pluck position is a constraint and then to do something with it, which means asking where the nodes of the nth harmonic are and whether one of them falls at L/3.
