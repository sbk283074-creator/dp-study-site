A stretch and a translation that do not commute

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL2.11-001

SL 2.11 (transformations of graphs: translations, stretches and reflections, and the effect of the order in which they are applied)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedFindDescribeSolve

Question

Let $f(x)=x^{2}-4x+5$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Find $f(x)$ in the form $(x-h)^{2}+k$ and state the coordinates of the vertex of $y=f(x)$.

    (Find)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Describe the graph of $y=f(2x-6)$ as a combination of a horizontal stretch and a horizontal translation of the graph of $y=f(x)$, and explain why the translation is $6$ units in one order and $3$ units in the other.

    (Describe)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the coordinates of the vertex of $y=f(2x-6)$.

    (Find)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Solve the equation $f(2x-6)=f(x)$, giving both solutions in exact form.

    (Solve)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Completing the square, $f(x)=x^{2}-4x+4+1=(x-2)^{2}+1$ (M1)(A1). Comparing with $(x-h)^{2}+k$ gives $h=2$ and $k=1$ (A1), so the vertex is $(2,1)$ (A1). The completed-square form is the most convenient way to read off the vertex, and it will be reused in the later parts.

(b) Factorising the argument, $f(2x-6)=f(2(x-3))$ (M1). Starting from $y=f(x)$, a horizontal stretch of scale factor $\frac{1}{2}$ about the $y$-axis replaces $x$ by $2x$ and gives $y=f(2x)$ (A1), and translating the result $3$ units in the positive $x$-direction replaces $x$ by $x-3$, giving $y=f(2(x-3))=f(2x-6)$ (A1). In the other order, translating first by $6$ units gives $y=f(x-6)$ (M1), and then stretching by factor $\frac{1}{2}$ replaces $x$ by $2x$, giving $y=f(2x-6)$ (A1). The translation distances differ because the stretch acts on the translation itself: $2(x-3)=2x-6$, so a translation of $3$ before the stretch corresponds to a translation of $6$ after it. Both descriptions end at the same curve, and the discrepancy in the translation distance is the whole content of the part.

(c) Substituting into the completed-square form, $f(2x-6)=(2x-6-2)^{2}+1=(2x-8)^{2}+1=4(x-4)^{2}+1$ (M1)(A1). Hence the vertex is $(4,1)$ (A1)(A1). The vertex has the same height as before, which is expected because the stretch and the translation are both horizontal.

(d) Setting the two expressions equal, $4x^{2}-32x+65=x^{2}-4x+5$ (M1), so $3x^{2}-28x+60=0$ (A1). The quadratic formula gives $x=\frac{28\pm\sqrt{784-720}}{6}=\frac{28\pm8}{6}$ (A1), so the required solutions are $x=6$ or $x=\frac{10}{3}$. The two roots are the points at which the original parabola and its horizontally transformed copy have the same height. A quick check confirms that both values satisfy the simplified quadratic and that their sum and product match the coefficients.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for an attempt at completing the square, and the (A1) for the correct completed-square form. The vertex marks are independent of the algebraic form: a candidate who reads the vertex directly from a correct graph earns them. Accept the form written with the square completed in an equivalent way.

(b) This is a description question, so the marks are for the structure of the argument. The (M1) is for factoring out the $2$; the two (A1) marks are for the stretch-then-translate route; the (M1)(A1) pair is for the translate-then-stretch route. A candidate who gives only one route can earn at most three of the five marks. The final explanation must connect the two distances through $2(x-3)=2x-6$.

(c) The (M1) is for substituting $2x-6$ into the function, and the (A1) for reaching $4(x-4)^{2}+1$. The vertex marks require the coordinates in the correct order. A candidate who writes $(1,4)$ forfeits both.

(d) The (M1) is for equating the two expressions and the (A1) for the simplified quadratic. The final (A1) requires both roots; a candidate who gives only $x=6$ earns two of the three marks. Accept $\frac{10}{3}$ written as $3\frac{1}{3}$.

Extension: the same two roots arise from $(2x-8)^{2}=(x-2)^{2}$.

Why this question is hard

The question looks like routine graph transformation, but the two orders in part (b) do not produce the same intermediate graph, and the reason is the whole point. A horizontal stretch by factor $\frac{1}{2}$ and a horizontal translation of $3$ units combine to give $f(2(x-3))$, whereas translating $6$ units first and then stretching gives the same final graph. The two descriptions are both correct because the stretch acts on whatever translation has already been applied.

The trap is to assume that transformations always commute. They do not: a horizontal translation and a horizontal stretch fail to commute unless the translation is zero. A candidate who writes 'stretch by $\frac{1}{2}$ then translate $6$' will produce the wrong intermediate expression, because the translation they have described belongs to the other stage. The mark scheme rewards the candidate who keeps the two orders separate and states the correct distance for each.

Part (c) is a straightforward substitution but is easy to get wrong if the completed-square form is not used. Expanding $f(2x-6)$ directly gives $4x^{2}-32x+65$, and completing the square on that expression is slower and more error-prone. Reading the vertex off the transformed form is the intended route.

Part (d) is an algebraic intersection problem. The two quadratics share the same $y$-intercept, which is why one root is far to the right and the other lies between $3$ and $4$. A candidate who tries to solve graphically without a calculator will struggle to locate $\frac{10}{3}$ precisely. The clean route is to cancel the $+1$ and take square roots, or to expand and use the formula, as shown. The base function was chosen so that both the vertex and the intersection points are simple fractions, which makes the transformation behaviour easy to check by hand.
