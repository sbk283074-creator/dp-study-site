3.3 The interior angle of a bearing triangle is the difference between a bearing and the back-bearing, not the difference between two bearings

Question

The figure shows the two legs of the journey, the north lines at $A$ and at $B$, and the line $AC$. (a) Explain why the angle $ABC$ is $60°$. [2 marks] (Explain) (b) Find the distance $AC$, giving your answer correct to three significant figures. [2 marks] (Find) (c) Find the bearing of $C$ from $A$, correct to one decimal place. [2 marks] (Find)
Reveal the answer

(a) The bearing of $B$ from $A$ is $020°$, so the bearing of $A$ from $B$, the back-bearing, is $020° + 180° = 200°$ (M1). The bearing of $C$ from $B$ is $140°$. Both of these are measured clockwise from the north line at $B$, so the angle $ABC$ between $BA$ and $BC$ is the difference $200° - 140° = 60°$ (A1). It is worth being explicit that the angle at $B$ is not $140° - 20° = 120°$: that difference is the change of direction between the two legs, and it is not an angle of the triangle, because the two legs do not meet at $A$. The two north lines drawn in the figure are parallel, which is exactly why the bearing of $AB$ measured at $A$ and the back-bearing of $BA$ measured at $B$ differ by $180°$.

(b) Applying the cosine rule to triangle $ABC$, with $AB = 12$, $BC = 9$ and the included angle $ABC = 60°$ (M1), gives $AC^2 = 12^2 + 9^2 - 2(12)(9)\cos 60° = 144 + 81 - 216(0.5) = 225 - 108 = 117$. Hence $AC = \sqrt{117} = 3\sqrt{13} = 10.8$ km to three significant figures (A1).

(c) Let $\alpha$ be the angle $BAC$. By the sine rule, $\frac{\sin \alpha}{9} = \frac{\sin 60°}{\sqrt{117}}$, so $\sin \alpha = \frac{9 \sin 60°}{\sqrt{117}} = 0.7206$ and $\alpha = 46.1°$ (M1). Since $C$ lies to the right of the line $AB$ in the figure, the bearing of $C$ from $A$ is $020° + 46.1° = 066.1°$ (A1). A candidate who uses the alternative solution $180° - 46.1°$ obtains $133.9°$, which would place $C$ on the other side of $AB$; the figure shows that this is not the case.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for the back-bearing $200°$ at $B$, or for any statement that the angle at $B$ requires the reverse direction; (A1) for $60°$ with the subtraction $200 - 140$ shown. Award no marks for $120°$, which is the difference of the two given bearings and is the error the part exists to detect. A candidate who draws the north line at $B$ and marks $200°$ and $140°$ from it has given the reason and should be credited even if the wording is terse.

(b) (M1) for the cosine rule with the correct included angle; (A1) for $10.8$, or for the exact $3\sqrt{13}$. A candidate who uses $120°$ as the included angle obtains $AC^2 = 12^2 + 9^2 - 2(12)(9)\cos 120° = 333$ and $AC = 18.2$ km, with a bearing of $045.3°$; this is consistent with their part (a) and should receive the method mark only, since the arithmetic is right and the angle is wrong. A candidate who reads the two legs as collinear obtains $AC = 12 + 9 = 21$ km and should receive no marks in this part, because no triangle has been used at all. Accept the exact form as a final answer on this paper.

(c) (M1) for the sine rule, or any correct route to the angle $BAC$; (A1) for $066.1°$. The choice between $46.1°$ and $133.9°$ must be justified from the figure or from the geometry, and a candidate who gives $133.9°$ without justification should not receive the accuracy mark. Accept a bearing written as $066.1°$ or as $66.1°$ in a sentence, but do not accept a bare $46.1°$, which is the angle rather than the bearing.

Why this question is hard

The item exists because bearings are the one place in school trigonometry where the obvious subtraction is the wrong one. Everywhere else, two angles given in a problem can usually be combined directly; here the two bearings are measured from two different north lines, and the angle that the triangle actually contains is measured from the reverse of the first leg. The difference $140 - 20 = 120$ is a real quantity -- it is the turn the ship makes -- but it is not an angle of the triangle, and a candidate who uses it obtains a consistent and entirely wrong answer.

The figure is what makes the correct construction available. The north line at $B$ is drawn, so the two bearings can both be marked from the same origin, and the reverse direction of $AB$ can be seen to point at a bearing of $200°$. The interior angle at $B$ is deliberately left unlabelled: naming it would give away part (a), and the point of the part is to have the candidate construct it. The line $AC$ is drawn as a dashed segment so that the triangle exists in the picture before its sides are computed.

Parts (b) and (c) then use the two rules in sequence and each has its own trap. The cosine rule in part (b) is only as good as the included angle, so an error in part (a) propagates without any further sign of trouble. Part (c) needs the sine rule and then an ambiguity: the equation $\sin \alpha = 0.7206$ has two solutions in the range, and only one of them places $C$ where the figure puts it. That choice is the second thing the figure is for.
