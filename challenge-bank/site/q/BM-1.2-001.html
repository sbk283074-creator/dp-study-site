Business management SL · BM-1.2-001

Unit 1: 1.2 Types of business entities; 1.3 Business objectives; 1.4 Stakeholders; 1.5 External environment. Toolkit: STEEPLE analysisP1 · Section A14 marksdifficulty 4ExplainAnalyseEvaluate

Marisol's Kitchen

Marisol's Kitchen is a bakery in a city centre, owned and run by Marisol as a sole trader. It has traded for nine years, employs fourteen staff, and is consistently profitable, with annual revenue of about €780 000 and net profit of about €94 000. Nearly all profit is reinvested in the shop.

Marisol works six days a week and has turned down two offers to open a second site because, she says, "the moment I'm not there, it stops being my bakery". She has no written business plan and funds everything from retained profit, having avoided borrowing since a difficult first year.

A former customer, Dev, has offered to invest €250 000 for a 40% share, on condition that the business becomes a private limited company and opens three more sites within two years. Dev's stated aim is to build the business and sell it to a regional chain within five years. Marisol would remain a director and keep 60%.

Two of Marisol's longest-serving staff have said they would like to become supervisors in the new sites. Her current landlord has indicated that the lease on the original shop will not be renewed beyond next year.

Question

Marisol must decide whether to accept Dev's investment.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain two features of a private limited company that would help the business expand, compared with Marisol remaining a sole trader.

    (Explain)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Using STEEPLE analysis, analyse one external factor from the case that could affect Marisol's decision.

    (Analyse)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    Evaluate whether Marisol's objectives and Dev's objectives can be reconciled if she accepts the investment.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Award two explained features:

  • Access to finance through share capital. As a private limited company the business can raise capital by issuing shares to new investors (and, unlike a sole trader, does not depend on Marisol's personal savings or retained profit). This matters because three new sites require far more than the €94 000 of annual profit Marisol currently reinvests. (A1)(A1)
  • Separate legal personality and limited liability. The company is a legal person in its own right, so it can own property, sign leases and borrow in its own name, and the shareholders' liability is limited to what they have invested. This matters because expansion into three leased sites would otherwise expose Marisol personally to unlimited liability for those commitments. (A1)(A1)
  • Also creditable: continuity of existence (the business survives changes in ownership, which matters given Dev's plan to sell), and the ability to bring in non-executive expertise through the board.

(b) Award up to four marks for one factor, analysed. For example:

  • Legal (or Economic) — the landlord's refusal to renew the lease. STEEPLE identifies the *legal* environment as an external constraint on property and tenancy. If the original site — the source of all current profit and the brand's home — must close next year, then Marisol's implicit objective of preserving the business she built is unachievable by doing nothing. This *strengthens* the case for accepting investment, because a second site would be needed simply to survive. (A1)(A1)(A1)(A1)
  • Alternatively: Social — changing consumer demand for independent bakeries, or the expectations of her staff, who want promotion opportunities; or Economic — the cost of fit-out and the interest rate environment affecting any borrowing. Credit any factor that is genuinely external, named as a STEEPLE category, and linked to the decision.

(c) Indicative evaluation:

The conflict is real. Marisol is a sole trader for reasons that are not financial: she has repeatedly declined expansion, and her own words ("it stops being my bakery") show that control and identity are her objectives. Dev's objective is explicit — build and sell within five years. These are different definitions of success: Marisol wants to keep a business, Dev wants to exit one. (A1)(A1)

Where they overlap. Both want the business to grow and be more profitable; three more sites would give her two supervisors the promotion they want and would solve the lease problem; and Marisol's 60% holding means she is not a passive minority — she retains majority control of ordinary decisions, and as a director she would shape how expansion happens. On a narrow reading, growth and profitability are compatible with both objectives for the first few years. (A1)(A1)

Why the reconciliation is fragile. The 60/40 split protects Marisol only until Dev decides to sell: a buyer would want control, and Dev's five-year exit plan means that in five years Marisol's 60% sits inside a business someone else has chosen. Objectives also diverge on *how* to grow — Marisol avoids borrowing and grows from retained profit, while three sites in two years requires exactly the speed and risk she has spent nine years avoiding. And if the new sites fail, limited liability protects her personal assets but not the business she identifies with. (A1)

Conclusion. The objectives can be reconciled *only* if the terms change: a shareholders' agreement giving Marisol a veto over any sale of the business, or an agreed timetable that ties the exit to her own retirement. Without such protections, accepting the investment means trading an objective she actually holds (independence) for one she has never expressed (capital growth). On the evidence of the case, Marisol should either negotiate control over the exit, or decline and seek a smaller, debt-funded second site that lets her keep both the business and her independence. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The feature must be *explained* and *contrasted with the sole trader form* — 'a company has limited liability' with no explanation of why that matters here is capped at one mark per feature. Do not credit 'it sounds more professional'.

(b) The mark is for the analysis, not for listing STEEPLE. A candidate who names all six categories but analyses none earns at most one mark. The factor must come from the case (lease, staff ambitions, Dev's offer is *internal* to the decision and not a STEEPLE factor — watch for this confusion).

(c) Six marks require: (i) identification of the two conflicting objectives with evidence, (ii) genuine analysis of where they coincide, (iii) analysis of why the coincidence is limited (the exit plan is the key), (iv) a supported conclusion. Candidates who simply list stakeholder groups without comparing objectives are capped at two. There is no single correct conclusion — 'accept with protections' and 'decline' are equally creditworthy if argued from the case.

Note on Toolkit use: STEEPLE in (b) is the required Toolkit application. Candidates who produce a full STEEPLE table but do not use it to reach a conclusion about the decision should be credited for the tool but not for the analysis marks.

Why this question is hard

Part (a) is the accessible entry point and most students handle it well. The question is designed so that (a) and (c) pull in opposite directions, and that is the whole point.

Having just explained in (a) why a private limited company is *better* for expansion, the natural answer to (c) is 'yes, they can be reconciled — convert and grow'. That answer is wrong, and not because the analysis in (a) is wrong. It is wrong because the objectives are about different things: Marisol's objective is control and identity, Dev's is a profitable exit. Converting to a company solves the finance problem; it does nothing about the fact that Dev intends to sell in five years and Marisol has spent nine years avoiding exactly that.

The detail that separates a strong answer from a competent one is the 60/40 split. Holding 60% looks like control, and for operating decisions it is. But Dev's exit plan means the *ownership* changes in five years regardless, and a buyer acquiring Dev's 40% would almost certainly demand a path to full control. A majority shareholding protects Marisol from being outvoted; it does not protect her from being out-negotiated at the point of sale.

The case also contains a fact that students routinely overlook: the landlord's decision. Without it, 'do nothing' is a viable option — Marisol keeps her profitable shop and declines. With it, the status quo is not stable, and any evaluation that assumes she can simply carry on has missed it. That is why part (b) asks for STEEPLE rather than a stakeholder map: the pressure here is external, not internal.

Classic wrong turns: describing unlimited liability as a *disadvantage* of the sole trader without linking it to the three new leases; treating Dev's investment offer as an external factor; and concluding 'she should accept because 60% means she is still in charge'.

Challenge lever
