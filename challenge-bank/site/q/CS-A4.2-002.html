A4.2 Choosing a scaling method when one outlier dominates the range

Computer science HL · CS-A4.2-002

Theme A: Concepts of computer scienceA4.2 Data preprocessing (HL only) (normalisation, standardisation, outlier handling)P2 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedCalculateDetermineCompareExplain

Question

A hospital logistics team is building a machine-learning model to predict delivery times.
One of the features is the loading time, in minutes, for the ten most recent deliveries:

$$10,\ 11,\ 12,\ 13,\ 14,\ 15,\ 16,\ 17,\ 18,\ 1400$$

The final value, $1400$ minutes, is a data-entry error: the true value was $14.00$ and the
decimal point was lost. The team has not yet cleaned the data and is comparing two ways of
scaling the feature before clustering.

(a) Calculate the mean and the standard deviation of the ten values as recorded. \tag{3}

(b) Determine the min–max scaled values of $10$, $18$ and $1400$, and calculate the scaled gap
between consecutive typical values. \tag{4}

(c) Determine the standardised (z-score) values of $10$, $18$ and $1400$, calculate the
corresponding scaled gap, and compare the two methods. \tag{5}

(d) Hence explain which scaling method the team should prefer for this data set. \tag{3}

  1. [3 marks](a)

Reveal the answer

(a) The nine typical values are the integers $10$ to $18$, whose sum is
$\frac{9(10 + 18)}{2} = 126$; adding $1400$ gives $1526$ and the mean is

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1526}{10} = 152.6 \tag{A1}$$

Note that the mean already sits far above every genuine reading: the true centre of the nine
typical values is $14$, and the single error has moved it by $138.6$.

The sum of squared deviations is most safely found from
$\sum x^{2} - n\bar{x}^{2}$ (M1). Here

$$\sum x^{2} = 100 + 121 + \cdots + 324 + 1960000 = 1824 + 1960000 = 1961824$$

and $n\bar{x}^{2} = 10 \times 152.6^{2} = 10 \times 23286.76 = 232867.6$, so

$$\sum (x - \bar{x})^{2} = 1961824 - 232867.6 = 1728956.4 \tag{A1}$$

The outlier contributes $1247.4^{2} = 1556006.8$ of that, which is $90.0\%$ of the whole
total. The population variance is $\frac{1728956.4}{10} = 172895.6$ and

$$\sigma = \sqrt{172895.6} = 415.8 \tag{A1}$$

(b) Min–max scaling maps $x$ to $\frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}$, and here
$x_{\min} = 10$ and $x_{\max} = 1400$, so the divisor is $1390$ (M1):

$$\frac{10 - 10}{1390} = 0.000, \qquad \frac{18 - 10}{1390} = 0.00576, \qquad
\frac{1400 - 10}{1390} = 1.000 \tag{A1}$$

The typical values therefore occupy the interval $[0,\ 0.00576]$ — the bottom $0.58\%$ of the
range — and the outlier sits alone at $1.000$ (A1). The scaled gap between consecutive typical
values, which are one minute apart in the original data, is

$$\frac{1}{1390} = 0.000719 \tag{A1}$$

(c) Standardising maps $x$ to $\frac{x - \bar{x}}{\sigma}$ (M1):

$$\frac{10 - 152.6}{415.8} = -0.343, \qquad \frac{18 - 152.6}{415.8} = -0.324, \qquad
\frac{1400 - 152.6}{415.8} = 3.00 \tag{A1}$$

The typical values occupy $[-0.343,\ -0.324]$ and the outlier sits at $3.00$ (A1). The scaled
gap between consecutive typical values is

$$\frac{1}{415.8} = 0.00241 \tag{A1}$$

which is $\frac{0.00241}{0.000719} = 3.34$ times the min–max gap (A1). Standardisation
therefore preserves about three and a third times as much resolution among the typical readings
as min–max does, even though it is far from ideal: the outlier still inflates $\sigma$ from
about $2.4$ to $415.8$ and drags the mean to $152.6$, more than ten times the largest genuine
reading. Note also that $3.00$ is the largest z-score any value in a data set of ten can have,
so the outlier is pressing against the ceiling that the sample size allows.

(d) Neither method should be used on the raw data (A1). The right first step is to correct or
remove the erroneous $1400$, since it is a known data-entry fault rather than a genuine
observation; with it removed the range is $8$, the mean is $14$ and the choice of scaler barely
matters (A1). If the value must be retained, standardisation is preferable to min–max because
the mean and standard deviation are less sensitive to a single extreme value than the minimum
and maximum are — the outlier moves $\sigma$ but does not set it, whereas it sets $x_{\max}$
directly (A1). Better still is a robust scaler based on the median and interquartile range,
which for this data gives a scaled gap of $\frac{1}{5} = 0.2$, about $278$ times the min–max
resolution.

Why this question is hard

Min–max scaling has a single point of failure: it is defined by the two most extreme
values in the data set. If one of those is wrong, the whole scaling is wrong, and it is wrong in
a way that is easy to miss because the output still looks like a properly scaled feature —
values in $[0, 1]$, outlier at the top, everything else near the bottom. Nothing announces the
problem.

Quantifying the damage is what makes the choice a decision rather than an opinion. Consecutive
typical readings are one minute apart, and after min–max scaling they sit $0.000719$ apart. For
a distance-based algorithm such as k-means, that is very close to indistinguishable: the
distances between ordinary points have been compressed by a factor of $1390$ relative to what
they were, while the distance to the outlier has been set to the maximum the scale allows.

Standardisation does better, and the reason is structural. It is defined by the mean and the
standard deviation, which are computed from every value, so a single extreme reading shifts them
without determining them. Here the outlier is worth $3.0\sigma$ — large, but finite, and it
does not set the scale. The resolution improves by a factor of about $3.3$. That is a real
improvement, but it should not be oversold: the outlier has inflated $\sigma$ from roughly
$2.4$ to $415.8$, so standardisation is compromised too, just less catastrophically.

The honest answer to (d) is that both are the wrong question. The value $1400$ is a known
decimal-point error; there is no statistical method that is the right response to a typo. Clean
it, and with the remaining nine values the range is $8$, the standard deviation is about $2.4$,
and the choice between scalers has almost no effect on the model. Where cleaning is not
possible — because the extreme value might be genuine — a robust scaler based on the median and
interquartile range gives a resolution of $0.2$ here, roughly $278$ times what min–max
delivers.

The more general lesson is that preprocessing choices are not neutral. Two defensible scalings
of the same feature can put the ordinary values $0.0007$ apart or $0.2$ apart, and a
distance-based model will behave completely differently in the two cases. Measuring the
resolution, rather than asserting that one method is "better", is what turns that choice into
something a data scientist can defend.
