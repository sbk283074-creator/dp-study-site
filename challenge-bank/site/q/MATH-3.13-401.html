3.13 The closest point of a line segment is an endpoint when the foot of the perpendicular falls outside it

Question

The points $A$, $B$ and $P$ have position vectors

$$\mathbf{a} = 2\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{j}, \qquad \mathbf{b} = 4\mathbf{i} + 5\mathbf{j} + 2\mathbf{k}, \qquad \mathbf{p} = 9\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j} + 3\mathbf{k}.$$

The figure shows the plane containing all three points, with the line through $A$ and $B$ drawn horizontally and the segment $AB$ marked on it.

(a) Show that the point of the line through $A$ and $B$ that is closest to $P$ does not lie on the segment $AB$.

(b) Find the shortest distance from $P$ to the segment $AB$.

(c) A candidate obtains $\sqrt{73/3} \approx 4.93$. State what this value is the distance from $P$ to, and explain why it is not the answer to part (b).

Reveal the answer

(a) $\overrightarrow{AB} = 2\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j} + 2\mathbf{k}$ and $\overrightarrow{AP} = 7\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j} + 3\mathbf{k}$, so $\overrightarrow{AP}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB} = 14 + 12 + 6 = 32$ and $|\overrightarrow{AB}|^{2} = 4 + 16 + 4 = 24$ (M1). The closest point of the line is $\mathbf{a} + t\,\overrightarrow{AB}$ with $t = \dfrac{\overrightarrow{AP}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}}{|\overrightarrow{AB}|^{2}} = \dfrac{32}{24} = \dfrac{4}{3}$, and since $\tfrac{4}{3} > 1$ that point lies beyond $B$ and therefore outside the segment $AB$ (A1).

(b) Because the closest point of the *line* is outside the segment, the closest point of the segment is its nearest endpoint, namely $B$ (M1), and the distance is $|\overrightarrow{BP}| = |5\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j} + \mathbf{k}| = \sqrt{25 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt{27} = 3\sqrt{3} \approx 5.20$ (A1).

(c) $\sqrt{73/3}$ is the perpendicular distance from $P$ to the infinite line through $A$ and $B$: $|\overrightarrow{AP}|^{2} = 49 + 9 + 9 = 67$, and $67 - \dfrac{32^{2}}{24} = 67 - \dfrac{128}{3} = \dfrac{73}{3}$ (M1). It is not the answer to (b) because the foot of that perpendicular is not a point of the segment. A segment is a bounded set, and the closest point of a bounded set need not be where the perpendicular falls: it can be, and here is, an endpoint. The perpendicular distance is smaller because it is measured to a point the segment does not contain, so it is a lower bound that the segment cannot attain (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for the two ingredients of the projection, $\overrightarrow{AP}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB} = 32$ and $|\overrightarrow{AB}|^{2} = 24$, however they are obtained; (A1) for $t = \tfrac{4}{3}$ together with the observation that the point corresponding to $t > 1$ lies beyond $B$. The comparison with $1$ is the mark: a candidate who computes $t = \tfrac{4}{3}$ and says nothing about what it means has done the arithmetic and missed the part.

(b) (M1) for recognising that the endpoint is now the closest point; (A1) for $\sqrt{27} = 3\sqrt{3}$. Accept $5.20$.

(c) (M1) for identifying the value as the distance to the line and reproducing the calculation that produces it; (A1) for the point that the segment is bounded so the minimum over the segment is attained at an endpoint. Do not credit 'it is wrong because the foot is outside' without the consequence, which is that the smaller number is unattainable.

Why this question is hard

The formula for the distance from a point to a line is a formula about a line, and nothing in it remembers where the segment ends. That gap is the entire question. The parameter $t$ carries the information -- $0 \leq t \leq 1$ means the foot is on the segment, $t < 0$ means it is before $A$, $t > 1$ means it is beyond $B$ -- and a candidate who computes $t$ only in order to substitute it into a distance formula will never read it.

The two numbers are close enough to be uncomfortable: $4.93$ against $5.20$. That is deliberate. A large discrepancy would give the game away, whereas this one looks like rounding, and a candidate who has not checked $t$ has no reason to prefer the larger of two plausible answers. The right habit is to read the parameter before using it, which is also the right habit in the optimisation questions where a maximum turns out to sit at the end of an interval rather than at a stationary point.

There is a second lesson in the arithmetic itself. Nothing here is difficult: a scalar product, a squared length, and a comparison of one fraction with one integer. What makes the item worth six marks is that the comparison is the whole question, and it is the step a candidate is most likely to skip on the way to a formula they have already memorised. A bank that only ever asks for the perpendicular distance teaches that formula; this one asks whether the formula is the right thing to reach for.

Challenge lever
The standard method -- project the displacement on to the direction vector and take the length of the remainder -- answers a different question. It gives the distance from P to the infinite line, and it is the right answer only when the foot of the perpendicular happens to lie between A and B. Here the parameter comes out at 4/3, so the foot is beyond B and the closest point of the segment is B itself. The constraint that the point must lie on the segment, which is the whole content of the word segment, is the one thing the formula does not know about, and a candidate who has internalised the formula as 'the distance from a point to a line' will not think to check it.
