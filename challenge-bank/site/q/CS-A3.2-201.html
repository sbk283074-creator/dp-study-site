A3.2 A relation that satisfies third normal form because the attribute on the right of the offending dependency is prime, and therefore fails Boyce-Codd normal form, so that no decomposition of it can be lossless, in Boyce-Codd normal form and dependency preserving at the same time

Theme A: Concepts of computer scienceA3.2 Database design (functional dependencies, candidate keys, and normalisation to third normal form), with A3.1 Database fundamentalsP1 · Section A13 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedDetermineShow thatDetermineEvaluate

A timetabling relation

A school keeps a single relation of the classes it runs. Each row records a student, a subject that the student is taking, and the teacher who takes that student for that subject. The relation is called Enrolment and has the attributes Student, Subject and Teacher. Two rules hold. First, a student is taught a given subject by exactly one teacher. Second, each teacher teaches exactly one subject, although a subject may be taught by more than one teacher. The table shows part of the relation. The school has found that when a teacher changes the subject they teach, many rows must be changed, and that it cannot record the fact that a teacher teaches a subject unless at least one student is taking that subject from that teacher.

Question

The relation Enrolment has the attributes Student, Subject and Teacher, and the school has stated two rules. A student is taught a given subject by exactly one teacher, so Student together with Subject determines Teacher. Each teacher teaches exactly one subject, so Teacher determines Subject, although a subject may be taught by more than one teacher. The school has observed two problems in practice. When a teacher changes the subject they teach, many rows have to be changed together, and a change that touches only some of them would leave the relation contradicting its own rule. The school also cannot record that a teacher teaches a subject unless some student is taking that subject from that teacher, so a newly appointed teacher who has not yet been assigned a student cannot be entered at all. The head of computing has proposed splitting the relation into two relations, one over Student and Teacher and one over Teacher and Subject, and has asked whether that proposal solves the problems.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Determine every candidate key of the relation Enrolment, and justify each one by naming the attributes that it determines.

    (Determine)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that the relation satisfies third normal form but does not satisfy Boyce-Codd normal form, naming the functional dependency that makes the difference and explaining why it is tolerated by one normal form and not by the other.

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine whether the proposed decomposition into the relations (Student, Teacher) and (Teacher, Subject) is lossless and whether it preserves every functional dependency, justifying each answer.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate whether the school should adopt the proposed decomposition or leave the relation in third normal form, given the two problems the school has already observed.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) The attribute set is {Student, Subject, Teacher}. The given dependencies are {Student, Subject} -> Teacher and Teacher -> Subject.

The pair {Student, Subject} determines Teacher by the first dependency, so its closure is the whole attribute set {Student, Subject, Teacher} and the pair is a superkey. Neither Student alone nor Subject alone determines anything else, so no proper subset of the pair is a superkey, and {Student, Subject} is a candidate key.

The pair {Student, Teacher} determines Subject by the second dependency, since Teacher alone determines Subject, so its closure is also the whole attribute set and the pair is a superkey. Neither Student alone nor Teacher alone determines the other, so {Student, Teacher} is a candidate key as well.

There are exactly two candidate keys, {Student, Subject} and {Student, Teacher}. The prime attributes, which are the attributes belonging to at least one candidate key, are therefore Student, Subject and Teacher: all three attributes of the relation are prime.

(b) Third normal form requires that for every functional dependency X -> A, either X is a superkey or A is a prime attribute. Take the first dependency, {Student, Subject} -> Teacher. The determinant {Student, Subject} is a candidate key and therefore a superkey, so this dependency satisfies the condition. Take the second, Teacher -> Subject. The determinant Teacher is not a superkey, because the closure of Teacher is {Teacher, Subject}, which does not contain Student. The dependent, Subject, is a prime attribute, since Subject belongs to the candidate key {Student, Subject}. The dependency therefore satisfies the condition through its second clause, and the relation is in third normal form.

Boyce-Codd normal form requires that for every functional dependency X -> A, X is a superkey, with no alternative clause for prime attributes. The dependency Teacher -> Subject fails this test, because Teacher is not a superkey. The relation is therefore not in Boyce-Codd normal form.

The difference between the two normal forms is exactly the clause that third normal form adds and Boyce-Codd normal form removes. Third normal form allows a determinant that is not a superkey provided the attribute it determines is part of some candidate key. That allowance exists because removing such a dependency can cost more than it saves, which is what part (c) shows. Here the dependent, Subject, is prime, so the allowance applies and the relation is in third normal form but not in Boyce-Codd normal form.

(c) The proposed decomposition replaces Enrolment with R1 over {Student, Teacher} and R2 over {Teacher, Subject}.

The decomposition is lossless. A binary decomposition is lossless when the common attribute set determines at least one of the two relations, that is, when the common attributes form a superkey of at least one of them. The common attribute here is Teacher, and Teacher determines Subject, so Teacher is a superkey of R2 over {Teacher, Subject}. The condition holds, so the join of R1 and R2 reproduces exactly the rows of Enrolment, with no spurious rows and no lost rows.

The decomposition is not dependency preserving. A decomposition preserves the dependencies when the union of the dependencies that hold within the separate relations implies every dependency of the original relation. R2 over {Teacher, Subject} carries Teacher -> Subject, so that dependency survives. R1 over {Student, Teacher} carries no non-trivial dependency at all, because neither Student alone nor Teacher alone determines the other in the original relation. The dependency {Student, Subject} -> Teacher has Student and Subject in different relations, since Student appears only in R1 and Subject only in R2, so it cannot be enforced by either relation and cannot be recovered by joining them. That dependency is lost.

The proposal therefore buys Boyce-Codd normal form and losslessness at the price of the dependency that made {Student, Subject} a key. Losing it means the database can no longer prevent a student from being recorded with two different teachers for the same subject, because no single relation in the schema holds enough information to detect it.

(d) The school faces a genuine choice, because no decomposition of this relation is lossless, in Boyce-Codd normal form and dependency preserving at the same time. The proposed decomposition and the original relation each give up one of the three.

Leaving the relation in third normal form keeps the dependency {Student, Subject} -> Teacher enforceable by the relation itself, so the database can still reject a row that gives one student two teachers for one subject. It keeps the update anomaly the school has observed, because Teacher -> Subject is a dependency between two attributes of the same relation, and changing a teacher's subject means changing every row in which that teacher appears. It also keeps the insertion anomaly, because a row is a class and a teacher with no students cannot be represented.

Adopting the decomposition removes both anomalies. R2 over {Teacher, Subject} holds one row per teacher, so a teacher's subject is recorded once and a newly appointed teacher can be entered before any student is assigned, which also removes the insertion anomaly. The cost is that the lost dependency has to be enforced outside the schema, by a constraint or a check in the application that verifies a student has only one teacher per subject.

The better choice is to adopt the decomposition, because both of the problems the school has actually observed are cured by it and the guarantee that is given up can be replaced by a single constraint that is easy to state and cheap to enforce. The anomalies are present today and cause work every time a teacher changes subject; the lost dependency is a guarantee the school can rebuild in one place. A school with a large and changing timetable will feel the anomalies far more often than it will be inconvenienced by the extra constraint. The judgement is a judgement, however, and an answer that keeps the relation in third normal form and argues that an enforceable dependency is worth more than the convenience of one-row updates is defensible, provided the trade is stated.

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for each candidate key correctly identified with a closure argument, and one mark for stating that the prime attributes are all three of them. A candidate who identifies only {Student, Subject} and misses {Student, Teacher} has missed the dependency Teacher -> Subject and scores at most one mark.

(b) One mark for the statement that third normal form holds, one mark for the reason, that Subject is prime, one mark for the statement that Boyce-Codd normal form fails, and one mark for naming Teacher -> Subject as the offending dependency with the reason that Teacher is not a superkey. A candidate who says the relation fails both normal forms has not used the prime attribute and scores at most two marks.

(c) One mark for lossless with the reason that Teacher is a superkey of (Teacher, Subject), one mark for not dependency preserving, and one mark for naming the lost dependency as {Student, Subject} -> Teacher with the reason that its two attributes cannot lie in the same relation. An answer that claims the decomposition preserves all dependencies scores zero for this part.

(d) No mark for the choice itself; one mark for correctly describing what each option gives up, one mark for connecting the choice to the two anomalies the school has actually observed, and one mark for a reasoned recommendation with its cost stated. Either recommendation can earn full marks if the trade is stated accurately; a recommendation with no mention of the lost dependency cannot exceed two marks.

Why this question is hard

Third normal form and Boyce-Codd normal form are usually presented as a sequence, with the second being a stricter version of the first, and the difference between them is usually reduced to a clause to be memorised. This item makes the difference do work. The relation here satisfies third normal form for a reason that is easy to miss, namely that the attribute on the right of the offending dependency is part of a key, and it fails Boyce-Codd normal form because that reason is not available.

The relation is small, three attributes and two dependencies, and it is deliberately small. With three attributes there is nowhere for the candidate to hide: either the closures are computed and the keys found, or they are not. The two candidate keys are the substance of part (a), and a candidate who finds only one of them will not be able to answer part (b), because the whole argument depends on Subject being prime, and Subject is prime only because it belongs to the key {Student, Subject}. The parts are chained in that way on purpose.

The second half of the item is about the cost of normalisation, which is the part that is usually omitted. Decomposing to Boyce-Codd normal form is presented as an unqualified improvement, and here it is not: the natural decomposition is lossless, so no data is lost, but the dependency that made the pair a key cannot be expressed by either of the two relations, so the schema can no longer prevent a student from being given two teachers for one subject. The candidate has to notice that the price is real and then decide whether it is worth paying.

The final part is an evaluation and has no single right answer, which is why the marking gives no marks for the choice and all three marks for the reasoning. The school has two observed problems and both are cured by the decomposition, which makes adopting it the more natural recommendation, but a candidate who prefers to keep the dependency enforceable and accept the anomalies can take full marks by saying so accurately. What cannot take marks is a recommendation that does not mention what is being given up, because the whole point of the item is that something always is.

Challenge lever
The two functional dependencies point in opposite directions and the candidate has to hold both at once. The first, that a student and a subject together determine the teacher, makes the pair a key. The second, that a teacher determines the subject, points from an attribute that is not a key to an attribute that is part of a key, which is exactly the configuration that third normal form tolerates and Boyce-Codd normal form does not. The whole item turns on the word prime. Third normal form permits a non-superkey determinant provided its dependent is a prime attribute, and Subject is prime because it belongs to the key {Student, Subject}; Boyce-Codd normal form grants no such permission. The last two parts then ask what can be done about it, and the answer is that nothing fully can: the natural decomposition is lossless but loses the dependency that made the pair a key, so the candidate has to choose which guarantee to give up rather than find a design that keeps them all.

Adaptation
Original. The classic example of a relation that is in third normal form and not in Boyce-Codd normal form is a well-known teaching example and is not new; the attributes are usually a student, a subject and a teacher, and the two dependencies point in opposite directions in the usual way. What is constructed here is the framing: the school is given two observed operational problems, one an update anomaly and one an insertion anomaly, and the final part asks the candidate to decide whether to accept the loss of a dependency in order to cure them. The school, the table of rows, the two observed problems and the evaluation are original, and every closure and every candidate key was computed and checked programmatically.
Verification
The closures were computed attribute by attribute and the candidate keys established by checking that no proper subset of either key has a closure equal to the whole attribute set. The prime attributes were read off the candidate keys rather than assumed. The lossless join test was applied to the proposed decomposition by identifying the common attribute and checking whether it is a superkey of one of the two relations, and the dependency preservation test was applied by checking, for each original dependency, whether its attributes lie within a single relation of the decomposition. The three guarantees were then compared to confirm that no decomposition of this relation can hold all three.
