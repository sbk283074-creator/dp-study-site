A strength the outside world is quietly converting into a threat

Business management SL · BM-6.1-001

Unit 6: The SL ToolkitUnit 6: 6.1 SWOT analysis and 6.3 STEEPLE analysis (the SL Toolkit). P1 Section A.P1 · Section A15 marksdifficulty 4ExplainCalculateEvaluate

Calderbank Print & Packaging

Calderbank Print & Packaging is a family printing firm in a market town, employing 40 people. For eighteen years it has printed the weekly leaflets of a supermarket chain under a rolling annual contract that is renewed each spring. Each year the directors write a SWOT: they have again listed the supermarket contract as the business's greatest strength and 'no experience of packaging design' as a weakness, and they propose to spend EUR 60,000 on a faster leaflet press to protect the contract.

Around the firm, however, the market is moving. Shoppers increasingly use supermarket apps instead of paper leaflets, and a rival has installed a digital press that prints short runs cheaply. The government has introduced a tax on virgin paper and placed extended producer responsibility for packaging waste on producers. Energy costs for the presses have risen by about 40% in two years, and trade customers now ask for recyclable cardboard cartons rather than plastic film. Local environmental groups have publicly praised firms that cut paper use.

The table shows how the firm's revenue has actually moved across its three service lines over the last three years.

Question

The directors of Calderbank Print & Packaging must decide whether to spend EUR 60,000 on a faster leaflet press or to invest in packaging capability instead.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Explain how a SWOT analysis and a STEEPLE analysis differ in what they can tell the directors, using one example of each from the case.

    (Explain)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Using the table, calculate each service line's share of total revenue in 2023 and in 2025, the change in total revenue over the period, and revenue per employee in each year. Comment on what the figures show.

    (Calculate)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    Evaluate whether Calderbank should follow the SWOT and spend EUR 60,000 on the leaflet press, or act on the STEEPLE findings instead. Justify your answer.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) SWOT is an audit of the firm's own position. It sorts factors into strengths and weaknesses, which are internal and controllable, and opportunities and threats, which are external, and it is used to match the firm's resources to its environment. STEEPLE examines the external environment alone, across social, technological, economic, environmental, political, legal and ethical factors, and is used to identify change that the firm does not control. (A1)(A1)

The difference matters here. The case example for SWOT is the supermarket leaflet contract, which the directors call a strength because it is long-standing and gives a predictable order book. The case example for STEEPLE is the social and technological shift away from printed leaflets towards supermarket apps, which is outside the firm's control and is shrinking the market for the product it prints. (A1)(A1)

(b) Total revenue: 2023 = 900 + 250 + 150 = EUR 1,300k; 2025 = 590 + 610 + 170 = EUR 1,370k. (M1)(A1)

Shares of total: leaflet printing falls from 900/1,300 = 69.2% to 590/1,370 = 43.1%; packaging rises from 250/1,300 = 19.2% to 610/1,370 = 44.5%; signage rises from 150/1,300 = 11.5% to 170/1,370 = 12.4%. (A1)

Total revenue rose by EUR 70k, or 5.4%, so the business looks as though it is growing. Revenue per employee: 2023 = 1,300,000/40 = EUR 32,500; 2025 = 1,370,000/40 = EUR 34,250. (M1)(A1)

The comment mark is for seeing that the headline is misleading. The total is up only because packaging has grown by 144%, from EUR 250k to EUR 610k, while the line the directors are defending has fallen by EUR 310k, or 34.4%, and is now smaller than packaging. The firm's future is already being decided by the line its SWOT calls a weakness. (A1)

(c) Indicative evaluation. The SWOT points one way. The leaflet contract is the firm's oldest relationship and its largest single order, and losing it would remove about 43% of revenue at a stroke. Spending EUR 60,000 on a faster press lowers the unit cost of the work the contract needs and may make Calderbank the cheapest bidder when the contract is next renewed. On this reading the EUR 60,000 protects the core of the business.

The STEEPLE reading contradicts it. The fall in leaflet revenue is not caused by anything inside the firm: readers are moving to apps, the supermarket has begun cutting print volumes, and the tax on virgin paper and extended producer responsibility raise the cost of the very product the press would print. A faster press makes Calderbank better at producing something its customer is buying less of. The weakness the SWOT admits, no packaging design experience, sits in the one line that is growing, and packaging faces rising demand because of the same environmental and legal pressures that are hurting leaflets. (A1)(A1)

A defensible answer can still support the press, because it buys time and cash while packaging capability is built. But the strongest answers notice that the two tools classify the same contract differently, as a strength internally and a threat externally, and recommend using the leaflet cash flow to fund packaging rather than to defend the declining line. Credit any supported conclusion. (A1)(A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark for each of the two tool descriptions and one for each correctly applied example. A candidate who describes SWOT and STEEPLE accurately but uses no case example earns at most two. Do not credit a SWOT that lists only internal factors or a STEEPLE that includes the firm's own capabilities.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for the two totals and (A1) for the shares; accept shares to one decimal place. Award (M1)(A1) for revenue per employee. The comment mark requires the candidate to contrast the falling leaflet line with the rising packaging line; a candidate who reports only that total revenue grew forfeits it. Follow through on arithmetic slips.

(c) The two discriminating marks are for recognising that the decline is externally driven and that the admitted weakness sits in the growing line. A candidate who simply repeats the SWOT and recommends the press cannot earn more than two marks, however well written. Credit a conclusion that keeps the press only if it is justified by timing or cash flow, not by the claim that leaflets will recover. Do not credit generic statements about the internet that are not tied to the case.

Why this question is hard

The item is built so that the two tools disagree about the same fact. The directors' SWOT is not careless: the supermarket contract really is the firm's largest and oldest relationship, and losing it would remove roughly 43% of revenue. Read internally, it is a strength and defending it is rational. Read through STEEPLE, the same contract is a threat, because the force reducing it - the shift to apps, the paper tax and extended producer responsibility - lies entirely outside the firm and cannot be managed by a better press.

The lever is that the obvious tool is the wrong one. A student who reaches for SWOT because the case is about the firm's position will produce a tidy four-box list and recommend the investment, but the analysis cannot show the trend, because a SWOT is a snapshot of factors, not a model of change. STEEPLE reveals the direction of travel, and the revenue table makes it quantitative: leaflets fall from 69.2% to 43.1% of revenue while packaging rises from 19.2% to 44.5%, so the firm's centre of gravity has already moved.

The trap is the total. Revenue rises by EUR 70k, or 5.4%, and revenue per employee rises from EUR 32,500 to EUR 34,250. A candidate who checks only these concludes that the business is healthy and the contract worth defending. The figure that contradicts this is the 34.4% fall in the leaflet line and the fact that it is now smaller than the packaging line the SWOT calls a weakness.

Classic wrong turns: treating the packaging weakness as a reason to stay with leaflets; listing STEEPLE factors without linking them to the falling line; and recommending diversification without noticing that the capability to diversify already exists inside the firm.
