B.5 Current and circuits

Physics HL · PHYS-B.5-101

Theme B: The particulate nature of matterB.5 (Current and circuits: internal resistance, non-ideal meters, power transfer)P1 · Section A5 marksdifficulty 5technology: allowedCalculateDetermineState

Question

Five independent multiple-choice questions in which a circuit is altered by the act of measuring it, or by the load connected to it. In each case the supply is treated as ideal unless an internal resistance is stated explicitly.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    A cell of emf $12\ \mathrm{V}$ and internal resistance $3.0\ \Omega$ is connected to a variable external resistor. What is the greatest power that can be dissipated in that external resistor?

    (Calculate)
  2. [1 mark](b)

    For the cell in the previous question, at the external resistance that gives the greatest power in the external resistor, what fraction of the total power generated by the cell is delivered to that external resistor?

    (State)
  3. [1 mark](c)

    A cell of negligible internal resistance and emf $6.0\ \mathrm{V}$ is connected in series with a $400\ \Omega$ resistor and a $600\ \Omega$ resistor. A voltmeter of resistance $2.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ is connected across the $600\ \Omega$ resistor. What does the voltmeter read?

    (Calculate)
  4. [1 mark](d)

    A circuit consists of a $12\ \mathrm{V}$ supply of negligible internal resistance connected to a $100\ \Omega$ resistor. An ammeter of resistance $5.0\ \Omega$ is inserted in series to measure the current. What is the percentage error in the current it reads?

    (Calculate)
  5. [1 mark](e)

    A cell is connected to a $4.0\ \Omega$ resistor and the terminal potential difference is $6.0\ \mathrm{V}$. When the same cell is connected to a $9.0\ \Omega$ resistor the terminal potential difference is $7.2\ \mathrm{V}$. What is the internal resistance of the cell?

    (Determine)

Total: 5 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The power in the load is $P = \dfrac{E^2 R}{(R+r)^2}$, which is greatest when $R = r = 3.0\ \Omega$. Then $I = 12/6.0 = 2.0\ \mathrm{A}$ and $P = I^2R = 4.0 \times 3.0 = 12\ \mathrm{W}$, which is also $E^2/(4r)$. (A1) Option B is $E^2/r$, the power dissipated inside the cell at short circuit, and options C and D come from dropping or doubling the factor of four.

(b) At $R = r$ the same current passes through both resistances, so $I^2R = I^2r$ and exactly one half of the generated power reaches the load. (A1) This is why maximum power transfer and high efficiency are different aims: option B needs an ideal cell, and options C and D correspond to $R = 2r$ and $R = r/3$ respectively.

(c) The voltmeter is in parallel with the $600\ \Omega$ resistor, giving $R_p = \dfrac{600 \times 2000}{2600} = 462\ \Omega$. The circuit total becomes $400 + 462 = 862\ \Omega$, so $I = 6.0/862 = 6.96\ \mathrm{mA}$ and the reading is $I R_p = 3.21\ \mathrm{V}$. (A1) Option B assumes an ideal meter, option C mixes the loaded resistance with the unloaded total, and option D loads the wrong resistor.

(d) True current $I_0 = 12/100 = 0.120\ \mathrm{A}$; with the ammeter in place $I = 12/105 = 0.1143\ \mathrm{A}$. The fractional error is $\dfrac{0.120 - 0.1143}{0.120} = 4.76\%$, which equals $R_A/(R + R_A) = 5.0/105$. (A1) Option B uses $R_A/R$, option C ignores the meter, and option D is the ratio of the two currents rather than the error.

(e) The currents are $I_1 = 6.0/4.0 = 1.5\ \mathrm{A}$ and $I_2 = 7.2/9.0 = 0.80\ \mathrm{A}$. Since $V = E - Ir$, subtracting gives $6.0 - 7.2 = -r(1.5 - 0.80)$, so $r = 1.2/0.70 = 1.71\ \Omega$ and $E = 8.57\ \mathrm{V}$. (A1) Option B inverts the gradient, option C quotes the voltage change, and option D quotes the lost volts.

Markscheme notes

One mark per correct option, no negative marking. In (a) accept 12 W reached by differentiating the power expression or by quoting the maximum power theorem, provided the condition R equals r is stated. In (b) accept 50% or one half; note that a candidate who answers (a) correctly but (b) as two thirds has applied the theorem without understanding it. In (c) accept any value between 3.20 V and 3.22 V from premature rounding of the parallel pair. In (d) accept 4.8% to two significant figures, and credit the exact form 5/105. In (e) accept 1.7 ohms, and allow a graphical solution from a V against I plot provided the gradient is taken with the correct sign. Across the cluster, a candidate who rounds the parallel resistance in (c) prematurely may report anything from 3.20 V to 3.22 V and should be credited; a comparable tolerance applies in (e). In (d) the exact form 5.0 over 105 earns the mark even when left as a fraction. Note that (a) and (b) are formally independent, so a candidate may answer either without the other, and no follow-through between them should be assumed either way.

Why this question is hard

The whole cluster tests one idea that is easy to state and hard to hold on to under time pressure: connecting something to a circuit changes that circuit. In (c) and (d) the instrument is the thing that changes it, and the natural instinct to treat a voltmeter as invisible or an ammeter as free is exactly what options B and C are for. In (a) and (b) the load is the thing that changes it, and the pair together tests whether a candidate has memorised the maximum power theorem or understands it: knowing that maximum power occurs at R equals r is worth one mark, but knowing that this is also the point at which half the generated power is thrown away inside the cell is what distinguishes understanding from recall. Item (e) is the hardest to set up because neither the emf nor the internal resistance is given; the route in is to notice that the difference between the two terminal voltages is caused entirely by the difference between the two lost-volt terms, which turns a problem with two unknowns into a single subtraction. The cluster also carries a lesson about experimental design that is easy to state and worth carrying into the investigation: an instrument is only invisible when its resistance is negligible compared with the circuit it is measuring, and the ratio in (c) and (d) is the quantitative form of that rule.
