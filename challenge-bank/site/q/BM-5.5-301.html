5.5 A contract that crosses the relevant range: the range of order sizes for which a positive contribution still destroys profit

Kestrel Foods Ltd: a contract offer and the relevant range

Kestrel Foods Ltd makes and sells one chilled dessert. The selling price is £25 per unit and the variable cost per unit is £13. Fixed costs are £48 000 per month for output up to and including 6 000 units per month. Output above 6 000 units in any month requires a second production shift, which adds £18 000 per month to fixed costs; no other cost changes at that point. Current output and sales are 4 000 units per month, all sold at £25.

A national retailer has offered to buy q units per month for one year at a price of £20 per unit. The retailer distributes in a region where Kestrel does not currently sell, so the contract displaces none of the existing sales, and Kestrel has enough spare capacity on the first shift to supply any contract size the retailer names. The finance director argues that the contract must be accepted because every unit sold at £20 still adds to contribution.

Question

The graph shows Kestrel's monthly profit plotted against the size of the contract. Answer every part with reference to the relevant range, and give money answers to the nearest pound and output answers to the nearest whole unit.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the contribution per unit at the current selling price, the monthly break-even output, and Kestrel's current monthly profit.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the contribution per unit on the contract, and Kestrel's monthly profit if a contract for 2 500 units per month is accepted.

    (Calculate)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the range of contract sizes, if any, for which accepting the contract would leave Kestrel with a lower monthly profit than it earns now, and determine the smallest contract size at which crossing into the second shift becomes worthwhile.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    The retailer's final offer is 2 500 units per month for one year. Evaluate the finance director's claim that the contract must be accepted because every unit adds to contribution, and recommend one change to the terms that would make the contract worth accepting.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) Contribution per unit at the current price is £25 − £13 = £12 (A1). Break-even output is fixed costs divided by the contribution per unit, £48 000 ÷ £12 = 4 000 units per month (M1)(A1). Current output is exactly 4 000 units, so the current monthly profit is 4 000 × £12 − £48 000 = £0: the business is precisely at break-even.
(b) On the contract the price is £20, so the contribution per unit is £20 − £13 = £7 (A1). A contract of 2 500 units takes total output to 4 000 + 2 500 = 6 500 units, which is above the 6 000-unit ceiling of the first shift, so fixed costs rise to £48 000 + £18 000 = £66 000 (M1). Total contribution is 4 000 × £12 + 2 500 × £7 = £48 000 + £17 500 = £65 500, so the monthly profit becomes £65 500 − £66 000 = −£500 (A1). Kestrel would move from breaking even to losing £500 a month.
(c) Let q be the contract size. While 4 000 + q ≤ 6 000, that is q ≤ 2 000, no second shift is needed and profit is 7q, which is positive for every q > 0 (M1). Once q > 2 000 the extra £18 000 of fixed cost is incurred, so profit is 7q − 18 000, which is negative for q < 18 000 ÷ 7 = 2 571.43, that is up to and including 2 571 units (M1)(A1). The contract therefore leaves Kestrel worse off than it is now for every size from 2 001 to 2 571 units inclusive (A1), and the smallest size at which the step repays its own cost is 2 572 units, where profit is 7 × 2 572 − 18 000 = £4 (A1). Profit is not monotonic in the size of the contract: 2 000 units earns £14 000, 2 001 units loses £3 993, and 2 572 units earns £4.
(d) The director's claim is true of the unit and false of the decision. Each contract unit does add £7, but the relevant comparison is not between £7 and zero; it is between the £17 500 the contract contributes and the £18 000 of fixed cost that exists only because the contract was taken (M1). At 2 500 units the contract therefore reduces profit from £0 to −£500 a month, and because the contract runs for a year the loss is about −£6 000 over its life, before any allowance for the disruption of running a second shift for twelve months (A1). Two things qualify this. First, the arithmetic is knife-edge: a loss of £500 a month is 0.8% of monthly contribution, so a small change in the terms reverses the answer, and the business may rationally accept a marginal loss to secure a national listing (A1). Second, the loss is a property of the size and not of the idea: the contract is profitable below 2 001 units and above 2 571 units. The recommendation is to renegotiate one of the two (M1). At 2 500 units the contribution per unit must satisfy 2 500c ≥ 18 000, so c ≥ £7.20 and the price must be at least £20.20; alternatively the contract should be capped at 2 000 units or raised to at least 2 572 units (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for £12, (M1) for dividing fixed costs by contribution, (A1) for 4 000 units and £0. Accept break-even quoted as £100 000 of revenue if the unit figure accompanies it.
(b) (A1) for £7, (M1) for noticing that 6 500 units exceeds the 6 000-unit ceiling and adding £18 000, (A1) for −£500. A candidate who computes £17 500 of contribution and ignores the step scores at most one of the three; this is the whole item, so do not award follow-through from it.
(c) (M1) for treating the two ranges separately, (M1) for solving 7q ≥ 18 000, (A1) for the closed range 2 001 to 2 571 units, (A1) for 2 572 units. Accept 2 571.4 units quoted as the break-even size provided the candidate then rounds *up* to 2 572 for the smallest whole-unit size; a candidate who rounds down to 2 571 has not answered the question and loses the final mark.
(d) (M1) for comparing £17 500 with £18 000 rather than with zero, (A1) for the conclusion that the contract as offered reduces profit, (A1) for a developed qualitative point such as the twelve-month commitment or the strategic value of the listing, (M1)(A1) for a recommendation supported by a figure — £20.20, 2 000 units or 2 572 units. Do not credit 'accept because contribution is positive' however well argued; the question asks for an evaluation of that claim, not a restatement of it.

Why this question is hard

The item is built around a quantity that the syllabus names but that questions almost never test: the relevant range. Break-even analysis is linear inside one range and stops being linear at its edge, and the standard classroom treatment of a special order asks only whether the order's contribution is positive. Here it is, and the order is still wrong, because the order is also the cause of a step in fixed costs that no unit-level calculation can see.

The structure is chosen so that the obvious remedy and the correct remedy are different in kind. A candidate who reaches for contribution answers (b) as £17 500 and stops. The candidate has to discover the step, then discover that the step is caused by the decision rather than merely coincident with it, and then discover the shape of the result: profit is discontinuous at 2 000 units and *rises* again afterwards, so both 'small enough to stay on one shift' and 'large enough to pay for the second' are correct answers and the sizes between them are the only wrong ones. That is a genuinely different task from computing a break-even output, and it is the task a manager actually faces when a large customer's order arrives.

Part (d) is not decoration. Because the loss is £500 against £65 500 of contribution, the arithmetic alone does not settle the decision, and the candidate has to say so while still reaching a recommendation — which is why the mark scheme rewards a renegotiated figure rather than a bare accept or reject.

Challenge lever
The contract earns a positive contribution of £7 a unit, so the obvious test says accept it. But the contract is also the only reason output crosses 6 000 units, and crossing that line costs £18 000 a month whether the contract is for one unit above the line or for four thousand. The decision therefore turns on a threshold the student has to locate: contribution must cover the step, and between the step and the point where it does, the business is worse off than it was before the contract existed.
