Lines and planes in three dimensions

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL3.16-001

AHL 3.13 (scalar product; angle between vectors), AHL 3.16 (vector/cross product), AHL 3.17 (vector equations of a plane)P1 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatFindHence

Question

Two straight lines are given by

$$L_{1}:\ \mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}+s\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
L_{2}:\ \mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix},\qquad s,\,t\in\mathbb{R}.$$

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that $L_{1}$ and $L_{2}$ are skew.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Find the acute angle between $L_{1}$ and $L_{2}$.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the equation of the plane $\Pi$ that contains $L_{1}$ and is parallel to $L_{2}$, giving your answer in the form $\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n}=d$.

    (Find)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Hence find the shortest distance between $L_{1}$ and $L_{2}$.

    (Hence)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Direction vectors: $\mathbf{d}_{1}=(2,-1,1)$, $\mathbf{d}_{2}=(1,1,-2)$.

$\mathbf{d}_{1}$ is not a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{d}_{2}$, so the lines are not parallel. (A1)

At an intersection:

$$1+2s=4+t\ \ (1),\qquad 2-s=1+t\ \ (2),\qquad 3+s=-2t\ \ (3)$$ (M1)

From (2): $t=1-s$. Substituting into (1): $1+2s=4+1-s\Rightarrow3s=4\Rightarrow s=\dfrac43$, so $t=-\dfrac13$. (A1)

Check in (3): LHS $=3+\dfrac43=\dfrac{13}{3}$; RHS $=-2\!\left(-\dfrac13\right)=\dfrac23$. Since $\dfrac{13}{3}\neq\dfrac23$, the equations are inconsistent and the lines do not intersect. (R1)

Not parallel and not intersecting $\Rightarrow$ skew.

(b)

$$\mathbf{d}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{2}=2(1)+(-1)(1)+1(-2)=2-1-2=-1$$ (M1)

$$|\mathbf{d}_{1}|=\sqrt{4+1+1}=\sqrt6,\qquad|\mathbf{d}_{2}|=\sqrt{1+1+4}=\sqrt6$$

$$\cos\theta=\frac{|\mathbf{d}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{2}|}{|\mathbf{d}_{1}||\mathbf{d}_{2}|}=\frac{1}{6}$$ (A1)

$$\theta=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac16\right)=80.4^{\circ}\ (1\ \text{d.p.})$$ (A1)

(c) $\Pi$ contains $\mathbf{d}_{1}$ and is parallel to $\mathbf{d}_{2}$, so its normal is

$$\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{d}_{1}\times\mathbf{d}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}(-1)(-2)-(1)(1)\\(1)(1)-(2)(-2)\\(2)(1)-(-1)(1)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\5\\3\end{pmatrix}$$ (M1)(A1)

(Check: $\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{1}=2-5+3=0$ and $\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{2}=1+5-6=0$.)

$\Pi$ passes through $(1,2,3)$ on $L_{1}$, so

$$\mathbf{r}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\5\\3\end{pmatrix}=1(1)+2(5)+3(3)=20$$ (M1)(A1)

$$\boxed{\ \mathbf{r}\cdot(1,\,5,\,3)=20\ }\qquad(\text{i.e. }x+5y+3z=20)$$

(d) Since $L_{2}$ is parallel to $\Pi$ and does not lie in it (its point $(4,1,0)$ gives $4+5+0=9\neq20$), the shortest distance between the skew lines is the perpendicular distance from $L_{2}$ to $\Pi$. (M1)

$$\text{distance}=\frac{|9-20|}{\sqrt{1^{2}+5^{2}+3^{2}}}=\frac{11}{\sqrt{35}}$$ (A1)

$$=\frac{11}{5.916}=1.86\ \text{units}\ (3\ \text{s.f.})$$ (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Both conditions are needed: not parallel AND not intersecting. Award (M1) for setting up the three component equations; (A1) for solving two of them; (R1) for the explicit check that the third is violated — a candidate who solves only two equations and declares 'no solution' has not shown skew.

(b) Accept $80.4^{\circ}$ or $1.403$ radians. Using the negative dot product directly (giving $\theta=\cos^{-1}(-1/6)=99.6^{\circ}$) earns (M1)(A1) but forfeits the final mark, since the *acute* angle was asked for.

(c) Credit an equivalent method: writing $\mathbf{n}=(a,b,c)$ and solving $2a-b+c=0$, $a+b-2c=0$ simultaneously (giving $a:b:c=1:5:3$). The check that $(4,1,0)$ is not in the plane is desirable but award it in (d) if omitted here.

(d) The (M1) is for the *reasoning* — that the distance from a point on $L_2$ to $\Pi$ equals the shortest distance between the lines. A correct formula with no justification earns at most one mark. Accept the equivalent scalar-projection method $\dfrac{|(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{n}|}{|\mathbf{n}|}$.

Why this question is hard

The individual techniques here are standard; the difficulty is that part (d) has no obvious starting point unless you have deliberately built the plane in part (c) as a tool.

The idea that unlocks it: if you construct the unique plane that contains one line and is parallel to the other, then the second line lies at a *constant* perpendicular distance from that plane, and that constant is the shortest distance between the lines. Students who do not make this connection usually try to minimise $|\mathbf{r}_1(s)-\mathbf{r}_2(t)|$ directly — a two-variable minimisation that is possible but slow and error-prone without technology (and this is a no-technology paper).

Two smaller traps:

  • In (a), solving the first two component equations always produces values of $s$ and $t$; the question is only settled by substituting into the *third*. Candidates who stop after two equations will happily conclude 'they intersect'.
  • In (c) the normal must be perpendicular to *both* directions. Using $\mathbf{d}_1$ or $\mathbf{d}_2$ as the normal, or averaging them, are both common and both wrong.

The numbers were chosen so the arithmetic stays clean: the two direction vectors have equal magnitude $\sqrt6$, and the cross product gives a small-integer normal.

Classic wrong turns: using $\mathbf{d}_1\cdot\mathbf{d}_2$ without the modulus; omitting the check in (a); and in (d), computing the distance from a point on $L_1$ (which lies *in* the plane and so gives zero).
