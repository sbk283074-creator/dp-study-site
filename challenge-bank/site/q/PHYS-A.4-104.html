A.4 An Atwood machine with a massive pulley: the effective inertia is m1 + m2 + I/R^2

Physics HL · PHYS-A.4-104

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.4 Rigid body mechanics (moment of inertia, Newton's second law for rotation)P2 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedStateShow thatCalculateDetermine

Question

Two blocks of masses $m_1 = 4.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $m_2 = 2.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ are connected by a light inextensible string that passes over a pulley. The pulley is a uniform disc of radius $R = 0.120\,\mathrm{m}$ and moment of inertia $I = 0.0180\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ about its axis. The string does not slip on the pulley, the axle is frictionless, and the blocks move vertically. Take $g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

The block of mass $m_1$ is released from rest and moves downwards, so the block of mass $m_2$ moves upwards.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    State, with a reason, why the tension in the string is not the same on the two sides of the pulley. Write an equation relating the two tensions to the angular acceleration of the pulley.

    (State)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that the acceleration $a$ of the blocks is given by

    $$a = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)g}{m_1 + m_2 + \dfrac{I}{R^2}}.$$

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Calculate the acceleration of the blocks and the two tensions in the string. Verify that your values satisfy the rotational equation you wrote in part (a).

    (Calculate)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the moment of inertia the pulley would need for the acceleration to be exactly half of what it would be if the pulley were massless. Express your answer both as the equivalent mass $I/R^2$ and as a moment of inertia in $\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$.

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The pulley has moment of inertia, so a resultant torque is needed to give it an angular acceleration (A1). If the tensions were equal, the torques they exert about the axle would cancel exactly and the pulley could not accelerate, while the blocks would be accelerating -- which would mean the string was slipping (A1). Hence $T_1 > T_2$, and with $\tau = I\alpha$ and $a = \alpha R$:
$$ (T_1 - T_2)R = I\alpha = \frac{Ia}{R} \quad (\mathrm{A1}). $$

(b) For $m_1$ moving downwards: $m_1g - T_1 = m_1a$ (A1). For $m_2$ moving upwards: $T_2 - m_2g = m_2a$ (A1). Adding these and using the rotational equation from (a) divided by $R$, $T_1 - T_2 = \dfrac{Ia}{R^2}$ (M1):
$$m_1g - m_2g - (T_1 - T_2) = (m_1 + m_2)a \quad \Rightarrow \quad (m_1 - m_2)g - \frac{Ia}{R^2} = (m_1 + m_2)a,$$
so $(m_1 - m_2)g = a\left(m_1 + m_2 + \dfrac{I}{R^2}\right)$ and the result follows (AG).

(c) First $\dfrac{I}{R^2} = \dfrac{0.0180}{0.120^2} = \dfrac{0.0180}{0.0144} = 1.25\,\mathrm{kg}$ (A1), so the effective inertia is $4.00 + 2.00 + 1.25 = 7.25\,\mathrm{kg}$. Hence
$$a = \frac{(4.00 - 2.00)(9.81)}{7.25} = \frac{19.62}{7.25} = 2.71\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \quad (\mathrm{A1}).$$
From the linear equations: $T_1 = m_1(g - a) = 4.00(9.81 - 2.7062) = 28.4\,\mathrm{N}$ (A1) and $T_2 = m_2(g + a) = 2.00(9.81 + 2.7062) = 25.0\,\mathrm{N}$ (A1).
Verification: $T_1 - T_2 = 28.415 - 25.032 = 3.383\,\mathrm{N}$, so the resultant torque is $3.383 \times 0.120 = 0.4059\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. Also $\alpha = \dfrac{a}{R} = \dfrac{2.7062}{0.120} = 22.55\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$ and $I\alpha = 0.0180 \times 22.55 = 0.4059\,\mathrm{N\,m}$, which agrees (A1).

(d) With a massless pulley, $a_0 = \dfrac{(m_1 - m_2)g}{m_1 + m_2} = \dfrac{19.62}{6.00} = 3.27\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ (A1). Half of this is $1.635\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, so (M1)
$$\frac{19.62}{6.00 + \frac{I}{R^2}} = 1.635 \quad \Rightarrow \quad 6.00 + \frac{I}{R^2} = \frac{19.62}{1.635} = 12.0,$$
giving the equivalent mass $\dfrac{I}{R^2} = 6.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ (A1) and hence
$$I = 6.00 \times 0.120^2 = 6.00 \times 0.0144 = 0.0864\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2} \quad (\mathrm{A1}).$$
This is $4.8$ times the original moment of inertia, which is why the acceleration falls by only a factor of two: doubling the effective inertia from $6.00$ to $12.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ is exactly what halving the acceleration requires.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (A1) for stating that a resultant torque is needed to accelerate the pulley, (A1) for a correct consequence -- either that equal tensions would give zero resultant torque, or that equal tensions would imply slipping -- and (A1) for the rotational equation in any correct form, including (T1 - T2)R = I*alpha with a = alpha*R stated separately.
(b) Award (A1) for each correct linear equation of motion with signs consistent with the stated directions, (M1) for combining all three equations and eliminating T1 and T2, and the final mark is (AG): the result is given, so the elimination must be shown. Candidates who add the two linear equations but neglect the rotational term earn (A1)(A1) only.
(c) Award (A1) for 1.25 kg, (A1) for a = 2.71 m s^-2 (accept 2.70-2.71; units required), (A1) for each tension (accept 28.3-28.5 and 25.0-25.1), (A1) for the check. The check must compare two torques, not merely restate one of the equations used.
(d) Award (A1) for a0 = 3.27 m s^-2, (M1) for setting up the equation with a0/2, (A1) for I/R^2 = 6.00 kg, (A1) for I = 0.0864 kg m^2. Follow-through applies from an incorrect but stated value of a0; award the last three marks on the candidate's own value.

Why this question is hard

The physics here is one idea wearing several costumes: the pulley has to be spun up, and spinning it up costs torque. That cost is why the two tensions differ, and the difference between the two tensions is the only thing in the problem that knows the moment of inertia exists.

The quantity $I/R^2$ deserves attention because it is the point where most candidates lose the thread. It has the units of kilograms and it sits in the formula added to two genuine masses, but it is not a mass: nothing is being lifted that weighs $I/R^2$ newtons. It is the rotational inertia of the pulley expressed in the translational units the rest of the problem is using, and the conversion factor is $1/R^2$ because the pulley's rim has to move at the same speed as the string. A candidate who understands this can predict the direction of the answer: a heavier pulley reduces the acceleration but never reverses it, and the limit of a very heavy pulley is $a \to 0$, not something else.

Part (d) is built to expose whether that understanding is there. The question asks for the moment of inertia that halves the acceleration, and the natural guess is that it doubles the moment of inertia. It does not: it takes the effective inertia from $6.00$ kg to $12.0$ kg, which means adding $6.00$ kg of equivalent mass, which is $4.8$ times the original pulley. The factor $4.8$ is not a round number and cannot be guessed, so the part discriminates cleanly.

This is adapted from the US AP Physics C: Mechanics treatment of the Atwood machine with a massive pulley. The AP version typically supplies the rotational equation and asks for the acceleration. Here the reasoning about why the tensions differ is asked for explicitly, the general formula is derived rather than given, and the final part inverts the relationship -- asking for the inertia that produces a stated acceleration, which is a different direction of travel from anything the source asks.
