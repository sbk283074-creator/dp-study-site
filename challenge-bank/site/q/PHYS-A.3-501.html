A.3 Work and power: the area under a force-distance graph against a constant friction the graph does not show

Physics HL · PHYS-A.3-501

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.3 (Work, energy and power: work done as the area under a force-distance graph, the work-energy theorem, kinetic energy, and power as the product of a force and the velocity of the body on which it acts)P1 · Section A5 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedDetermineCalculate

Question

A trolley of mass 3.0 kg starts from rest at $x = 0$ and is pushed along a straight, level track by a force whose magnitude varies with the distance $x$ exactly as shown in the figure. The force rises uniformly from zero at the start to 6.0 N at $x = 2.0$ m, stays at 6.0 N until $x = 5.0$ m, and then falls uniformly to zero at $x = 8.0$ m, where the pushing stops. Throughout the motion a constant frictional force of 2.0 N acts on the trolley, always opposing its motion; this force is not shown on the graph. The trolley may be treated as a particle. Questions (a) to (e) all refer to this motion. Each is worth one mark and numerical answers should be given to three significant figures.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    Determine the total work done by the applied force on the trolley over the whole 8.0 m of the motion.

    (Determine)
  2. [1 mark](b)

    Calculate the kinetic energy of the trolley at the instant it reaches $x = 8.0$ m, taking the friction into account.

    (Calculate)
  3. [1 mark](c)

    Calculate the speed of the trolley at the instant it reaches $x = 8.0$ m.

    (Calculate)
  4. [1 mark](d)

    Determine the distance from the start at which the kinetic energy of the trolley has its greatest value.

    (Determine)
  5. [1 mark](e)

    Calculate the power developed by the applied force at the instant the trolley passes $x = 5.0$ m.

    (Calculate)

Total: 5 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) A. The work done by a force that varies with distance is the area between
its graph and the distance axis, so the region has to be cut into shapes whose areas
are known. It splits into a triangle of area $\tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)(6.0) = 6.0$ J, a
rectangle of area $(3.0)(6.0) = 18.0$ J and a second triangle of area
$\tfrac{1}{2}(3.0)(6.0) = 9.0$ J. Their sum is $6.0 + 18.0 + 9.0 = 33.0$ J. (A1)

(b) C. Friction acts over the whole 8.0 m and always opposes the motion, so it
does $-2.0 \times 8.0 = -16.0$ J of work. The net work is therefore
$33.0 - 16.0 = 17.0$ J. The trolley started from rest, so by the work-energy theorem
the final kinetic energy is exactly this net work, 17.0 J. (A1)

(c) B. Setting $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2} = 17.0$ J with $m = 3.0$ kg gives
$v = \sqrt{2(17.0)/3.0} = \sqrt{11.33} = 3.37$ m s$^{-1}$. (A1)

(d) D. The kinetic energy is the *accumulated* net work, so what matters is not
how large the applied force is but whether it exceeds the friction. While $F > 2.0$ N
the trolley keeps gaining energy; once $F < 2.0$ N it starts losing it. On the
falling branch the graph is straight from $(5.0,\ 6.0)$ to $(8.0,\ 0)$, so
$F = 6.0 - 2.0(x - 5.0) = 16.0 - 2.0x$. Setting $F = 2.0$ N gives $x = 7.0$ m. This
lies two metres beyond the point where the applied force is largest, and a metre
before the force disappears. (A1)

(e) B. Power at an instant is $P = Fv$ with both quantities evaluated at that
instant, so the speed at $x = 5.0$ m is needed first. The applied work to that point
is $6.0 + 18.0 = 24.0$ J and friction has removed $2.0 \times 5.0 = 10.0$ J, so the
kinetic energy there is 14.0 J and $v = \sqrt{2(14.0)/3.0} = 3.06$ m s$^{-1}$. Hence
$P = 6.0 \times 3.06 = 18.3$ W. (A1)

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking and no method marks: the key is the answer.
Each distractor is the exact result of a named error, so a wrong option identifies the
error rather than being an arbitrary number.

(a) Accept 33.0 J. An answer of 48.0 J has treated the force as constant at its
maximum value; 27.0 J means the first triangle was left out; 24.0 J means a mean
force of 3.0 N was assumed, and the mean height of this graph is 33.0/8.0 = 4.1 N,
not 3.0 N.

(b) Accept 17.0 J. An answer of 33.0 J shows that the 16.0 J removed by friction was
never subtracted. An answer of 25.0 J has charged friction over half the distance
only, and 11.0 J follows from the incomplete work in part (a).

(c) Accept 3.37 m s$^{-1}$. Do not accept 4.69 m s$^{-1}$, which is the frictionless
answer. A candidate who used 25.0 J in (b) and then computes 4.08 m s$^{-1}$ here has
been consistent but earns no mark, because the value carried forward was itself wrong.

(d) Accept 7.0 m and no other value. This is the discriminating question of the
cluster. An answer of 5.0 m takes the point of greatest force for the point of
greatest energy; 8.0 m assumes the energy is still rising when the force has gone;
6.5 m treats the friction as though it were the 3.0 N mean of the falling branch.

(e) Accept 18.3 W. The commonest error is to reuse the final speed from part (c),
which gives 20.2 W; allow it only if the candidate has also used the speed at the end
in part (c), which it does not. An answer of 24.0 W has confused the work done up to
that point with the rate of doing work.

Why this question is hard

The whole cluster rests on one idea that a single formula cannot carry: a graph of
force against distance is a record of *accumulation*, and the quantity that
accumulates is the net work, not the applied work.

Part (a) is the mechanical one, and the only trap is that the mean height of the
graph is not the average of its endpoint values. Three shapes, three areas, 33.0 J.
Everything afterwards either carries that number forward or loses it.

Part (b) adds the friction, and this is where the cluster first separates candidates.
A constant 2.0 N over 8.0 m is 16.0 J, which is very nearly half of the 33.0 J the
push supplied. Forgetting it is not a slip in method but a change of question: 33.0 J
is the right answer to "how much work did the push do", which is not what was asked.

Part (d) is the question the cluster exists for. The instinct that the kinetic energy
is greatest where the force is greatest is a very natural one, and it is wrong here
because kinetic energy responds to the *net* force integrated over distance, not to
the applied force at a point. Between 5.0 m and 7.0 m the push is weakening but is
still stronger than the friction, so the trolley is still accelerating and still
gaining energy. It is only at 7.0 m, where the falling graph crosses the value of the
friction, that the accumulation turns round. After that the trolley is still moving
forward, and still being pushed, but it is slowing down. The same distinction —
between a quantity and the rate at which that quantity is changing — is what makes
the point of greatest force, the point of greatest speed and the point of greatest
acceleration three different places on the same graph.

Part (e) closes the loop by asking for an instantaneous rate rather than an
accumulated amount. Power is $Fv$ at one instant, so it needs the speed at
$x = 5.0$ m, which is not the speed at $x = 8.0$ m and has to be recovered from the
net work over the first five metres. A candidate who reaches for the final speed has
not made an arithmetic error; they have answered a question about the end of the
motion when asked about the middle of it.
