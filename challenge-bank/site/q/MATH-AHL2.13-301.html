2.13 A rational function whose oblique asymptote is crossed at exactly one point, found from the remainder of the division rather than from its quotient

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL2.13-301

Topic 2: FunctionsAHL 2.13 (rational functions of the form (ax+b)/(cx^2+dx+e) and of the form (ax^2+bx+c)/(dx+e)) with SL 2.4 (determine key features of graphs)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedStateShow thatDetermineShow that

Question

A function $f$ is defined by
$$f(x) = \frac{x^{3} - 2x^{2} + 3x - 1}{x^{2} - 1}, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}, \; x \neq -1,\; x \neq 1.$$
The graphs of $y = f(x)$ and of the oblique (non-vertical) asymptote to that graph are to be considered. Note that the two branches of the graph of $y = f(x)$ are not separated by the oblique asymptote in the way they are separated by the vertical asymptotes.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    State the equations of the two vertical asymptotes of the graph of $y = f(x)$, and determine the coordinates of the point at which the graph meets the $y$-axis.

    (State)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Show that $f(x)$ may be written in the form $x - 2 + \dfrac{4x - 3}{x^{2} - 1}$, and hence state the equation of the oblique asymptote to the graph of $y = f(x)$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine the coordinates of the point at which the graph of $y = f(x)$ crosses its oblique asymptote, and verify by direct substitution that the point you have found does lie on the graph.

    (Determine)
  4. [5 marks](d)

    Show that the equation $f(x) = 0$ has exactly one real solution, and determine that solution correct to three significant figures. Explain why the fact you have proved also tells you how many times the graph crosses the $x$-axis.

    (Show that)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The denominator $x^{2} - 1$ is zero when $x = 1$ and when $x = -1$, and the numerator is non-zero at both of those values, since $1 - 2 + 3 - 1 = 1 \neq 0$ and $-1 - 2 - 3 - 1 = -7 \neq 0$ (A1). The vertical asymptotes are therefore
$$x = 1 \quad\text{and}\quad x = -1 \;(A1).$$
At the $y$-axis, $x = 0$ and
$$f(0) = \frac{-1}{-1} = 1 ,$$
so the graph meets the $y$-axis at $(0,\,1)$ (A1).
(b) Dividing the numerator by the denominator,
$$(x - 2)(x^{2} - 1) = x^{3} - 2x^{2} - x + 2 ,$$
and subtracting this from the numerator gives
$$\bigl(x^{3} - 2x^{2} + 3x - 1\bigr) - \bigl(x^{3} - 2x^{2} - x + 2\bigr) = 4x - 3 \;(M1),$$
so that
$$f(x) = x - 2 + \frac{4x - 3}{x^{2} - 1} \;(A1),$$
as required. As $\lvert x \rvert \to \infty$ the final term tends to zero, because its numerator is of degree one and its denominator of degree two, so the graph approaches the line
$$y = x - 2 \;(A1),$$
which is the oblique asymptote.
(c) The graph crosses the oblique asymptote at those values of $x$ for which $f(x) = x - 2$, that is, values for which the remainder term vanishes (M1):
$$\frac{4x - 3}{x^{2} - 1} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 4x - 3 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \tfrac{3}{4} \;(A1).$$
This value lies in the domain, since $\tfrac{3}{4}$ is neither $1$ nor $-1$. The corresponding value of $y$ is taken from the asymptote itself, because at a crossing point the curve and its asymptote share a value:
$$y = \tfrac{3}{4} - 2 = -\tfrac{5}{4} \;(A1),$$
so the crossing point is $\bigl(\tfrac{3}{4},\,-\tfrac{5}{4}\bigr)$. Checking by direct substitution into $f$ itself,
$$f\Bigl(\tfrac{3}{4}\Bigr) = \frac{\tfrac{27}{64} - \tfrac{18}{16} + \tfrac{9}{4} - 1}{\tfrac{9}{16} - 1} = \frac{\tfrac{35}{64}}{-\tfrac{7}{16}} = -\tfrac{5}{4} \;(A1),$$
which agrees, confirming that the point is genuinely on the curve and not merely on the asymptote.
(d) $f(x) = 0$ exactly when its numerator is zero and its denominator is not, so consider $g(x) = x^{3} - 2x^{2} + 3x - 1$ (M1). Differentiating,
$$g'(x) = 3x^{2} - 4x + 3 ,$$
whose discriminant is $(-4)^{2} - 4(3)(3) = 16 - 36 = -20 < 0$ (A1). Since the leading coefficient of $g'$ is positive and $g'$ has no real roots, $g'(x) > 0$ for every real $x$, so $g$ is strictly increasing on the whole of $\mathbb{R}$ and can vanish at most once (R1). Now $g(0.43) = -0.000293 < 0$ and $g(0.431) = 0.001541 > 0$, so by the intermediate value theorem $g$ has a real root between $0.430$ and $0.431$, and by strict increase it has no other (A1). Refining to three significant figures gives
$$x = 0.430 \;(A1),$$
and this lies in the domain, since it is neither $1$ nor $-1$. Because $g$ is strictly increasing the equation $f(x) = 0$ has exactly one real solution, so the graph crosses the $x$-axis exactly once.

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for both vertical asymptotes, (A1) for verifying that the numerator is non-zero at each, (A1) for the intercept $(0,\,1)$. Accept the two equations stated without the verification if the candidate has at least established that the denominator vanishes there. Candidates who give the vertical asymptotes as $x = \pm 1$ are condoned; $x^{2} - 1 = 0$ written alone without solving to $x = \pm 1$ does not score the first mark.
(b) (M1) for evidence of dividing the two polynomials, for example a correctly formed product $(x - 2)(x^{2} - 1)$ or a long-division layout, (A1) for the stated quotient-plus-remainder form, (A1) for the oblique asymptote $y = x - 2$. The asymptote may be reasoned from degrees rather than from limits: the remainder term has numerator degree one against denominator degree two, hence tends to zero. Follow-through applies: a candidate whose remainder is wrong but who then reads the asymptote correctly from a wrong expression scores (A1) for the method but loses the accuracy mark.
(c) (M1) for setting the remainder term equal to zero rather than, say, differentiating the difference, (A1) for $x = \tfrac{3}{4}$, (A1) for the $y$-value $-\tfrac{5}{4}$ taken from the asymptote, (A1) for the verification by substitution. Do not accept a candidate who finds the intersection of the two *vertical* asymptotes with the oblique line: those two points are not on the curve at all, since $f$ is undefined at $x = \pm 1$. A candidate who states that no crossing point exists because 'curves never meet their asymptotes' scores no marks in this part, however well argued.
(d) (M1) for reducing the problem to the numerator being zero, (A1) for forming $g'(x) = 3x^{2} - 4x + 3$ and evaluating its discriminant, (R1) for reasoning that a positive leading coefficient together with a negative discriminant forces $g' > 0$ everywhere, (A1) for locating the single root in a stated interval, (A1) for $0.430$ to three significant figures. Accept any correct method for locating the root, including a Newton iteration or a graphical argument from a calculator, but the uniqueness argument must be present for the reasoning mark: solving the cubic numerically and asserting that there is only one root scores the accuracy mark only. Forfeits: giving the root as $0.431$ through failing to interpolate, or rounding to three decimal places rather than three significant figures; both lose the final mark.

Why this question is hard

The single idea here is that an oblique asymptote is not a barrier. Students are taught the vertical case first and most thoroughly, and in the vertical case the curve genuinely cannot cross: the function is undefined where the asymptote is. Nothing in that argument transfers to the oblique case, where the asymptote is a line the curve gets close to, and where nothing prevents the approximation from being exact at one particular point.

What makes the item require actual work rather than a slogan is that the crossing point lives in the remainder and not in the quotient. Anyone who performs the division tends to keep only the quotient, because the quotient is what the asymptote question asks for, and once the quotient has been written down the remainder looks like a leftover. But the crossing condition is precisely that the difference between the curve and its asymptote is zero, and that difference is the remainder term. Somebody who never writes the remainder down has thrown away the only part of the expression that can answer part (c).

The numbers were chosen so that the crossing point is exact and clean. With the denominator $x^{2} - 1$ the natural candidates are $x = \pm 1$; the root of the remainder is instead $x = \tfrac{3}{4}$, comfortably inside the gap between the two vertical asymptotes, so the crossing happens on the small central branch and not far out on a tail. That also makes the verification cheap: substituting $x = \tfrac{3}{4}$ gives a numerator of $\tfrac{35}{64}$ and a denominator of $-\tfrac{7}{16}$, and the quotient is $-\tfrac{5}{4}$ exactly.

Part (d) is included because the number of $x$-axis crossings is the other quantity a diagram gets wrong. The obvious move is to try the discriminant of the cubic or to plot it; neither settles uniqueness. The derivative is a quadratic with negative discriminant and positive leading coefficient, which forces it to be positive everywhere, and a strictly increasing cubic crosses zero exactly once. That is a three-line argument and it is the only one available, because there is no discriminant test for a cubic in this syllabus.

The wrong turn worth naming explicitly: reporting that no crossing exists. It is not a slip in arithmetic, it is a rule learned in one setting and applied in another where its justification does not survive. The vertical-asymptote rule rests on the function being undefined on the line; remove that and the rule has nothing under it.

Challenge lever
A candidate who has been taught that a curve approaches its asymptote without meeting it will draw two branches that never touch it and will report no crossing point at all. That rule is true for vertical asymptotes and false for oblique ones. Writing the function as quotient plus remainder, the remainder term changes sign at one value of x, and at that value it is exactly zero, so the curve passes through the line it is approaching. The point cannot be found without carrying out the division and keeping the remainder, because it is the remainder alone that carries the information -- the quotient, which is all the asymptote is, has discarded it.
