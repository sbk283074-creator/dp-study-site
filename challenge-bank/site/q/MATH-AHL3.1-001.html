The two points on the line that are exactly three units from the origin

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL3.1-001

SL 3.1 (the distance between two points in three-dimensional space)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatFindDetermine

Question

The points $A(1,0,2)$, $B(3,4,0)$ and $C(5,8,-2)$ are given, and $O$ denotes the origin. Each part below refers to these three points and to the vectors that join them.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that the points $A(1,0,2)$, $B(3,4,0)$ and $C(5,8,-2)$ are collinear.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the distance $AC$, giving your answer in the form $k\sqrt{6}$ where $k$ is a positive integer.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the coordinates of the point $P$ on the segment $AC$ such that $AP:PC=1:3$.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the coordinates of the two points on the line through $A$ and $C$ whose distance from the origin is exactly three units.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The vector $\overrightarrow{AB}=(3-1,4-0,0-2)=(2,4,-2)$ (M1) and $\overrightarrow{BC}=(5-3,8-4,-2-0)=(2,4,-2)$ (A1). The two vectors are equal, so the segments $AB$ and $BC$ are parallel (R1). Since they also share the point $B$, the three points must lie on one straight line (A1)(AG). Equal displacement vectors force $A$, $B$ and $C$ onto a single line, and no other arrangement of the points is consistent with the two vectors just found.

(b) The vector $\overrightarrow{AC}=(5-1,8-0,-2-2)=(4,8,-4)$ (M1). By the distance formula, $AC=\sqrt{4^{2}+8^{2}+(-4)^{2}}$ (M1) $=\sqrt{16+64+16}=\sqrt{96}$ (A1) $=4\sqrt{6}$ (A1), so $k=4$. The factor $16$ inside the root is what produces the exact surd form rather than a decimal. The distance formula used here is simply the three-dimensional form of Pythagoras applied to the three coordinate differences.

(c) Since $AP:PC=1:3$, the point $P$ divides $AC$ internally in that ratio, so $P=A+\frac{1}{4}(C-A)$ (M1). Now $\frac{1}{4}(C-A)=\frac{1}{4}(4,8,-4)=(1,2,-1)$ (A1), hence $P=(1,0,2)+(1,2,-1)$ (A1) $=(2,2,1)$ (A1). The ratio places $P$ one quarter of the way from $A$ towards $C$. The fraction multiplies the whole vector $C-A$, so reversing the ratio would place the point three quarters of the way along instead.

(d) A general point of the line is $(1+4t,8t,2-4t)$ (M1). Its squared distance from $O$ is $(1+4t)^{2}+(8t)^{2}+(2-4t)^{2}$ (M1) $=96t^{2}-8t+5$ (A1). Setting this equal to $9$ gives $96t^{2}-8t-4=0$, that is $24t^{2}-2t-1=0$ (M1), so $t=\frac{1}{4}$ or $t=-\frac{1}{6}$ (A1). The two points are therefore $(2,2,1)$ and $\left(\frac{1}{3},-\frac{4}{3},\frac{8}{3}\right)$ (A1); the first of these is exactly the point $P$ found in part (c) (R1). The negative root describes the point reached by travelling in the opposite direction from $A$, away from $C$, and it lies on the same infinite line.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for forming at least one of the vectors $\overrightarrow{AB}$ or $\overrightarrow{BC}$; the (A1) requires both vectors correct. The (R1) is the reasoning mark: candidates must state that equal vectors have the same direction and that the points share $B$, so a candidate who computes both vectors but draws no collinearity conclusion forfeits it. The (AG) confirms the printed result.

(b) Award (M1) for the vector $\overrightarrow{AC}$ and (M1) for the distance formula. The two (A1) marks are independent: one for reaching $\sqrt{96}$, one for the exact surd form $4\sqrt{6}$. Do not accept a decimal such as $9.798$.

(c) The (M1) is for the section formula in any correct form. A candidate who uses $\frac{3}{4}A+\frac{1}{4}C$ earns full credit. Accept the coordinates in any order provided they are clearly labelled.

(d) This is the discriminating part. The (M1) is for parametrising the line, and the second (M1) for forming the squared distance. The (A1) for the quadratic $24t^{2}-2t-1=0$ may be awarded for any equivalent form. Both values of $t$ are required for the (A1), and both points for the final (A1). The (R1) recognises that $(2,2,1)$ coincides with $P$; follow through from an incorrect $P$ is allowed.

Why this question is hard

The first three parts are deliberately gentle so that the final part can carry the difficulty. Parts (a) and (b) ask for collinearity and a distance, which most candidates can do, and part (c) introduces the point $P$ that will reappear at the end. The lever is the final part: the line is fixed by $A$ and $C$, and the question asks for the points on that line at a given distance from a point that does not lie on it. Candidates who try to solve this by inspection or by drawing will not find the second point.

The natural route is to parametrise the line as $A+t(C-A)=(1+4t,8t,2-4t)$ and then impose $|OP|^{2}=9$. Expanding gives the quadratic $96t^{2}-8t+5$, and setting it equal to $9$ gives $24t^{2}-2t-1=0$, whose roots are $t=\frac{1}{4}$ and $t=-\frac{1}{6}$. The two roots correspond to the two points, one lying between $A$ and $C$ and one beyond $A$ on the other side. A candidate who assumes the answer lies on the segment $AC$ will find only $(2,2,1)$ and lose the final marks.

The most common error is to set the distance rather than the squared distance equal to $9$, which introduces a square root and hides the quadratic. Another is to use a parameter measured from $O$ instead of along $AC$. The appearance of $P$ as one of the two solutions is the intended check: it confirms that the algebra is consistent with part (c) and rewards candidates who notice the connection rather than treating the parts as unrelated. A candidate who has met only two-dimensional coordinate geometry will find the arithmetic familiar, but the extra coordinate is what makes the parametrisation of the line the natural tool rather than a sketch.
