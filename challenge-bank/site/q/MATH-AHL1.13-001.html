1.13 Complex numbers in polar and Euler form

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.13-001

AHL 1.13 (modulus–argument/polar and Euler form; sums, products and quotients and their geometric interpretation)P1 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatHence

Question

Let $\alpha=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{5}}$, so that $\alpha$ is a non-real fifth root of unity.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that $1+\alpha+\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{3}+\alpha^{4}=0$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Show that $\alpha^{k}+\alpha^{-k}=2\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi k}{5}\right)$ for $k=1$ and $k=2$.

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Hence show that $\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{5}\right)+\cos\!\left(\dfrac{4\pi}{5}\right)=-\dfrac12$.

    (Hence)
  4. [5 marks](d)

    Hence show that $\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{5}\right)=\dfrac{\sqrt5-1}{4}$.

    (Hence)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) $\alpha^{5}=\left(e^{2\pi i/5}\right)^{5}=e^{2\pi i}=1$ and $\alpha\neq1$. (A1)

The terms form a geometric series with first term $1$ and ratio $\alpha$, so

$$1+\alpha+\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{3}+\alpha^{4}=\frac{\alpha^{5}-1}{\alpha-1}$$ (M1)

$$=\frac{1-1}{\alpha-1}=0\quad(\alpha\neq1).\ \ \textbf{(A1)}$$ *(AG)*

(b) By Euler's form, $\alpha^{k}=e^{2\pi ik/5}=\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi k}{5}\right)+i\sin\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi k}{5}\right)$ (M1)

and $\alpha^{-k}=e^{-2\pi ik/5}=\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi k}{5}\right)-i\sin\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi k}{5}\right)$, since cosine is even and sine is odd. (A1)

Adding, the imaginary parts cancel:

$$\alpha^{k}+\alpha^{-k}=2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{5}\right).\ \ \textbf{(A1)}$$ *(AG)*

(c) Since $\alpha^{5}=1$ we have $\alpha^{4}=\alpha^{-1}$ and $\alpha^{3}=\alpha^{-2}$. (M1)

Grouping the result of (a) as $1+(\alpha+\alpha^{4})+(\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{3})=0$ and applying (b) with $k=1$ and $k=2$:

$$1+2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right)+2\cos\!\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right)=0$$ (A1)

$$\Rightarrow\ \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right)+\cos\!\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right)=-\frac12.\ \ \textbf{(A1)}$$ *(AG)*

(d) Using the double-angle identity $\cos2\theta=2\cos^{2}\theta-1$ with $\theta=\dfrac{2\pi}{5}$:

$$\cos\!\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right)=2\cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right)-1.$$ (M1)

Let $c=\cos\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{5}\right)$. Substituting into (c):

$$c+2c^{2}-1=-\frac12\ \Rightarrow\ 2c^{2}+c-\frac12=0\ \Rightarrow\ 4c^{2}+2c-1=0$$ (M1)(A1)

$$c=\frac{-2\pm\sqrt{4+16}}{8}=\frac{-2\pm2\sqrt5}{8}=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt5}{4}$$ (A1)

Since $0<\dfrac{2\pi}{5}<\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, we have $c>0$, so

$$\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right)=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{4}.\ \ \textbf{(A1)}$$ *(AG)*

Markscheme notes

(a) The geometric-series route is intended, but credit any correct argument — for example multiplying the sum by $(\alpha-1)$ and telescoping. Candidates who simply assert that 'the sum of the roots of $z^{5}-1$ is zero' should be credited only if they justify why the coefficient of $z^4$ in $z^5-1$ gives the sum.

(b) Award (M1) for converting to Euler or polar form. The even/odd symmetry step must be visible for the second mark.

(c) The key observation is $\alpha^{4}=\alpha^{-1}$ and $\alpha^{3}=\alpha^{-2}$. Candidates who write $\alpha^{4}=\bar{\alpha}$ and $\alpha^{3}=\overline{\alpha^{2}}$ and then use $z+\bar z=2\,\mathrm{Re}(z)$ are taking an equivalent route and must be credited.

(d) Allow the use of $\cos(4\pi/5)=-\cos(\pi/5)$ instead of the double-angle identity. The rejection of the negative root must be justified by the quadrant; a candidate who picks the positive root 'because cosines are positive' without noting that $72^{\circ}$ is acute forfeits the final (A1). Condone $\dfrac{\sqrt5-1}{4}$ left unsimplified as $\dfrac{-2+2\sqrt5}{8}$.

Extension (not required): it follows that $\cos(2\pi/5)\cos(4\pi/5)=-\tfrac14$ and $\cos(\pi/5)=\tfrac{\sqrt5+1}{4}$.

Why this question is hard

Nothing here is individually difficult; the question is hard because of the length of the chain and because each part depends on the one before. There is no calculator, so a candidate who cannot see where the chain is going has no way to check whether an intermediate result is right.

The two places where candidates come off the rails:

1. Part (c) is where the 'hence' is real. Recognising that $\alpha^{4}=\alpha^{-1}$ (and not, say, $\overline{\alpha}$ used vaguely) is what turns a statement about complex numbers into a statement about cosines. Without it, parts (a) and (b) sit unconnected.
2. Part (d) requires a substitution that is not signposted. Once $c$ is introduced, the equation in (c) becomes a quadratic, and the double-angle identity has to be applied *before* substituting, not after.

The elegant feature is that the answer is a surd involving $\sqrt5$ — the golden ratio — emerging from a purely algebraic root-of-unity argument. A candidate who sketches the five roots on a unit circle will see why $\sqrt5$ appears: the regular pentagon is built from it.

Classic wrong turns: assuming $\alpha^{4}=\alpha^{5}\alpha^{-1}$ is something other than $\alpha^{-1}$; using degrees inside an exponent written in terms of $\pi$; and choosing the negative root in (d) because $\cos(4\pi/5)$ is negative (it is the *other* cosine that is negative, not $c$).

A further wrong turn is to apply the double-angle identity to the wrong angle, expanding $\cos(4\pi/5)$ as $2\cos^{2}(4\pi/5)-1$; this is true but circular, leaving an equation in the quantity sought one step higher. A related slip is to drop the sign in $\cos(4\pi/5)=-\cos(\pi/5)$, which turns the quadratic into one with a spurious positive root.
