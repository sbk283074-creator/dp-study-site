4.9 The normal distribution and 4.12 Standardisation

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.9-101

Topic 4: Statistics and probabilitySL 4.9 (the normal distribution and curve) and SL 4.12 (standardisation of normal variables, z-values)P2 · Section B13 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateDetermineComment

Question

The masses of a large population of eggs are normally distributed with mean $62.0$ g and standard deviation $5.5$ g. The figure shows the standard normal curve with the region between $z = -1.2$ and $z = 1.6$ shaded. In parts (a) and (b) a single egg is chosen at random from this population.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the probability that the mass of the chosen egg lies between the two masses that correspond to $z = -1.2$ and $z = 1.6$.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the mass $m$ such that $12\%$ of the eggs in the population are heavier than $m$.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Eggs are packed in boxes of six, and the six eggs in a box may be treated as an independent random sample. Calculate the probability that the mean mass of the six eggs in a box is greater than $65.0$ g.

    (Calculate)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Comment on whether the normal model is more, or less, defensible for the mean mass of six eggs than it is for the mass of a single egg.

    (Comment)

Total: 13 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Standardising in reverse, the two masses are $62.0 - 1.2(5.5) = 55.4$ g and $62.0 + 1.6(5.5) = 70.8$ g. (M1)(A1) The required probability is the shaded area, $P(-1.2 < Z < 1.6) = 0.94520 - 0.11507 = 0.830$. (A1)

(b) If $12\%$ of the eggs are heavier than $m$ then $P(X < m) = 0.88$. (M1) The inverse normal gives $z = 1.17499$. (A1) Hence $m = 62.0 + 1.17499(5.5) = 68.5$ g to three significant figures. (A1)

(c) The mean of an independent sample of six has standard deviation $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{5.5}{\sqrt{6}} = 2.2454$ g. (M1) The standardised value is $z = \frac{65.0 - 62.0}{2.2454} = 1.3361$. (A1) From the normal tables $P(Z < 1.3361) = 0.9092$, so the required probability is $1 - 0.9092 = 0.0908$. (M1)(A1)

(d) The mean of six independent observations is closer to normally distributed than a single observation is, because of the central limit theorem, so the model is more defensible for the mean than for one egg. (A1) The gain is limited by the small sample size: with $n = 6$ the approximation is only reliable when the population is not strongly skewed. (M1) Egg masses are usually close to symmetric with a slight skew, so the normal model for the mean of six is reasonable, but the claim that the six masses are independent is the stronger assumption and the one more likely to fail in practice. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Award M1 for converting at least one z-value back to a mass, A1 for both masses $55.4$ and $70.8$ g, A1 for the probability $0.830$. Accept $0.8301$. A candidate who reads the shaded area straight off the figure without doing the conversion scores the final A1 only, because the question asks for the probability of a mass interval and the conversion is the method. (b) M1 for recognising that the upper tail is $0.12$ and therefore $P(X < m) = 0.88$; A1 for $z = 1.175$; A1 for $m = 68.5$ g. Accept $68.4$ to $68.5$. A candidate who uses $z = 1.175$ for a lower tail has the sign wrong and scores at most 1 of the 3. (c) M1 for the standard deviation of the mean, $\frac{5.5}{\sqrt{6}} = 2.245$; A1 for $z = 1.336$; M1 for the upper tail; A1 for $0.0908$. A candidate who uses $\sigma = 5.5$ instead of the standard error obtains $z = 0.545$ and $0.293$, which scores 0 of 4. (d) A1 for invoking the central limit theorem or for stating that averaging reduces the influence of the shape of the population; M1 for a limitation that is actually a limitation, such as the small sample size or the independence assumption; A1 for a conclusion that is consistent with the reasoning given. Do not award the final A1 for a bare assertion that one is better without a reason.

Why this question is hard

The figure fixes the item's centre of gravity. It shows the standard normal curve with the band between $z = -1.2$ and $z = 1.6$ shaded, and the shaded area is the number $0.830$. That number is a probability about $Z$, not about the mass of an egg, and part (a) is the step that converts between the two. The conversion is the standardisation run backwards: instead of taking a mass, subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation, the candidate takes a z-value, multiplies by the standard deviation and adds the mean. The two masses are $55.4$ g and $70.8$ g, and the probability of landing between them is the shaded area.

Part (b) reverses the direction again. Here the probability is given and the mass is wanted, so the candidate needs the inverse normal. The one thing that reliably goes wrong is the tail: the question says that $12\%$ are heavier than $m$, so the area to the left of $m$ is $0.88$, not $0.12$. Taking $z$ from a lower tail of $0.12$ gives $z = -1.175$ and a mass of $55.5$ g, which is a plausible-looking number answering a question nobody asked.

Part (c) keeps the population and changes the random variable. The mass of a single egg has standard deviation $5.5$ g, but the mean mass of six eggs has standard deviation $\frac{5.5}{\sqrt{6}} = 2.245$ g. Everything else is the same calculation. This is the part that separates a candidate who has understood the standard error from one who has memorised a procedure, because the two routes look identical until the division by $\sqrt{n}$ either happens or does not.

Part (d) is a judgement, not a calculation, and the honest answer has two halves. The central limit theorem makes the mean of six observations more normal than a single observation, which supports the model. But six is a small sample, so the improvement is modest, and the assumption that the six masses are independent is doing at least as much work as the normality does. A candidate who says only that the sample mean is normally distributed has not engaged with the question.
