5.11 The fundamental theorem applied across a point where the integrand does not exist

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.11-102

Topic 5: CalculusAHL 5.11 (definite integrals, including analytical approach; areas between curves)P3 · Section B13 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedExplainShow thatFind

Question

A student is asked to evaluate $\displaystyle \int_{-1}^{2} \frac{1}{x^{2}} \, dx$ and writes

$$\int_{-1}^{2} \frac{1}{x^{2}} \, dx = \left[ -\frac{1}{x} \right]_{-1}^{2}
= -\frac{1}{2} - \left(-\frac{1}{-1}\right) = -\frac{1}{2} - 1 = -\frac{3}{2}.$$

The graph of $y = \frac{1}{x^{2}}$ on $[-1, 2]$ is shown.

(a) Explain, without doing any integration, why the student's answer cannot be correct. \tag{3}

(b) Show that $\displaystyle \int_{0}^{2} \frac{1}{x^{2}} \, dx$ does not converge. \tag{4}

(c) Explain what this implies about $\displaystyle \int_{-1}^{2} \frac{1}{x^{2}} \, dx$. \tag{3}

(d) Now let $a > 0$ and define $I(a) = \displaystyle \int_{-1}^{2} \frac{1}{x^{2} + a^{2}} \, dx$.
Find $I(a)$ in terms of $a$, and deduce that $I(a) \to \infty$ as $a \to 0^{+}$. \tag{3}

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Explain, without integrating, why the student's answer cannot be correct.

    (Explain)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that $\int_{0}^{2} \frac{1}{x^{2}} \, dx$ does not converge.

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain what this implies about $\int_{-1}^{2} \frac{1}{x^{2}} \, dx$.

    (Explain)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Find $I(a)$ in terms of $a$ and deduce that $I(a) \to \infty$ as $a \to 0^{+}$.

    (Find)

Reveal the answer

(a) On its domain, $\frac{1}{x^{2}} > 0$ for every $x \neq 0$ (A1). Wherever a definite
integral of a non-negative function exists it must be non-negative, being a limit of sums of
non-negative terms (R1). The student's value $-\frac{3}{2}$ is negative, so it cannot be the
value of the integral (A1). The figure makes the point visually: the curve never goes below
the axis, so no sensible notion of "area under the curve" can give $-1.5$.

(b) Treat the integral as an improper integral and integrate up to the singularity (M1):

$$\int_{\varepsilon}^{2} \frac{1}{x^{2}} \, dx = \left[-\frac{1}{x}\right]_{\varepsilon}^{2}
= -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\varepsilon}, \qquad \varepsilon > 0 \tag{A1}$$

As $\varepsilon \to 0^{+}$, $\frac{1}{\varepsilon} \to \infty$ (A1), so the limit does not
exist as a finite number and

$$\int_{0}^{2} \frac{1}{x^{2}} \, dx = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^{+}} \int_{\varepsilon}^{2}
\frac{1}{x^{2}} \, dx$$

diverges (AG). The divergence is not caused by the interval being long but by the integrand
being unbounded near a single point.

(c) The interval $[-1, 2]$ contains $x = 0$, at which the integrand is undefined (A1). An
integral over $[-1, 2]$ can only be defined by splitting at that point, as
$\int_{-1}^{0} + \int_{0}^{2}$, and each piece must converge separately for the whole to
converge (R1). Since the second piece diverges by part (b),
$\int_{-1}^{2} \frac{1}{x^{2}} \, dx$ does not converge and has no value at all — not
$-\frac{3}{2}$, not any other number (A1). The student's calculation smuggled a
discontinuous antiderivative across a point where the function it differentiates to does not
exist.

(d) For $a > 0$ the integrand is continuous everywhere, so the fundamental theorem applies
directly (M1):

$$I(a) = \left[ \frac{1}{a}\arctan\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) \right]_{-1}^{2}
= \frac{1}{a}\left(\arctan\left(\frac{2}{a}\right) + \arctan\left(\frac{1}{a}\right)\right)
\tag{A1}$$

As $a \to 0^{+}$, both $\frac{2}{a}$ and $\frac{1}{a}$ tend to $+\infty$, so both arctangents
tend to $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and their sum tends to $\pi$. Hence
$I(a) \sim \frac{\pi}{a} \to \infty$ as $a \to 0^{+}$ (AG). The parameter $a$ is a
regularisation: it removes the singularity at the cost of making the integral finite, and the
value blows up at exactly the rate one would expect as the regularisation is removed.

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for observing that the integrand is positive wherever it is defined. (R1) for
appealing to the fact that a definite integral of a non-negative function cannot be negative.
(A1) for the conclusion. A candidate who says only "areas cannot be negative" earns (A1) and
(A1) but not the (R1) unless the link to the integrand's sign is made explicit.

(b) (M1) for introducing a limit at the singular endpoint. (A1) for the antiderivative
evaluated correctly between $\varepsilon$ and $2$. (A1) for observing that
$\frac{1}{\varepsilon}$ is unbounded. (AG) for the conclusion — no mark for restating it.
Forfeiture: a candidate who writes $\left[-\frac{1}{x}\right]_{0}^{2}$ and then says "$\frac{1}{0}$
is undefined" has not set up an improper integral and earns at most the (M1).

(c) (A1) for identifying $x = 0$ as an interior point of the interval where the integrand
fails to exist. (R1) for the splitting condition. (A1) for the conclusion that the integral
diverges rather than taking some other value. Condone the statement "the integral does not
exist" as equivalent to "does not converge".

(d) (M1) for using the standard antiderivative $\frac{1}{a}\arctan\frac{x}{a}$; allow the
substitution $x = a\tan\theta$ instead. (A1) for the correct expression in $a$. (AG) for the
limit. A candidate who correctly differentiates $\frac{1}{a}\arctan\frac{x}{a}$ to verify the
antiderivative earns the (M1). Note the sign: $\arctan\left(\frac{-1}{a}\right)$ is negative,
so the two terms add as a sum of magnitudes, which is why $I(a)$ grows like $\frac{\pi}{a}$
rather than cancelling.

Why this question is hard

What makes this a good trap is that it can be caught before any analysis is done. The
integrand is positive; the answer is negative. That is a contradiction, and it is available to
a candidate who has not yet thought about improper integrals at all. Part (a) exists to hand
the candidate that observation, and everything afterwards is an explanation of it.

The student's error is a specific and common one. The fundamental theorem of calculus requires
the integrand to be continuous on the closed interval of integration. Here the interval
$[-1, 2]$ contains $x = 0$, where $\frac{1}{x^{2}}$ is not defined, and no amount of correct
antidifferentiation on either side repairs that. The function $-\frac{1}{x}$ differentiates to
$\frac{1}{x^{2}}$ on $(-\infty, 0)$ and on $(0, \infty)$ separately, and it is continuous
across $x = 0$ in no sense at all — it jumps from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$. Substituting the
endpoints into it computes something, but not the area under the curve.

Part (d) is the part that makes the item more than a cautionary tale. Replacing $x^{2}$ by
$x^{2} + a^{2}$ with $a > 0$ gives an integrand that is continuous on the whole real line, so
the fundamental theorem genuinely applies and $I(a)$ is finite for every $a$. Letting
$a \to 0^{+}$ recovers the original integrand pointwise but not in integral: the limit of the
integrals is infinite, and the rate, $\frac{\pi}{a}$, is the same $\frac{1}{a}$ blow-up that
appeared in part (b) when the lower limit $\varepsilon$ was sent to zero. The two calculations
are the same phenomenon seen from two directions, and seeing that is the real payoff.

There is a useful general lesson in the shape of the answer. When a computation returns a
value with the wrong sign, the wrong order of magnitude, or the wrong units, the fastest
diagnostic is almost never to redo the algebra — it is to ask whether the theorem being used
actually applies. Here the sign check takes about five seconds and rules out an entire
calculation.
