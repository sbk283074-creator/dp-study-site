The probability that nobody gets their own coat back

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-010

Topic 1: Number and algebraAHL 1.10 (counting principles, including permutations and combinations); SL 1.8 (sum of infinite convergent geometric sequences — used here as the series for e)P316 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatShow thatDetermineShow that

Question

At the end of a party, $n$ guests collect their coats at random from a rack. A derangement of $n$ objects is a permutation in which no object is left in its original position. Let $D_{n}$ be the number of derangements of $n$ objects, and let $p_{n} = D_{n}/n!$ be the probability that no guest takes their own coat. This problem derives three descriptions of the same sequence and asks what they tell you about each other.

  1. [5 marks](a)

    Let $A_{i}$ be the set of permutations that fix object $i$. By applying the inclusion–exclusion principle to the union of the $A_{i}$, show that
    $$D_{n} = n!\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}.$$

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    By conditioning on what happens to object 1, show that for $n \ge 2$
    $$D_{n} = (n-1)\bigl(D_{n-1} + D_{n-2}\bigr),$$
    with $D_{0} = 1$ and $D_{1} = 0$. Hence verify that your formula from part (a) satisfies this recurrence for $n = 4$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} p_{n}$, giving your answer in terms of $e$. Determine also the smallest value of $n$ for which $p_{n}$ is within $0.001$ of this limit, justifying your answer with a bound on the error.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Show that for every $n \ge 1$, $D_{n}$ is the nearest integer to $\dfrac{n!}{e}$.

    (Show that)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) A permutation fails to be a derangement exactly when at least one object is fixed, so $D_{n} = n! - \bigl|\bigcup_{i} A_{i}\bigr|$. (M1) For any $k$ distinct indices, $\bigl|A_{i_{1}} \cap \cdots \cap A_{i_{k}}\bigr| = (n-k)!$, since those $k$ objects are fixed and the remaining $n-k$ may be arranged freely. (A1) There are $\dbinom{n}{k}$ ways to choose the $k$ indices, so by inclusion–exclusion (M1)
$$\Bigl|\bigcup_{i} A_{i}\Bigr| = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k+1}\binom{n}{k}(n-k)!$$
Therefore (M1)
$$D_{n} = n! - \sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}(n-k)! = n!\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}$$ (A1)(AG)

(b) Consider object 1. In a derangement it must land in some position $j \neq 1$, and there are $n-1$ choices of $j$. (M1) Now consider where object $j$ goes.

*Case 1.* Object $j$ lands in position 1. Then objects 1 and $j$ have swapped, and the remaining $n-2$ objects must be deranged among themselves: $D_{n-2}$ arrangements. (A1)

*Case 2.* Object $j$ does not land in position 1. Then, regarding position 1 as the 'forbidden' position for object $j$ and every other remaining object as forbidden from its own position, the remaining $n-1$ objects must be deranged: $D_{n-1}$ arrangements. (A1)

Hence $D_{n} = (n-1)(D_{n-1} + D_{n-2})$. For $n = 4$: the formula in (a) gives $D_{4} = 24\left(1 - 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{24}\right) = 24 \times \tfrac{9}{24} = 9$, and $D_{3} = 2$, $D_{2} = 1$, so $(4-1)(D_{3} + D_{2}) = 3(2 + 1) = 9$, which agrees. (A1)

(c) $p_{n} = \dfrac{D_{n}}{n!} = \displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}$, which is the $n$th partial sum of the Maclaurin series for $e^{x}$ at $x = -1$. (M1) Hence (A1)
$$\lim_{n\to\infty} p_{n} = e^{-1} = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.3679$$
The series is alternating with terms decreasing in magnitude, so the error after $n$ terms is at most the first neglected term: $|p_{n} - 1/e| \le \dfrac{1}{(n+1)!}$. (M1) We need $\dfrac{1}{(n+1)!} < 0.001$, i.e. $(n+1)! > 1000$. Since $6! = 720$ and $7! = 5040$, we need $n + 1 \ge 7$, so the smallest such $n$ is $n = 6$. (A1) Checking directly: $p_{5} = 1 - 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{24} - \tfrac{1}{120} = 0.36667$, which differs from $1/e$ by about $0.0012$, while $p_{6} = p_{5} + \tfrac{1}{720} = 0.36806$, which differs by about $0.00018$.

(d) From (a), $D_{n} = n!\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}$, while $\dfrac{n!}{e} = n!\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}$. (M1) Subtracting, (M1)
$$\left|\frac{n!}{e} - D_{n}\right| = n!\left|\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\right| \le \frac{n!}{(n+1)!} = \frac{1}{n+1}$$
using the alternating-series bound again. For $n \ge 1$ this is at most $\tfrac{1}{2}$, and in fact strictly less than $\tfrac{1}{2}$ for $n \ge 2$. (A1) A number within $\tfrac{1}{2}$ of $D_{n}$ has $D_{n}$ as its nearest integer, so $D_{n}$ is the nearest integer to $n!/e$. For $n = 1$, $1!/e \approx 0.368$ and $D_{1} = 0$, which is indeed the nearest integer.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for setting up the complement, (A1) for the count $(n-k)!$ of a $k$-fold intersection, and (M1) for the inclusion-exclusion sum with alternating signs. The final (AG) is generous with follow-through: a candidate whose signs are reversed throughout has made one error and should lose only the accuracy mark. (b) The two cases are the entire argument and each earns a mark; the most common failure is to treat case 2 as $D_{n-2}$, which double-counts the swap. Accept any explicit verification for $n = 4$, including a direct listing of the nine derangements. (c) The identification of the partial sum with the series for $e^{-1}$ is the key step and is worth a mark on its own; a candidate who computes several values and guesses the limit cannot reach the error-bound mark. Accept $n = 6$ with the alternating-series bound; do not accept a value obtained by comparing decimals without a bound. (d) Award (M1) for writing both expressions as sums and (M1) for the subtraction; the mark is for the bound $1/(n+1)$ and the conclusion that it is below a half. Candidates who note that the $n=1$ case is borderline should not be penalised for mentioning it.

Why this question is hard

This problem is a study in how much two descriptions of the same sequence can differ in what they let you see. The inclusion-exclusion formula in part (a) is exact, closed-form, and almost useless for computation: it is an alternating sum of nearly cancelling terms, so evaluating $D_{20}$ from it on a calculator loses most of the significant figures. The recurrence in part (b) is computationally perfect and conceptually opaque: it lets you generate $D_{n}$ for any $n$ without ever explaining why the answer behaves the way it does. Neither hints that $p_{n}$ settles down at all, let alone that it settles on $1/e$.

The moment of the problem is part (c), and it only works because of the closed form. Once $p_{n}$ is recognised as the $n$th partial sum of $\sum (-1)^{k}/k!$, the limit is $e^{-1}$ immediately and the error bound follows from the alternating series estimate, which is a theorem students meet early and rarely apply. The answer to 'how large must $n$ be' is $6$, which is startlingly small: with seven guests the probability that nobody gets their own coat is already within a tenth of a percentage point of its limiting value. That is a genuinely counter-intuitive fact, and it is worth saying out loud, because most people guess that convergence to $1/e$ would be slow.

Part (d) ties the two formulas together in a way neither suggests alone. Since $D_{n}$ is the $n$th partial sum multiplied by $n!$, and $n!/e$ is the full sum multiplied by $n!$, the difference is the tail, and the alternating bound puts that tail below $1/(n+1)$, hence below a half. So $D_{n}$ is the nearest integer to $n!/e$. This is a memorable and slightly absurd statement — a counting problem whose answer is obtained by dividing by $e$ and rounding — and it is the kind of result that makes the whole investigation worth doing. It is also the reason the problem asks for all three methods: the recurrence proves nothing about $e$, and the limit proves nothing about integers, but together they prove something neither could reach alone.
