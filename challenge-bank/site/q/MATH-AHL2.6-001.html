The parameter that turns the quadratic into a straight line

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL2.6-001

SL 2.6 (the quadratic function f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c: its graph, vertex, axis of symmetry and discriminant)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedShow thatFindDetermineExplain

Question

For each real number $m$, define $f(x)=(m-1)x^{2}+2(m+1)x+(m+2)$. The family of curves $y=f(x)$ changes character as $m$ varies, and several parts of this question ask you to describe that change.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that when $m=1$ the graph of $y=f(x)$ is a straight line, and state its equation.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    For $m\neq1$, find the coordinates of the vertex of $y=f(x)$, giving your answer in terms of $m$.

    (Find)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Determine the set of values of $m$ for which the graph of $y=f(x)$ does not meet the $x$-axis.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain why, for every $m>1$, the vertex lies below the $x$-axis, and deduce the number of real roots of $f(x)=0$.

    (Explain)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Setting $m=1$ makes the coefficient of $x^{2}$ equal to zero, so the quadratic degenerates to a linear expression (M1). Substituting gives $f(x)=2(1+1)x+(1+2)=4x+3$ (A1). Hence the graph is the straight line $y=4x+3$ (A1) *(AG)*. The coefficient of $x^{2}$ is the only term that can vanish, so this is the single value at which the family changes type.

(b) Completing the square for $m\neq1$, write $f(x)=(m-1)\left[x^{2}+\frac{2(m+1)}{m-1}x\right]+(m+2)$ (M1). The bracket becomes $\left(x+\frac{m+1}{m-1}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{m+1}{m-1}\right)^{2}$ (A1), so the axis of symmetry is $x=-\frac{m+1}{m-1}$ (A1). Substituting this value and simplifying gives $-\frac{m+3}{m-1}$ (M1)(A1). The vertex is therefore $\left(-\frac{m+1}{m-1},-\frac{m+3}{m-1}\right)$. This is the standard vertex formula for a parabola, and it is valid for every value of $m$ except the one excluded by the question.

(c) The discriminant is $[2(m+1)]^{2}-4(m-1)(m+2)$ (M1) $=4(m+1)^{2}-4(m-1)(m+2)$ (A1) $=4(m+3)$ (A1). The graph misses the $x$-axis exactly when the discriminant is negative, so $4(m+3)<0$ (M1), giving $m<-3$ (A1). Because the discriminant is linear in $m$, its sign changes at exactly one value, so the set of such $m$ is a single ray rather than a bounded interval.

(d) For $m>1$ the leading coefficient $m-1$ is positive, so the parabola opens upwards (A1). The $y$-coordinate of the vertex is $-\frac{m+3}{m-1}$, and both $m+3$ and $m-1$ are positive, so this value is negative (R1). An upward parabola whose vertex lies below the $x$-axis must cross the axis twice (M1), so $f(x)=0$ has two distinct real roots (A1), consistent with $4(m+3)>0$. The sign of the discriminant gives independent confirmation of this count, so the number of real roots is exactly two.

Markscheme notes

(a) The intended observation is that $m=1$ removes the $x^{2}$ term. Accept any statement that the graph has degree one. A candidate who substitutes $m=1$, writes $f(x)=4x+3$ and states the equation of the line earns all three marks; the word 'straight' is not required if the equation is given.

(b) The (M1) is for an honest attempt at completing the square, or for using $x=-\frac{b}{2a}$. The (A1) for the axis is independent, so a candidate with a correct $x$-coordinate but an arithmetic slip in the $y$-coordinate keeps the axis mark. Accept the vertex written in any equivalent algebraic form.

(c) Award (M1) for a correct discriminant expression even if the simplification is wrong. The final (A1) requires the strict inequality $m<-3$; writing $m\le-3$ forfeits it, because at $m=-3$ the curve is tangent to the axis and does meet it.

(d) This part tests reasoning rather than computation. The (R1) requires both signs to be justified. A candidate who says only that the vertex is below the axis, without saying that the parabola opens upwards, cannot reach the conclusion. Follow through from an incorrect vertex in (b) is allowed here.

Extension: the case $m<-3$ gives no real roots, while $-3<m<1$ gives two roots, and $m=1$ gives one.

Why this question is hard

The trap is that the leading coefficient depends on the parameter. Most candidates treat $f$ as a quadratic throughout, and part (a) is designed to catch exactly that: when $m=1$ there is no $x^{2}$ term at all, and the curve is a line. A candidate who divides by $m-1$ without first checking that $m\neq1$ will produce an undefined vertex.

The second trap is the discriminant. Expanding $[2(m+1)]^{2}-4(m-1)(m+2)$ is routine, but the cancellation is delicate: the $m^{2}$ terms disappear and leave the linear expression $4(m+3)$. Candidates who lose a factor of four, or who expand $(m-1)(m+2)$ carelessly, will get the wrong threshold. The correct reading is that the sign of the discriminant is controlled entirely by $m+3$, so the boundary is at $m=-3$, not at $m=1$ or $m=0$.

Part (d) is a reasoning question that rewards understanding the shape of a parabola. The vertex formula from part (b) is reused, but the point is not to compute anything new: it is to notice that an upward-opening parabola whose lowest point is below the axis has to cross the axis on both sides. The discriminant from part (c) confirms this, since $m>1$ makes $4(m+3)$ positive.

Classic wrong turns include assuming the family is always a parabola; writing the vertex with the wrong signs; treating 'does not meet the $x$-axis' as 'discriminant less than or equal to zero'; and in part (d) concluding that there are no real roots because the vertex is negative. A candidate who sketches the family for two or three values of $m$ will see the transition from a line at $m=1$ to very narrow parabolas for values of $m$ close to one. The family is a useful illustration of how a single parameter can change the nature of a curve.
