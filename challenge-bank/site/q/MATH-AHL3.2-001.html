The triangle the data allow twice

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL3.2-001

SL 3.2 (use of the sine, cosine and tangent ratios to find the sides and angles of right-angled triangles) and SL 3.3 (applications of right and non-right angled trigonometry, including the sine rule and the ambiguous case)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatFindDetermine

Question

In triangle $ABC$, the side $AB$ has length $6$ cm, the side $BC$ has length $2\sqrt{3}$ cm, and the angle $BAC$ is $30^{\circ}$. The remaining angles and sides are not yet known.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that there are two possible values of angle $BCA$, and find both of them.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the two possible values of angle $ABC$, giving each of them in degrees.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the two possible lengths of $CA$, giving each answer in the form $k\sqrt{3}$.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the area of each triangle, and explain which one a student who assumes the triangle is right-angled would find.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) By the sine rule, $\frac{BC}{\sin BAC}=\frac{AB}{\sin BCA}$ (M1), so $\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\sin 30^{\circ}}=\frac{6}{\sin BCA}$ (A1). Since $\sin 30^{\circ}=\frac{1}{2}$, the left-hand side is $4\sqrt{3}$, giving $\sin BCA=\frac{6}{4\sqrt{3}}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ (A1). Hence angle $BCA=60^{\circ}$ or $120^{\circ}$ (A1)(AG). Both values are admissible, because with angle $BAC=30^{\circ}$ each one leaves a positive third angle. The equation $\sin BCA=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ is the crucial step, because the sine function takes the same value at two different angles in the range from $0^{\circ}$ to $180^{\circ}$.

(b) The angle sum gives $ABC=180^{\circ}-30^{\circ}-BCA$ (M1). For $BCA=60^{\circ}$ this is $90^{\circ}$ (A1), and for $BCA=120^{\circ}$ it is $30^{\circ}$ (A1). Both values are positive, so both triangles exist (R1). The two third angles come from the same angle-sum relation, and neither is negative, which is the test that decides whether a candidate angle is admissible.

(c) Again by the sine rule, $\frac{CA}{\sin ABC}=4\sqrt{3}$ (M1), so $CA=4\sqrt{3}\sin ABC$ (A1). When $ABC=90^{\circ}$, $CA=4\sqrt{3}$ cm (A1); when $ABC=30^{\circ}$, $CA=4\sqrt{3}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=2\sqrt{3}$ cm (A1). The length of $CA$ depends on the angle opposite it, so the two possible values of angle $ABC$ produce two different lengths rather than one.

(d) The area is $\frac{1}{2}(AB)(BC)\sin ABC$ (M1) $=\frac{1}{2}(6)(2\sqrt{3})\sin ABC=6\sqrt{3}\sin ABC$ (A1). For $ABC=90^{\circ}$ this is $6\sqrt{3}$ cm$^{2}$ (A1), and for $ABC=30^{\circ}$ it is $6\sqrt{3}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=3\sqrt{3}$ cm$^{2}$ (A1). A candidate who assumes the triangle is right-angled obtains only the first value and never sees the obtuse case at all (R1). The area formula uses the two given sides and the angle between them, so it changes with the angle even though the sides themselves are fixed.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for the sine rule with the given sides and angle; the (A1) for substituting $AB=6$, $BC=2\sqrt{3}$ and $\sin 30^{\circ}=\frac{1}{2}$. The third (A1) is for the exact value $\sin BCA=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, and the final (A1) requires both angles. A candidate who gives only $60^{\circ}$ forfeits the last mark but keeps the rest.

(b) Award (M1) for using the angle sum; the two (A1) marks are independent, one for each value of angle $ABC$. The (R1) requires the candidate to state that both third angles are positive, which is what makes both triangles admissible.

(c) The (M1) is for identifying the sine-rule constant $4\sqrt{3}$; the (A1) is for the general expression $CA=4\sqrt{3}\sin ABC$. The two (A1) marks are for the two lengths, which must be exact surds. Accept $2\sqrt{3}$ and $4\sqrt{3}$ in either order provided they are correctly matched to their angles.

(d) The (M1) is for the area formula using two sides and the included angle; the (A1) for the factorised expression $6\sqrt{3}\sin ABC$. Each area carries its own (A1). The final (R1) is a reasoning mark for explaining that the right-angled assumption discards the obtuse triangle, and it is not awarded for a bare statement of the larger area.

Why this question is hard

This item is built on the ambiguous case of the sine rule, and the ambiguity is genuine rather than decorative. The data are one side, a second side and a non-included angle, exactly the configuration in which two triangles can satisfy the same three measurements. The value $\sin BCA=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ has two solutions in the interval from $0^{\circ}$ to $180^{\circ}$, namely $60^{\circ}$ and $120^{\circ}$, and both must be tested against the angle sum rather than discarded automatically.

The common error is to assume that the larger angle is impossible. Candidates who have memorised that a triangle cannot contain two obtuse angles sometimes reject $120^{\circ}$ without checking the sum. Here $30^{\circ}+120^{\circ}=150^{\circ}$, so the third angle is $30^{\circ}$ and the triangle is perfectly valid. The other error is the reverse: to accept only the acute solution because it is the one the calculator returns, since the inverse sine reports $60^{\circ}$ and hides $120^{\circ}$.

Part (d) gives the ambiguity a consequence. The two triangles have areas $6\sqrt{3}$ and $3\sqrt{3}$, and a candidate who silently assumes a right angle obtains only the larger one. The lever is therefore not the sine rule itself, which is routine, but the requirement to keep both branches alive through four linked parts and then to explain what is lost by collapsing them. The side $BC=2\sqrt{3}$ was chosen so that the sine-rule constant is the clean value $4\sqrt{3}$ and both lengths and both areas come out as exact multiples of $\sqrt{3}$. A sketch of both triangles, one with a right angle at $B$ and one with an obtuse angle at $C$, makes the two cases visually distinct. The final part asks what is lost when the right angle is assumed silently.
