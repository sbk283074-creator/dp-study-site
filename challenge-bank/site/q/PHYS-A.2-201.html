A.2 A pulley with a non-negligible moment of inertia, and why the two tensions differ

Physics HL · PHYS-A.2-201

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.2 Forces and momentum (Newton's second law; rotational form of the second law; connected bodies)P212 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatDetermineExplainShow that

Question

A block of mass $m_{1} = 2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ hangs vertically from a light inextensible string. The string passes over a pulley and is attached to a second block of mass $m_{2} = 3.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ resting on a horizontal frictionless table. The pulley is a uniform solid disc of mass $M = 1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ and radius $r = 0.10\ \mathrm{m}$, free to rotate about a fixed horizontal axis through its centre. The string does not slip on the pulley. Take $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    By applying Newton's second law to each block and the rotational form of the second law to the pulley, show that the acceleration of the blocks is $a = 3.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ to two significant figures.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the tension in the string on the hanging-block side and the tension on the table side, each to three significant figures.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain, referring to a torque, why the two tensions are not equal even though the string is light and does not slip.

    (Explain)
  4. [2 marks](d)

    The hanging block falls through $1.0\ \mathrm{m}$. Show that the energy lost by the falling block equals the sum of the translational and rotational kinetic energies gained.

    (Show that)

Total: 12 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Let the tension on the hanging side be $T_{1}$ and on the table side $T_{2}$, and let the blocks accelerate at $a$.

For the hanging block, taking downwards as positive (M1):
$$m_{1}g - T_{1} = m_{1}a$$

For the table block, the only horizontal force is $T_{2}$ (M1):
$$T_{2} = m_{2}a$$

The pulley is a uniform solid disc, so $I = \tfrac{1}{2}Mr^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(1.0)(0.10)^{2} = 5.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{2}}$ (A1).

Because the string does not slip, $\alpha = a/r$, and the net torque on the pulley is $(T_{1} - T_{2})r$:
$$(T_{1} - T_{2})r = I\alpha = I\frac{a}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_{1} - T_{2} = \frac{I}{r^{2}}a$$

Substituting $I = \tfrac{1}{2}Mr^{2}$ gives $T_{1} - T_{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}Ma$. Adding this to the two block equations eliminates both tensions (M1):
$$m_{1}g = \left(m_{1} + m_{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}M\right)a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = \frac{m_{1}g}{m_{1} + m_{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}M}$$

Numerically $\tfrac{1}{2}M = 0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$, so
$$a = \frac{(2.0)(9.81)}{2.0 + 3.0 + 0.50} = \frac{19.62}{5.50} = 3.57\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \tag{AG}$$

(b) From the hanging block (M1)(A1):
$$T_{1} = m_{1}(g - a) = 2.0\,(9.81 - 3.567) = 12.5\ \mathrm{N}$$

From the table block (M1)(A1):
$$T_{2} = m_{2}a = 3.0 \times 3.567 = 10.7\ \mathrm{N}$$

The difference is $T_{1} - T_{2} = 1.78\ \mathrm{N}$.

(c) The pulley is not in translational equilibrium in the rotational sense: it must gain angular speed at rate $\alpha = a/r = 35.7\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}$, and a body with moment of inertia needs a net torque to do that. The two tensions act on opposite sides of the axis, so their torques oppose; only their difference is left over to accelerate the pulley. If the tensions were equal the net torque would be zero and the pulley could never start turning. Because the string does not slip, the pulley must turn as the blocks move, so a non-zero net torque is unavoidable and the tensions cannot be equal.

(d) Energy lost by the falling block:
$$m_{1}gh = 2.0 \times 9.81 \times 1.0 = 19.6\ \mathrm{J}$$

Speed after falling $1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ from rest: $v^{2} = 2ah = 2(3.567)(1.0) = 7.13\ \mathrm{m^{2}\,s^{-2}}$, so $v = 2.67\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

Translational kinetic energy:
$$\tfrac{1}{2}(m_{1} + m_{2})v^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(5.0)(7.13) = 17.8\ \mathrm{J}$$

Rotational kinetic energy, with $\omega = v/r = 26.7\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$:
$$\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(5.0 \times 10^{-3})(26.7)^{2} = 1.78\ \mathrm{J}$$

The sum is $17.8 + 1.78 = 19.6\ \mathrm{J}$, equal to the energy lost by the falling block (AG). The rotational term is exactly the $\tfrac{1}{2}M$ in the denominator of part (a): the pulley's share of the energy is the reason the acceleration is smaller than the ideal-pulley value.

Markscheme notes

Award M1 for each of the three force or torque equations in (a), A1 for the moment of inertia, M1 for eliminating the tensions, and AG for the final value; accept $3.57$ or $3.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

In (b) the two tensions must be different values. A candidate who gives $T_{1} = T_{2} = 11.8\ \mathrm{N}$ has assumed a massless pulley and scores 0 for the part, even if the arithmetic is perfect, because the physics is the thing being tested.

In (c) the mark is for a torque argument, not for a restatement of the numbers. "The pulley has mass" earns nothing on its own; "the net torque $(T_{1}-T_{2})r$ is needed to give the pulley angular acceleration" earns the mark. Accept a reference to rotational inertia or to $I\alpha$.

In (d) allow the energy audit to be done with $v$ found from $v^{2} = 2ah$ or from the kinetic energies alone. Follow-through applies from an incorrect $a$ in (a): if the three energies still sum to $m_{1}gh$, award the AG. Forfeits: quoting $I = Mr^{2}$ (that is a hoop), or omitting $\tfrac{1}{2}$ from the rotational kinetic energy.

Common condonations: using $g = 10\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ loses the accuracy marks in (a) and (b) but not the method marks; a candidate who carries full precision and rounds only at the end should be given the accuracy marks even if the intermediate values differ in the third figure.

Why this question is hard

The discriminating move is recognising that a pulley with mass breaks the assumption every introductory treatment makes: that the tension is uniform along a light string. Here the string is light and inextensible, so the temptation is to carry the ideal-string result over and set $T_{1} = T_{2}$. That is the single most common error, and it is worth about ten per cent of the acceleration, which is large enough to be visible but small enough to look plausible on a calculator.

The physical content is that the pulley is a body with moment of inertia that must acquire angular speed, and the only torques available are the two tension torques, which oppose. Their difference is what remains. The cleanest route is to write the three equations and add them, which makes the cancellation of the tensions obvious and yields the effective mass $\tfrac{1}{2}M$ in the denominator. That $\tfrac{1}{2}M$ has a direct energy meaning, which is exactly what part (d) is for: the pulley stores $1.78\ \mathrm{J}$ of rotational kinetic energy out of the $19.6\ \mathrm{J}$ released, and that share is why the blocks accelerate more slowly than they would with an ideal pulley.

Part (c) is where a student who has merely memorised a formula is separated from one who has understood it. There is no equation to write; the answer is a statement about the necessity of a net torque. Part (d) then closes the loop, because the energy that "disappeared" from the blocks reappears in the pulley's rotation. A candidate who finds the three energies do not balance has usually used $I = Mr^{2}$ instead of $\tfrac{1}{2}Mr^{2}$ and should be sent back to the definition of the moment of inertia for a disc.
