2.2 Intersections of the graphs of a decreasing function and its inverse: only one of the three points lies on y = x

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-2.2-401

Topic 2: FunctionsSL 2.2 Concept of a function, domain, range and graph; SL 2.3 The graph of a function and its equation y = f(x); inverse functions and the reflection of a graph in the line y = xP1 · Section A6 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedWrite downFindExplain

Question

A function $f$ is defined on the domain $0 \leq x \leq 6$. Its graph consists of the three straight line segments joining $(0,\,6)$ to $(2,\,3)$, $(2,\,3)$ to $(4,\,2)$ and $(4,\,2)$ to $(6,\,0)$, as shown in the figure.

Because $f$ is decreasing throughout its domain it has an inverse function $f^{-1}$, also defined on $0 \leq x \leq 6$. The graph of $y = f^{-1}(x)$ is the reflection of the graph of $y = f(x)$ in the line $y = x$, and it is drawn dashed in the figure.

(a) Write down the coordinates of two points at which the graphs of $y = f(x)$ and $y = f^{-1}(x)$ intersect, and explain why the two graphs must intersect there.

(b) Find the coordinates of the third point of intersection.

(c) The two graphs enclose two separate regions. Explain why those two regions have equal areas.

Reveal the answer

(a) The function is decreasing and maps $[0,\,6]$ onto $[0,\,6]$, so it takes its greatest value at the left end of the domain and its least at the right: $f(0) = 6$ and $f(6) = 0$ (M1). Hence $(0,\,6)$ and $(6,\,0)$ both lie on the graph of $y = f(x)$. The graph of $y = f^{-1}(x)$ is the reflection of that graph in $y = x$, and the reflection of $(0,\,6)$ is $(6,\,0)$; so $(6,\,0)$ lies on the graph of the inverse, and it also lies on the graph of $f$. Equivalently $f^{-1}(0) = 6$ and $f^{-1}(6) = 0$. Both points are therefore intersections, and neither lies on $y = x$ (A1).

(b) A fixed point of $f$ is a point of intersection on $y = x$, so solve $f(x) = x$ on each segment. On $0 \leq x \leq 2$, $f(x) = 6 - \tfrac{3}{2}x$, giving $6 - \tfrac{3}{2}x = x$, so $x = 2.4$, which is not in $[0,\,2]$. On $2 \leq x \leq 4$, $f(x) = 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}x$, giving $4 - \tfrac{1}{2}x = x$, so $x = \tfrac{8}{3}$ (M1), which does lie in $[2,\,4]$. On $4 \leq x \leq 6$, $f(x) = 6 - x$, giving $x = 3$, which is not in $[4,\,6]$. The only fixed point is therefore $\left(\tfrac{8}{3},\,\tfrac{8}{3}\right)$ (A1).

(c) Reflection in $y = x$ exchanges the two graphs: it maps the graph of $f$ onto the graph of $f^{-1}$ and the graph of $f^{-1}$ onto the graph of $f$ (M1). The boundary of the first region consists of the arc of $f$ from $(0,\,6)$ to $\left(\tfrac{8}{3},\,\tfrac{8}{3}\right)$ together with the arc of $f^{-1}$ running back; reflecting it gives the arc of $f^{-1}$ from $(6,\,0)$ to $\left(\tfrac{8}{3},\,\tfrac{8}{3}\right)$ together with the arc of $f$ running back, which is exactly the boundary of the second region. A reflection is an isometry and preserves area, so the two regions have equal areas (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for stating $f(0) = 6$ and $f(6) = 0$, which needs the observation that a decreasing bijection of an interval onto itself must exchange the endpoints; (A1) for explaining that the reflection of one of these points is the other, so each lies on both graphs. Accept $f^{-1}(0) = 6$ and $f^{-1}(6) = 0$ as the justification. Do not credit a candidate who merely reads two crossing points off the figure without justifying them: the point of the part is that they must be there.

(b) (M1) for setting $f(x) = x$ on a segment and obtaining a candidate value; (A1) for $\left(\tfrac{8}{3},\,\tfrac{8}{3}\right)$ together with a check that the value lies in the segment it came from. A candidate who obtains $x = 2.4$ or $x = 3$ and fails to reject it earns the method mark only, because the rejection is precisely what makes $8/3$ the answer rather than one of three.

(c) (M1) for identifying the two arcs that bound one region and tracking where the reflection sends them; (A1) for concluding that a reflection preserves area. A candidate who says simply that the figure is symmetric has not yet said which region maps to which, and should be pressed: the symmetry that matters is the symmetry of the pair of curves, not of either curve alone.

Why this question is hard

The item exists because the usual rule of thumb -- a curve meets its reflection on the mirror line -- is a theorem about increasing functions that gets repeated as if it were a fact about all of them. It fails here for a reason that can be stated in one sentence and that a candidate can find without any algebra: a decreasing function that maps $[0,\,6]$ onto $[0,\,6]$ has to send $0$ to $6$ and $6$ to $0$, and those two facts are two points of intersection in their own right. Nothing in the question says the function is decreasing merely to guarantee that an inverse exists; it is decreasing so that the endpoint argument works.

Part (b) then asks for the one point that the usual method does find, and part (c) closes the loop: with three intersections there are two enclosed regions, and a candidate who found only the fixed point has no way to account for the second. The equality of the two areas is not an extra calculation to be performed -- it is the observation that the whole configuration is invariant under the reflection, which sends one region to the other. The figure is load-bearing in a way that figures often are not: it shows the two enclosed regions, and a candidate who has found only one intersection point can see that something is missing before they know what it is.

Challenge lever
The line y = x is a decoy. Every textbook figure that shows a curve and its inverse together shows them crossing on that line, and the rule behind the picture -- a point that a curve shares with its own reflection must be a fixed point -- is true for an increasing function and false for a decreasing one. Because f here is a decreasing bijection of [0, 6] onto itself it must swap the two endpoints: f(0) = 6 and f(6) = 0. The points (0, 6) and (6, 0) therefore lie on the graph of f and are each other's reflections, so each lies on the graph of the inverse as well, and neither is on y = x. A candidate who solves f(x) = x finds the third point and nothing else, and then has to explain an enclosed region that the figure plainly shows twice over.
