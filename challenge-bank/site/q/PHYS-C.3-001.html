A fringe that the envelope removes

Physics HL · PHYS-C.3-001

C.3 Wave phenomena (AHL): single-slit diffraction, theta = lambda/b, single-slit pattern modulating the double-slit pattern, multiple-slit interferenceP2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedCalculateShow thatStateDetermine

Question

Monochromatic light of wavelength $\lambda=589\ \mathrm{nm}$ falls normally on a double slit. The centres of the two slits are separated by $d=0.400\ \mathrm{mm}$ and each slit has width $b=0.100\ \mathrm{mm}$. The interference pattern is observed on a screen at a distance $D=2.00\ \mathrm{m}$ from the slits. The angles involved are small enough that $\sin\theta\approx\tan\theta\approx\theta$ in radians, where convenient.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the separation of adjacent bright fringes in the interference pattern.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the distance from the centre of the pattern to the first minimum of the single-slit diffraction envelope.

    (Calculate)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Show that the fourth-order bright fringe is missing from the pattern, and state which other orders would be missing if the screen were large enough to show them.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine the number of bright fringes that are visible within the central maximum of the envelope.

    (Determine)
  5. [2 marks](e)

    The source is replaced by a white light source. Describe one change to the pattern, and explain it.

    (Describe)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) $s=\dfrac{\lambda D}{d}=\dfrac{589\times 10^{-9}\times 2.00}{0.400\times 10^{-3}}$ (M1) $=2.94\times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m}=2.94\ \mathrm{mm}$. (A1)

So adjacent bright fringes are $2.94\ \mathrm{mm}$ apart. (A1)

(b) The first minimum of the single-slit envelope is at the smallest angle satisfying $b\sin\theta=\lambda$: (M1)

$\sin\theta=\dfrac{\lambda}{b}=\dfrac{589\times 10^{-9}}{0.100\times 10^{-3}}=5.89\times 10^{-3}$ (A1)

The distance from the centre is $y=D\tan\theta\approx 2.00\times 5.89\times 10^{-3}=1.18\times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m}=11.8\ \mathrm{mm}$. (A1)

(c) A double-slit maximum of order $n$ occurs where $d\sin\theta=n\lambda$; the first single-slit minimum occurs where $b\sin\theta=\lambda$. (M1)

These coincide when $\dfrac{n\lambda}{d}=\dfrac{\lambda}{b}$, that is when $n=\dfrac{d}{b}$. (M1)

Here $\dfrac{d}{b}=\dfrac{0.400}{0.100}=4.0$ (A1), so the third-order condition places the minimum at $\theta$ with $\sin\theta=\dfrac{4\lambda}{d}=\dfrac{4\times 589\times 10^{-9}}{0.400\times 10^{-3}}=5.89\times 10^{-3}$, exactly the angle found in part (b). The fourth-order maximum therefore falls on a minimum of the envelope and is not visible. (A1)

More generally the missing orders are multiples of $d/b$, so orders $4,\ 8,\ 12,\ \ldots$ are missing. (A1) Order 8 would require $\sin\theta=\dfrac{8\lambda}{d}=1.18\times 10^{-2}$, which is still less than $1$, so order 8 is geometrically accessible and would also be missing. Orders beyond the first few visible ones fall outside the envelope's central region anyway.

(d) Within the central maximum of the envelope, $\left|\sin\theta\right|<\dfrac{\lambda}{b}$, so the allowed orders satisfy $\left|n\right|<\dfrac{d}{b}=4$. (M1)

So $n=-3,\,-2,\,-1,\,0,\,1,\,2,\,3$ are present (A1), giving seven bright fringes. (A1) Orders $n=\pm 4$ coincide exactly with the envelope minima from part (c) and are not visible.

(e) Each order becomes a continuous spectrum with the same angular spread, with red deviated most because $\theta$ increases with $\lambda$; the central maximum remains white since all wavelengths coincide there and it has no order number. (A1)(A1) Higher-order spectra overlap, so beyond the first order the colours are no longer separated cleanly.

Markscheme notes

Parts (a) and (b) are single-formula calculations; accept full marks for any self-consistent use of the small-angle approximation. In (a), the (A1) for the unit conversion is separate and should be awarded for 2.94 mm, 2.9 mm or 2.95 mm, but not for 2.94 without a unit. Part (c): the essential reasoning is the equality of the two conditions, not the arithmetic; a candidate who argues from the ratio $d/b$ alone earns (M1)(M1)(A1) and the final (A1) if the identification of order 4 is complete. Stating that 'all multiples of 4 are missing' without recognising that order 8 is accessible loses the last (A1). Part (d): the mark scheme does not require the exclusion of $n=\pm 4$ to be argued twice, provided the reasoning in (c) is present; a candidate who counts nine fringes has included the two missing orders and can earn at most the first (M1). Part (e): award one mark for a correct change and one for the reason; 'the fringes become coloured' with no reference to wavelength-dependent deviation earns only the first.

Why this question is hard

This question is about two patterns with different length scales occupying the same screen, and about the fact that one of them governs the other. The double-slit fringes are set by $d$; the envelope is set by $b$; and because the two slits have a finite width, the brightness of the fringes is modulated by the diffraction of each individual slit. The envelope is not a curiosity — it determines which fringes exist.

The key step in part (c) is setting the two conditions equal. Because the fringe order is an integer and the ratio $d/b$ is exactly 4, the fourth-order maximum lands precisely on the first envelope minimum. The general result is worth remembering in the form 'orders that are multiples of $d/b$ are missing', and the follow-up question — whether those orders are actually reachable — is what distinguishes a complete answer. With $d/b=4$, order 8 requires $\sin\theta\approx 0.0118$, which is small, so it is reachable and also missing; a student who stops at 'four and eight are missing' without checking is right by luck rather than by reasoning, and the markscheme is written to notice the difference.

Part (d) converts a counting problem into an inequality. The central maximum of the envelope spans $\left|\sin\theta\right|<\lambda/b$, and within it the double-slit maxima satisfy $\left|n\right|<d/b=4$. The integers strictly between $-4$ and $4$ are seven, not nine, because the boundary values are exactly the missing orders. Students who count the boundaries produce nine and have not connected the two parts.

The final part moves from monochromatic light to white light, where each order becomes a spectrum. The central maximum stays white because the path difference is zero for every wavelength simultaneously; every other order is dispersed, with red deviated most. This is the observation that makes a diffraction grating a spectrometer rather than a decoration, and it is worth being able to state in one sentence.

Adaptation
Missing orders in a double-slit pattern are a standard AHL idea, but questions usually ask for the ratio d/b given a missing order, or for the envelope diagram. Here the ratio is fixed at exactly 4 by the chosen geometry, and the question then requires the student to count the surviving fringes inside the envelope and to decide whether the next missing order is geometrically accessible. The values, the counting part and the white-light extension are original; all angles were recomputed numerically.
Verification
Fringe separation lambda*D/d = 589e-9*2.00/4.00e-4 = 2.945e-3 m. First envelope minimum at sin(theta) = lambda/b = 5.89e-3, giving y = 1.178e-2 m. The ratio d/b = 4.0 exactly, so the fourth-order maximum coincides with the first envelope minimum; the fourth order sits at 4 x 2.945 mm = 11.78 mm, matching the envelope minimum. Orders within the central envelope: |n| < 4.
