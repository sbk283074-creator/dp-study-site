Deriving the arctangent derivative by implicit differentiation, then using it

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL5.15-001

Topic 5: CalculusAHL 5.15 (derivatives of arctan x and other inverse trigonometric and exponential functions; indefinite integration)P316 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatFindEvaluate

Question

Let $y=\arctan x$.

  1. [5 marks](a)

    By writing $x=\tan y$ and differentiating implicitly with respect to $x$, show that $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Hence find the indefinite integral of $\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}$ with respect to $x$.

    (Find)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    Evaluate the definite integrals $\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\,dx$ and $\int_{1}^{\sqrt{3}}\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\,dx$, giving exact answers.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

Write $x=\tan y$, so $\frac{dx}{dy}=\sec^{2}y$ (M1). By the chain rule $1=\sec^{2}y\,\frac{dy}{dx}$, giving $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{\sec^{2}y}$ (A1). Using the identity $\sec^{2}y=1+\tan^{2}y=1+x^{2}$ we obtain $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}$ (A1)(AG). This is the derivative of $\arctan x$, derived rather than recalled.

Because integration reverses differentiation, $\int\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\,dx=\arctan x+C$ (M1)(A1)(AG), where the constant of integration is essential and must be stated.

Thus $\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\,dx=\arctan 1-\arctan 0=\frac{\pi}{4}-0=\frac{\pi}{4}$ (M1)(A1), and $\int_{1}^{\sqrt{3}}\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\,dx=\arctan(\sqrt{3})-\arctan 1=\frac{\pi}{3}-\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{\pi}{12}$ (A1)(AG). Exact multiples of $\pi$ are required; decimal approximations alone are not sufficient for full credit, though a numerical check such as $0.7854$ and $0.2618$ may be offered as confirmation. The indefinite integral also follows by the substitution $x=\tan u$, $dx=\sec^{2}u\,du$, which reduces the integrand to $1$ and gives $\arctan x+C$, confirming the result by a second method. For the definite integrals the antiderivative is continuous on the closed interval, so the fundamental theorem applies directly with no improper-integral care required. The continuity of the antiderivative on the whole interval guarantees that the endpoint evaluation is valid and that no singularity occurs within the range of integration, so both definite integrals are proper. The method also shows why the antiderivative is unique up to the additive constant, since any two antiderivatives of the same function differ by a constant.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for implicit differentiation of $x=\tan y$ with respect to $x$, (A1) for $1=\sec^{2}y\,dy/dx$, (A1) for replacing $\sec^{2}y$ by $1+\tan^{2}y$, and (A1)(AG) for $1/(1+x^{2})$. (b) Award (M1) for reversing the derivative to obtain the antiderivative and (A1)(AG) for $\arctan x+C$; deduct for a missing $+C$. (c) Award (M1) for applying the limits to the antiderivative, (A1) for $\pi/4$, (A1) for $\pi/3-\pi/4$, and (A1)(AG) for $\pi/12$. Exact multiples of $\pi$ are required; award partial credit if a correct numerical method is shown but the exact form is missing, and deduct (A1) for each integral given only as a decimal. The substitution method in part (b) is an acceptable alternative route to the same antiderivative. The exact forms pi/4 and pi/12 must be shown; a candidate who gives only decimal approximations loses the relevant (A1) even when the numerical value is correct, and the link back to the part (a) derivative should be acknowledged when awarding the indefinite integral.

Why this question is hard

The derivative of $\arctan x$ is not handed to the candidate on the SL formula sheet, so it must be reconstructed. The trick is to invert the definition: set $x=\tan y$ and differentiate implicitly with respect to $x$, which turns a transcendental derivative into the algebraic identity $\sec^{2}y=1+\tan^{2}y$. Once the derivative is known, the indefinite integral in part (b) is immediate, and the two definite integrals in part (c) are just the standard arctangent values at $1$ and $\sqrt{3}$. The classic errors are to 'remember' the derivative as $1/\sqrt{1+x^{2}}$ (confusing it with the derivative of arcsine) or to drop the $+C$ in part (b), and to give decimal approximations instead of the exact multiples $\pi/4$ and $\pi/12$ in part (c). The derivation is the lever: the item is unsolvable by recall alone but becomes routine once the implicit step is seen, and it reinforces the inverse relationship between differentiation and integration that underpins the whole of Topic 5. A useful consolidation is that every inverse-trigonometric derivative can be obtained this way, so the method generalises far beyond arctangent. Practising the derivation builds the habit of reconstructing formulas under exam pressure, when the formula booklet may not list the exact case required. Reassuringly, both the implicit-differentiation route and the tangent-substitution route arrive at the same antiderivative, which is a useful cross-check, and this convergence deepens the student's confidence in the result.
