The differences are the geometric part

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.2-001

SL 1.2 (arithmetic sequences and series) and SL 1.3 (geometric sequences and series)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedCalculateShow thatHenceFind

Question

A sequence $(u_n)$ is defined by $u_1 = 5$ and $u_{n+1} = u_n + 3 \times 2^{n-1}$ for every integer $n \ge 1$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate $u_2$, $u_3$ and $u_4$, and explain why the sequence $(u_n)$ is neither arithmetic nor geometric.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that the general term is $u_n = 3 \times 2^{n-1} + 2$ for every integer $n \ge 1$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Hence find the smallest value of $n$ for which $u_n > 10^{6}$.

    (Hence)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Find the sum of the first $20$ terms, giving your answer in the form $3 \times 2^{k} + c$.

    (Find)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Applying the recurrence, $u_2 = 5 + 3 \times 2^{0} = 8$ (M1), $u_3 = 8 + 3 \times 2^{1} = 14$ (A1) and $u_4 = 14 + 3 \times 2^{2} = 26$ (A1). The successive differences are $3$, $6$ and $12$, which are not constant, so the sequence is not arithmetic; the successive ratios $8/5$, $14/8$ and $26/14$ are not constant either, so it is not geometric. (R1)

(b) The successive differences are $u_{k+1} - u_k = 3 \times 2^{k-1}$ for $k \ge 1$, so they form the geometric sequence $3$, $6$, $12$ and so on. (M1) Summing the first $n-1$ of them, $u_n = u_1 + \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} 3 \times 2^{k-1} = 5 + 3(2^{n-1} - 1)$. (M1) Expanding, $u_n = 5 + 3 \times 2^{n-1} - 3 = 3 \times 2^{n-1} + 2$. (A1)(AG)

(c) Setting $3 \times 2^{n-1} + 2 > 10^{6}$ gives $2^{n-1} > 333332.66$ and so on. (M1) Since $2^{18} = 262144$ and $2^{19} = 524288$, the smallest integer with $2^{n-1}$ above that threshold has $n - 1 = 19$. (M1) Hence $n = 20$. (A1) Checking, $u_{19} = 3 \times 2^{18} + 2 = 786434$, which is below $10^{6}$, while $u_{20} = 3 \times 2^{19} + 2 = 1572866$, which is above it. (R1)

(d) The sum of the first $20$ terms is $\sum_{n=1}^{20} (3 \times 2^{n-1} + 2)$. (M1) The geometric part sums to $3(2^{20} - 1) = 3 \times 1048575 = 3145725$, and the constant part contributes $2 \times 20 = 40$. (M1) Adding, the total is $3145725 + 40 = 3145765 = 3 \times 2^{20} + 37$. (A1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a): the (M1) is for substituting into the recurrence at least twice, and the two (A1) marks are for the correct values $u_3 = 14$ and $u_4 = 26$. The (R1) requires both negative conclusions, so a candidate who shows only that the differences are not constant earns nothing unless the ratios are also dismissed. Part (b): the first (M1) is for identifying the differences as $3 \times 2^{k-1}$ and the second for summing that geometric series; the (A1) is for the simplified form and the (AG) confirms the printed result, so an answer that stops at $5 + 3(2^{n-1} - 1)$ must still be simplified. Part (c): the (M1) is for the inequality $2^{n-1} > 333332.66$ and the second (M1) for locating $2^{18}$ and $2^{19}$; the (A1) is for $n = 20$. The (R1) is for the numerical check of both sides, which is what rules out $n = 19$. Part (d): the two (M1) marks are for splitting the sum into its geometric and constant parts, and the (A1) for the exact total. Accept $3 \times 2^{20} + 37$, $3145765$ or $3(2^{20} - 1) + 40$ as equivalent. Follow-through from an incorrect $u_n$ is allowed in parts (c) and (d) if the candidate uses it consistently.

Why this question is hard

The sequence is built so that it looks like a standard geometric or arithmetic question and is in fact neither. The recurrence adds a term that doubles each time, so the sequence itself grows too quickly to be arithmetic and too irregularly to be geometric. Part (a) asks the candidate to say this with evidence: the differences $3$, $6$, $12$ are themselves a geometric sequence, while the ratios $1.6$, $1.75$, $1.857$ are plainly not constant.

The structural idea is that the differences are geometric even though the sequence is not. Part (b) exploits this: summing the first $n-1$ differences, which form $3$, $6$, $12$ and so on, gives $3(2^{n-1} - 1)$, and adding the initial value $5$ produces $u_n = 3 \times 2^{n-1} + 2$. The closed form is therefore a geometric term plus a constant, and this drives every later part.

Part (c) is a threshold question. Because the constant $2$ is negligible beside $10^{6}$, the candidate solves $2^{n-1} > 333332.66$, finds $2^{18} = 262144$ and $2^{19} = 524288$, and concludes $n = 20$. The check matters: $u_{19} = 786434$ lies below the threshold and $u_{20} = 1572866$ lies above it, so the answer is $n = 20$.

Part (d) uses the closed form to sum a series that is neither arithmetic nor geometric. Splitting the sum into the geometric part $3(2^{20} - 1)$ and the constant part $40$ is the clean route, and the answer $3 \times 2^{20} + 37$ is exact. The classic errors are assuming the sequence is geometric with ratio $2$, forgetting the constant $2$ when summing, and answering $n = 19$ by misreading the index shift. Each traces back to treating the sequence itself as geometric instead of its differences.
