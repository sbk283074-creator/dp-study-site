A funding plan that balances on assumptions the bank has already refused

Business management SL · BM-3.1-001

Unit 3: 3.1 Introduction to finance (the role of the finance function; why businesses need finance; capital and revenue expenditure). Toolkit: business plan.P2 · Section A14 marksdifficulty 4DistinguishCalculateEvaluate

Wagtail & Co

Wagtail & Co is a dog daycare and grooming business that Nia and Rob, two former veterinary nurses, plan to open in a converted unit on an industrial estate. Neither has run a business before, and they are preparing the financial section of a business plan in order to raise the money they need.

The unit needs converting before it can open. The conversion, including kennels, flooring, fencing and a secure outdoor yard, will cost EUR 52 000. Grooming equipment - tables, dryers, clippers and a hydrobath - will cost EUR 16 000. A van for collecting and returning dogs will cost EUR 28 000. Booking and payment software will cost EUR 6 000, and signage and the fit-out of the reception area will cost EUR 8 000. Nia and Rob have written quotations for all five items and believe the figures to be accurate.

The business will also have to meet running costs every month. Rent is EUR 3 000, wages for two full-time and two part-time staff are EUR 7 800, utilities are EUR 1 200, insurance is EUR 400, dog food and consumables are EUR 4 600, and marketing is EUR 500. These costs will start as soon as the lease is signed.

Nia and Rob expect the unit to take two months to convert and the staff to need two months of training, so the business will receive no income at all until the start of the third month. Their business plan therefore sets aside three months of running costs as working capital, and adds a contingency of 10% of the total of the capital items and the working capital.

The plan assumes the money will come from three places. Nia and Rob will put in EUR 45 000 of their own savings. They have asked the bank for a loan of EUR 115 000. They expect to receive a government start-up grant of EUR 20 000, which they have seen advertised. On that basis the plan shows total funding of EUR 180 000.

Two things have since changed. The bank has replied that it lends to start-ups only against assets, and will advance at most 60% of the value of the capital items in the plan. The grant body has confirmed in writing that the maximum award under the scheme is EUR 12 000. Rob's aunt has offered to provide the difference, but she wants 25% of the business in return, and both founders have said they do not want to give up any ownership.

A supplier has also offered to lease the grooming equipment and the van, which together cost EUR 44 000, for EUR 980 a month, instead of selling them outright.

Question

Nia and Rob must decide whether the funding plan in their business plan is realistic enough to open Wagtail & Co.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Distinguish between capital expenditure and revenue expenditure, using examples from the case, and explain why the distinction matters when a start-up is deciding how much finance it needs.

    (Distinguish)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Calculate the total finance required at start-up, showing the capital expenditure, the working capital requirement and the contingency separately.

    (Calculate)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Using the financial section of the business plan, evaluate whether the funding plan is realistic, referring to the bank's lending limit, the grant, the lease offer and Rob's aunt's proposal.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Capital expenditure is spending on non-current assets that will be used in the business for more than a year - it buys something the business keeps. Revenue expenditure is spending on the day-to-day running of the business, which is consumed within the year. (A1)(A1)

In this case the five items in the table are all capital expenditure: the conversion of the unit, the grooming equipment, the van, the software and the signage are assets the business will use for several years. The rent, wages, utilities, insurance, dog food and marketing are revenue expenditure, because they are incurred month by month and create no lasting asset. (A1)

The distinction matters because the two types of spending have to be financed differently. Capital expenditure is a large, one-off sum that a bank will often lend against, because the asset can be secured. Revenue expenditure recurs every month, is not secured by an asset, and must be financed from cash or from working capital - so it is easy to underestimate and hard to borrow against. (A1)

(b) Capital expenditure $= EUR 52 000 + EUR 16 000 + EUR 28 000 + EUR 6 000 + EUR 8 000 = EUR 110 000$. (M1)(A1)

Monthly running costs $= EUR 3 000 + EUR 7 800 + EUR 1 200 + EUR 400 + EUR 4 600 + EUR 500 = EUR 17 500$. Three months of running costs must be financed before the business earns anything, so working capital $= 3 \times EUR 17 500 = EUR 52 500$. (M1)(A1)

Contingency $= 10\% \times (EUR 110 000 + EUR 52 500) = 10\% \times EUR 162 500 = EUR 16 250$. Total finance required $= EUR 110 000 + EUR 52 500 + EUR 16 250 = EUR 178 750$. (A1)

(c) Indicative evaluation, using the financial section of the business plan.

The plan looks balanced on its own assumptions: it claims EUR 180 000 of funding against a requirement of EUR 178 750, a margin of EUR 1 250. But two of the three funding assumptions are wrong.

The bank will lend at most 60% of the value of the capital items, which is $60\% \times EUR 110 000 = EUR 66 000$, not the EUR 115 000 requested. The grant is capped at EUR 12 000, not EUR 20 000. The realistic funding is therefore $EUR 45 000 + EUR 66 000 + EUR 12 000 = EUR 123 000$, against a requirement of EUR 178 750. The shortfall is EUR 55 750, and the plan's apparent EUR 1 250 surplus becomes a EUR 55 750 hole. (M1)(A1)

The shortfall is almost entirely a working-capital problem. The business needs EUR 52 500 of cash to survive three months with no income, and the bank will not lend against it. The founders have focused on the visible assets and underestimated the cost of simply keeping the doors open.

The lease offer helps the capital side but not enough. Leasing the grooming equipment and the van (EUR 44 000) would cut capital expenditure to EUR 66 000, but it adds EUR 980 a month to running costs, raising monthly costs to EUR 18 480 and working capital to EUR 55 440. With a 10% contingency on EUR 121 440, the total requirement becomes EUR 133 584. The bank's limit then falls to EUR 39 600, so available funding is EUR 96 600 and the shortfall is EUR 36 984. Leasing reduces the gap by about EUR 19 000 but increases the monthly cost permanently.

Rob's aunt's EUR 56 000 would close the gap, but it costs 25% of the business, which both founders have said they do not want to give up. It also transfers a quarter of any future growth to someone who is contributing money rather than work.

A supported conclusion is required. A candidate who recommends delaying the opening, phasing the spend, reducing the contingency, or accepting the aunt's money, and who justifies it with the figures, should be credited. The key judgement is that the plan is not realistic as written: it understates the finance needed by about EUR 55 750 and misunderstands how a bank lends to a start-up.

Markscheme notes

(a) Two marks for the distinction, one for applying it correctly to the case, one for explaining why it affects the finance decision. A candidate who says only 'capital is long term, revenue is short term' earns one mark. Do not credit an answer that classifies the monthly rent as capital because it is paid under a lease.

(b) Award (M1)(A1) for the capital total, (M1)(A1) for the monthly running cost and the three-month working capital, and (A1) for the contingency and the total. Follow through on arithmetic errors. Do not penalise a candidate who presents the contingency as 10% of capital plus working capital in a different order. Do not accept a total that omits the working capital: that omission is the whole point of the question.

(c) The two discriminating marks are for the bank's 60% limit and the grant cap. A candidate who accepts the plan's EUR 180 000 at face value and concludes that it is adequate cannot earn more than one mark, however well written. The lease marks require the candidate to see that leasing reduces the upfront requirement but raises the recurring one - a candidate who says only 'leasing is cheaper' forfeits them. Credit any supported conclusion, including one that keeps the plan but changes its funding. Do not credit answers that drift into evaluating the business idea itself rather than the finance plan.

Why this question is hard

The question is designed so that the plan appears to work. EUR 180 000 of funding against a requirement of EUR 178 750 is a surplus of EUR 1 250, and a candidate who checks only the addition will conclude that the plan is sound. The trap is that the funding side contains assumptions the case has already contradicted: the bank's lending rule and the grant cap are stated plainly, and both reduce the money actually available.

The deeper point, and the reason the item sits in Unit 3.1 rather than in sources of finance, is the difference between capital and revenue expenditure and what it means for how much finance a start-up actually needs. The visible, exciting items - the conversion, the van, the equipment - are the ones founders plan for. The invisible item is working capital: EUR 52 500 of rent, wages, insurance and consumables that must be paid for three months before the business earns a penny. Banks will lend against a van; they will not lend against next quarter's wages. So the shortfall is concentrated precisely where borrowing is hardest.

The lease option is a genuine second lever. Converting EUR 44 000 of capital expenditure into a monthly payment reduces the upfront requirement from EUR 178 750 to EUR 133 584 and cuts the shortfall from EUR 55 750 to EUR 36 984 - but it raises the monthly running cost and therefore the working capital requirement too. Students who treat leasing as a pure saving have missed the interaction between the two.

Classic wrong turns: adding only the capital items and reporting EUR 110 000 as the finance required; accepting the EUR 115 000 loan; forgetting the contingency; and concluding that the aunt's offer solves everything without noticing that it costs a quarter of the business, which the founders have said they will not give up.
