A.1 A bouncing ball that comes to rest in finite time after infinitely many bounces

Physics HL · PHYS-A.1-401

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.1 Kinematics: velocity-time graphs, acceleration, displacement from the area under a graph, motion under gravity. With the mathematics of geometric seriesP26 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatDetermineExplain

Question

A ball is released from rest at a height of $1.25\ \mathrm{m}$ above a horizontal floor and falls freely. Take $g = 10\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

Immediately after each bounce the speed of the ball is $0.8$ times its speed immediately before that bounce, and it leaves the floor vertically. The figure shows the velocity--time graph for the motion.

(a) Show that the interval between the first and second bounces is $0.8\ \mathrm{s}$, and that the intervals between successive bounces form a geometric sequence with common ratio $0.8$.

(b) Determine the time at which the ball stops bouncing.

(c) State how many bounces the ball makes before it stops, and explain how that is consistent with your answer to part (b).

Reveal the answer

(a) The speed just before the first bounce follows from $v^{2} = 2gh$: $v = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 1.25} = 5.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (M1). Just after that bounce the speed is $0.8 \times 5.0 = 4.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ upwards, so the time to rise and fall back to the floor is

$$\frac{2u}{g} = \frac{2 \times 4.0}{10} = 0.8\ \mathrm{s} \quad \text{(A1)}.$$

Each bounce multiplies the speed by $0.8$ and the flight time is proportional to the speed, so each interval is $0.8$ times the previous one: the intervals are $0.8,\ 0.64,\ 0.512,\ \ldots$, a geometric sequence with common ratio $0.8$ (A1).

(b) The first fall takes $\tfrac{v}{g} = \tfrac{5.0}{10} = 0.5\ \mathrm{s}$ (M1). The total time is that first fall plus the sum to infinity of the bounce intervals:

$$T = 0.5 + \frac{0.8}{1 - 0.8} = 0.5 + 4.0 = 4.5\ \mathrm{s} \quad \text{(A1)}.$$

(c) Infinitely many (A1). The intervals form a convergent geometric series, and a convergent series of positive terms can have infinitely many of them: the partial sums approach $4.5\ \mathrm{s}$ from below and never exceed it. The model has no smallest bounce and no last bounce -- for every bounce there is a later one -- yet the total time is finite, because the time each bounce takes shrinks faster than the number of bounces grows (A1). The ball does not stop at the instant of a bounce: $4.5\ \mathrm{s}$ is the limit of the bounce times, and it is approached, not attained.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for the impact speed of 5.0 m/s from v-squared = 2gh or from an equivalent route; (A1) for the rebound speed 4.0 m/s and the flight time 0.8 s; (A1) for the statement that the flight time scales with the speed, so the ratio of successive intervals is also 0.8. A candidate who computes the second interval explicitly as 0.64 s and infers the ratio from that is creditable.

(b) (M1) for the 0.5 s first fall; (A1) for recognising the infinite sum and evaluating it as 4.0 s, giving 4.5 s in total. A candidate who adds a finite number of terms and rounds should not receive the second mark: the question asks when the ball stops, and the answer is the limit, not an approximation to it.

(c) (A1) for 'infinitely many'; (A1) for the explanation. The explanation must do more than repeat that the series converges -- it should say why that answers the apparent contradiction, namely that the number of terms and the size of their sum are different questions. Credit the observation that 4.5 s is a limit that is approached rather than an instant at which a bounce occurs.

Why this question is hard

This is Zeno's paradox wearing a kinematics costume, and it is worth asking because both halves of it are counter-intuitive to a candidate who has met geometric series only as a means of adding up a finite list. An infinite list of positive terms is expected to sum to infinity; it does not, and the reason is that the terms shrink in a fixed ratio.

The physics is doing real work here too. The rebound speed is a fixed fraction of the impact speed, the flight time is proportional to the speed, and so the times inherit the ratio 0.8 directly from the speeds. A candidate who reaches for the heights instead -- each rebound height is 0.64 of the previous one -- gets the same ratio after an extra step, since the time varies as the square root of the height. Either route lands on the same sum, and that is a useful thing to check: if the two routes disagreed, the model would be inconsistent.

What the item is really testing is whether a candidate can hold two facts at once: the motion has a definite end at 4.5 s, and it has no final bounce. Both are true, and the discomfort of holding them together is the difficulty.

Challenge lever
Two intuitions collide and both are wrong. The first is that infinitely many bounces must take infinitely long; the second, its mirror image, is that a ball which stops after 4.5 s must have made some finite number of bounces. The resolution is that the flight times form a geometric sequence with ratio 0.8, whose sum converges, and that a convergent sum of infinitely many positive terms is exactly what 'comes to rest' means here. Nothing in the arithmetic is harder than summing a geometric series; what is hard is accepting that the answer to 'how many bounces' is not a number.
