A quadratic in disguise whose two roots must both be positive

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL2.10-001

SL 2.10 (solving equations, both graphically and analytically, including equations of the form e^(2x) - 5e^x + 4 = 0) and SL 2.9 (exponential functions and their graphs)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatDetermineSolve

Question

Consider the function $f(x)=e^{2x}-5e^{x}+4$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that the substitution $u=e^{x}$ reduces the equation $f(x)=0$ to $u^{2}-5u+4=0$, and hence solve $f(x)=0$ exactly.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Determine the set of values of $k$ for which $e^{2x}-5e^{x}+k=0$ has no real solutions.

    (Determine)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Solve the inequality $f(x)>0$, giving your answer in exact form.

    (Solve)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the exact minimum value of $f(x)$ and the value of $x$ at which it occurs.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Since $e^{2x}=(e^{x})^{2}$, writing $u=e^{x}$ turns $f(x)=0$ into $u^{2}-5u+4=0$ (M1)(A1). Factorising, $(u-1)(u-4)=0$, so $u=1$ or $u=4$ (A1). Because $u=e^{x}$, this gives $e^{x}=1$ or $e^{x}=4$, that is $x=0$ or $x=\ln 4$ (A1). Both values of $u$ are positive, so both survive the substitution and give genuine real solutions for $x$.

(b) With $u=e^{x}>0$ the equation is $u^{2}-5u+k=0$ (M1). If the discriminant $25-4k$ is negative the quadratic has no real roots, so there is no positive $u$ and hence no real $x$ (A1)(M1). This requires $25-4k<0$, that is $k>\frac{25}{4}$ (A1). Conversely, for $k\le\frac{25}{4}$ the quadratic has at least one positive root, so real solutions exist; the answer is $k>\frac{25}{4}$. The converse direction is needed to show that no value of $k$ below the threshold can be added to the set.

(c) The inequality $f(x)>0$ becomes $u^{2}-5u+4>0$, that is $(u-1)(u-4)>0$ (M1)(A1), so $u<1$ or $u>4$ (A1). Translating back, $e^{x}<1$ or $e^{x}>4$, so $x<0$ or $x>\ln 4$ (A1). The two rays are disjoint, so the solution set is not an interval but the union of two separate pieces.

(d) Completing the square in $u$, $u^{2}-5u+4=\left(u-\frac{5}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{9}{4}$ (M1)(A1), so the least value is $-\frac{9}{4}$ (A1). This is attained when $u=\frac{5}{2}$, and since $\frac{5}{2}>0$ this corresponds to a real $x$ with $x=\ln\frac{5}{2}$ (A1). The positivity of the location is what guarantees that the stationary point of the quadratic is actually reached by the exponential curve, so the value is a true minimum rather than a lower bound.

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for recognising that $e^{2x}$ is the square of $e^{x}$; the (A1) is for the reduced quadratic. Any correct method of solving it is accepted, including the formula. The final (A1) requires both exact roots $x=0$ and $x=\ln4$; a decimal approximation is not sufficient.

(b) The (M1) is for the substitution and the (A1) for the discriminant. The second (M1) is for the reasoning that a negative discriminant means no real $u$, and the final (A1) for the strict inequality $k>\frac{25}{4}$. Candidates who write $k\ge\frac{25}{4}$ forfeit the final mark, because at that value there is a repeated positive root and therefore one real solution.

(c) Award (M1) for the factorisation and (A1) for the sign analysis $u<1$ or $u>4$. The two translation marks are for converting the inequalities back to inequalities in $x$. Accept $\ln4$ written as $2\ln2$.

(d) The (M1) is for completing the square or differentiating; the (A1) for the completed square. The minimum value must be exact, and the location must be justified by noting that $u=\frac{5}{2}$ is positive. A candidate who reports the minimum value but not its location earns three of the four marks.

Extension: the range of $f$ is $\left[-\frac{9}{4},\infty\right)$.

Why this question is hard

This is the classic quadratic in disguise, but the question pushes past the standard version by asking for a parameter range and an exact minimum. The disguise is the point: $e^{2x}-5e^{x}+4$ looks like an exponential expression, yet the substitution $u=e^{x}$ makes it a quadratic. The whole question turns on remembering that $u$ is constrained to be positive.

Part (a) is routine once the substitution is seen. Part (b) is the subtle one. A candidate who requires only that the discriminant be negative obtains $k>\frac{25}{4}$ and is right, but for the wrong reason: they have not checked that the absence of real roots of the quadratic is enough. It is enough, because a negative discriminant means no real $u$ at all, positive or otherwise. The converse direction is what needs care, and the answer justifies it through the product of the roots.

Part (c) is a sign question. The factorisation $(u-1)(u-4)>0$ gives a union of two rays, not an interval. A candidate who writes $1<u<4$ has the sign backwards. Translating back to $x$ uses the fact that $e^{x}$ is increasing, so the inequalities are preserved.

Part (d) is an optimisation in the $u$-variable. The vertex of the quadratic is at $u=\frac{5}{2}$, which is positive, so the minimum of the quadratic is actually attained by the exponential function. This check is essential: had the vertex been at a negative value of $u$, the minimum of $f$ would not occur. A candidate who sketches the graph will see that the curve dips below the axis between the two roots and returns above it afterwards, which is exactly the sign pattern recorded in part (c). The value is attained, not merely approached, because the required value of the substituted variable is positive.
