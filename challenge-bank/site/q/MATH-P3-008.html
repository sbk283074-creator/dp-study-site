P3 extended problem: constrained optimisation with a cost parameter

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-008

Topic 5: CalculusSL 5.8 (local maximum and minimum points), SL 5.4 (tangents and normals), AHL 5.14 (related rates)P315 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatDetermineHence

Question

A closed cylindrical can is to be manufactured with a fixed volume $V$. The curved surface is made from a material costing $p$ per unit area. The two circular ends are made from a thicker material costing $kp$ per unit area, where $k > 0$ is a constant. Let the can have base radius $r$ and height $h$.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that the total material cost can be written as
    $$C(r) = 2\pi p k r^{2} + \frac{2pV}{r}, \qquad r > 0.$$

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Determine the value of $r$ that minimises $C$, and show that it is indeed a minimum. Give your answer in terms of $V$, $k$ and $\pi$.

    (Determine)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    Hence show that the ratio of the height to the radius at the optimum is $2k$, independent of $V$ and $p$. Comment on what this predicts when $k=1$ and when $k$ is very large, and explain why both predictions are physically sensible.

    (Hence)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

A closed cylindrical can is to be manufactured with a fixed volume $V$. The curved surface is made from a material costing $p$ per unit area. The two circular ends are made from a thicker material costing $kp$ per unit area, where $k > 0$ is a constant. Let the can have base radius $r$ and height $h$. (a) Show that the total material cost can be written as $$C(r) = 2\pi p k r^{2} + \frac{2pV}{r}, \qquad r > 0.$$ [4 marks] (Show that) (b) Determine the value of $r$ that minimises $C$, and show that it is indeed a minimum. Give your answer in terms of $V$, $k$ and $\pi$. [5 marks] (Determine) (c) Hence show that the ratio of the height to the radius at the optimum is $2k$, independent of $V$ and $p$. Comment on what this predicts when $k=1$ and when $k$ is very large, and explain why both predictions are physically sensible. [6 marks] (Hence)
Reveal the answer

(a) The two circular ends have total area $2\pi r^{2}$ and cost $kp$ per unit area, contributing $2\pi r^{2}\cdot kp$. (A1) The curved surface has area $2\pi r h$, contributing $2\pi r h\cdot p$. (A1) The volume is fixed: $\pi r^{2}h = V$, so $h = \dfrac{V}{\pi r^{2}}$. (M1)
$$C = 2\pi p k r^{2} + 2\pi r p\cdot\frac{V}{\pi r^{2}} = 2\pi p k r^{2} + \frac{2pV}{r}$$ (A1)(AG)

(b) Differentiating with respect to $r$: (M1)
$$C'(r) = 4\pi p k r - \frac{2pV}{r^{2}}$$
Setting $C'(r) = 0$: (M1)
$$4\pi p k r = \frac{2pV}{r^{2}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad r^{3} = \frac{2pV}{4\pi p k} = \frac{V}{2\pi k}$$
$$r = \left(\frac{V}{2\pi k}\right)^{1/3}$$ (A1)
To confirm it is a minimum: (M1)
$$C''(r) = 4\pi p k + \frac{4pV}{r^{3}} > 0 \quad \text{for all } r>0$$
since $p$, $k$ and $V$ are all positive, so the stationary point is a minimum. (A1) (It is the only stationary point on $r>0$, and $C\to\infty$ both as $r\to0^{+}$ and as $r\to\infty$, so it is the global minimum.)

(c) Using $h = \dfrac{V}{\pi r^{2}}$ and $r^{3} = \dfrac{V}{2\pi k}$: (M1)
$$\frac{h}{r} = \frac{V}{\pi r^{3}} = \frac{V}{\pi\cdot\dfrac{V}{2\pi k}} = \frac{V\cdot 2\pi k}{\pi V} = 2k$$ (M1)(A1)(AG)
The ratio depends on $k$ only: $V$ and $p$ both cancel. (A1)

When $k=1$ the ends and the curved surface cost the same per unit area, and the optimum has $h = 2r$, so the height equals the diameter: the can is as tall as it is wide. (A1) This is the familiar equal-cost result and is sensible because the shape is then equally 'spread' in every direction.

When $k$ is very large the ends are very expensive relative to the curved surface, so the optimum makes the ends small and the can tall and thin, consistent with $h/r = 2k$ growing without bound. (A1) Conversely, if the ends were very cheap, $k\to0$ and the can becomes short and wide, which also matches intuition. The limiting cases therefore both confirm the result.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for using the volume constraint to eliminate $h$; a candidate who leaves the cost in terms of both $r$ and $h$ has not completed the reduction. (b) Accept the unsimplified form of $r$ provided it is equivalent. The second-derivative check must be shown, not asserted; a candidate who argues from the shape of $C$ should be credited only if they state that $C\to\infty$ at both ends of the domain. Watch for the error of differentiating $2pV/r$ as $+2pV/r^{2}$. (c) The ratio derivation earns (M1)(A1) and the observation that $V$ and $p$ cancel earns a further mark, since it is the insight the question exists to produce. Accept any sensible physical commentary for the final two marks; the key point for $k=1$ is $h=2r$ and for large $k$ is that the can becomes tall and thin. A candidate who says the can becomes short and wide for large $k$ has the direction inverted. Watch for the candidate who differentiates with respect to $h$ after eliminating $r$ instead: the algebra is valid but the resulting ratio is harder to read, so credit the equivalent form. A useful check the examiner can invite is dimensional: $r^{3}=V/(2\pi k)$ has dimensions of volume, so the cube root is obligatory, and a candidate who reports $r=V/(2\pi k)$ has lost a power. A candidate who treats $k$ as multiplying the curved surface rather than the ends has inverted the ratio and will report $h/r=2/k$; the limiting case test in (c) catches this immediately, which is why the commentary marks are not free.

Why this question is hard

The interesting result here is not the optimal radius but the optimal *shape*, and the question is arranged so that the shape is what survives. Once the volume constraint is used to eliminate the height, the cost is a function of one variable and differentiating it is routine. What is not routine is noticing that the answer should be expressed as a ratio: dividing $h$ by $r$ makes both $V$ and $p$ cancel, leaving $h/r = 2k$. That is a genuinely useful engineering statement, because it says the proportions of the cheapest can depend only on how much more the end material costs than the side material, and not at all on how big the can is or on the absolute price of the material.

This is why a student who reaches for a calculator early is punished. If $V$ and $p$ are given numerical values, the arithmetic goes through and produces a correct radius and height, but the cancellation is invisible and the general result is lost. The question deliberately withholds numerical values for $V$ and $p$ and supplies only the dimensionless parameter $k$, which is a strong hint about what the answer should look like. Getting a dimensionless quantity on one side of an equation and only dimensionless quantities on the other is a reliable way to check that an optimisation has been set up correctly.

The two limiting cases are not decorative. At $k=1$ the answer $h=2r$ reproduces the well-known result that the cheapest equal-cost can has its height equal to its diameter, which gives a student something to recognise. As $k$ grows the can becomes tall and thin, which is what a manufacturer would do if the end material were very expensive; as $k$ shrinks it becomes short and wide. That both limits behave sensibly is a check on the algebra that costs nothing and catches sign errors.
