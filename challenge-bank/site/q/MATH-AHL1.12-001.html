Which powers n make z^n = conj(z) reachable at real part one half

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.12-001

AHL 1.12 (complex numbers: the number i, where i^2 = -1), AHL 1.13 (modulus-argument form and Euler form)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedSolveShow thatHenceDetermine

Question

Let $z$ be a complex number and let $\bar z$ denote its complex conjugate.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    By writing $z=a+bi$, where $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$, solve the equation $z^{2}=\bar z$, giving every solution in the form $a+bi$.

    (Solve)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Show that every non-zero solution of $z^{2}=\bar z$ satisfies $|z|=1$, and deduce that every non-zero solution is a cube root of unity.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Hence, using modulus-argument form, solve the equation $z^{n}=\bar z$ for a general integer $n\geq1$, and state how many solutions it has.

    (Hence)
  4. [5 marks](d)

    Determine the smallest positive integer $n$ for which $z^{n}=\bar z$ has a solution whose real part is exactly $\dfrac12$, and describe all such integers $n$.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Put $z=a+bi$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$. Then $z^{2}=(a^{2}-b^{2})+2abi$ and $\bar z=a-bi$. (M1) Equating real and imaginary parts,

$$a^{2}-b^{2}=a,\qquad 2ab=-b.$$ (A1)

The second equation gives $b(2a+1)=0$, so either $b=0$ or $a=-\tfrac12$.

If $b=0$, then $a^{2}=a$, so $a=0$ or $a=1$, giving $z=0$ and $z=1$. (A1)

If $a=-\tfrac12$, then $a^{2}-b^{2}=a$ gives $\tfrac14-b^{2}=-\tfrac12$, so $b^{2}=\tfrac34$ and $b=\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, giving $z=-\tfrac12\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$. (A1)

The solutions are $z=0$, $z=1$, $z=-\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$ and $z=-\tfrac12-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$.

(b) Let $z\neq0$ satisfy $z^{2}=\bar z$. Taking moduli, $|z^{2}|=|\bar z|$, so $|z|^{2}=|z|$. Since $|z|\neq0$ we may divide, giving $|z|=1$. (M1)(A1) Then

$$z^{3}=z\cdot z^{2}=z\bar z=|z|^{2}=1.$$ (A1)

So every non-zero solution satisfies $z^{3}=1$, that is, is a cube root of unity. Indeed the three non-zero solutions found in (a) are $1$, $e^{2\pi i/3}$ and $e^{4\pi i/3}$.

(c) Write $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $r\geq0$. Then $z^{n}=r^{n}e^{in\theta}$ and $\bar z=re^{-i\theta}$. (M1) Equating moduli gives $r^{n}=r$, so $r=0$ or $r=1$. (A1)

If $r=0$ then $z=0$, which satisfies the equation. If $r=1$ then $e^{in\theta}=e^{-i\theta}$, so $e^{i(n+1)\theta}=1$, giving $\theta=\dfrac{2\pi k}{n+1}$ for $k=0,1,\dots,n$. (A1) Hence the non-zero solutions are the $n+1$ roots of $z^{n+1}=1$, and together with $z=0$ the equation has $n+2$ solutions. (A1)

(d) A solution with real part $\tfrac12$ cannot be $z=0$, so by the argument in (b) it has $|z|=1$. (M1) Writing $z=e^{i\theta}$ with $\cos\theta=\tfrac12$ gives $\theta=\pm\tfrac{\pi}{3}+2\pi m$, so $z=e^{\pm i\pi/3}$, a primitive sixth root of unity. (A1)

From (c), the solutions of $z^{n}=\bar z$ are $0$ together with the $(n+1)$th roots of unity. So $e^{i\pi/3}$ is a solution exactly when it is an $(n+1)$th root of unity, that is when $\left(e^{i\pi/3}\right)^{n+1}=1$, which is $e^{i\pi(n+1)/3}=1$, so $\dfrac{n+1}{3}$ must be an even integer. (M1)

Hence $n+1$ is a multiple of $6$, that is $n\equiv5\pmod6$. (A1) The smallest such integer is $n=5$; indeed $z=e^{i\pi/3}$ satisfies $z^{5}=e^{i5\pi/3}=e^{-i\pi/3}=\bar z$. All integers $n=5,11,17,23,\dots$ work. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Equating real and imaginary parts must be explicit for the first (M1). The case split $b=0$ or $a=-\tfrac12$ is essential: a candidate who divides the imaginary equation by $b$ without considering $b=0$ loses the solutions $z=0$ and $z=1$ and forfeits the corresponding (A1). Accept the solutions in any order.

(b) The modulus argument requires dividing by $|z|$, justified by $z\neq0$; a candidate who writes $|z|^{2}=|z|$ and concludes $|z|=1$ without noting $|z|\neq0$ loses the second (A1). The deduction $z^{3}=1$ must use $z\bar z=|z|^{2}$; a candidate who multiplies $z^{2}=\bar z$ by $z$ and writes $z^{3}=1$ directly has skipped the key step and should be asked to justify it.

(c) Award (M1) for the polar substitution, (A1) for $r=0$ or $r=1$, (A1) for the arguments, and (A1) for the count. Accept 'the $(n+1)$th roots of unity together with zero'. A candidate who forgets $z=0$ loses the final (A1).

(d) The critical step is realising that a non-zero solution has modulus $1$, so $\mathrm{Re}(z)=\tfrac12$ forces $z=e^{\pm i\pi/3}$. Award (M1) for this, (A1) for identifying a primitive sixth root, (M1) for the divisibility condition, and (A1)(A1) for $n\equiv5\pmod6$ and the smallest value. Follow-through from a wrong count in (c) is allowed, but a candidate who gives only $n=5$ and misses the infinite family loses the last (A1).

Why this question is hard

Parts (a) and (b) are algebraic and accessible; the question becomes hard at (c) and (d), where the same equation has to be understood structurally rather than solved case by case.

The unifying observation is that $\bar z=\dfrac{|z|^{2}}{z}$ for $z\neq0$. Substituting into $z^{n}=\bar z$ gives $z^{n+1}=|z|^{2}$, so on taking moduli $|z|^{n+1}=|z|^{2}$, which forces $|z|=1$ (or $z=0$). Once $|z|=1$ we have $\bar z=\dfrac1z$, and the equation becomes $z^{n+1}=1$. The whole problem collapses to a statement about roots of unity: the solutions are $0$ together with the $(n+1)$th roots of unity. This is the insight that part (c) is designed to make unavoidable, and it explains why part (b) asks about $|z|=1$: that is the hinge.

Part (d) inverts the question. Instead of solving for $z$ given $n$, it fixes a property of $z$, namely real part $\tfrac12$, and asks which $n$ allow it. The set of solutions is a cyclic group of order $n+1$; a real part of $\tfrac12$ means the solution is $e^{\pm i\pi/3}$, a primitive sixth root of unity. For this element to lie in the group of order $n+1$, the order must be divisible by $6$, giving $n\equiv5\pmod6$. The answer is an arithmetic progression, which is the kind of result that appears only once the group structure is visible.

Classic wrong turns: dividing the imaginary equation by $b$ in (a) and losing two solutions; using polar form in (c) but forgetting $z=0$; assuming in (d) that a solution with real part $\tfrac12$ must be found by solving one fixed equation rather than searching over $n$; and concluding that only $n=5$ works, missing the infinite family. A useful check is that $n=1$ gives $z=\bar z$, whose solutions are all real, so none has real part $\tfrac12$, consistent with $1\not\equiv5\pmod6$.

Verification
Numerical verification with complex arithmetic: the four solutions of $z^{2}=\bar z$ were checked against $z^{2}-\bar z$; the non-zero ones satisfy $z^{3}=1$ and $|z|=1$; for $n=1,\dots,5$ the solution set $\{0\}\cup\{z^{n+1}=1\}$ was checked to satisfy $z^{n}=\bar z$ and has $n+2$ elements; and the values of $n$ admitting real part $\tfrac12$ were found to be exactly $n\equiv5\pmod6$.
