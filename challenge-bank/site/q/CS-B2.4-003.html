B2.4 A nested loop whose inner trip count depends on the outer index

Computer science HL · CS-B2.4-003

Theme B: Computational thinking and problem-solvingB2.4 Programming algorithms (analysis of algorithms; efficiency of an algorithm)P1 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: not allowedDetermineShow thatExplain

Question

A programmer writes the following routine to count the divisors of every integer up to
$N$.

COUNT = 0
for I = 1 to N
    for J = I to N step I
        COUNT = COUNT + 1
    next J
next I
output COUNT

(a) Trace the routine for $N = 6$ and state the value output. \tag{4}

(b) Show that the number of times the inner loop body executes is

$$\sum_{I=1}^{N} \left\lfloor \frac{N}{I} \right\rfloor . \tag{4}$$

(c) Determine the value output when $N = 1000$, and hence determine the ratio of the number of
iterations to $N^{2}$. \tag{5}

(d) Explain why the time complexity of the routine is $\mathrm{O}(N \log N)$ rather than
$\mathrm{O}(N^{2})$. \tag{3}

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Trace the routine for $N = 6$ and state the value output.

    (Determine)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that the number of inner-loop executions is the sum from I = 1 to N of the floor of N divided by I.

    (Show that)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Determine the value output when $N = 1000$, and hence determine the ratio of the number of iterations to $N$ squared.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain why the time complexity is O(N log N) rather than O(N squared).

    (Explain)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Tracing the outer loop (M1):

| $I$ | values taken by $J$ | inner iterations |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 | 6 |
| 2 | 2, 4, 6 | 3 |
| 3 | 3, 6 | 2 |
| 4 | 4 | 1 |
| 5 | 5 | 1 |
| 6 | 6 | 1 |

The inner counts are $6, 3, 2, 1, 1, 1$ (A1) and the total is $6 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 14$
(A1). So the routine outputs $14$ (A1). Note that only the first of the six passes runs the
full $N$ times; by the fourth pass the inner loop executes once.

(b) On the pass with outer index $I$, the inner variable $J$ takes the values
$I, 2I, 3I, \ldots$ up to $N$ (M1). The largest multiple of $I$ not exceeding $N$ is
$\left\lfloor \frac{N}{I} \right\rfloor \cdot I$ (A1), so the number of values taken is exactly
$\left\lfloor \frac{N}{I} \right\rfloor$ (A1). Summing over the $N$ passes of the outer loop
gives the stated expression (AG).

(c) Evaluating the sum for $N = 1000$ (M1):

$$\sum_{I=1}^{1000} \left\lfloor \frac{1000}{I} \right\rfloor = 7069 \tag{A1}$$

The ratio to $N^{2}$ is (M1)

$$\frac{7069}{1000^{2}} = \frac{7069}{1000000} = 0.00707 \tag{A1}$$

so the routine performs about $0.7\%$ of the iterations a quadratic algorithm would. Equally
tellings is the comparison with $N\ln N$:

$$N\ln N = 1000 \times 6.908 = 6908 \qquad \frac{7069}{6908} = 1.02 \tag{A1}$$

so the actual count is within about $2\%$ of $N\ln N$. A candidate who assumed $\mathrm{O}(N^{2})$
would predict roughly half a million iterations and would be wrong by a factor of about
seventy.

(d) From (b), the iteration count is $\sum_{I=1}^{N} \left\lfloor \frac{N}{I} \right\rfloor
\le \sum_{I=1}^{N} \frac{N}{I} = N \sum_{I=1}^{N} \frac{1}{I}$ (M1). The sum on the right is
the $N$th harmonic number, $H_{N}$, which satisfies $\ln(N+1) \le H_{N} \le 1 + \ln N$ and is
therefore $\mathrm{O}(\log N)$ (A1). It follows that the whole expression is
$\mathrm{O}(N\log N)$ (AG). The bound is tight rather than merely an upper limit, as the
numerical comparison in (c) shows: at $N = 1000$ the actual count of $7069$ sits about $2\%$
above $N\ln N = 6908$, and the gap between the two is the linear term that the floors and the
Euler–Mascheroni constant contribute.

It is worth being explicit about what the bound does and does not say. Replacing the floor by
$\frac{N}{I}$ can only increase the total, so the argument gives a genuine upper bound on the
running time and never an underestimate; and since the harmonic sum is also $\Omega(\log N)$,
the estimate is of the right order and not merely safe. What the argument does not do is give
a constant: $\mathrm{O}(N\log N)$ says nothing about whether the constant is $1$ or $100$, and
for small $N$ the constant can matter more than the growth rate. The numerical work in (c) is
what supplies the constant, which is why both parts are needed.

One further observation, for a candidate who wants to check the result independently: the sum
$\sum \lfloor N/I \rfloor$ counts the pairs $(I, J)$ with $I \mid J$ and $J \le N$, so it is
the total number of divisors possessed by all the integers from $1$ to $N$. It is therefore
also the number of times a naive divisor-counting routine would increment its counter, which
is exactly what the code in the stem is doing. Recognising that gives the answer a second
meaning and a second way to verify it.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for a systematic trace — a table, or an equivalent listing of the values taken by
$J$ for each $I$. (A1) for the inner counts $6, 3, 2, 1, 1, 1$. (A1) for the total $14$.
(A1) for the observation that only the first pass runs the full $N$ times; this is the
observation that drives the rest of the item and a bare answer of $14$ with no trace earns only
the first (A1).

(b) (M1) for identifying the values taken by $J$ as the multiples of $I$. (A1) for
$\left\lfloor \frac{N}{I} \right\rfloor \cdot I$ as the largest multiple. (A1) for the count.
(AG) for the summation. Allow a candidate who argues via $\lfloor (N-I)/I \rfloor + 1$ and
simplifies, but the simplification must be shown.

(c) (M1) for attempting the sum; accept a stated value obtained by computation, since summing
a thousand terms by hand is not the point. (A1) for $7069$. (M1) for dividing by $N^{2}$.
(A1) for $0.00707$. (A1) for the comparison with $N \ln N$ or with the quadratic estimate.
Accept the ratio as $0.0071$.

(d) (M1) for bounding the floor by $\frac{N}{I}$. (A1) for identifying the harmonic sum as
$\mathrm{O}(\log N)$. (AG) for the conclusion. A candidate who merely says "the inner loop does
not run $N$ times" earns the first (M1) only.

Note: do not accept an argument that the answer is $\mathrm{O}(N)$ — the harmonic sum is not
constant.

Why this question is hard

Nested loops are taught with a rule of thumb — multiply the trip counts — and the rule
of thumb is right often enough to become a reflex. It fails here because it assumes the inner
count is independent of the outer index, and here it is the outer index itself that sets it.

Once the count is written as $\sum \lfloor N/I \rfloor$, the growth rate is a known quantity.
The floors can only reduce the total, so bounding above by $N\sum \frac{1}{I}$ is legitimate,
and the harmonic sum is $\mathrm{O}(\log N)$. That gives $N \log N$ rather than $N^{2}$, and
the numerical check in part (c) confirms the bound is not merely an upper limit: $N \ln N$ at
$N = 1000$ is $6908$ against an actual count of $7069$, about $2\%$ apart.

The factor-of-seventy gap between $7069$ and the half-million a quadratic estimate predicts is
the practical reason this matters. A programmer who believes the routine is quadratic will
refuse to run it at $N = 1000$ and will rewrite it unnecessarily; one who recognises
$N \log N$ will not. The difference between the two growth rates only widens with $N$: at
$N = 10^{6}$ the true count is about $1.4 \times 10^{7}$ against $10^{12}$ for a genuinely
quadratic routine.

There is also a substantive point hiding in the trace. The routine looks like it might be
counting something more exotic than it is, but $\sum \lfloor N/I \rfloor$ counts the number of
pairs $(I, J)$ with $I \mid J$ and $J \le N$ — that is, the total number of divisors possessed
by all the integers up to $N$. Recognising that gives the result a second, independent meaning,
and it is why the count grows the way it does: most integers have few divisors, and only the
small values of $I$ contribute many terms.

Finally, note that the floors matter for the exact value but not for the order of growth.
Dropping them changes the answer by at most $N$ out of roughly $N \ln N$, which is a lower-order
term — and knowing which simplifications change the answer and which do not is most of what
algorithm analysis consists of.
