A2.4 A security incident caused by a legitimate account with excessive privilege, where the control that would have prevented it conflicts with the requirement that clinical staff must not be blocked out of records in an emergency

Computer science HL · CS-A2.4-201

Theme A: Concepts of computer scienceA2.4 Network security, with A4.4 Ethical considerations and A3.2 Database designP2 · Section B13 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedAnalyseDesignDetermineEvaluate

An incident at a three-site hospital trust

A hospital trust runs one patient-records system across three sites, with six clinical departments. Access is granted through roles. In the year of the incident the trust operated forty roles; the largest was TechSupport, held by thirty-four members of the IT service desk, which carried the union of every permission any of its holders had ever asked for, including read and write access to the full records of every department at every site. The trust's written policy is that no member of staff may be denied access to a record they need in order to treat a patient, and that any access outside a member's own department must be visible in the audit log within one working day.

The incident: a service-desk analyst whose employment was being terminated copied the records of $1{,}180$ patients from four departments he had never worked in and had never had a legitimate reason to view. The copy was made over eleven days using his own account and his own credentials during normal working hours. It was found two months later during a routine audit. Nothing he did required an exploit, a stolen password or a disabled control, and every copy operation was recorded in the log. The log had $4.2$ million entries for the year and was reviewed by one auditor two days a week.

The proposal on the table is to replace the forty roles with a strict role-based scheme in which a role exists for every combination of site, department and job function that the trust employs, and in which any access outside a member's own role is refused by the system and escalated to a manager. The trust employs six departments, five job functions and three sites. The trust's information-governance officer has objected, on the grounds that the strict scheme will create an administration burden the trust cannot carry and that a refused request in a clinical emergency is a patient-safety event.

Question

The trust's board has asked for an analysis of the incident against the three components of the CIA triad, a design for a replacement role-based access-control scheme, the working arithmetic of the current audit coverage and the proposed quarterly permission review, and an evaluation of the information-governance officer's objection.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Analyse the incident against the three components of the CIA triad, identifying for each component whether it was breached and giving the specific evidence from the case that supports your answer.

    (Analyse)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Design a role-based access-control scheme for this trust, stating how many roles the strict scheme described in the case would need and explaining how your design resolves the conflict between the trust's policy on emergency access and the principle of least privilege.

    (Design)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    The auditor reviews $4.2$ million log entries a year in two days a week. Determine what fraction of the log is currently reviewed, and determine the review workload the proposed scheme implies if each role must be reviewed quarterly at fifteen minutes per role.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate the proposal against the information-governance officer's objection, and state which of the two requirements -- least privilege or unblocked clinical access -- your design sacrifices and why.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) *Confidentiality* was the component actually breached, and the breach was large and deliberate: $1{,}180$ patients' records were copied by someone who had no legitimate reason to see any of them. The evidence is the volume, the four departments outside his own and the eleven days over which he worked (A1).

*Integrity* was not breached on the evidence given. Every operation was a copy out of the system; the case records no modification, deletion or insertion of records, and none of the $1{,}180$ records is stated to have been altered. It is worth saying plainly that the absence of an integrity breach is a fact about this incident and not a property of the design -- the same account carried write access to the full records of every department, so integrity was equally exposed and was simply not attacked (A1).

*Availability* was not breached either. The systems remained available to the clinicians who needed them throughout, and nothing in the case suggests service loss. The relevant observation is that the compromise was found two months late, which is a failure of detection rather than of availability; the log contained the evidence from the first day (A1).

The interesting finding is that two of the three components were untouched and the third was breached completely, by a user whose permissions were only what the trust had given him. In a CIA-triple analysis of this incident the useful output is not the tally but the reason each answer comes out as it does.

(b) The strict scheme described in the case needs a role for every combination of site, department and job function:
$$3 \times 6 \times 5 = 90 \ \text{roles} \;\; (A1).$$
Against the forty roles in use now, that is a little over twice the number of roles and it is what the governance officer means by an administration burden -- part (c) puts a figure on it.

The design should keep least privilege as the default and treat emergency access as an explicitly-granted, self-expiring exception (M1). Concretely:

1. Roles are the union of department and job function, not the cross product with site. A ward nurse is a ward nurse at any of the three sites; the site is an attribute of the *record*, not of the person. That reduces the role count to $6 \times 5 = 30$ and leaves the site as a condition evaluated at request time (A1).
2. Emergency access is a break-glass grant, not a role. A clinician who needs a record outside their role requests it, states a reason code, and is given time-limited access -- an hour is long enough for a clinical decision -- with notification to the responsible manager and an entry in the log that is machine-flagged. The clinical requirement is met in seconds, and the exception is narrow, time-bound and attributable (A1).
3. The audit becomes targeted. What matters for detection is the small set of accesses outside a member's usual pattern, not the $4.2$ million entries. Flagging break-glass grants, out-of-own-department reads and reads at volume per user per day reduces the reviewer's queue to something a human can actually read (A1).

(c) The auditor works two days a week, which is $2 \times 52 = 104$ working days a year, or about $\dfrac{104}{5} = 20.8$ working weeks (M1). Reviewing $4.2$ million entries at, say, one entry per second would take
$$\frac{4{,}200{,}000}{3600 \times 7} = 166.7 \ \text{seven-hour days},$$
against the $104$ days available; so even at one entry per second the log cannot be read in the time the trust gives it, and the true coverage is a small fraction of a per cent (A1). The practical statement is that the log as configured is not a control at all: it is a record that exists so that a breach can be reconstructed afterwards, which is exactly how this incident was found.

The proposed scheme's review workload is a different quantity and it is small by comparison. Ninety roles reviewed quarterly at fifteen minutes each:
$$90 \times 15 = 1{,}350 \ \text{minutes} = 22.5 \ \text{hours per quarter},$$
or $7.5$ hours a month (A1). Under the reduced design of part (b), with thirty roles, the same review costs $30 \times 15 = 450$ minutes $= 7.5$ hours per quarter, a third of the strict scheme's burden. Note the direction of the comparison: the *permission review* is affordable under either scheme and the *log review* is not affordable under either, so the governance officer's objection is about the wrong quantity.

(d) The objection is half right. The administration burden of ninety roles is real but small in absolute terms -- $22.5$ hours a quarter is a fraction of one person's time -- and it is not the reason the strict scheme should be rejected. The patient-safety objection is the serious one, because a system that refuses a clinician access to a record in an emergency has converted a security control into a clinical hazard, and no audit finding justifies that (A1).

My design sacrifices something, and it is least privilege in the strict sense: a break-glass grant means that for a short period a member of staff holds access their role does not include. What is preserved is the part of least privilege that matters -- that the excess access is bounded in time, requires a stated reason, is attributable to a person and is reported rather than discovered (A1). The alternative, refusing the request, protects the principle absolutely and harms patients; the alternative of granting standing access to everyone who might ever need it, which is what the trust actually did, is what produced this incident. Sacrificing a strict reading of the principle in favour of an auditable exception is the trade the case is designed to force, and the mark is for saying which side of it the design comes down on and why.

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for each component with evidence. The marks are for the *evidence*, not the verdict: "confidentiality breached because 1,180 records were copied by someone with no reason to see them" scores, "confidentiality was breached" does not. A candidate who claims integrity or availability was breached must point to a modification or an outage in the case; the case contains neither and claiming one forfeits that component's mark.

(b) (A1) for $3 \times 6 \times 5 = 90$, (M1) for a design that separates the two requirements rather than trading one away, (A1) for the site-as-attribute reduction, (A1) for the break-glass mechanism with its time limit, reason code and notification, (A1) for the targeted audit. Full marks do not require all four design elements; a candidate who gives the role count, the site attribute and the break-glass mechanism has the substance. A design that answers "give everyone less access" without saying what happens in an emergency scores (A1) and (M1) only.

(c) (M1) for converting the auditor's time into days or weeks, (A1) for the conclusion that the log cannot be read at any realistic rate, (A1) for $22.5$ hours per quarter from the ninety roles. A candidate who computes $90 \times 15$ and stops has the third mark; the first two require the comparison. Accept $104$ days, $20.8$ weeks or a "small fraction of a per cent" as the statement of coverage.

(d) (A1) for separating the two objections and treating the clinical one as the serious one, (A1) for naming what the design gives up, (A1) for tracing a consequence of that choice -- what bounds the relaxation (its lifetime, its reason code, its reportability) or what fails clinically if the request is refused instead. A candidate who says "both requirements can be satisfied" without saying which is relaxed in the emergency case scores at most the first mark.

Forfeits: describing the incident as an external attack, which the case explicitly denies; proposing encryption as the primary control, which does nothing about an authorised user; proposing to remove the technical-support account, which leaves the support function unable to work. Condonation: role counts of $30$ or $90$ are both accepted in (b) provided the scheme described matches the number given.

Why this question is hard

The most useful thing about this case is what it does not contain. There is no attacker, no exploit, no stolen credential and no unpatched system. A member of staff with a valid account read records he was allowed to read. Every control that a checklist would name as present *was* present, which is why the case is worth studying: the failure is in the permission model, and the permission model was somebody's deliberate answer to a real operational problem.

The problem it answered is stated in the case and it is usually stated in real hospitals too: staff must never be blocked from a record they need in order to treat a patient. The cheapest way to guarantee that is to give a support role every permission any of its thirty-four holders might need, because then no request is ever refused. The cost of that answer is invisible until it is not: one analysis of the log would have shown that a service-desk analyst was reading four departments he had never worked in, eleven days running, and the log had the evidence from the first day. Detection failed because there was nothing small enough to look at -- $4.2$ million entries, one auditor, two days a week -- and the arithmetic in part (c) is there to make that failure a number rather than an opinion.

The design question is therefore not "how do we stop insiders" but "how do we keep the guarantee without the blanket permission", and the answer that works is to make the exception explicit and short-lived. Break-glass access is a genuine technique and its properties are exactly the ones the case needs: the clinician gets the record in seconds, the grant expires on its own, the reason is recorded, and the manager is notified. Least privilege in the strict sense -- nobody holds any permission they do not need -- is given up for the duration of the grant, and part (d) asks the candidate to say so out loud rather than pretend the trade does not exist.

The role arithmetic is deliberately unremarkable. Three sites times six departments times five functions is ninety, and a candidate who sees that the site belongs to the record rather than to the person gets it down to thirty. Neither number is frightening on its own -- twenty-two hours a quarter is not a burden -- and the point of computing both is to show that the governance officer has objected to the wrong quantity. The scheme that the trust actually ran had forty roles and still produced this incident, which is the clearest evidence that counting roles is not the control.

Challenge lever
The incident has no attacker and no vulnerability: the account was genuine, the password was correct and the network was up to date. What made the breach possible is that a single technical-support role had been granted every permission that any of its thirty-four holders might ever need, so the requirement that staff are never locked out of a record in an emergency and the requirement that no account holds more privilege than its job needs cannot both be met by one role per person. The quantitative part turns on the number of roles a pure role-based scheme needs and on what that number costs to audit.
