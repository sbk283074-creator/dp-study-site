B.3 Recovering an unknown compartment volume from two pressure readings and one known volume, with the temperature and the amount of gas never being given

Physics HL · PHYS-B.3-301

Theme B: The particulate nature of matterB.3 Gas laws: the equation of state for an ideal gas pV = nRT, and its use when a fixed amount of gas occupies a region whose volume is initially unknownP2 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatDetermineDetermineDetermine

Question

The figure shows a rigid vessel divided into two compartments, A and B, by a partition containing a valve that is initially closed. The vessel is held in good thermal contact with surroundings large enough that the temperature of the gas inside returns to its original value after any change; the value of that temperature is, however, not known to the experimenter and cannot be measured with the apparatus available.
Compartment A contains an ideal gas at a pressure of $3.0 \times 10^{5}\ {\rm Pa}$. Compartment B contains the same ideal gas at a pressure of $2.0 \times 10^{5}\ {\rm Pa}$, and the volume of compartment B is known to be $3.0\ {\rm dm^{3}}$ $(3.0 \times 10^{-3}\ {\rm m^{3}})$. The valve is opened, the gas is allowed to mix, sufficient time elapses for the temperature to return to its original value, and the pressure throughout the whole vessel is then measured to be $2.4 \times 10^{5}\ {\rm Pa}$. The gas in both compartments may be treated as one ideal gas of a single, unknown molar mass.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Show that the final reading is related to the initial readings by
    $$p\,(V_{A} + V_{B}) = p_{A}V_{A} + p_{B}V_{B},$$
    where $V_{A}$ is the unknown volume of compartment A, and state the single assumption about the temperature that this relationship requires.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the volume of compartment A and hence the total internal volume of the vessel, giving your answers in ${\rm dm^{3}}$.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    The temperature is now revealed to have been $300\ {\rm K}$ throughout. Determine the amount of substance, in moles, that was initially present in compartment A and in compartment B separately, and comment on what you notice.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    The total mass of gas in the vessel is measured separately to be $13.8\ {\rm g}$. Determine the molar mass of the gas, in ${\rm g\,mol^{-1}}$, and hence suggest what the gas might be. (Molar masses, in ${\rm g\,mol^{-1}}$: helium $4.0$, nitrogen $28.0$, oxygen $32.0$, carbon dioxide $44.0$.)

    (Determine)
  5. [3 marks](e)

    Explain why the recovery in part (b) succeeded without any knowledge of the temperature, and state what would happen to the method if part (a) had instead been carried out with the vessel thermally isolated and the final temperature measured to be above the initial one.

    (Explain)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Applying $pV = nRT$ to each compartment before the valve is opened gives $n_{A} = p_{A}V_{A}/RT$ and $n_{B} = p_{B}V_{B}/RT$, where $T$ is the common initial temperature (M1). The total amount of gas is conserved when the valve opens, so $n = n_{A} + n_{B}$, and applying the equation of state once more to the whole vessel of volume $V_{A} + V_{B}$ at the final pressure $p$:
$$\frac{p(V_{A} + V_{B})}{RT} = \frac{p_{A}V_{A}}{RT} + \frac{p_{B}V_{B}}{RT} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(V_{A} + V_{B}) = p_{A}V_{A} + p_{B}V_{B} \;(A1),$$
since $RT$ is common to every term. The single assumption is that the final temperature equals the initial temperature, which the thermal contact with the large surroundings ensures (A1). Note that no numerical value of $T$ was used.
(b) Substituting, and working in ${\rm dm^{3}}$ since the units of pressure cancel between the two sides,
$$2.4\,(V_{A} + 3.0) = 3.0\,V_{A} + 2.0(3.0) \;(M1)$$
$$2.4V_{A} + 7.2 = 3.0V_{A} + 6.0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1.2 = 0.6V_{A} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_{A} = 2.0\ {\rm dm^{3}} \;(A1),$$
so the total internal volume is $V_{A} + V_{B} = 2.0 + 3.0 = 5.0\ {\rm dm^{3}}$ (A1). Checking: $p_{A}V_{A} + p_{B}V_{B} = 3.0(2.0) + 2.0(3.0) = 12.0$ and $p(V_{A}+V_{B}) = 2.4(5.0) = 12.0$, which agree.
(c) With $T = 300\ {\rm K}$ and $R = 8.31\ {\rm J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$:
$$n_{A} = \frac{p_{A}V_{A}}{RT} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{5} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3}}{8.31 \times 300} = \frac{600}{2493} = 0.241\ {\rm mol} \;(M1)(A1),$$
$$n_{B} = \frac{p_{B}V_{B}}{RT} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{5} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-3}}{8.31 \times 300} = \frac{600}{2493} = 0.241\ {\rm mol} \;(A1),$$
to three significant figures. The two amounts are identical. This is not a coincidence: both compartments have the same product $pV$, equal to $600\ {\rm J}$, and by the equation of state equal values of $pV$ at equal $T$ imply equal amounts of substance (A1). The total is therefore $0.481\ {\rm mol}$.
(d) The molar mass is total mass divided by total amount of substance:
$$M = \frac{m}{n} = \frac{13.8 \times 10^{-3}}{0.481} = 2.87 \times 10^{-2}\ {\rm kg\,mol^{-1}} = 28.7\ {\rm g\,mol^{-1}} \;(M1)(A1),$$
where $n = 1200/2493 = 0.481\ {\rm mol}$ has been used in full rather than rounded. The value lies closest to nitrogen at $28.0\ {\rm g\,mol^{-1}}$ (A1), with the remaining $2.5\%$ difference attributable to the uncertainty in the measured mass and pressures; oxygen at $32.0$ and carbon dioxide at $44.0$ are too heavy, and helium at $4.0$ far too light. The gas is therefore most plausibly nitrogen, or air, whose mean molar mass is about $29\ {\rm g\,mol^{-1}}$.
(e) The recovery worked because $RT$ was a common factor of every term in part (a) and therefore cancelled before any substitution was made: the relation $p(V_{A}+V_{B}) = p_{A}V_{A} + p_{B}V_{B}$ is a statement about the conservation of $nRT$, and dividing through by $RT$ removes both unknowns at once (A1). The method did not need $T$ because the quantity being conserved is not $n$ but $nRT$ divided by a temperature that is the same before and after (A1). If the vessel had been thermally isolated and the final temperature had risen, then the final state would carry a different $RT'$, giving $p(V_{A}+V_{B}) = RT'\bigl(n_{A}+n_{B}\bigr) = \tfrac{T'}{T}\bigl(p_{A}V_{A} + p_{B}V_{B}\bigr)$, and the simple relation of part (a) would no longer hold: with $T' > T$ the candidate using part (a) unchanged would obtain too large a value for $V_{A}$, since the measured final pressure is then higher than it would have been under isothermal conditions for the same total volume (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for writing the equation of state for each compartment or for the total, (A1) for deducing the stated relation with the cancellation of $RT$ evident, (A1) for identifying that the final temperature must equal the initial one. A candidate who merely asserts conservation of $pV$ without referring to the equation of state scores (A1) for the relation only. Note that candidates may work in ${\rm m^{3}}$ or in ${\rm dm^{3}}$ provided they are consistent; no mark is awarded for the conversion itself.
(b) (M1) for substituting correctly into the relation from part (a), (A1) for $V_{A} = 2.0\ {\rm dm^{3}}$, (A1) for $5.0\ {\rm dm^{3}}$ total. Follow-through is available for candidates who use the relation from part (a) but make an arithmetic slip, provided they then check against $p(V_{A}+V_{B}) = p_{A}V_{A} + p_{B}V_{B}$. Forfeits: using ratios of pressures alone, which implicitly assumes the two volumes are equal and yields $V_{A} = 2.5\ {\rm dm^{3}}$, and losing a factor of ten in the conversion between ${\rm dm^{3}}$ and ${\rm m^{3}}$ in one direction only, which gives a manifestly inconsistent check.
(c) (M1) for applying $n = pV/RT$ with each compartment separately, (A1) for both $0.241\ {\rm mol}$ values, (A1) for the comment that they are equal and for linking that to equal values of $pV$. Accept $0.24\ {\rm mol}$ if consistent. A candidate who reports the values without noticing they are identical loses the comment mark; this is a designed observation, not an incidental one.
(d) (M1) for using total mass over total amount, (A1) for $28.7\ {\rm g\,mol^{-1}}$ (accept $28.6$ to $28.8$ from rounding differences), (A1) for identifying nitrogen and ruling out the alternatives explicitly. Condoned: identifying the gas as air rather than nitrogen, since $29\ {\rm g\,mol^{-1}}$ is within $1\%$ of the value found; do not condone leaving the identification open when four candidates were listed. Do not award the final mark for naming nitrogen without mentioning the comparison against the other three values.
(e) (A1) for the cancellation argument, (A1) for the further point that it is $nRT$, not $n$, that is conserved and common, (A1) for the consequence for an isolated vessel, including the direction of the error. Accept any clear statement that a higher final temperature would inflate the apparent $V_{A}$ under the isothermal formula. Mark generously for the physical reasoning here and strictly for arithmetic -- no numerical example is expected in this part.

Why this question is hard

The item is a test of whether a candidate reaches for a temperature because one is missing, or notices that a temperature is missing because none is needed. Nearly every ideal-gas problem a student has met supplies $T$ explicitly and uses it, so the absence reads as carelessness in the question rather than as information. It is the information.

The structure that makes it work is that the conserved quantity and the measured quantity are not the same one. What is conserved is $n$; what is measured is $pV$, and $pV = nRT$. When the single temperature is common to every term the factor $RT$ divides out of the conservation equation, leaving a relation purely between products of pressure and volume. Nothing in that relation mentions the gas constant, the temperature, or the amount of substance, and that is precisely why it can be solved for $V_{A}$.

The equality of the two amounts in part (c) is the design paying itself back. Once $V_{A} = 2.0\\ {\\rm dm^{3}}$ is found, $p_{A}V_{A} = 600\\ {\\rm J}$ equals $p_{B}V_{B} = 600\\ {\\rm J}$, so the two compartments held equal amounts all along and the mixed gas is simply doubled. A candidate who reaches part (c) and finds two identical numbers has discovered why the problem was solvable and can say so; one who got here by a wrong route will find two different numbers and has a chance to notice.

Part (e) is where the assumption hiding in part (a) finally has to be named. The relation was derived by dividing through by a temperature that is the same before and after, which is why the thermal reservoir was described at length in the stem. Isolate the vessel and the bilinear relation in $pV$ fails: the final state carries a factor $T'/T$, and using the isothermal formula anyway inflates the inferred volume. Being able to say which way the error goes, rather than merely that there is one, is the mark of having understood where the method came from rather than having recognised it.

Worth adding: nothing here needs the molar mass until part (d), and when it arrives it arrives through a separate measurement. The chain therefore runs 'unknown geometry from pressures only, then unknown substance from the temperature once it is revealed, then unknown identity from a balance reading', and each link adds exactly one piece of information. That ordering is what lets every part be answered independently and every part be checked independently.

Challenge lever
Every instinct says to convert a pressure and a volume into an amount of substance, and that instinct cannot be followed here because neither the temperature nor the amount of gas in either compartment is given. The route that works never evaluates $n$ or $T$ at all: it adds the two amounts symbolically and lets $RT$ cancel against itself. Reading the word 'ideal' in the stem, most candidates start scanning for a temperature to substitute, and the absence of one is read as an omission rather than as the design of the question.
