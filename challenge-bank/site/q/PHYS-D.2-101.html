D.2 Electric and magnetic fields

Physics HL · PHYS-D.2-101

Question

Five independent multiple-choice questions on electric and magnetic fields. Each stands alone. Take $k = 8.99 \times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{N\,m^{2}\,C^{-2}}$, $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$, $m_{e} = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $m_{p} = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $\mu_{0} = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\,m\,A^{-1}}$ where needed.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    A charge of $+4.0\ \mathrm{\mu C}$ and a charge of $-1.0\ \mathrm{\mu C}$ are fixed $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ apart. At which point on the line joining them is the resultant electric field zero?

    (Determine)
  2. [1 mark](b)

    What is the electric potential at a distance of $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ from an isolated point charge of $+4.0\ \mathrm{\mu C}$?

    (Calculate)
  3. [1 mark](c)

    An electron, initially at rest, is accelerated through a potential difference of $2.5\ \mathrm{kV}$ in a vacuum. What is its final speed?

    (Calculate)
  4. [1 mark](d)

    A proton enters a uniform magnetic field of $0.40\ \mathrm{T}$ at $3.0 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, moving at right angles to the field. What is the radius of its path?

    (Calculate)
  5. [1 mark](e)

    Two long straight parallel wires are $5.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ apart and each carries $10\ \mathrm{A}$ in the same direction. What is the force per unit length between them?

    (Determine)

Total: 5 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) A. Between opposite charges both fields point towards the negative charge, so they add and cannot cancel; the zero therefore lies outside, on the side of the smaller charge. Solving $4.0/x^{2} = 1.0/(x-0.30)^{2}$ gives $x = 0.60\ \mathrm{m}$ from the $+4.0\ \mathrm{\mu C}$ charge, that is $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ beyond the $-1.0\ \mathrm{\mu C}$ charge. (A1)

(b) A. $V = kQ/r = 8.99\times10^{9} \times 4.0\times10^{-6}/0.30 = 1.20 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{V}$. (A1) Potential is a scalar and falls as $1/r$, whereas field strength is a vector and falls as $1/r^{2}$; using the wrong power gives $4.0 \times 10^{5}$, which is option B and has the wrong units entirely.

(c) A. Energy conservation gives $eV = \tfrac{1}{2}m_{e}v^{2}$, so $v = \sqrt{2 \times 1.60\times10^{-19} \times 2500/9.11\times10^{-31}} = 2.97 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. (A1) The result is about $0.10c$, so the relativistic correction is below one per cent and the classical expression is adequate here.

(d) A. The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes the direction and not the speed, and the path is circular with $Bqv = mv^{2}/r$. Hence $r = mv/Bq = 1.67\times10^{-27} \times 3.0\times10^{6}/(0.40 \times 1.60\times10^{-19}) = 0.078\ \mathrm{m} = 7.8\ \mathrm{cm}$. (A1)

(e) A. Each wire lies in the field of the other, and $F/L = \mu_{0}I_{1}I_{2}/(2\pi d) = 2\times10^{-7} \times 10 \times 10/0.050 = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. (A1) The force is attractive for currents in the same direction and repulsive for opposite directions, and that distinction is worth as much as the magnitude.

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking. (a) Accept $0.60\ \mathrm{m}$ from the $+4.0\ \mathrm{\mu C}$ charge stated explicitly; a candidate who gives the distance without saying which side has not answered the question. Do not credit option B on the grounds that the algebra works: recognising that the fields must oppose is the physics, and the square root introduces the spurious root precisely to test it. (b) Accept $1.19$ to $1.20 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{V}$; note that the sign is positive because the source charge is positive, and a candidate who worries about sign here is confusing potential with field. (c) Accept $2.9$ to $3.0 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; relativistic correction at this speed is under one per cent and need not be applied. (d) Accept $7.8$ to $7.9\ \mathrm{cm}$ or $0.078\ \mathrm{m}$; the mark is for $r = mv/Bq$, so a candidate who writes the correct expression and then makes an arithmetic slip should be given the benefit of the doubt in bookwork terms. (e) Accept $4.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ with the direction stated; the direction is half the item and should not be treated as a bonus.

Why this question is hard

Item (a) is the one that separates candidates who can do the algebra from those who can read a field diagram. Setting the two field magnitudes equal and squaring produces a quadratic with two roots, and only one of them lies in a region where the fields oppose. A candidate who checks directions first rules out the space between the charges before writing anything down and is left with a single candidate region; one who squares first has to test both answers, and option B is exactly the root that survives algebraically and fails physically. The same trap appears in any two-charge problem where the charges are unequal, which is why the ratio was made four to one rather than two to one.

Items (b) and (c) are paired around a distinction that the notation does nothing to help with: $V$ is used both for potential and for potential difference, and the two expressions $kQ/r$ and $kQ/r^{2}$ differ by a single character. The reliable defence is dimensional, and it is worth teaching explicitly. Potential is energy per unit charge, so it must fall off more slowly than the field, which is force per unit charge; in (c) the same idea reappears as $eV = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}$, where the left side is an energy and the right side has to be an energy too.

Item (e) is a direction question wearing a calculation, and the common failure is to compute the magnitude correctly and then guess the direction. The rule is not arbitrary, and it is worth deriving rather than memorising: each wire sits in the field produced by the other, and applying the left-hand rule to that field and to the current in the second wire gives a force towards the first. Parallel currents attract, and that is why a tightly wound solenoid under heavy current experiences a compressive load.
