2.1 The cost of labour turnover against the cost of a retention pay rise, where the wrong comparison is total recruitment spend against total salary increase rather than the saving the proposal actually buys

Business management SL · BM-2.1-201

A call centre weighing a pay rise against recruitment costs

Brightline Support operates a single call centre employing $400$ staff. The average salary is $£22{,}000$ a year. In the year just ended the labour turnover rate was $30\%$, and the finance function calculates that it costs $£2{,}500$ to recruit and train one replacement, covering advertising, agency fees, induction and the lost productivity of the new starter's first weeks.

The directors are worried about retention and propose raising pay by $4\%$ for every member of staff, from the start of the next financial year. The human resource manager expects this to halve the turnover rate, from $30\%$ to $15\%$, on the grounds that pay is the reason most leavers give in their exit interviews, and argues that a pay rise which halves turnover would save a great deal on recruitment and training. The directors have asked for the arithmetic before deciding. They have not asked about anything other than the effect on recruitment and salary costs.

Question

The directors of a call centre are considering a pay rise in order to keep staff. Using the figures in the stimulus, calculate the present turnover rate and the present annual recruitment cost; calculate the annual cost of the proposed pay rise; show what the pay rise would save and what it would add; determine how many leavers it would have to prevent to pay for itself; and evaluate whether the pay rise should be approved.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the number of staff who left in the year just ended, the present annual cost of recruitment and training, and express the turnover rate as it is normally stated.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the annual cost of the proposed $4\%$ pay rise across the whole workforce.

    (Calculate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Show that if the pay rise did halve the turnover rate it would save $£150{,}000$ a year in recruitment and training, and that against its own cost this increases the call centre's total annual costs by $£202{,}000$.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine how many leavers the pay rise would have to prevent in a year in order to pay for itself, and compare that number with the total number of leavers at the present turnover rate.

    (Determine)
  5. [4 marks](e)

    Evaluate whether the directors should approve the pay rise, referring to the figures you have calculated and to the assumptions on which the human resource manager's expectation rests.

    (Evaluate)

Reveal the answer

(a) A turnover rate of $30\%$ on a workforce of $400$ means
$$400 \times 0.30 = 120 \;\; (A1)$$
staff left during the year. At $£2{,}500$ to recruit and train one replacement, the present annual cost of turnover is
$$120 \times \u00a32{,}500 = \u00a3300{,}000 \;\; (A1).$$
The turnover rate is already stated as a percentage of the workforce, so it is $30\%$; it may also be expressed as $120$ leavers against an average workforce of $400$, which is the same figure $(A1)$. It is worth noting that the $£300{,}000$ is money the call centre is *already* spending, not a new cost, and that nothing in the pay rise changes it except by changing the number of leavers.

(b) A $4\%$ rise on an average salary of $£22{,}000$ is
$$\u00a322{,}000 \times 0.04 = \u00a3880 \;\; (M1)$$
per member of staff per year. Across $400$ staff this is
$$400 \times \u00a3880 = \u00a3352{,}000 \;\; (A1).$$
The whole of that $\u00a3352{,}000$ is a new, recurring cost $(A1)$: it is paid every year for as long as the pay rate stands, and it is paid to all $400$ staff, not only to the ones the rise is meant to keep.

(c) If turnover halved, from $30\%$ to $15\%$, the number of leavers would fall from $120$ to $400 \times 0.15 = 60$. The saving is the cost of the $60$ replacements the call centre no longer needs:
$$(120 - 60) \times \u00a32{,}500 = \u00a3150{,}000 \;\; (A1).$$
The pay rise costs $\u00a3352{,}000$. The change in the call centre's total costs is therefore
$$+\u00a3352{,}000 - \u00a3150{,}000 = +\u00a3202{,}000,$$
a rise of $\u00a3202{,}000$ a year $(A1)$, not a saving. Put as totals: recruitment and training would fall to $60 \times \u00a32{,}500 = \u00a3150{,}000$, and with the new salary bill of $\u00a3352{,}000$ sitting on top of it the two costs together come to about $\u00a3502{,}000$ against the present $\u00a3300{,}000$ $(A1)$.

The essential point is which quantity changes. The present turnover cost of $\u00a3300{,}000$ does not become zero when turnover halves; it becomes $\u00a3150{,}000$. The proposal buys a *reduction* of $\u00a3150{,}000$, and it must be set against a cost of $\u00a3352{,}000$, not against the $\u00a3300{,}000$ that was being spent anyway.

(d) To pay for itself, the recruitment saving must equal the cost of the rise, so the number of replacements the rise must prevent is
$$\frac{\u00a3352{,}000}{\u00a32{,}500} = 140.8 \;\; (M1)(A1),$$
that is $141$ leavers must be prevented. But at the present turnover rate only $120$ staff leave in a whole year, so there are not $141$ leavers to prevent: even a turnover rate of zero would remove only $120$ replacements and save $120 \times \u00a32{,}500 = \u00a3300{,}000$, which is $£52{,}000$ short of the cost of the rise $(A1)$. The conclusion is sharp and worth stating plainly: on the figures given, the pay rise cannot pay for itself at any turnover rate, because its maximum possible saving is smaller than its cost.

(e) On the arithmetic, the directors should not approve the rise as a way of saving money, because it does the opposite: it adds $\u00a3202{,}000$ a year to costs even while delivering the halving of turnover the manager expects $(A1)$. The maximum saving available from the whole of the call centre's turnover problem is $\u00a3300{,}000$, and the rise costs $\u00a3352{,}000$, so the retention case cannot be made on recruitment savings at all -- the manager's mechanism fails before any assumption is tested.

The assumption should then be examined on its own terms $(A1)$. Halving turnover from $30\%$ to $15\%$ is a very large change to attribute to a pay rise of $4\%$, and the evidence for it is that pay is what leavers *say* in exit interviews; what people say when they leave is not the same as what would have kept them. A $4\%$ rise is below the level of inflation in most years, so it may not even be felt as a real improvement. If the manager is optimistic and turnover falls by less than half, the saving shrinks further while the $\u00a3352{,}000$ does not.

A judgement is still required, and there is an honest case on the other side $(A1)$. The figures only cover salary and recruitment, and the directors explicitly did not ask about anything else; turnover also carries a cost in service quality, in the loss of experienced agents, and in the morale of the staff who remain, none of which is in the $£2{,}500$ figure. If those costs are large, a pay rise could be justified as a *service* investment rather than a *recruitment* saving -- but that is a different argument from the one the manager made, and it would have to be supported by evidence the call centre has not gathered.

The recommendation should be direct $(A1)$. Approve no pay rise on the case as put, because the retention argument is arithmetically impossible: a rise costing $\u00a3352{,}000$ cannot recover more than $\u00a3300{,}000$ in avoided recruitment even if nobody left. If the directors want to reduce turnover, a smaller, targeted measure -- a retention bonus paid only to staff who stay, or an increase confined to the roles that actually leave -- would cost less than a rise paid to all $400$ and would not repeat every year for the staff it is not needed for.

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for $120$ leavers, (A1) for $£300{,}000$, (A1) for stating the rate as $30\%$. A candidate who gives $120$ and $£300{,}000$ without restating the percentage has the substance and keeps the third mark if the rate appears in the working.

(b) (M1) for the per-head increase of $£880$, (A1) for $£352{,}000$, (A1) for noting that it is recurring and paid to the whole workforce. A candidate who applies the $4\%$ to the $120$ leavers only, or to the recruitment cost, has misread the proposal and forfeits the marks.

(c) (A1) for the $£150{,}000$ saving, (A1) for the net increase of $£202{,}000$ (accept $£502{,}000$ against $£300{,}000$ as the statement of totals), (A1) for explaining that the comparison is against the *reduction* in recruitment cost, not the total. The third mark is for the reasoning, not the arithmetic: a candidate who computes both figures but writes that the rise "saves $£150{,}000$" without saying it costs $£352{,}000$ has not earned it.

(d) (M1) for dividing the cost of the rise by the cost per replacement, (A1) for $140.8$ or $141$, (A1) for the comparison with the $120$ leavers available and the conclusion that the maximum saving of $£300{,}000$ is still short. A candidate who answers "140 leavers" and stops, without noticing that only 120 exist, forfeits the third mark. The point of the part is that the target is unattainable.

(e) (A1) for refusing the rise as a cost-saving measure with the shortfall as the reason, (A1) for examining the assumption that a $4\%$ rise halves turnover, ideally with the observation that the evidence is what leavers say rather than what would have kept them, (A1) for identifying the costs the figures exclude (service quality, experience, morale of stayers), (A1) for a judgement that either proposes a cheaper targeted alternative or explains why the excluded costs could justify the rise despite the arithmetic. Full marks do not require the model answer's recommendation: a candidate who approves the rise on clearly stated service grounds, with the $£202{,}000$ acknowledged, can score the fourth mark.

Forfeits: comparing $£300{,}000$ with $£352{,}000$ and calling the difference the cost, which is the error the item is built to catch; assuming the whole $£300{,}000$ disappears if turnover halves; treating the pay rise as a one-off cost. Condonation: $140$ leavers instead of $141$ loses the second mark in (d) but not the first or third, since the comparison with $120$ is unaffected; the net figure may be given as $£202{,}000$ or $£200{,}000$ if the exact value is shown once.

Why this question is hard

The trap in this item is a comparison that looks natural and is wrong. Presented with a recruitment bill of $£300{,}000$ and a proposed pay rise of $£352{,}000$, almost everyone's first move is to set the two side by side, and the two are so close that the pay rise appears to be very nearly self-financing. The manager's case then seems to rest on the small gap, and the retention benefit is assumed to close it.

The comparison is invalid because the $£300{,}000$ is not what the pay rise saves. It is what the call centre is already spending, and it would still be spending a good part of it if the rise were approved, because halving turnover does not abolish recruitment; it halves it. What the proposal actually buys is the difference between two recruitment bills, and that difference is $£150{,}000$ against a salary increase of $£352{,}000$. This is the total-versus-marginal confusion in its most common business form, and it is worth more than the arithmetic that exposes it.

Part (d) then closes the escape that a candidate who has understood part (c) might still look for. If the pay rise falls short by $£202{,}000$, the obvious next thought is that a bigger fall in turnover would close the gap. It would not. The call centre has $120$ leavers a year; preventing every single one of them, which means a turnover rate of zero, saves $£300{,}000$, and the rise costs $£352{,}000$. The target of $141$ leavers prevented simply does not exist in a workforce that loses $120$ people. The proposal fails structurally, not marginally, and the difference between those two verdicts is what the part is for.

The evaluation mark sits on top of that. There is a real argument for paying staff more -- turnover damages service quality and drains experience in ways the $£2{,}500$ figure does not price -- and a good answer may make it. But it must make it honestly, as a service investment whose benefits are outside the arithmetic, rather than dressing it up as a saving the numbers refute. The stimulus is deliberately explicit that the directors asked only about recruitment and salary, so a candidate who invents other savings to rescue the proposal is answering a question that was not asked.

Challenge lever
The two headline figures are within six per cent of one another -- three hundred thousand pounds of recruitment and three hundred and fifty-two thousand pounds of salary -- and put side by side they invite the conclusion that the pay rise very nearly pays for itself. It does not. The three hundred thousand pounds is already being spent and does not change unless turnover changes; what the proposal buys is the sixty leavers it prevents, worth one hundred and fifty thousand. The numbers are also fixed so that no achievable fall in turnover can cover the increase: even a turnover rate of zero would save only three hundred thousand.
