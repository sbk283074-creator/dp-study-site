4.10 The regression line of x on y against the rearranged regression line of y on x: two different predictions because the two lines minimise residuals in different directions

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-4.10-301

Topic 4: Statistics and probabilitySL 4.10 (equation of the regression line of x on y) with SL 4.4 (linear correlation of bivariate data)P2 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedCalculateCalculateCalculateShow that

Question

A teacher records, for each of $40$ students, the number of hours $x$ spent revising for an examination and the mark $y$ obtained in it, expressed as a percentage. The following summary statistics were obtained from the data:
$$\bar{x} = 12, \quad \bar{y} = 62, \quad S_{xx} = 480, \quad S_{yy} = 3000, \quad S_{xy} = 960,$$
where $S_{xx} = \sum (x_{i} - \bar{x})^{2}$, $S_{yy} = \sum (y_{i} - \bar{y})^{2}$ and $S_{xy} = \sum (x_{i} - \bar{x})(y_{i} - \bar{y})$. A scatter diagram shows the relationship to be approximately linear, and both variables may be treated as random variables rather than as fixed values chosen by the experimenter.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Calculate the equation of the regression line of $y$ on $x$, giving coefficients to two decimal places where they are not exact.

    (Calculate)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Calculate the product-moment correlation coefficient for these data, and interpret its value in the context of the investigation.

    (Calculate)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Calculate the equation of the regression line of $x$ on $y$, giving coefficients to two decimal places where they are not exact.

    (Calculate)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    A student obtained a mark of $70$. Show that the mean mark was exceeded, and determine the revision time predicted by the regression line of $x$ on $y$. Show also that inverting the line found in part (a) would give a different prediction, and state by how much the two predictions differ.

    (Show that)
  5. [3 marks](e)

    Explain, in terms of the quantities being minimised, why the regression line of $x$ on $y$ cannot be obtained by rearranging the regression line of $y$ on $x$, and why the two lines would nevertheless be identical if the correlation coefficient were exactly $-1$ or $+1$.

    (Explain)

Reveal the answer

(a) The slope of the regression line of $y$ on $x$ is
$$b_{yx} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{960}{480} = 2 \;(M1)(A1),$$
and the line passes through $(\bar{x},\,\bar{y}) = (12,\,62)$, so
$$y - 62 = 2(x - 12) \quad\text{that is}\quad y = 2x + 38 \;(A1).$$
(b) The product-moment correlation coefficient is
$$r = \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}} = \frac{960}{\sqrt{480 \times 3000}} = \frac{960}{\sqrt{1\,440\,000}} = \frac{960}{1200} = 0.80 \;(M1)(A1).$$
This is positive, so higher revision times are associated with higher marks, and at $0.80$ it indicates a strong but by no means perfect linear association: roughly $64\%$ of the variation in marks is accounted for by variation in revision time $\bigl(r^{2} = 0.64\bigr)$ (A1).
(c) The slope of the regression line of $x$ on $y$ is
$$b_{xy} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{yy}} = \frac{960}{3000} = 0.32 \;(M1)(A1),$$
and the line again passes through $(12,\,62)$, so
$$x - 12 = 0.32(y - 62) \quad\text{that is}\quad x = 0.32y - 7.84 \;(A1).$$
(Check: at $y = 62$ this gives $x = 19.84 - 7.84 = 12$, as it must.)
(d) The mean mark is $62$ and $70 > 62$, so this student exceeded the mean (A1). Using the line of $x$ on $y$ from part (c), the predicted revision time is
$$x = 0.32(70) - 7.84 = 22.40 - 7.84 = 14.56 \;\text{hours} \;(M1)(A1).$$
Inverting the line $y = 2x + 38$ instead gives
$$x = \frac{y - 38}{2} = \frac{70 - 38}{2} = \frac{32}{2} = 16.00 \;\text{hours} \;(A1),$$
which exceeds the correct prediction by $16.00 - 14.56 = 1.44$ hours, an overstatement of almost $10\%$ of the value being predicted (A1). Note that both predictions lie inside the observed range of the data, so the difference is not an artefact of extrapolation.
(e) The regression line of $y$ on $x$ is the line $y = a + bx$ that minimises $\sum \bigl(y_{i} - a - bx_{i}\bigr)^{2}$, the sum of squared *vertical* residuals, because it is $y$ that is to be predicted and the error that matters is the error in the predicted $y$ (M1). The regression line of $x$ on $y$ minimises $\sum \bigl(x_{i} - c - dy_{i}\bigr)^{2}$, the sum of squared *horizontal* residuals (A1). Writing one line as $x = (y - a)/b$ evaluates it backwards along a least-squares fit that was never optimised in that direction, so it does not minimise anything meaningful and systematically over-predicts how far from the mean explanatory variable corresponds to a value far from the mean response -- which is why the error grows as the value of $y$ moves away from $\bar{y}$ (A1). Formally, the product of the two slopes is
$$b_{yx}\,b_{xy} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} \cdot \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{yy}} = \frac{S_{xy}^{2}}{S_{xx}S_{yy}} = r^{2} \;(M1),$$
and here $2 \times 0.32 = 0.64 = 0.80^{2}$, verifying the identity (A1). The two lines both pass through the mean point, so they coincide exactly when their slopes are reciprocals, that is when $r^{2} = 1$, hence when $r = \pm 1$ (A1). Only when every point lies on a single straight line do the two residual directions amount to the same thing.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for correctly identifying $S_{xy}/S_{xx}$ as the slope, (A1) for $b_{yx} = 2$, (A1) for $y = 2x + 38$. Accept the intercept as $38.00$. Condoned: omitting the units on the coefficients. Do not accept a line obtained from $S_{yy}/S_{xy}$, which inverts the roles of the two variables; that mistake is followed through into (c) but forfeits the accuracy mark there as well.
(b) (M1) for the correct formula, (A1) for $r = 0.80$, (A1) for an interpretation that says more than 'they are correlated': it must note the positive direction, or the strength, or quote $r^{2} = 0.64$ as the proportion of variation explained. 'Strong positive correlation' alone earns this third mark only if both adjectives are justified by the numerical value given before it. A candidate who writes $r = 0.8$ without working scores (A1) only. Note that the sign matters: a negative value here is a computational slip, not a valid alternative reading.
(c) (M1) for $S_{xy}/S_{yy}$, (A1) for $0.32$, (A1) for $x = 0.32y - 7.84$. The check that the line passes through $(12,\,62)$ is not required but may be credited in place of the final mark if the intercept is otherwise unaccounted for.
(d) (A1) for noting $70 > 62$, (M1) for substituting into the $x$-on-$y$ line, (A1) for $14.56$ hours, (A1) for the inverted-line value $16.00$ hours, (A1) for the difference $1.44$ hours. Follow-through applies from a wrong line in (a) or (c): a candidate whose two lines are both correct in form but whose arithmetic slips keeps every subsequent mark provided the difference is computed consistently with the two lines they hold. Forfeits: predicting with the $y$-on-$x$ line and treating the answer as a mark rather than a revision time, which is a unit error and loses the final three marks; and quoting only one of the two predictions, which loses the marks for the comparison.
(e) (M1) for naming what the $y$-on-$x$ line minimises, (A1) for naming what the $x$-on-$y$ line minimises, (A1) for the consequence that inverting does not recover the second line and that the error grows away from the mean, (M1) for forming the product $b_{yx}b_{xy}$ from the two slope formulae, (A1) for showing it equals $r^{2}$ here, (A1) for concluding that coincidence requires $r = \pm 1$. The algebraic identity $b_{yx}b_{xy} = r^{2}$ is the cleanest route but any argument reaching $r^{2} = 1$ earns the last two marks; in particular a candidate may argue geometrically that identical lines would have to make both sets of residuals zero, which forces every point onto one line.

Why this question is hard

Every student leaves this topic knowing one regression line. Part (a) asks for exactly that line, part (c) asks for the other one, and the hard part is noticing that 'the other one' is a real thing rather than an algebraic rearrangement of the first. Nothing in the part-(c) wording says 'this is different from part (a)', so the candidate has to bring the distinction themselves.

The mechanism is the direction of the residuals, and it is worth being concrete about why it bites. Fitting $y$ on $x$ chooses the line that makes the predicted marks as close as possible to the actual marks; it has no brief whatever to make predicted revision times close to actual revision times. Those are two different quantities to minimise, and the line that minimises one generally will not minimise the other. Inverting the first line and reading off a revision time takes a least-squares answer and applies it to minimise nothing at all.

The numbers make the size of the error visible, which is why part (d) asks for the difference rather than merely for the correct value. A score of $70$ is eight marks above the mean of $62$, and with $r = 0.80$ the honest prediction regresses most of the way towards the mean revision time: $14.56$ hours against a mean of $12$. The inverted line, having been fitted without any horizontal penalty, refuses to regress and gives $16.00$ hours. The gap of $1.44$ hours is not rounding; it is the difference between a line that knows the scatter is imperfect and one that was never told.

The identity $b_{yx} b_{xy} = r^{2}$ is the structural point, and it is worth carrying away. It says the two slopes are reciprocals exactly when the correlation is perfect, and that whenever $|r| < 1$ the two lines are genuinely two lines that intersect at the mean point at an angle. It also quantifies the asymmetry directly: the two lines differ by a factor of $r^{2}$, so at $r = 0.80$ they differ by thirty-six per cent in slope, and at $r = 0.30$ they differ by more than ninety per cent. A student who computes it once will not invert a regression line again.

Finally, part (d) plants a false escape and closes it. It is tempting to attribute any discrepancy to extrapolating beyond the data, but $70$ is well inside the range of marks and $14.56$ is well inside the range of revision times. Discrepancy of this kind is structural, not a warning about going outside the observed range, and the two sources of error must not be confused.

Challenge lever
There are two regression lines through the same mean point and they are not the same line. The one fitted from part (a) minimises the squares of the vertical distances to the data points, and the one needed to predict the study time minimises the squares of the horizontal distances. Rearranging the first line and using it to go backwards appears to answer the question, produces a number, and is systematically biased: the further the score sits above the mean of sixty-two, the further the inverted line overshoots. The candidate has to notice that the direction of prediction was fixed by the direction of the residuals, which is a decision already made in part (a) and never revisited afterwards.
