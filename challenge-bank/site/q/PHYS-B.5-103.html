B.5 Systematic error from meter resistance, and why two wrong readings bracket the truth

Physics HL · PHYS-B.5-103

B.5 Current and circuits (practical circuits; ideal and non-ideal ammeters and voltmeters)P2 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedShow thatDetermineExplain

Question

A student determines an unknown resistance $X$ using a voltmeter of resistance
$R_{V} = 4.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ and an ammeter of resistance $R_{A} = 2.0\ \Omega$, in the two
circuits described below.

Circuit 1. The voltmeter is connected directly across $X$. The ammeter is outside the
voltmeter, so it measures the current through $X$ and the current through the voltmeter
combined. The ratio of the voltmeter reading to the ammeter reading is $R_{1} = 97.6\ \Omega$.

Circuit 2. The ammeter is in series with $X$ and the voltmeter is connected across the
combination. The ratio of the voltmeter reading to the ammeter reading is
$R_{2} = 102\ \Omega$.

(a) Show that in circuit 1 the ratio of the readings is

$$R_{1} = \frac{X R_{V}}{X + R_{V}}. \tag{3}$$

(b) Show that in circuit 2 the ratio of the readings is

$$R_{2} = X + R_{A}. \tag{3}$$

(c) Hence determine the true value of $X$, and state the systematic error, in ohms, of each
circuit. \tag{4}

(d) Determine the value of $X$ for which the two circuits have systematic errors of equal
magnitude. \tag{4}

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that in circuit 1 the ratio of the readings is $R_{1} = \dfrac{X R_{V}}{X + R_{V}}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Show that in circuit 2 the ratio of the readings is $R_{2} = X + R_{A}$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Hence determine the true value of $X$, and state the systematic error, in ohms, of each circuit.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the value of $X$ for which the two circuits have systematic errors of equal magnitude.

    (Determine)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) In circuit 1 the voltmeter and $X$ are in parallel, so the voltage $V$ across them is
the same and the currents add (M1). The ammeter reads

$$I = I_{X} + I_{V} = \frac{V}{X} + \frac{V}{R_{V}} = V\left(\frac{1}{X} + \frac{1}{R_{V}}\right)
\tag{A1}$$

so the ratio of the readings is

$$R_{1} = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{1}{\dfrac{1}{X} + \dfrac{1}{R_{V}}} = \frac{X R_{V}}{X + R_{V}}
\tag{AG}$$

which is the parallel combination of $X$ and $R_{V}$. It is therefore always less than $X$.

(b) In circuit 2 the ammeter is in series with $X$, so it reads the true current $I$ through
$X$ (M1), while the voltmeter is across both and reads

$$V = I X + I R_{A} = I(X + R_{A}) \tag{A1}$$

so $R_{2} = \dfrac{V}{I} = X + R_{A}$ (AG). This reading is always greater than $X$, because
it includes the resistance of the ammeter itself.

(c) From (b), $X = R_{2} - R_{A} = 102 - 2.0 = 100\ \Omega$ (M1)(A1). Substituting back into
(a) as a check,

$$R_{1} = \frac{100 \times 4000}{100 + 4000} = \frac{400000}{4100} = 97.56\ \Omega \tag{A1}$$

which agrees with the reading of $97.6\ \Omega$ to the precision quoted. The systematic
errors are therefore

$$\text{circuit 1: } 97.56 - 100 = -2.44\ \Omega \qquad
\text{circuit 2: } 102 - 100 = +2.00\ \Omega \tag{A1}$$

so the two readings bracket the true value, the first low and the second high.

(d) The magnitude of the error in circuit 1 is

$$X - \frac{X R_{V}}{X + R_{V}} = \frac{X(X + R_{V}) - X R_{V}}{X + R_{V}} = \frac{X^{2}}{X + R_{V}}
\tag{M1}$$

and the magnitude of the error in circuit 2 is $R_{A}$ (A1). Setting them equal,

$$\frac{X^{2}}{X + R_{V}} = R_{A} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X^{2} - R_{A}X - R_{A}R_{V} = 0
\tag{M1}$$

$$X = \frac{R_{A} + \sqrt{R_{A}^{2} + 4R_{A}R_{V}}}{2}
= \frac{2.0 + \sqrt{4.0 + 4(2.0)(4000)}}{2} = \frac{2.0 + 178.9}{2} = 90.4\ \Omega \tag{A1}$$

The negative root is rejected as unphysical. Below about $90\ \Omega$ circuit 1 is the more
accurate of the two; above it, circuit 2 is. Note that the crossover is not where the two
readings are equal — it is where their *errors* are, and the two conditions are different.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for recognising the parallel arrangement and that the currents add. (A1) for
$I = V\left(\frac{1}{X} + \frac{1}{R_{V}}\right)$. (AG) for the result. Allow a candidate who
simply writes "$R_{1}$ is the parallel combination of $X$ and $R_{V}$" to earn (M1) and (AG)
but not the (A1), since the mark is for showing where the expression comes from.

(b) (M1) for noting that the ammeter now reads the true current through $X$. (A1) for
$V = I(X + R_{A})$. (AG) for the result.

(c) (M1) for using $X = R_{2} - R_{A}$ in preference to manipulating the expression in (a);
either route is acceptable but the (a) route requires solving a quadratic and is slower.
(A1) for $100\ \Omega$. (A1) for the numerical check against $97.56\ \Omega$ — this mark is
for confirming consistency and should not be given to a candidate who does not perform it.
(A1) for both errors with correct signs. Follow-through: a candidate who correctly obtains
$X$ from (b) but miscalculates the check loses only the check mark.

(d) (M1) for the error expression $\frac{X^{2}}{X + R_{V}}$. (A1) for identifying the error in
circuit 2 as $R_{A}$, independent of $X$. (M1) for forming and solving the quadratic. (A1) for
$90.4\ \Omega$; accept $90\ \Omega$.

Common error to penalise: equating the two *readings*, $\frac{XR_{V}}{X+R_{V}} = X + R_{A}$,
which gives a different and meaningless answer. This earns neither of the last two marks.
Condonation: allow the approximation $X \ll R_{V}$, giving the error as
$\frac{X^{2}}{R_{V}}$ and the crossover at $\sqrt{R_{A}R_{V}} = 89.4\ \Omega$, with full
marks, since the difference from the exact value is just over one per cent.

Why this question is hard

Both circuits are wrong, and that is the point. A voltmeter with finite resistance
necessarily draws some current, and an ammeter with non-zero resistance necessarily drops some
voltage, so the ratio $V/I$ is never quite the resistance of the device under test. The
question is only whether the error is large enough to matter and whether it can be removed.

The two circuits fail in opposite directions, which is what makes the pair useful. Circuit 1
measures a parallel combination and therefore reads low; circuit 2 includes the ammeter in
series and therefore reads high. A candidate who takes one reading and stops has no way of
knowing which way the error went, but a candidate who takes both has bracketed the true value
and can locate it exactly, because circuit 2's error does not depend on $X$ at all — it is
just the ammeter's resistance, given in the stem.

Part (d) is where the item stops being a correction exercise and becomes a design question.
The error in circuit 1 grows with $X$ (roughly as $\frac{X^{2}}{R_{V}}$ while $X$ is small
compared with $R_{V}$), and the error in circuit 2 is the constant $R_{A}$. So there is a
crossing point: for small resistances the voltmeter's shunting effect is negligible and
circuit 1 wins, and for large resistances the ammeter's $2\ \Omega$ is negligible and circuit
2 wins. Working out where the crossing is — about $90\ \Omega$ here — is the practical content
of choosing a circuit.

The mistake the item is really fishing for appears at the end of the markscheme. Candidates
naturally equate the two *readings*, because that is what "find where they agree" usually
means. That is the wrong condition: what has to be equal is the size of the *errors*, and
since one reading is below $X$ and the other above, equality of readings cannot happen at all
for a value of $X$ that both circuits can measure. Keeping the two conditions apart is a
discipline worth more than the algebra.

Metadata

Challenge lever
Neither circuit measures the resistance it claims to. The voltmeter draws current in the first and the ammeter's own resistance is included in the second, so one reading is systematically low and the other systematically high, and a candidate who trusts either alone reports a value that is wrong by about two per cent. The true value is recovered only by using both readings together, and the choice of which circuit to prefer depends on a comparison the candidate has to make rather than quote.

The source treats the two circuits separately in different questions and asks only for the direction of the error. Part (d) converts a qualitative discussion of which circuit is better into a quantitative crossover condition, and deliberately distinguishes equality of errors from equality of readings. Context, structure, given-versus-asked and the reasoning chain all change.
Verification
Both circuit expressions are evaluated at $X = 100\ \Omega$ and compared with the quoted readings; the crossover value is verified by substituting it back into the error expression for circuit 1 and checking that it returns $R_{A}$.
