Question

Marlowe Supplies is a single-product wholesaler. The figures above are taken from its final accounts for the year just ended. [3 marks] (Show that) (e) The business then buys $\pounds 30{,}000$ of inventory on credit. Show that the acid test ratio falls to 0.81, and evaluate whether the two transactions together leave Marlowe Supplies in a stronger or a weaker liquidity position than it began in. Reveal the answer

(a) Gross profit is revenue less cost of goods sold, so (M1)

$$\pounds 480{,}000 - \pounds 300{,}000 = \pounds 180{,}000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{180{,}000}{480{,}000} = 37.5\% \tag{A1}$$

Operating profit deducts the operating expenses: $\pounds 180{,}000 - \pounds 120{,}000 = \pounds 60{,}000$, so the operating profit margin is $60{,}000/480{,}000 = 12.5\%$ (A1).

Net profit also deducts interest and tax: $\pounds 60{,}000 - \pounds 12{,}000 - \pounds 9{,}600 = \pounds 38{,}400$, so the net profit margin is $38{,}400/480{,}000 = 8.0\%$ (A1).

The three margins fall by design: each one deducts a further layer of cost, so the sequence $37.5\%$, $12.5\%$, $8.0\%$ is a description of where the revenue goes. The largest single deduction is the $\pounds 120{,}000$ of operating expenses, which takes 25 percentage points of revenue and is the reason the gross margin is three times the operating margin.

(b) Capital employed is the long-term money in the business, which here is equity plus non-current liabilities (M1):

$$\pounds 200{,}000 + \pounds 100{,}000 = \pounds 300{,}000$$

Return on capital employed is operating profit divided by capital employed (M1):

$$\frac{60{,}000}{300{,}000} = 20.0\% \tag{A1}$$

The numerator must be operating profit, not net profit. Using net profit would give $38{,}400/300{,}000 = 12.8\%$, which understates the return on the assets because it charges the capital employed with interest that has already been deducted in arriving at net profit. Using equity alone as the denominator would give $60{,}000/200{,}000 = 30.0\%$, which overstates it by leaving the non-current liabilities out of the capital they helped to fund.

(c) Current assets are inventory, receivables and cash (M1):

$$\pounds 45{,}000 + \pounds 60{,}000 + \pounds 15{,}000 = \pounds 120{,}000$$

The current ratio is current assets over current liabilities (A1):

$$\frac{120{,}000}{60{,}000} = 2.00$$

The acid test removes inventory from the numerator, because inventory is the current asset least likely to convert to cash at its book value (A1):

$$\frac{120{,}000 - 45{,}000}{60{,}000} = \frac{75{,}000}{60{,}000} = 1.25$$

(d) Paying $\pounds 10{,}000$ in cash reduces cash to $\pounds 5{,}000$, so current assets become $\pounds 110{,}000$ and current liabilities become $\pounds 50{,}000$ (M1).

$$\text{current ratio} = \frac{110{,}000}{50{,}000} = 2.20 \qquad \text{acid test} = \frac{110{,}000 - 45{,}000}{50{,}000} = \frac{65{,}000}{50{,}000} = 1.30 \tag{A1}$$

Both ratios rise, from 2.00 to 2.20 and from 1.25 to 1.30, even though the business is now smaller by $\pounds 10{,}000$ of cash (A1).

The reason is that the same amount leaves the top and the bottom of the fraction. Writing the current ratio as $a/b$ with $a > b$, the new ratio is $(a-x)/(b-x)$, and

$$\frac{a-x}{b-x} > \frac{a}{b} \iff b(a-x) > a(b-x) \iff ab - bx > ab - ax \iff a > b$$

so the ratio rises whenever the transaction removes equal amounts from both and the ratio was above one. The size of the payment does not matter, and neither does the fact that it is a payment: any transaction that reduces current assets and current liabilities by the same amount has this effect. The same algebra run in reverse shows that adding equal amounts to both *lowers* a ratio above one, which is what part (e) does.

(e) Buying $\pounds 30{,}000$ of inventory on credit raises inventory to $\pounds 75{,}000$ and current liabilities to $\pounds 80{,}000$, so current assets become $\pounds 140{,}000$ (M1).

$$\text{current ratio} = \frac{140{,}000}{80{,}000} = 1.75 \tag{A1}$$

The acid test numerator is unchanged, because the $\pounds 30{,}000$ added to inventory is subtracted again (M1):

$$\frac{140{,}000 - 75{,}000}{80{,}000} = \frac{65{,}000}{80{,}000} = 0.8125 \approx 0.81 \tag{A1}$$

The two transactions together have moved the current ratio from 2.00 to 1.75 and the acid test from 1.25 to 0.81, so on balance the liquidity position is weaker, not stronger (A1).

The acid test is the ratio that should be read here, and the current ratio is the one that flatters. At 1.75 the current ratio still looks comfortable, and it has fallen by less than the acid test because inventory is doing the work: inventory is now $\pounds 75{,}000$ of $\pounds 140{,}000$ of current assets, so more than half of what the business counts as liquid is stock that has to be sold before it becomes cash. The acid test has crossed below 1.00, which means the business cannot settle its current liabilities from the assets it already holds in near-cash form. It would have to sell inventory, and selling inventory is what generates the receivables and cash in the first place, so the position is circular rather than merely tight.

The evaluation is not one-sided, however. The $\pounds 30{,}000$ of inventory was bought to be sold, and if it turns over as the existing stock does it will convert into receivables and then cash within the working capital cycle, restoring the acid test without any new financing. The $\pounds 10{,}000$ paid to the supplier also released credit and may have earned a settlement discount. So the transactions weaken the *reported* liquidity position while leaving the *underlying* trading position intact, and the honest conclusion is that the ratios have moved in a way the business should monitor rather than a way that shows it is in difficulty. What the figures cannot settle is how quickly the new inventory will turn over, and that is the question a lender would ask next.

Markscheme notes

(a) One mark each for 37.5%, 12.5% and 8.0%, with the working shown for at least one. Award the marks for the three percentages even if the candidate does not comment on the pattern; the comment is offered in the answer and is not required.

(b) One mark for capital employed of 300,000, one for the ROCE of 20.0%, and one for using operating profit rather than net profit in the numerator. A candidate who uses net profit and obtains 12.8% should be awarded the first mark only, and the same applies to a candidate who uses equity alone and obtains 30.0%, because in both cases the denominator or the numerator has been chosen wrongly rather than miscalculated.

(c) One mark for current assets of 120,000, one for 2.00, one for 1.25 with inventory removed from the numerator. A candidate who divides inventory by current liabilities, or who includes inventory in the acid test, scores the first mark only.

(d) One mark for the revised figures, one for both ratios and the direction of change, one for the explanation that equal amounts leave the top and bottom of a fraction above one. The explanation mark requires the condition that the ratio exceeds one; a candidate who says "paying a debt always improves liquidity" has stated a rule that is false for a ratio below one and earns no marks for the explanation.

(e) One mark for the revised current assets and liabilities, one for 1.75, one for the observation that the acid test numerator is unchanged, one for 0.81. The evaluation mark is awarded for a conclusion that distinguishes the reported ratio movement from the underlying trading position; a bare "liquidity is worse because the acid test fell" scores 0 for the evaluation, because it does not engage with the fact that the inventory was bought to be sold.

Forfeits: treating the acid test numerator as unchanged in part (d), which happens if the candidate applies the cancellation rule to the wrong transaction and obtains 1.25 again; reporting the acid test as 0.83 by adding the inventory to current assets without adjusting the denominator. Condonation: a candidate who writes the ratios as fractions rather than decimals, or who rounds 0.8125 to 0.81 or 0.813, should be given full credit.

Why this question is hard

Ratio analysis is taught as a set of formulas and marked as a set of answers, and the formulas are reliable enough that a student can score well on them without ever forming an opinion about what a ratio is. This item is aimed at the gap between using a ratio and reasoning with one. Parts (a) to (c) are the routine application, and they are included so that the item has a floor: a student who can do the arithmetic has something to show for the work. Parts (d) and (e) ask a different kind of question, and they cannot be answered by substituting into a formula at all.

The transaction in part (d) is counter-intuitive in a way that catches almost everyone the first time. A business pays its debts and its liquidity ratios improve; a business that pays its debts is poorer in cash and its ratios improve. Both statements are true, and the reason is that a ratio is a comparison rather than a measurement, so changing the numbers on both sides of it changes the comparison itself. The condition that decides the direction is whether the ratio was above one, and it is a property of the ratio and not of the transaction. Once a student has seen the algebra, the result stops being paradoxical, and the size of the payment stops mattering at all -- a theme the item makes explicit, because the instinct is to explain the movement by saying that ten thousand pounds is small.

Part (e) then turns the same machinery through ninety degrees. Buying inventory on credit adds equal amounts to current assets and current liabilities, so the equal-amounts rule applies and the current ratio falls. The acid test is the interesting case, because it subtracts inventory from the numerator, and the addition and the subtraction cancel exactly: the numerator is untouched while the denominator rises, so the ratio must fall and it falls below one. A student who has only memorised the equal-amounts rule will get the current ratio right and the acid test wrong, and the two answers will disagree with each other in a way that is visible on the page.

The final evaluation is deliberately open. The ratios have weakened and the conclusion that the position is weaker is defensible; so is the conclusion that the position is unchanged, because the inventory was bought to be sold and the acid test will recover as it turns over. What the item will not accept is a conclusion that ignores the reason the inventory is there. This is the standard AO3 demand in Business Management -- use the figures to reach a judgement, and recognise the limit of what the figures can show -- and it is the part of the subject that a purely computational preparation leaves out.

Challenge lever
A ratio is not a quantity, and reasoning about it by reasoning about the numbers inside it gives the wrong direction of change. Whether a transaction that reduces both current assets and current liabilities raises or lowers the current ratio depends on whether the ratio was above or below one, which is a property of the ratio rather than of the transaction. The acid test adds a second trap: buying inventory on credit raises current assets and current liabilities by the same amount, so the equal-amounts rule applies to the current ratio, but the acid test subtracts inventory out of the numerator, so the addition and the subtraction cancel and the numerator does not move at all.
