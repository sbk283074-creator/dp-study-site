The condition for a real cubic to have a purely imaginary root

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.14-001

AHL 1.14 (complex conjugate roots of quadratic and polynomial equations with real coefficients), AHL 1.12 (the number i, where i^2 = -1)P2 · Section B16 marksdifficulty 4technology: requiredExplainShow thatHenceDetermine

Question

Let $P(z)=z^{3}+pz^{2}+qz+r$, where $p$, $q$ and $r$ are real numbers.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Explain why, if $z_{0}$ is a non-real root of $P$, then $\bar z_{0}$ is also a root of $P$, and deduce that $P$ has exactly one real root.

    (Explain)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Suppose $P$ has a root of the form $z_{0}=ti$, where $t$ is a non-zero real number. Show that $r=pq$ and that $q>0$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Hence determine the value of $k$ for which $z^{3}-3z^{2}+kz-12$ has a root of the form $ti$ with $t\neq0$, and find all three roots in that case.

    (Hence)
  4. [5 marks](d)

    Determine all real values of $m$ for which $z^{3}+mz^{2}+mz+1$ has a non-real purely imaginary root, and find all the roots of the cubic in each such case.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Since $p$, $q$ and $r$ are real, conjugating the equation $P(z_{0})=0$ gives $\overline{P(z_{0})}=0$. Conjugation respects sums and products with real coefficients, so $\overline{P(z_{0})}=P(\bar z_{0})$. (M1) Hence $P(\bar z_{0})=0$, so $\bar z_{0}$ is also a root. (A1)

If $z_{0}$ is non-real then $z_{0}\neq\bar z_{0}$, so $P$ has at least the two distinct non-real roots $z_{0}$ and $\bar z_{0}$. A cubic has exactly three roots counted with multiplicity; if the third root were also non-real, its conjugate would be a fourth root. Hence the third root is real, so $P$ has exactly one real root. (R1)(A1)

(b) Let $z_{0}=ti$ with $t\in\mathbb{R}$ and $t\neq0$. Substituting,

$$P(ti)=(ti)^{3}+p(ti)^{2}+q(ti)+r=-t^{3}i-pt^{2}+qti+r.$$ (M1)

So $P(ti)=\left(r-pt^{2}\right)+\left(qt-t^{3}\right)i=0$, and both the real and imaginary parts must vanish. (A1)

The imaginary part gives $t(q-t^{2})=0$; since $t\neq0$ this forces $t^{2}=q$, and therefore $q=t^{2}>0$. (A1) The real part gives $r-pt^{2}=0$, so $r=pt^{2}=pq$. (A1) *(AG)*

(c) Here $p=-3$, $q=k$ and $r=-12$. The condition $r=pq$ becomes $-12=-3k$, so $k=4$, and $q=k=4>0$ is satisfied. (M1)(A1) Then $t^{2}=q=4$, so the purely imaginary roots are $\pm2i$. The third root is real and equals $-p=3$, since the sum of the roots of $z^{3}+pz^{2}+qz+r$ is $-p$. (M1) Indeed $(z-3)(z^{2}+4)=z^{3}-3z^{2}+4z-12$, so the roots are $3$, $2i$ and $-2i$. (A1)

(d) For $z^{3}+mz^{2}+mz+1$ we have $p=m$, $q=m$ and $r=1$. The necessary condition $r=pq$ gives $1=m^{2}$, so $m=\pm1$. (M1)(A1) But the condition from (b) also requires $q>0$, that is $m>0$, so $m=-1$ must be rejected. (M1)

Indeed for $m=-1$ the cubic is $z^{3}-z^{2}-z+1=(z-1)^{2}(z+1)$, whose roots $1$, $1$ and $-1$ are all real, so it has no purely imaginary root. (A1) Hence the only admissible value is $m=1$, and then $z^{3}+z^{2}+z+1=(z+1)(z^{2}+1)$, with roots $-1$, $i$ and $-i$. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) The (M1) is for the conjugation argument; the (R1) is for the counting argument that a cubic cannot have exactly one non-real root. A candidate who asserts 'non-real roots come in pairs' without mentioning multiplicity still earns the (R1), provided the cubic is named. Do not accept 'it must have one real root because the graph crosses the axis', which assumes what is being proved.

(b) The substitution must be shown with the real and imaginary parts separated; a candidate who writes down $r=pq$ with no working earns nothing. The hypothesis $t\neq0$ is used to divide, and its use should be visible. A candidate who also considers $t=0$ and notes that then $z=0$ is real rather than purely imaginary should be credited.

(c) Award (M1) for substituting into $r=pq$ and (A1) for $k=4$; the check $q>0$ is required for the second (A1). The third root may be found by division, by Vieta, or by the factorisation; accept any. A candidate who mis-signs Vieta and writes the sum of the roots as $p$ will get $3$ anyway here, so look for the reasoning rather than the value.

(d) This is the discriminating part. Both $m=\pm1$ satisfy $r=pq$, but only $m=1$ has $q=m>0$. A candidate who answers $m=\pm1$ loses the final three marks. Award (M1)(A1) for the two candidates, (M1) for invoking $q>0$, (A1) for exhibiting the real-root factorisation of the rejected case, and (A1) for the roots in the valid case. Follow-through is allowed only within (d).

Why this question is hard

The conjugate-root theorem is usually met as a fact to quote. Here it is used three times in different ways, and the last use separates candidates who understand it from those who have memorised it.

Part (a) is the theorem itself, with a small but real subtlety: the deduction that there is exactly one real root uses the fact that a cubic has three roots counted with multiplicity, so a non-real root consumes its conjugate as a second root and leaves precisely one. Students who argue from the graph, or who say 'the other roots are complex' without counting, miss the point.

Part (b) is the technical heart. Substituting $z=ti$ splits the cubic into a real part and an imaginary part, and the imaginary part factors as $t(q-t^{2})$. Because the root is assumed non-zero, $t\neq0$, and this forces $q=t^{2}>0$. The real part then forces $r=pt^{2}=pq$. So the single algebraic condition $r=pq$, together with the inequality $q>0$, is necessary for a purely imaginary root.

Part (c) applies the condition to a concrete cubic and produces the clean factorisation $(z-3)(z^{2}+4)$.

Part (d) is the lever. The condition $r=pq$ alone admits two values, $m=\pm1$, because $r=pq$ becomes $1=m^{2}$. The inequality $q>0$ is not decoration: it is what removes $m=-1$. And the reason is visible when the rejected case is factored, since $z^{3}-z^{2}-z+1$ has roots $1,1,-1$, all real, so it has no purely imaginary root at all. A candidate who treats $r=pq$ as sufficient will confidently answer $m=\pm1$ and be wrong.

The classic wrong turns are: forgetting that a non-real root is a pair; dividing by $t$ without recording that $t\neq0$; using only $r=pq$ and ignoring $q>0$ in (d); and mis-signing Vieta, since the sum of the roots of $z^{3}+pz^{2}+qz+r$ is $-p$, not $p$. A useful check is that $(z+p)(z^{2}+q)=z^{3}+pz^{2}+qz+pq$ reproduces the cubic exactly, which is why the condition is both necessary and sufficient.
