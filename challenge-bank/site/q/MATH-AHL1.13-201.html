1.13 The sum of 1/(1 - w^k) over the non-trivial n-th roots of unity: every term has real part exactly one half, the terms pair into conjugates that each sum to one, and the imaginary parts cancel in pairs

Topic 1: Number and algebraAHL 1.13 (modulus-argument form, conjugate roots and the geometry of the complex plane) with AHL 1.12 (roots of unity and de Moivre's theorem)P315 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatShow thatDetermineDetermine

Question

Let $n \ge 2$ be an integer and let
$$w = e^{2\pi i/n} = \cos\frac{2\pi}{n} + i\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}$$
be a primitive $n$-th root of unity, so that the $n$ complex numbers $1, w, w^{2}, \ldots, w^{n-1}$ are the vertices of a regular $n$-gon inscribed in the unit circle. The figure shows the case $n = 8$, with each root joined by a dashed chord to the root obtained by reflecting it in the real axis. Define
$$T_{n} = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{1 - w^{k}}.$$
No numerical approximation of any term is required anywhere in this question; every answer is to be given exactly.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that for every $k$ the real part of $\dfrac{1}{1 - w^{k}}$ is exactly $\dfrac{1}{2}$, and state the imaginary part in terms of a cotangent.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Show that for any complex number $z$ of modulus $1$ with $z \ne 1$, $\dfrac{1}{1 - z} + \dfrac{1}{1 - \bar{z}} = 1$, and use this to evaluate the contribution of each conjugate pair to $T_{n}$.

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Determine $T_{n}$ for odd $n$ and for even $n$, stating which term does not pair with a different term in the even case and what it contributes.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine the value of $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\cot\frac{k\pi}{n}$ and explain why the imaginary parts of the terms of $T_{n}$ cancel in pairs.

    (Determine)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Put $w^{k} = e^{i\theta}$ with $\theta = 2\pi k/n$. Then
$$\frac{1}{1 - e^{i\theta}} = \frac{1 - e^{-i\theta}}{(1 - e^{i\theta})(1 - e^{-i\theta})} = \frac{1 - \cos\theta + i\sin\theta}{2 - 2\cos\theta} \;(M1).$$
The denominator used is the squared modulus $|1 - e^{i\theta}|^{2} = 2 - 2\cos\theta$, which is real and non-zero for $1 \le k \le n-1$ (A1). The real part is therefore
$$\frac{1 - \cos\theta}{2 - 2\cos\theta} = \frac{1}{2},$$
because $2 - 2\cos\theta = 2(1 - \cos\theta)$ and the numerator is exactly half of it (A1). This is independent of $k$ and of $n$: every single term of $T_{n}$ has real part $\tfrac{1}{2}$. The imaginary part is $\dfrac{\sin\theta}{2 - 2\cos\theta} = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{4\sin^{2}(\theta/2)} = \dfrac{2\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2)}{4\sin^{2}(\theta/2)} = \dfrac{1}{2}\cot\dfrac{\theta}{2} = \dfrac{1}{2}\cot\dfrac{\pi k}{n}$.
(b) Let $z$ be of modulus $1$ and $z \ne 1$. Since $\bar{z} = 1/z$ we have $1 - \bar{z} = 1 - 1/z = (z - 1)/z$, so
$$\frac{1}{1 - \bar{z}} = \frac{z}{z - 1} = -\frac{z}{1 - z} \;(M1).$$
Therefore
$$\frac{1}{1 - z} + \frac{1}{1 - \bar{z}} = \frac{1}{1 - z} - \frac{z}{1 - z} = \frac{1 - z}{1 - z} = 1 \;(A1),$$
which is exactly one for every such $z$ and does not depend on $z$ at all (A1). Now the map $k \mapsto n - k$ pairs the integers $1, \ldots, n-1$, and $w^{n-k} = \overline{w^{k}}$ (A1), so each pair $\{k, n-k\}$ contributes exactly $1$ to $T_{n}$.
(c) If $n$ is odd then $n - 1$ is even and the pairing is complete, with $(n-1)/2$ pairs and no term left over, so
$$T_{n} = \frac{n-1}{2} \quad (n \text{ odd}) \;(A1).$$
If $n$ is even, the index $k = n/2$ is paired with itself, because $n - n/2 = n/2$ (M1). At that index $w^{k} = w^{n/2} = e^{i\pi} = -1$, and the term is
$$\frac{1}{1 - (-1)} = \frac{1}{2},$$
which is exactly the real part every term has, so nothing is lost (A1). The remaining $n - 2$ indices form $(n-2)/2$ pairs contributing $1$ each, so
$$T_{n} = \frac{n-2}{2}\cdot 1 + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{n-1}{2} \quad (n \text{ even}) \;(A1).$$
Both cases therefore give $T_{n} = \dfrac{n-1}{2}$.
(d) From part (a), $\operatorname{Im}\dfrac{1}{1 - w^{k}} = \dfrac{1}{2}\cot\dfrac{\pi k}{n}$, and from part (b) the imaginary parts of a conjugate pair cancel, since $\overline{w^{k}} = w^{n-k}$ implies $\operatorname{Im}(1/(1 - w^{n-k})) = -\operatorname{Im}(1/(1 - w^{k}))$ (M1). Reading that cancellation in terms of the cotangents, $\cot\dfrac{\pi(n-k)}{n} = \cot\left(\pi - \dfrac{\pi k}{n}\right) = -\cot\dfrac{\pi k}{n}$, so every cotangent with $1 \le k < n/2$ is cancelled by its partner (A1). When $n$ is even the middle index $k = n/2$ gives $\cot(\pi/2) = 0$, so it contributes nothing (A1). Hence
$$\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\cot\frac{k\pi}{n} = 0 \quad\text{for every } n \ge 2 \;(A1).$$
For $n = 5$ the sum is $\cot(\pi/5) + \cot(2\pi/5) + \cot(3\pi/5) + \cot(4\pi/5)$, and since $\cot(3\pi/5) = -\cot(2\pi/5)$ and $\cot(4\pi/5) = -\cot(\pi/5)$ this is $0$ exactly. As a check, $T_{5} = 5 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + 0 \cdot i = \tfrac{5}{2}$, which is $(n-1)/2$ for $n = 5$.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for multiplying numerator and denominator by the conjugate $1 - e^{-i\theta}$, (A1) for identifying the denominator as $2 - 2\cos\theta$ and noting it is non-zero, (A1) for the value $1/2$. The cotangent form is not required for the marks but must be correct if offered: $\tfrac{1}{2}\cot(\pi k/n)$, not $\cot(\pi k/n)$ and not $\tfrac{1}{2}\cot(2\pi k/n)$.
(b) (M1) for using $\bar{z} = 1/z$ to rewrite the second denominator, (A1) for the algebraic cancellation giving $1$, (A1) for recognising that the value does not depend on $z$, (A1) for the statement that $k$ and $n-k$ index conjugate roots. A candidate who proves the identity for a specific $z$ and asserts it in general scores (M1)(A1) only. A candidate who argues geometrically -- that $1 - z$ and $1 - \bar{z}$ are mirror images, so the two reciprocal vectors have equal and opposite imaginary parts and real parts summing to one -- scores full marks, and this is the more illuminating route.
(c) (A1) for $T_{n} = (n-1)/2$ for odd $n$, (M1) for identifying the self-paired index $k = n/2$, (A1) for the value $1/2$ of that term, (A1) for $T_{n} = (n-1)/2$ for even $n$. A candidate who writes $(n-2)/2$ for even $n$ has forgotten the middle term and scores (M1) only in that branch; a candidate who writes $(n-1)/2$ but claims all terms pair into twos has the right answer by a wrong route and scores (A1) for the odd case, (A1) for the even value, and loses the (M1) for the middle term.
(d) (M1) for reading the imaginary parts off part (a), (A1) for the cotangent reflection identity $\cot(\pi - x) = -\cot x$ applied to the pairs, (A1) for noting that the middle cotangent is zero when $n$ is even, (A1) for the value $0$. Accept any correct numerical check such as the $n = 5$ case. A candidate who answers $\pi/2$ or who confuses this sum with $\sum \cot^{2}$ scores nothing in this part.
Follow-through: a candidate whose part (a) cotangent is out by a factor of two but who then argues the cancellation consistently keeps the method marks in (d). Forfeits: attempting to put the $n-1$ fractions over a common denominator; using $|1 - e^{i\theta}| = 1 - \cos\theta$ instead of the squared modulus; and treating $w^{k}$ as if its conjugate were $w^{-k}$ while also claiming $k$ and $n - k$ are different indices when $k = n/2$.

Why this question is hard

This is a problem-solving item in the strict sense: the answer is not reachable by computation, and the two parts before it exist to supply the two facts that make the answer obvious once they are in hand.

The first fact is that every term has the same real part. That is not a coincidence of the arithmetic; it follows from the geometry of the unit circle. The complex number $1 - e^{i\theta}$ is the vector from the point $e^{i\theta}$ on the unit circle to the point $1$, and for a point on the unit circle the perpendicular bisector of that chord passes through the midpoint of the arc. The reciprocal of a vector of modulus $2\sin(\theta/2)$ at angle $(\theta - \pi)/2$ therefore has real part $\cos((\pi - \theta)/2)/(2\sin(\theta/2)) = \sin(\theta/2)/(2\sin(\theta/2)) = \tfrac{1}{2}$. The algebra in part (a) is that geometry written out.

The second fact is the pairing identity, and it is the one that finishes the problem. There is no need to know what $z$ is: the identity holds for every $z$ on the unit circle other than $1$, and it says that a term and its conjugate partner sum to exactly one. So the sum is one per pair, and the only question left is how many pairs there are and whether anything is left over. For odd $n$ nothing is left over. For even $n$ the root $w^{n/2} = -1$ is its own partner, and a candidate who has been pairing terms mechanically will either drop it or count it as a whole pair. It contributes $1/(1+1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, which is precisely the common real part, so the total is unchanged. The answer $(n-1)/2$ is the same in both cases, and the reason it is the same is worth saying out loud: the self-paired term contributes exactly half of what a pair contributes.

Part (d) turns the same pairing into a statement about cotangents. The imaginary parts cancel in conjugate pairs, and the middle term has cotangent zero, so the cotangent sum vanishes for every $n$. It is a genuinely useful identity and it costs nothing once the pairing has been seen.

The figure is the whole argument in one picture. The eighth roots of unity sit on the circle, and the dashed chords join $w$ to $w^{7}$, $w^{2}$ to $w^{6}$ and $w^{3}$ to $w^{5}$. Each chord is a mirror pair about the real axis, and $w^{4} = -1$ sits on the axis with no partner other than itself.

Challenge lever
The sum looks like an exercise in adding algebraic fractions and it is not: there is no useful common denominator, and expanding each term with de Moivre produces expressions that grow rather than simplify. The way through is a pairing. The map k to n - k sends each root to its complex conjugate, and for any z on the unit circle with z not equal to one, the two terms 1/(1 - z) and 1/(1 - z-bar) add to exactly one, whatever z is. That identity is not obvious and cannot be reached by manipulating the fractions one at a time; it needs the observation that 1 - z and 1 - z-bar have the same modulus, so their reciprocals share a denominator. Once the pairing is seen the answer is immediate, and the imaginary parts cancel because each pair is a conjugate pair. When n is even the middle term z = -1 is its own partner and contributes exactly one half, which is the case a candidate who assumes all terms pair up will mishandle.
