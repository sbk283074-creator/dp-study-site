The product of the chords of a regular polygon

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-P3-011

Topic 1: Number and algebraAHL 1.13 (modulus-argument (polar) form); AHL 1.14 (complex conjugate roots of quadratic and polynomial equations)P316 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatShow thatShow thatDetermine

Question

Throughout, $n \ge 2$ is an integer and $\omega = e^{2\pi i/n}$, so that the numbers $1, \omega, \omega^{2}, \ldots, \omega^{n-1}$ are the vertices of a regular $n$-gon inscribed in the unit circle of the complex plane. This problem asks for the product of the distances from the vertex at $1$ to all the other vertices, and the answer turns out not to depend on any trigonometric detail at all.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Show that $1 + \omega + \omega^{2} + \cdots + \omega^{n-1} = 0$, and interpret this result geometrically as a statement about the vertices of the polygon.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    By factorising $z^{n} - 1$ and comparing it with the factorisation of $z^{n-1} + z^{n-2} + \cdots + 1$, show that
    $$\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\bigl(1 - \omega^{k}\bigr) = n.$$

    (Show that)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Show that $\bigl|1 - \omega^{k}\bigr| = 2\sin\left(\dfrac{k\pi}{n}\right)$ for $1 \le k \le n-1$. Hence show that
    $$\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\sin\left(\frac{k\pi}{n}\right) = \frac{n}{2^{\,n-1}},$$
    and state what part (b) says about the product of the chord lengths from one vertex of a regular $n$-gon to all the others.

    (Show that)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Determine the exact value of $\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{6}\sin\left(\frac{k\pi}{7}\right)$, and evaluate it as a decimal to confirm your answer.

    (Determine)

Total: 16 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The sum is a finite geometric series with first term $1$, common ratio $\omega$ and $n$ terms, and $\omega \neq 1$ because $n \ge 2$: (M1)
$$1 + \omega + \cdots + \omega^{n-1} = \frac{1 - \omega^{n}}{1 - \omega}$$
Since $\omega^{n} = e^{2\pi i} = 1$, the numerator is zero and the denominator is not, so the sum is $0$. (A1)(A1) Geometrically, the $n$ vertices are $n$ equal vectors whose sum is zero: the polygon closes up. Equivalently, the centre of mass of the vertices, treated as unit masses, is at the origin. (A1)

(b) The $n$ numbers $1, \omega, \ldots, \omega^{n-1}$ are precisely the $n$ roots of $z^{n} - 1$, so (M1)
$$z^{n} - 1 = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\bigl(z - \omega^{k}\bigr) = (z-1)\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\bigl(z - \omega^{k}\bigr)$$
Dividing by $z - 1$ and using the standard factorisation $z^{n} - 1 = (z-1)(z^{n-1} + \cdots + z + 1)$, valid for all $z$, (M1)
$$z^{n-1} + z^{n-2} + \cdots + 1 = \prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\bigl(z - \omega^{k}\bigr)$$
Both sides are polynomials in $z$, so they are equal for every $z$, including $z = 1$ even though the division by $z-1$ is not valid there. (M1)(A1) Substituting $z = 1$ gives $n$ on the left, from the $n$ terms, and the required product on the right: (A1)(AG)
$$\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\bigl(1 - \omega^{k}\bigr) = n$$

(c) With $\omega^{k} = \cos\frac{2k\pi}{n} + i\sin\frac{2k\pi}{n}$, (M1)
$$\bigl|1 - \omega^{k}\bigr|^{2} = \left(1 - \cos\frac{2k\pi}{n}\right)^{2} + \sin^{2}\frac{2k\pi}{n} = 2 - 2\cos\frac{2k\pi}{n}$$
Using $1 - \cos 2\theta = 2\sin^{2}\theta$ with $\theta = \dfrac{k\pi}{n}$, this is $4\sin^{2}\dfrac{k\pi}{n}$, so $|1 - \omega^{k}| = 2\sin\dfrac{k\pi}{n}$, which is positive for $1 \le k \le n-1$. (A1) Taking the modulus of the result in (b), and noting that the modulus of a product is the product of the moduli, (M1)
$$n = \left|\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\bigl(1 - \omega^{k}\bigr)\right| = \prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\left|1 - \omega^{k}\right| = \prod_{k=1}^{n-1} 2\sin\frac{k\pi}{n} = 2^{\,n-1}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\sin\frac{k\pi}{n}$$
so $\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\sin\frac{k\pi}{n} = \frac{n}{2^{\,n-1}}$. (A1) Since $|1 - \omega^{k}|$ is the distance from the vertex at $1$ to the vertex at $\omega^{k}$, part (b) says that the product of the $n-1$ chord lengths from any one vertex of a regular $n$-gon inscribed in the unit circle is exactly $n$, independent of everything except the number of vertices.

(d) Taking $n = 7$, (M1)
$$\prod_{k=1}^{6}\sin\frac{k\pi}{7} = \frac{7}{2^{6}} = \frac{7}{64}$$ (A1)
Evaluating, $\dfrac{7}{64} = 0.109375$. As a check, $\sin\frac{\pi}{7}\sin\frac{2\pi}{7}\sin\frac{3\pi}{7} = \frac{\sqrt{7}}{8}$ and the remaining three factors are the same three values in reverse order, so the full product is $\dfrac{7}{64}$, which agrees. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Accept the geometric-series argument or the observation that multiplying the sum by $\omega$ permutes its terms, so $S = \omega S$ and hence $S = 0$; the geometric interpretation must say something about the vertices or the centre of mass and not merely restate the equation. (b) Award (M1) for the factorisation of $z^n - 1$ over its roots and (M1) for cancelling the factor $(z-1)$; the subtle step is the substitution $z = 1$, and a candidate who says the identity is only valid for $z \neq 1$ has missed that both sides are polynomials and should lose one mark. (c) The identity $|1-\omega^k| = 2\sin(k\pi/n)$ must be derived rather than quoted; accept the route through $|1-\omega^k|^2 = (1-\omega^k)(1-\omega^{-k})$ as an alternative. The final remark about the product of chord lengths is a separate mark and should be credited even if the algebraic product is muddled. (d) Accept $7/64$ or $0.109375$; the pairing check is optional but shows the candidate has noticed the symmetry $\sin(k\pi/7) = \sin((7-k)\pi/7)$.

Why this question is hard

The reason this problem works is that the number $n$ appears in two unrelated-looking ways and the problem forces them into the same equation. On one side, $z^{n-1} + \cdots + 1$ evaluated at $z = 1$ is $n$, for the trivial reason that it has $n$ terms each equal to $1$. On the other side, the same polynomial factorises as a product of distances, because its roots are the non-trivial $n$th roots of unity and the modulus of $z - \omega^{k}$ is the distance from $z$ to $\omega^{k}$. Put $z = 1$ and the product of all the chord lengths from one vertex comes out as $n$. Nothing trigonometric enters, which is the surprise: a question about sines has an answer with no sines in it.

The step that candidates find genuinely hard is the substitution $z = 1$ in part (b), and it is worth dwelling on because it is a recurring idea. The factorisation $z^{n} - 1 = (z-1)(z^{n-1} + \cdots + 1)$ holds identically, and so does the factorisation over roots, but the cancellation of $(z-1)$ is only legitimate where $z \neq 1$. The resolution is that after cancelling, both sides are polynomials, and two polynomials that agree at infinitely many points agree everywhere, including at the one point the cancellation excluded. This is the removable-singularity argument in its simplest form, and a student who has met it here will recognise it later.

Part (c) converts the algebraic statement into a trigonometric one and part (d) cashes it in for $n = 7$. The specific value $7/64$ is not something anyone would guess, and the product of six sines is not something anyone would want to compute directly. The general result $\prod \sin(k\pi/n) = n/2^{n-1}$ is a standard identity, but arriving at it through complex numbers rather than through repeated application of double-angle formulae is the point: the geometry does the work that the trigonometry would find laborious. The final observation is the one worth carrying away — for a regular $n$-gon on the unit circle, the product of the chords from any vertex to the others is exactly $n$, whether $n$ is $3$ or $3$ million.
