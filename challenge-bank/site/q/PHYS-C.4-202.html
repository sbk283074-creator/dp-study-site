C.4 Two determinations of the same speed by standing waves in a tube, where the neglected end correction is worth more than either measurement's uncertainty

Physics HL · PHYS-C.4-202

Theme C: Wave behaviourC.4 Standing waves and resonance, with C.2 Wave modelP1 · Section B11 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedCalculateDetermineDetermineEvaluate

Two students measure the speed of sound in air

Student A held a loudspeaker driven by a tuning fork marked $512\ \mathrm{Hz}$ over the open end of a tube of variable length and moved the tube's water level until he heard the note become loud. He recorded the position of the tube's open upper end at the first three resonances, reading the metre rule to the nearest $\pm 1\ \mathrm{mm}$. Student B used a tube of fixed length $0.500 \pm 0.001\ \mathrm{m}$ and a signal generator connected to a loudspeaker held against the open end; he raised and lowered the frequency until the note became loud and recorded the frequency each time. The generator scale has a resolution of $1\ \mathrm{Hz}$. He recorded successive resonances at $169\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $510\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $843\ \mathrm{Hz}$. Both experiments were done at the same time and the air temperature was $19.0\ \mathrm{^{\circ}C}$, which both students took to be $20\ \mathrm{^{\circ}C}$. The internal diameter of both tubes was $36\ \mathrm{mm}$.

Question

The two determinations of the speed of sound, and the readings each student took, are described in the stimulus and its table. Answer all parts using those readings.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Calculate the wavelength of the sound in Student A's tube from the spacing of his three resonance positions, and hence calculate his value for the speed of sound.

    (Calculate)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Determine the end correction of the tube from Student A's first resonance position, and explain why the spacing of the positions cannot give it.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    One of Student B's three frequencies is inconsistent with the others. Identify it, justify your choice, determine Student B's value for the speed of sound from the remaining two readings, and explain why his value is systematically lower than Student A's.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate which of the two determinations of the speed of sound should be quoted, comparing the effect of random uncertainty on each with the effect of the systematic term.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 11 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Successive resonances in a tube closed at one end occur at $L = \dfrac{\lambda}{4} - e$, $\dfrac{3\lambda}{4} - e$, $\dfrac{5\lambda}{4} - e$, so consecutive positions differ by $\dfrac{\lambda}{2}$ (M1).

$$0.494 - 0.161 = 0.333\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad 0.827 - 0.494 = 0.333\ \mathrm{m} \;\; (A1).$$
$$\lambda = 2 \times 0.333 = 0.666\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad v = f\lambda = 512 \times 0.666 = 341\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \;\; (A1).$$
To three significant figures, $v = 341\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The two spacings agree to the nearest millimetre, so the random uncertainty on $\lambda$ is about $\dfrac{0.002}{0.333} = 0.6\%$ at worst, and on $v$ it is smaller still.

(b) The first resonance occurs where $L + e = \dfrac{\lambda}{4}$ (M1), so
$$e = \frac{\lambda}{4} - L_{1} = 0.1665 - 0.161 = 0.0055\ \mathrm{m} = 5.5\ \mathrm{mm} \;\; (A1).$$

The spacing cannot give it because $e$ is common to every resonance position. Subtracting one position from the next removes $e$ entirely: $L_{2} - L_{1} = \dfrac{\lambda}{2}$ whatever the end correction is. The spacing therefore determines $\lambda$ but carries no information about $e$, and it is only by referring a position to the *beginning* of the pattern -- which requires knowing that the first resonance is a quarter wavelength -- that $e$ can be extracted. For this tube the correction is $5.5\ \mathrm{mm}$, which is about $0.3$ of the $18\ \mathrm{mm}$ radius, as expected for a tube of this diameter.

(c) Two of the three frequencies should differ by twice the fundamental. Using the first and third,
$$\frac{843 - 169}{4} = \frac{674}{4} = 168.5\ \mathrm{Hz} \;\; (M1),$$
so the middle resonance should be at $169 + 2(168.5) = 506\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and $510\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is the inconsistent reading (A1). A $4\ \mathrm{Hz}$ error is four times the generator's $1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ resolution.

Student B used $v = 4f_{0}L$ with the first resonance at the fundamental:
$$v = 4 \times 168.5 \times 0.500 = 337\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \;\; (A1).$$

His value is lower by $341 - 337 = 4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, which is $1.2\%$. The cause is that he omitted the end correction. The correct relation is $v = 4f_{0}(L + e)$, so
$$v = 4 \times 168.5 \times (0.500 + 0.0055) = 341\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},$$
which is Student A's value to three significant figures. Omitting $e$ is not a random error: it always makes the effective length too short, so the inferred speed is always too low, and the size of the effect is fixed by the tube rather than by the care taken in reading the dial. The anomaly at $510\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is a separate matter -- probably the reading was taken while the generator's display was still settling -- and it does not affect the value used, because the fundamental was obtained from the first and third readings.

(d) Student A's value should be quoted (A1).

His random uncertainty is dominated by the $\pm 1\ \mathrm{mm}$ reading of the positions, which over a $333\ \mathrm{mm}$ spacing is $0.3\%$, and the tuning fork's frequency is a specification held to better than $0.1\%$. His systematic term is handled rather than ignored: the end correction is obtained from the data and applied, so what remains is the assumption that $e$ is the same at every resonance, which is good to well under a millimetre.

Student B's random uncertainties are comparable -- $1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ in $169\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is $0.6\%$ -- but he carries an unaddressed systematic term of $1.1\%$ from the omitted end correction, and a systematic term is not reduced by repeating the measurement. His result is therefore both lower than Student A's and less trustworthy, and the fact that correcting $e$ brings his value onto Student A's is itself evidence that the correction is the right explanation rather than an arbitrary adjustment (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for recognising that consecutive resonance positions differ by half a wavelength, (A1) for $\lambda = 0.666\ \mathrm{m}$, (A1) for $v = 341\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ with unit. A candidate who divides the full span by two instead of by four -- using $0.827 - 0.161$ as a single wavelength -- obtains $0.333\ \mathrm{m}$ and cannot score the last two marks.

(b) (M1) for the relation $L_{1} + e = \lambda/4$, (A1) for $5.5\ \mathrm{mm}$. The explanation is required for the second mark: a bare number with no statement about why the spacing is blind to $e$ scores (M1) only. Accept $5$ or $6\ \mathrm{mm}$ from a candidate who rounds $\lambda/4$ first; the argument is what is being marked.

(c) (M1) for using the first and third readings to obtain the spacing, (A1) for identifying $510\ \mathrm{Hz}$, (A1) for a value of $v$ determined from the remaining readings. A candidate who identifies the anomaly but then uses all three readings -- averaging, or fitting through all three -- forfeits the third mark; the question asks for the value from the remaining two. The explanation of the direction of the discrepancy is required somewhere in the response for full credit; a value of $337\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ with no comment about the end correction scores the three marks but the candidate should be reminded of the cause.

(d) (A1) for choosing Student A, (A1) for the comparison involving both a random and a systematic term. "Student A is more accurate" alone scores the first mark only; the comparison must reach the point that a systematic term is not reduced by repetition, or the equivalent statement that the discrepancy in (c) is predicted rather than observed.

Condonation: $v = 340\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ is accepted in (a) from a candidate who uses the accepted value of the speed of sound as a check; the calculated value must appear. Follow-through: a candidate whose $f_{0}$ in (c) is obtained from the first and third readings but whose arithmetic is wrong is credited the (M1) and, if the anomaly is correctly named, the (A1); the value mark follows their own $f_{0}$.

Forfeits: treating the $510\ \mathrm{Hz}$ reading as a random error to be averaged out, which removes the anomaly without explaining it; and quoting a percentage uncertainty from the three readings without noticing that the discrepancy between the two students is systematic.

Why this question is hard

Two students measure one number and get $341$ and $337$. The reflex is to ask which is careless, and the answer here is neither. The gap is $1.2\%$, and every random uncertainty in the experiment is smaller than that: the positions are read to a millimetre over a third of a metre, and the frequencies to a hertz in a hundred and seventy. Something systematic is at work, and the item is built so that it can be identified rather than guessed at.

The systematic term is the end correction. A tube closed at one end has an antinode slightly outside the open end, so the acoustically effective length is $L + e$ with $e$ roughly a third of the radius -- here $5.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ for an $18\ \mathrm{mm}$ radius. Student A's method extracts it because he refers an absolute position to the quarter-wavelength condition. Student B's method cannot, because he never uses a position at all: he treats the tube length as the whole of the acoustically effective length and computes $4fL$. The error is one-sided and always in the same direction, and $4fL$ with $f = 168.5\ \mathrm{Hz}$ gives $337$ against the $341$ that $4f(L+e)$ gives. The agreement after correction is the point of part (d): a candidate who finds the removal of $e$ turning $337$ into $341$ has located the cause, not merely adjusted a number.

The seeded anomaly is a different lesson. The three frequencies $169$, $510$ and $843$ should be equally spaced in steps of twice the fundamental, and $169$ to $843$ is four steps of $168.5$, which puts the middle reading at $506$ and not $510$. The reading is $4\ \mathrm{Hz}$ out -- four times the dial's resolution -- and the correct response is to identify it, exclude it and say what probably happened. Averaging the three frequencies to "fix" the anomaly is the classic wrong turn, because the average of a consistent pair and an inconsistent third is inconsistent with all three.

The two lessons are connected and it is worth saying how. A discrepancy that is larger than the uncertainties and always in the same direction is a systematic effect and needs a mechanism; a single reading that violates a pattern the others obey is an anomaly and needs to be excluded. Confusing the two -- treating the systematic offset as noise, or treating the anomaly as a discovery -- produces either a wrong answer or a wrong explanation, and the question is arranged so that both mistakes are available.
