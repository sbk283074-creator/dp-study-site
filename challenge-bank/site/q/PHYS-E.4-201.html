E.4 The fuel-cycle arithmetic of a fission reactor carried through from the energy per fission to the mass of enriched uranium per year, and the sensitivity of the comparison with coal to the assumptions behind it

Physics HL · PHYS-E.4-201

Theme E: Nuclear and quantum physicsE.4 Fission, with E.3 Radioactive decay and B.1 Thermal energy transfersP2 · Section B10 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedShow thatDetermineDetermineEvaluate

Question

A pressurised water reactor operates with a thermal power output of $3.0\ \mathrm{GW}$ and converts this to electrical energy with an efficiency of $33\%$. Each fission of a $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ nucleus releases $202\ \mathrm{MeV}$. The reactor is refuelled with uranium enriched to $4.0\%$ $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ by mass, and the remainder is $^{238}\mathrm{U}$, which does not undergo fission in this reactor. Use $N_{A} = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$, $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$ and one year $= 3.15 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{s}$.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Show that the fission of all the nuclei in $1.00\ \mathrm{kg}$ of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ would release about $8.3 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{J}$ of energy.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the mass of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ that undergoes fission in one year of continuous operation at the stated output.

    (Determine)
  3. [2 marks](c)

    Determine the mass of enriched uranium that must be loaded each year to supply that quantity of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$, and determine the mass of coal that would be needed each year to generate the same electrical energy in a station burning coal of energy content $2.4 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$ at an efficiency of $38\%$.

    (Determine)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Evaluate the comparison in part (c) as a measure of the environmental advantage of the reactor, referring to at least two assumptions that the comparison makes.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 10 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

A pressurised water reactor operates with a thermal power output of $3.0\ \mathrm{GW}$ and converts this to electrical energy with an efficiency of $33\%$. Each fission of a $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ nucleus releases $202\ \mathrm{MeV}$. The reactor is refuelled with uranium enriched to $4.0\%$ $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ by mass, and the remainder is $^{238}\mathrm{U}$, which does not undergo fission in this reactor. Use $N_{A} = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$, $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$ and one year $= 3.15 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{s}$. (a) Show that the fission of all the nuclei in $1.00\ \mathrm{kg}$ of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ would release about $8.3 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{J}$ of energy. [2 marks] (Show that) (b) Determine the mass of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ that undergoes fission in one year of continuous operation at the stated output. [3 marks] (Determine) (c) Determine the mass of enriched uranium that must be loaded each year to supply that quantity of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$, and determine the mass of coal that would be needed each year to generate the same electrical energy in a station burning coal of energy content $2.4 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$ at an efficiency of $38\%$. [2 marks] (Determine) (d) Evaluate the comparison in part (c) as a measure of the environmental advantage of the reactor, referring to at least two assumptions that the comparison makes. [3 marks] (Evaluate)
Reveal the answer

(a) The number of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ nuclei in $1.00\ \mathrm{kg}$ (M1):
$$N = \frac{1.00}{235 \times 10^{-3}}\ \mathrm{mol} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}} = \frac{1000}{235} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 2.56 \times 10^{24}.$$

The energy released per fission is
$$202\ \mathrm{MeV} = 202 \times 10^{6} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 3.23 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J},$$
so
$$E = 2.56 \times 10^{24} \times 3.23 \times 10^{-11} = 8.28 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{J} \approx 8.3 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{J} \;\; (A1).$$

(b) The electrical output is
$$P_{e} = 0.33 \times 3.0 \times 10^{9} = 9.9 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{W} \;\; (M1).$$
In one year the electrical energy delivered is
$$E_{e} = 9.9 \times 10^{8} \times 3.15 \times 10^{7} = 3.12 \times 10^{16}\ \mathrm{J} \;\; (M1).$$
All of this comes from fission, so the mass of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ fissioned is
$$m = \frac{3.12 \times 10^{16}}{8.28 \times 10^{13}} = 377\ \mathrm{kg} \;\; (A1).$$

It is worth noting that the efficiency cancels out of nothing here: the thermal power of $3.0\ \mathrm{GW}$ is what the fuel must supply, so an alternative route is to use $3.0 \times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{W}$ directly and compute $m = \dfrac{3.0 \times 10^{9} \times 3.15 \times 10^{7}}{8.28 \times 10^{13}} = 1141\ \mathrm{kg}$ of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ -- but that is the mass fissioned to supply the *thermal* output, and the electrical output is what the question asks about. Working in electrical energy throughout, as above, avoids the confusion.

(c) The $377\ \mathrm{kg}$ is $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ alone. Since the fuel contains only $4.0\%$ $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ by mass (M1):
$$m_{\text{fuel}} = \frac{377}{0.040} = 9.4 \times 10^{3}\ \mathrm{kg} \approx 9.4\ \mathrm{tonnes} \;\; (A1).$$
So just under ten tonnes of enriched uranium per year, of which about $9.0\ \mathrm{t}$ is $^{238}\mathrm{U}$ that does not fission.

For coal, the energy must be supplied as chemical energy and the conversion is less efficient:
$$m_{\text{coal}} = \frac{3.12 \times 10^{16}}{0.38 \times 2.4 \times 10^{7}} = 3.4 \times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{kg} = 3.4 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{tonnes} \;\; (A1).$$

The ratio of the two masses is $\dfrac{3.4 \times 10^{9}}{9.4 \times 10^{3}} \approx 3.6 \times 10^{5}$: the coal station consumes about three hundred and sixty thousand times the mass of fuel, each year.

(d) The comparison is a ratio of two masses and it is a real effect -- five and a half orders of magnitude is not a rounding difference -- but it is not a measure of environmental impact, and the assumptions that break the identification are worth naming.

First, the comparison counts only the fuel delivered to the station. It ignores everything upstream of that point, and the two chains are not alike. The coal figure of $3.4 \times 10^{6}$ tonnes is coal that has been mined, washed and transported, and those steps consume energy and produce emissions of their own; but the nuclear figure of $9.4$ tonnes is *enriched* uranium, and enrichment is an energy-intensive process that consumes electricity, historically generated by burning fossil fuel. A fair comparison adds the enrichment energy and the mining and milling energy to the nuclear side, and adds the transport energy to the coal side, before comparing (A1).

Second, the comparison assumes the two stations are otherwise equivalent in what they produce, and they are not. The coal station's $3.4$ million tonnes leave the plant every year as $\mathrm{CO_{2}}$ -- roughly $9 \times 10^{6}$ tonnes of it, since each tonne of coal of this energy content gives about $2.6$ tonnes of $\mathrm{CO_{2}}$ -- which is the environmental advantage the comparison is meant to capture, and it is a genuine one. But the nuclear station produces about $9.4$ tonnes of spent fuel per year which is intensely radioactive for decades and must be stored and eventually disposed of, and the comparison assigns that waste a mass of zero (A1).

Third, the numbers themselves are conditional. The $202\ \mathrm{MeV}$ per fission and the $4.0\%$ enrichment are both stated, and both could be different; more importantly the comparison assumes continuous operation at full output for a whole year, whereas a real reactor is shut for refuelling and maintenance and runs at part load at other times, so the $377\ \mathrm{kg}$ is a lower bound on the $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ consumed per year for a station of this rating. A station with a load factor of $80\%$ would consume about $80\%$ of the fuel but also generate $80\%$ of the energy, so the ratio in (c) is unchanged -- which is worth pointing out, because it shows the ratio is robust to that particular assumption even though the absolute masses are not (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for the number of nuclei from the molar mass and Avogadro's constant, (A1) for the energy, quoted as $8.3 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{J}$ or $8.28 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{J}$. A candidate who uses $235\ \mathrm{u}$ converted to kilograms ($3.90 \times 10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$) reaches the same answer by dividing rather than by using the mole, and scores full marks.

(b) (M1) for the electrical power, (M1) for the energy in one year, (A1) for $377\ \mathrm{kg}$. A candidate who obtains $1141\ \mathrm{kg}$ by using the thermal power has misread which output the question asks about and scores the first method mark and nothing else; the phrase "same electrical energy" in (c) settles it. Accept values in the range $3.7$ to $3.8 \times 10^{2}\ \mathrm{kg}$.

(c) (M1) for dividing by the enrichment fraction, (A1) for $9.4 \times 10^{3}\ \mathrm{kg}$; (A1) for the coal mass $3.4 \times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{kg}$. The coal calculation can use either the electrical energy (dividing by $0.38$ as well) or the thermal energy; a candidate who uses the electrical energy without dividing by the lower efficiency obtains $1.3 \times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{kg}$ and forfeits the third mark, because the whole point of the comparison is that the coal station wastes more of its input. Follow-through from (b) is allowed.

(d) (A1) for a first assumption stated and used, (A1) for a second, (A1) for the reasoning that connects the assumption to the conclusion -- that is, a candidate must say not only what the comparison ignores but whether ignoring it strengthens or weakens the claimed advantage. Naming the waste as an unaccounted cost scores the mark; listing "waste" and "emissions" and stopping does not.

Forfeits: treating the $4.0\%$ as the fraction of the uranium that fissions rather than as the fraction of the fuel that is $^{235}\mathrm{U}$; using $238$ in the molar mass because "most of the fuel is $^{238}\mathrm{U}$", which confuses the mass loaded with the mass fissioned. Condonations: answers quoted in tonnes or in kilograms are both accepted, and the ratio in (c) may be given as an order of magnitude ($\sim 10^{5}$).

Why this question is hard

This item is a chain of unit conversions and it is set because chains of unit conversions are where the interesting mistakes live. There are five steps: energy per fission in joules, nuclei per kilogram, energy per kilogram, energy per year at the stated output, mass per year. Every one of them is straightforward and the total is forty seconds of arithmetic for a candidate who knows what they are converting.

The mistake the item is built around is a substitution at the fourth step. The mass that fissions is $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ and it is $377\ \mathrm{kg}$. The mass that is *loaded* is fuel, and the fuel is $4.0\%$ $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ because natural uranium is only $0.7\%$ fissile and a reactor cannot run on that. So the loaded mass is twenty-five times the fissioned mass. A candidate who reports $377\ \mathrm{kg}$ as the annual fuel requirement has done every calculation correctly and answered a different question, and the numbers are arranged so that $377$ is a perfectly plausible-looking answer to the question that was actually asked one part earlier.

The second thing to notice is the direction of the efficiency correction. The reactor's $33\%$ efficiency means two thirds of the fission energy leaves as heat that is discarded, so the fuel requirement is set by the electrical output divided by the efficiency -- or, equivalently, by the thermal output directly. The coal station's $38\%$ efficiency works the same way and in the same direction, and a candidate who forgets it on one side of the comparison but not the other produces a ratio that is wrong by a factor of about $1.15$ for no reason at all.

Part (d) is deliberately open and the mark scheme says what it wants: two assumptions, each connected to whether it strengthens or weakens the claimed advantage. The strongest answer is the one that separates the two things the ratio measures. As a statement about *fuel mass* the comparison is excellent and robust -- five and a half orders of magnitude survive any plausible correction, including the enrichment energy, which is large but not a millionth of the coal chain. As a statement about *environmental impact* the ratio is much weaker, because the two waste streams are different in kind and not merely in mass: a coal station's annual output is millions of tonnes of carbon dioxide dispersed into the atmosphere, and a nuclear station's is ten tonnes of spent fuel which is dangerous for decades and must be stored. Neither the mass of the coal nor the mass of the fuel captures that, and the honest answer says so.
