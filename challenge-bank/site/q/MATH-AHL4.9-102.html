4.9 linear combinations of independent normal variables; scaling one observation is not the same as summing four

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL4.9-102

Topic 4: Statistics and probabilityAHL 4.9 (the normal distribution and curve; properties of the normal distribution); AHL 4.14 (variance of a discrete random variable; linear combinations of independent random variables)P2 · Section B14 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedStateFindShow thatDetermine

Question

The mass, $X$ grams, of a bag of flour from a production line is normally distributed with
mean $1000$ and standard deviation $12$. Bags are filled independently.

A quality-control procedure takes a random sample of four bags and weighs them together.
Let $S$ be the total mass of the four bags.

(a) State the distribution of $S$, giving its mean and standard deviation. \tag{3}

(b) Find $\mathrm{P}(S > 4040)$. \tag{3}

(c) A second, cheaper procedure weighs a single bag and multiplies the reading by $4$,
giving $T = 4X$. Show that $T$ has the same mean as $S$ but a different standard deviation,
and find $\mathrm{P}(T > 4040)$. \tag{4}

(d) Determine which of the two probabilities is the correct one to use for the total mass of
four bags, and explain why the two procedures agree about the mean but differ so sharply about
the tail. \tag{4}

  1. [3 marks](a)

    State the distribution of $S$, giving its mean and standard deviation.

    (State)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Find the probability that the total mass of the four bags exceeds $4040$ grams.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Show that $T = 4X$ has the same mean as $S$ but a different standard deviation, and find $\mathrm{P}(T > 4040)$.

    (Show that)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    Determine which probability is correct for the total mass of four bags, and explain why the two procedures agree about the mean but differ about the tail.

    (Determine)

Reveal the answer

(a) $S = X_1 + X_2 + X_3 + X_4$ where the $X_i$ are independent copies of $X$ (M1). A
linear combination of independent normal variables is normal, so

$$\mathrm{E}(S) = 4 \times 1000 = 4000, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(S) = 4 \times 12^{2} = 4 \times 144 = 576
\tag{A1}$$

$$S \sim \mathrm{N}(4000,\, 576), \qquad \sigma_S = \sqrt{576} = 24 \text{ g} \tag{A1}$$

Note that the standard deviation scales as $\sqrt{4} = 2$, not as $4$: variances add for
independent variables, standard deviations do not.

(b) Standardising (M1),

$$z = \frac{4040 - 4000}{24} = \frac{40}{24} = 1.6667 \tag{A1}$$

$$\mathrm{P}(S > 4040) = \mathrm{P}(Z > 1.6667) = 1 - 0.9522 = 0.0478 \text{ (3 s.f.)} \tag{A1}$$

(c) $T = 4X$ is a linear scaling of a single normal variable, so $T$ is normal with (M1)

$$\mathrm{E}(T) = 4 \times 1000 = 4000 \quad \text{— the same mean as } S \tag{A1}$$

$$\mathrm{Var}(T) = 4^{2} \times 12^{2} = 16 \times 144 = 2304, \qquad \sigma_T = \sqrt{2304} = 48 \text{ g}
\tag{A1}$$

which is twice $\sigma_S$. Standardising, $z = \frac{40}{48} = 0.8333$ and

$$\mathrm{P}(T > 4040) = \mathrm{P}(Z > 0.8333) = 1 - 0.7977 = 0.202 \text{ (3 s.f.)} \tag{A1}$$

(d) The correct probability for the total mass of four bags is the one from (b),
$\mathrm{P}(S > 4040) = 0.0478$ (A1), because the total really is a sum of four independent
random variables and $T$ is not that sum (R1). The two procedures agree about the mean
because expectation is linear and does not require independence:
$\mathrm{E}(X_1 + X_2 + X_3 + X_4) = 4\mathrm{E}(X) = \mathrm{E}(4X)$ (A1). They differ about
the tail because variance is not linear in the same way: for a sum of independent variables
the variances add, giving $4\sigma^2$, whereas scaling a single observation multiplies the
variance by $4^{2} = 16$, giving $16\sigma^{2}$ (R1). The single-bag procedure therefore
inflates the spread by a factor of $2$ and overstates the probability of an extreme total by a
factor of about $4.2$. Averaging four independent measurements cancels some of the noise;
multiplying one measurement by four amplifies it.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for writing $S$ as a sum of four independent variables. (A1) for the mean and
variance, (A1) for the standard deviation $24$ g. Condone the answer
$S \sim \mathrm{N}(4000, 24^{2})$. Do not accept $\mathrm{N}(4000, 48)$: the second parameter
of the normal distribution is a variance in this notation, and a candidate who writes $576$
as the standard deviation loses the final (A1).

(b) (M1) for the standardisation, (A1) for $z = 1.6667$, (A1) for $0.0478$. Accept any value
in $[0.0475, 0.0480]$. Follow-through from an incorrect standard deviation in (a) is allowed.

(c) (M1) for stating that a linear scaling of a normal variable is normal. (A1) for the mean
$4000$ and the observation that it matches, (A1) for the variance $2304$ and standard
deviation $48$. (A1) for $0.202$; accept $[0.2017, 0.2025]$.

(d) (A1) for identifying the probability from (b). (R1) for the reason, which must refer to
the total actually being a sum of four independent variables. (A1) for linearity of
expectation as the reason the means agree. (R1) for the contrast between adding variances and
scaling them. A candidate who says only "because $T$ uses one bag" earns the first (R1) but
not the last.

Why this question is hard

The pair of procedures is chosen so that every obvious check passes on both of them. Both
are unbiased: $\mathrm{E}(S) = \mathrm{E}(T) = 4000$. Both are normal. Both are measured in
grams. A candidate who verifies only that the two methods "give the same answer on average"
will conclude that they are equivalent, and will then use whichever is more convenient —
which, unfortunately, is the wrong one, because $T$ needs only one weighing.

The difference is entirely in the spread, and it comes from one structural fact: the four bags
are independent. Independence is what allows variances to add, so
$\mathrm{Var}(S) = \sigma^2 + \sigma^2 + \sigma^2 + \sigma^2 = 4\sigma^2$ and
$\sigma_S = 2\sigma = 24$. The cheap procedure has no independence to exploit. It takes one
observation and stretches it, and stretching by a factor of $4$ multiplies the variance by
$4^2 = 16$, so $\sigma_T = 4\sigma = 48$.

The $\sqrt{n}$ versus $n$ distinction is the single most useful idea in the item. Summing $n$
independent measurements grows the standard deviation like $\sqrt{n}$; scaling one measurement
by $n$ grows it like $n$. For $n = 4$ those differ by a factor of $2$, and because the normal
tail is steep near two standard deviations, a factor of $2$ in the standard deviation moves the
tail probability by a factor of about $4.2$ — from $4.8\%$ to $20\%$.

That is why the wrong answer is dangerous rather than merely wrong. It is not a small
misestimate; it says an event is more than four times as likely as it is. In a quality-control
setting the practical consequence is that a line which is performing perfectly will appear to
trip the alarm on roughly one inspection in five.

The general lesson transfers well beyond this question: whenever a model replaces an average
of several independent quantities by a scaled version of one of them, the mean survives and
the variance does not. Checking the mean is not a check at all.

Challenge lever
Two procedures have exactly the same expected value and very different spreads, so nothing about the mean reveals which is which. Weighing four bags and weighing one bag and multiplying by four both estimate the mean at $4000$ g, but the first has standard deviation $24$ g and the second $48$ g, and the two tail probabilities differ by a factor of about $4.2$. The governing quantity is the number of independent draws, and it is invisible in the mean.

Gaokao items of this type usually present a single transformation and ask for a single probability, so the choice of model is never in question. Here two procedures are placed side by side with identical means, so the candidate must decide which model represents the physical situation before computing anything, and then explain why the means agree while the variances do not. The contrast between $\sqrt{n}$ and $n$ scaling — the actual content of the item — is drawn out explicitly in part (d) and in the markscheme.
