B.4 A cycle whose path crosses itself, so the net work is the difference of two lobe areas rather than their sum

Physics HL · PHYS-B.4-401

Theme B: The particulate nature of matterB.4 Thermodynamics: the first law, work done in a cyclic process found from the area enclosed on a pressure-volume diagram, heat engines. With B.3 Gas laws: the ideal gas as the working substanceP26 marksdifficulty 5technology: allowedShow thatShow thatDetermine

Question

A fixed mass of an ideal gas is taken round the cycle shown in the figure. Pressure is measured in $\mathrm{kPa}$ and volume in $\mathrm{dm^{3}}$, so an area on the diagram is measured in joules.

The cycle follows the path $A \to B \to X \to C \to D \to X \to A$, where $A$ is $(2,\,100)$, $B$ is $(2,\,300)$, $X$ is $(4,\,200)$, $C$ is $(5,\,150)$ and $D$ is $(5,\,250)$. Each leg is a straight line on the diagram. The gas therefore passes through the point $X$ twice and the path crosses itself there.

(a) Show that the work done by the gas on the legs $A \to B$ and $B \to X$ together is $500\ \mathrm{J}$, and that the work done on the leg $X \to A$ is $-300\ \mathrm{J}$.

(b) Show that the work done by the gas as it goes round the smaller lobe, $X \to C \to D \to X$, is $-50\ \mathrm{J}$.

(c) Hence determine the net work done by the gas in one complete cycle and state, with a reason, whether the cycle is that of a heat engine.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Show that the work done by the gas on the legs $A \to B$ and $B \to X$ together is $500\ \mathrm{J}$ and that the work done on the leg $X \to A$ is $-300\ \mathrm{J}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Show that the work done by the gas as it goes round the lobe $X \to C \to D \to X$ is $-50\ \mathrm{J}$.

    (Show that)
  3. [2 marks](c)

    Hence determine the net work done by the gas in one complete cycle and state, with a reason, whether the cycle is that of a heat engine.

Reveal the answer

(a) Leg $A \to B$ is at constant volume, so no work is done (M1). On leg $B \to X$ the pressure falls linearly from $300$ to $200\ \mathrm{kPa}$ while the volume rises from $2$ to $4\ \mathrm{dm^{3}}$, so the work done by the gas is the mean pressure times the change in volume:

$$W_{BX} = \frac{300 + 200}{2} \times (4 - 2) = 250 \times 2 = 500\ \mathrm{J} \quad \text{(A1)}.$$

On leg $X \to A$ the pressure falls from $200$ to $100\ \mathrm{kPa}$ while the volume falls from $4$ to $2\ \mathrm{dm^{3}}$, so

$$W_{XA} = \frac{200 + 100}{2} \times (2 - 4) = 150 \times (-2) = -300\ \mathrm{J} \quad \text{(A1)},$$

negative because the gas is being compressed.

(b) On $X \to C$ the work done by the gas is $\tfrac{200 + 150}{2} \times (5 - 4) = 175\ \mathrm{J}$ (M1). Leg $C \to D$ is at constant volume, so no work is done. On $D \to X$ the volume falls, giving $\tfrac{250 + 200}{2} \times (4 - 5) = -225\ \mathrm{J}$. The total for the lobe is

$$175 + 0 - 225 = -50\ \mathrm{J} \quad \text{(A1)}.$$

(c) The larger lobe, $A \to B \to X \to A$, gives $0 + 500 - 300 = +200\ \mathrm{J}$ (M1). The net work for the whole cycle is therefore

$$200 + (-50) = 150\ \mathrm{J} \quad \text{(A1)},$$

positive, so over one complete cycle the gas does net work on its surroundings (A1). The cycle is that of a heat engine: it takes in heat, rejects less, and converts the difference into $150\ \mathrm{J}$ of work each cycle. Note that the net work is the *difference* of the two lobe areas, not their sum: the smaller lobe is traversed anticlockwise and undoes a quarter of what the larger lobe achieves.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for using the mean pressure on a straight-line leg, or equivalently for recognising that the work is the area of a trapezium; (A1) for 500 J; (A1) for -300 J. A candidate who integrates is equally acceptable but no faster. The mark for the zero on leg A to B is carried in the (M1): a candidate who gives a non-zero value for a constant-volume leg has misunderstood what the area represents.

(b) (M1) for the two non-zero legs; (A1) for -50 J. The sign is the point of the part and should be marked: -50 J and +50 J are different answers here, not a slip in convention, because the next part depends on which lobe undoes the other.

(c) (M1) for evaluating the larger lobe as +200 J; (A1) for the net 150 J; (A1) for the conclusion that it is a heat engine, which must be tied to the sign of the net work and not merely asserted from the word engine in the stem. Credit the observation that the net work is 150 J and not 250 J only where it is explained by the opposite senses of the two lobes.

Why this question is hard

The work done in a cycle is the signed area enclosed by the path, and a path that crosses itself encloses two areas with opposite signs. This is not a special case invented for the question: any self-intersecting closed curve on a $p$--$V$ diagram behaves this way, and the rule the candidate needs is the one they already have -- add up the work leg by leg and let the signs take care of themselves. What makes the item worth asking is that the *picture* suggests a single number. Two regions are shaded, both look enclosed, and the natural reading of the textbook sentence is to add them.

Once the leg-by-leg sum is done the physics is transparent. The gas does 500 J of work expanding from B to X and has 300 J taken back compressing from X to A, netting +200 J on the left. On the right it does 175 J expanding and has 225 J taken back, netting -50 J. The engine therefore works, but only just over half as well as the larger lobe alone would suggest, and a designer who read the diagram as 250 J would overestimate the output by two thirds. That is the practical reason the distinction matters, and it is also why the item asks for the conclusion in part (c) rather than leaving the number to speak for itself.

Challenge lever
The rule that the work done in a cycle is the area enclosed by the loop is learned on loops that do not cross themselves, where there is one area and the sign is settled by the direction of travel. Here there are two lobes and they are traversed in opposite senses, so the net work is the difference between them and the device is an engine by 150 J rather than a refrigerator by 50 J. Adding the two areas is not a slip in arithmetic but a slip in what the word enclosed means once the boundary crosses itself: the gas does positive work on one leg of the smaller lobe and has more taken back on another.
