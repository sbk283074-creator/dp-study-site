Business management SL · BM-2.4-001

Kestrel Studio

Kestrel Studio is a graphic design agency with 48 employees. It has a flat structure: there are no middle managers, and designers report directly to the two founding partners. Projects are chosen by whoever is interested, and there is no formal appraisal system.

The studio has grown from 12 to 48 people in three years. The founders say they kept the structure flat because "creative people hate being managed".

In the last year the following has happened:

  • Three senior designers have left. In exit interviews all three mentioned the same thing: no clear path to a more senior role.
  • Two client projects were delivered late after two designers independently assumed the other was leading.
  • A staff survey found that 71% of designers want to work from home three days a week. The founders have proposed requiring everyone in the office five days a week, arguing that "the best work happens when we are all in the same room".
  • The founders have offered a 5% pay rise to all staff.

Average pay at Kestrel is already 8% above the local market rate for designers.

Question

The founders must decide how to respond to rising staff turnover and the missed deadlines.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Describe two features of a flat organisational structure, and explain one drawback of keeping it as a business grows from 12 to 48 employees.

    (Describe)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Using one motivation theory, explain why the 5% pay rise is unlikely to solve the turnover problem.

    (Explain)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Evaluate whether the founders should require all staff to work from the office five days a week.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Features — award two:

  • Few or no levels of hierarchy between staff and the owners, so there is a wide span of control and a short chain of communication. (A1)
  • Decision-making is decentralised / delegated: designers choose their own projects and report directly to the founders rather than through managers. (A1)

Drawback — award one explained:

  • Role ambiguity and lack of accountability. With no manager assigning responsibility, two designers can each assume the other is leading — which is exactly what happened on the two late projects. At 12 people informal coordination works because everyone can see what everyone else is doing; at 48 it does not. (A1)(A1)
  • Also creditable: the founders' span of control has become unmanageable (48 direct reports); no appraisal system means no feedback or development; no promotion path, which is what the exit interviews identified; slower decision-making because everything escalates to two people.

(b) Using Maslow's hierarchy of needs (or Herzberg's two-factor theory):

  • Maslow arranges needs in a hierarchy: physiological → safety → social → esteem → self-actualisation, and a need only motivates when the level below it is substantially satisfied. (A1)
  • Kestrel's designers are paid 8% above the market rate, so their lower-order needs are comfortably met. (A1)
  • The exit interviews point to a higher-order need that is unmet: recognition and the esteem that comes with a more senior role, and the self-actualisation of developing a career. Maslow's point is that a satisfied need stops motivating — so adding more of what is already satisfied (pay) will not address a deficit at a higher level. (A1)(A1)
  • Hence the 5% rise is likely to be received as fair but not motivating; the turnover is driven by the absence of a promotion path, which money does not create. (A1)

Using Herzberg instead (credit equally): pay is a hygiene factor — its absence causes dissatisfaction, but its presence does not motivate. Pay is already above market, so the hygiene factor is satisfied. The motivators are recognition, responsibility and advancement; the flat structure offers none of the last, which is why designers leave. *(Award the same marks for a correctly applied Herzberg analysis.)*

(c) Indicative evaluation:

Case for requiring five days in the office: the founders' argument is not baseless — design work benefits from spontaneous critique, and the two late projects both arose from a coordination failure that co-location would probably have surfaced earlier; new and junior staff learn faster when they can see senior designers working; and the founders, as owners, have a legitimate interest in how the studio's culture develops. (A1)(A1)

Case against: 71% of designers want three days at home, and this is the group the studio is currently losing — the three leavers cited career issues, and imposing a policy that 71% have explicitly rejected risks converting a career-structure problem into a mass resignation problem. (A1) For experienced designers whose pay is already above market, flexibility is a non-financial motivator, and removing it removes the one thing the studio currently offers that a larger agency cannot. (A1)

Conclusion: the five-day requirement addresses a real problem (coordination) with the wrong tool, and it does so at the worst possible moment. The better response is to introduce *some* structure — project leads with named responsibility, and a promotion path — while allowing hybrid work on the 71%'s preferred pattern, with a fixed day or two when the whole studio is in. That separates the two issues the founders have conflated: coordination is a management problem, and attendance is a motivation problem. (A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Two marks for features, two for one explained drawback. The drawback must be linked to *growth* — 'flat structures cause confusion' with no reference to 12 → 48 employees is capped at one mark.

(b) Five marks: one for stating the theory correctly, one for identifying where pay sits in it, two for applying it to the evidence (8% above market; exit interviews cite seniority), one for the conclusion that pay will not fix this. A candidate who names Maslow or Herzberg but then argues in general terms ('people want more than money') earns the first mark only. Do not credit a theory not on the syllabus used vaguely.

(c) This is an evaluate question — a conclusion is required and must follow from the analysis. Credit either side. The discriminating observation is that the founders have *conflated two problems*: coordination (real, and fixable by naming project leads) and attendance (a motivation issue). Candidates who notice this should be rewarded even if their conclusion differs from the one above.

SL boundary: do not credit answers referring to organisational (corporate) culture (2.5) or industrial/employee relations (2.7) — both are HL only.

Why this question is hard

The case is built so that the obvious answer to each part is the wrong one, and in each case the evidence that shows it is wrong is sitting in the stimulus.

Part (a): flat structures are usually taught as modern and desirable, and the founders' justification ('creative people hate being managed') sounds enlightened. The two late projects are the counter-evidence, and they are there because role ambiguity is the specific failure mode of a flat structure at scale. Twelve people can coordinate by looking around the room; forty-eight cannot. Students who answer (a) by praising flat structures lose the two application marks.

Part (b) is where the theory has to do work. Both Maslow and Herzberg make the same prediction here, and both explain why a pay rise fails: once pay is above market it is a satisfied need (Maslow) or a satisfied hygiene factor (Herzberg), and neither theory says that adding more of a satisfied lower-level input produces motivation. The 8% figure is in the case precisely so that students can make this argument with evidence rather than assertion. The common weak answer is 'money isn't everything', which is a slogan, not a theory.

Part (c) is the payoff. The founders have one instrument — authority — and are applying it to a problem it cannot solve. The 71% figure is the trap: requiring five days in the office is not just ineffective, it is actively dangerous at the moment the studio is already losing people. And the deeper point, available to stronger candidates, is that the coordination failure and the attendance question are separate issues. Naming project leads fixes the late deliveries without touching working patterns at all — which means the five-day requirement is not even necessary for its own stated purpose.

Classic wrong turns: describing a tall structure instead of a flat one; using Herzberg but labelling pay as a motivator; and evaluating (c) purely on work-life balance without addressing the founders' coordination argument.
