A.3 The force needed to push a block up a ramp is greatest at an intermediate angle, while the work needed falls steadily as the ramp gets steeper

Physics HL · PHYS-A.3-401

Theme A: Space, time and motionA.3 Work, energy and power: work done by a force, conservation of energy. With A.2 Forces and momentum: resolution of forces on an inclined plane, frictionP27 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatDetermineExplain

Question

A block of mass $m$ is pushed at constant speed up a ramp of vertical height $h$, inclined at an angle $\theta$ to the horizontal. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the block and the ramp is $\mu = 0.50$. The figure shows the ramp and a graph of the required force against $\theta$.

(a) Show that the force $F$, applied parallel to the ramp, that is needed to keep the block moving at constant speed is

$$F = mg(\sin\theta + \mu\cos\theta).$$

(b) Determine the angle $\theta$ at which this force is greatest.

(c) Show that the total work done against gravity and friction together is

$$W = mgh\,(1 + \mu\cot\theta),$$

and explain why the work becomes smaller as the ramp gets steeper even though, for angles below your answer to part (b), the required force becomes larger. Take $m = 20\ \mathrm{kg}$, $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and $h = 3.0\ \mathrm{m}$ in your numerical illustration.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Show that the force parallel to the ramp needed to keep the block moving at constant speed is $F = mg(\sin\theta + \mu\cos\theta)$.

    (Show that)
  2. [2 marks](b)

    Determine the angle $\theta$ at which the force found in part (a) is greatest.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Show that the total work done is $W = mgh(1 + \mu\cot\theta)$ and explain why it falls as the ramp steepens while the required force may rise.

    (Explain)

Reveal the answer

(a) At constant speed the resultant force along the ramp is zero, so the applied force balances the component of the weight down the slope and the friction: $F = mg\sin\theta + f$ (M1). The normal reaction is $R = mg\cos\theta$ and $f = \mu R$, so

$$F = mg\sin\theta + \mu mg\cos\theta = mg(\sin\theta + \mu\cos\theta) \quad \text{(A1)}.$$

(b) Differentiate with respect to $\theta$:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}\theta} = mg(\cos\theta - \mu\sin\theta).$$

Setting this to zero gives $\tan\theta = \tfrac{1}{\mu} = 2$, so $\theta = 63.4^{\circ}$ (M1) (A1). For $\mu = 0.50$ the second derivative is negative there, so it is a maximum: at $\theta = 30^{\circ}$ the force is $183\ \mathrm{N}$, at $63.4^{\circ}$ it is $219\ \mathrm{N}$ and at $90^{\circ}$ it is $196\ \mathrm{N}$, so the force rises and then falls again.

(c) The distance travelled along the ramp is $s = \tfrac{h}{\sin\theta}$, so

$$W = Fs = mg(\sin\theta + \mu\cos\theta)\frac{h}{\sin\theta} = mgh\left(1 + \mu\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\right) = mgh(1 + \mu\cot\theta) \quad \text{(M1)}.$$

As $\theta$ increases, $\cot\theta$ decreases, so $W$ falls towards its limiting value $mgh$, the work against gravity alone (A1). Numerically, $W = 1098\ \mathrm{J}$ at $30^{\circ}$, $736\ \mathrm{J}$ at $63.4^{\circ}$ and $589\ \mathrm{J}$ at $90^{\circ}$. The two quantities move in opposite directions below $63.4^{\circ}$ because they are different things: the force is what the push must match at each instant, while the work is that force multiplied by a distance that shrinks as $\tfrac{1}{\sin\theta}$ as the ramp steepens. Between $30^{\circ}$ and $63.4^{\circ}$ the force grows by about 20 per cent while the distance falls by about a third, and the distance wins (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for resolving along the ramp with zero resultant, (A1) for the normal reaction and the friction law. A candidate who omits friction on the grounds that the block is moving at constant speed has confused constant speed with zero friction and cannot be credited beyond the first mark.

(b) (M1) for differentiating and setting the derivative to zero; (A1) for 63.4 degrees. Stating that the answer is arctan(1/mu) without the differentiation earns the method mark only if the result is justified. The check that it is a maximum rather than a minimum may be done by citing the values at the ends of the range, which is the safer route for most candidates.

(c) (M1) for the substitution s = h over sin theta and the simplification; (A1) for the observation that cot theta decreases so the work falls monotonically; (A1) for the explanation in terms of force and distance moving in opposite directions. The third mark is for the explanation, not the arithmetic: a candidate who produces the formula and says nothing about the opposite movement has answered a different question.

Why this question is hard

The two halves of the item are individually straightforward and jointly surprising, which is the shape the whole wave is aiming at. Resolving forces on an inclined plane is early-year work; multiplying by the distance along the slope is one line. What is not obvious until it is written down is that the force has a maximum in the middle of the range at all -- most people expect the steepest ramp to be the hardest to push up -- and that the work has no such maximum.

The maximum in the force is worth dwelling on. At small angles the friction term $\mu mg\cos\theta$ dominates and falls as the ramp steepens; at large angles the gravity term $mg\sin\theta$ dominates and rises. The sum peaks where the two effects balance, at $\tan\theta = 1/\mu$, which for a rough surface is a shallow angle and for a slippery one is steep. That is a genuinely useful piece of design intuition: if you are choosing a ramp angle and the limiting factor is the force available, the worst choice is not the steepest one.

The work is a different quantity and behaves differently, and the whole point of part (c) is that a candidate's physical intuition about 'harder' has to be attached to one of the two before it means anything.

Challenge lever
Force and work pull in opposite directions and the item asks about both. Raising the angle does two things at once: it increases the component of the weight along the slope, which raises the required force, and it shortens the slope for a given height, which lowers the work. Over most of the range both effects are present, so the answer to 'which ramp is hardest' depends on which word hardest means, and a candidate who answers one question and believes they have answered the other has walked into the only trap the item contains.
