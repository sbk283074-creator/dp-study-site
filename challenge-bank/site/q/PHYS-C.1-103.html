C.1 Which oscillators feel an accelerating reference frame and which do not

Physics HL · PHYS-C.1-103

Theme C: Wave behaviourC.1 Simple harmonic motion (the defining equation; the period is independent of amplitude); A.2 Forces and momentumP2 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 4technology: allowedShow thatFindExplainDetermine

Question

A lift accelerates upwards at a constant $a = 2.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Take
$g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Inside the lift are two oscillators.

* A simple pendulum of length $L = 0.80\ \mathrm{m}$.
* A mass–spring system: a mass $m = 0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$ hanging from a spring of
spring constant $k = 200\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$.

(a) Show that, in the frame of the accelerating lift, the effective gravitational field
strength is $g + a$, and state its value. \tag{3}

(b) Find the period of the pendulum in the accelerating lift. \tag{4}

(c) Find the period of the mass–spring system in the accelerating lift, and explain why it has
the same value as when the lift is at rest. \tag{4}

(d) The acceleration of the lift is now unknown. The period of the pendulum is measured to be
$1.600\ \mathrm{s}$. Determine $a$. \tag{4}

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the effective gravitational field strength in the lift is $g + a$ and state its value.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Find the period of the pendulum in the accelerating lift.

    (Find)
  3. [4 marks](c)

    Find the period of the mass–spring system in the accelerating lift, and explain why it matches the value at rest.

    (Find)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    The pendulum's period is measured to be $1.600\ \mathrm{s}$. Determine the lift's acceleration $a$.

    (Determine)

Reveal the answer

(a) In the non-inertial frame of the lift there is a fictitious force $ma$ downwards on
every mass $m$ (M1), in addition to the weight $mg$ downwards. The total downward force is

$$F = mg + ma = m(g + a) \tag{A1}$$

so the effective gravitational field strength is $g_{\text{eff}} = g + a = 9.81 + 2.0
= 11.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ (A1). Equivalently, an object released in the lift falls with
acceleration $g + a$ relative to the lift.

(b) For a simple pendulum the restoring force is the component of the effective weight along
the arc, so the standard result holds with $g$ replaced by $g + a$ (M1):

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g + a}} \tag{A1}$$

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.80}{11.81}} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.06774} = 2\pi(0.2603) = 1.64\ \mathrm{s}
\tag{A1}$$

For comparison, at rest the same pendulum has $T_{0} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.80}{9.81}} = 1.79\
\mathrm{s}$, so the acceleration shortens the period by about $9\%$ (A1). The amplitude does
not enter, as always for small oscillations.

(c) For a mass on a spring the restoring force is $-k$ times the extension, which has nothing
to do with gravity (M1). At equilibrium in the lift the spring is stretched by

$$x_{0} = \frac{m(g + a)}{k} = \frac{0.50 \times 11.81}{200} = 0.0295\ \mathrm{m} \tag{A1}$$

against $x_{0} = \frac{0.50 \times 9.81}{200} = 0.0245\ \mathrm{m}$ at rest, so the
acceleration shifts the equilibrium position downwards by $5.0\ \mathrm{mm}$ and nothing else.
Displacing the mass by a further $y$ from that new equilibrium gives the net force

$$F = -k(x_{0} + y) + m(g + a) = -ky \tag{A1}$$

since $kx_{0} = m(g + a)$ by definition of the equilibrium position. This is SHM with
$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$, so

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.50}{200}} = 2\pi(0.0500) = 0.314\ \mathrm{s}
\tag{A1}$$

exactly as at rest. The acceleration has dropped out entirely, because it is a constant force
and a constant force can only move the centre of oscillation, not change the curvature of the
potential about that centre.

(d) Rearranging the result of (b),

$$g + a = \frac{4\pi^{2}L}{T^{2}} = \frac{4\pi^{2} \times 0.80}{1.600^{2}}
= \frac{31.58}{2.560} = 12.34\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \tag{M1}$$

$$a = 12.34 - 9.81 = 2.53\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \tag{A1}$$

Only the pendulum measurement is of any use here (A1): the period of the mass–spring system is
$0.314\ \mathrm{s}$ whatever the lift is doing, so it carries no information about $a$ at all.
A candidate who tries to extract $a$ from the spring period will find the algebra returns no
solution, which is the correct outcome (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for introducing the fictitious force, or equivalently for arguing in the inertial
frame that the mass must accelerate with the lift so the tension exceeds the weight by $ma$.
(A1) for $F = m(g+a)$. (A1) for the numerical value $11.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Allow
$g_{\text{eff}} = 11.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

(b) (M1) for stating that $g$ is replaced by $g + a$ — this must be justified by a reference
to the restoring force, not asserted. (A1) for the correct formula. (A1) for
$1.64\ \mathrm{s}$; accept $1.635\ \mathrm{s}$. (A1) for the comparison with the value at
rest; accept any period in $[1.79, 1.80]\ \mathrm{s}$ for the at-rest value.

(c) (M1) for identifying the spring force, not gravity, as the restoring force. (A1) for the
equilibrium extension. (A1) for showing the force reduces to $-ky$ about the new equilibrium —
this is the key step and should not be credited to a candidate who merely asserts that "the
period of a spring does not depend on $g$". (A1) for $0.314\ \mathrm{s}$.

Forfeiture: a candidate who substitutes $g + a$ into $\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$ in any form has
misunderstood the physics and earns at most the (M1).

(d) (M1) for rearranging to make $g + a$ or $a$ the subject. (A1) for
$2.53\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$; accept $[2.52, 2.54]$. (A1) for stating that only the pendulum
helps. (A1) for the consequence that the spring period yields no information. Condonation:
allow a candidate who reports $a = 2.5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ from $g_{\text{eff}} = 12.3\
\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ without further figures.

Why this question is hard

The result that a spring-mass oscillator ignores a uniform acceleration surprises people,
and the reason is short enough to be worth stating precisely: what sets the period of any
oscillator is the *curvature* of the potential at equilibrium, and a constant force tilts the
potential without curving it. Adding a constant downward force moves the equilibrium point and
leaves the second derivative exactly where it was, so $\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ is
untouched.

The pendulum is different because gravity is not an added constant there — it *is* the
restoring force. The restoring torque comes from the component of weight along the arc, so
increasing the effective weight increases the restoring torque in direct proportion, and the
period shortens as $\frac{1}{\sqrt{g + a}}$. The same force that is irrelevant to the spring is
the whole mechanism for the pendulum.

This is why the item gives both oscillators and asks about both in the same lift. Any single
one of them could be answered by a memorised formula. Placed together, they force the question
"where does the restoring force come from?", and that question has a different answer for each.

Part (d) turns the distinction into something measurable. If you want to know how fast a lift
is accelerating, a pendulum is an accelerometer and a mass on a spring is not. The spring's
period carries no information, and the honest answer is not an approximate value of $a$ but the
statement that no value can be extracted. Candidates who have internalised the result in (c)
will say so immediately; candidates who have only memorised that "the period of a spring
does not depend on $g$" often try the substitution anyway and get stuck.

The numbers are chosen so that the effect is visible but not absurd: an acceleration of
$2.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ is a fifth of a gee, which shortens the pendulum period from
$1.79\ \mathrm{s}$ to $1.64\ \mathrm{s}$, about nine per cent. That is easily measurable with a
stopwatch over twenty oscillations, which is the practical reason the distinction matters.

Challenge lever
Two oscillators sit in the same accelerating lift and only one of them notices. The pendulum's restoring force is gravity, so the acceleration adds to $g$ and its period shortens by about nine per cent. The spring's restoring force is the spring, so the acceleration only shifts the equilibrium position by five millimetres and leaves the period untouched. The acceleration is therefore a governing variable for one system and a non-governing one for the other, and telling them apart requires knowing where each restoring force comes from.
