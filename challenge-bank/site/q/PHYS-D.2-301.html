D.2 Recovering two unknown point charges from a zero-field position and one potential reading, then finding where each quantity vanishes once one charge changes sign

Physics HL · PHYS-D.2-301

Theme D: FieldsD.2 Electric and magnetic fields: electric field strength and electric potential due to point charges; the inverse-square law for field strength and the inverse law for potential, and the addition of potentials as scalars against the addition of field strengths as vectorsP215 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedShow thatDetermineExplainDetermine

Question

Two point charges are held fixed on the $x$-axis. Charge $q_{1}$ is at $x = 0$ and charge $q_{2}$ is at $x = 0.60\ {\rm m}$. Initially both charges are positive. It is found experimentally that the resultant electric field strength is zero at the point on the $x$-axis at $x = 0.20\ {\rm m}$, and that the electric potential at that same point is $2.70 \times 10^{4}\ {\rm V}$.
Take $k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}} = 8.99 \times 10^{9}\ {\rm N\,m^{2}\,C^{-2}}$, take the potential and the field strength to be zero at infinity, and take the electric potential due to a point charge $q$ at distance $r$ to be $V = kq/r$ and the corresponding field strength along the line to have magnitude $E = kq/r^{2}$.

  1. [2 marks](a)

    Show that the observation about the field strength implies $q_{2} = 4q_{1}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [3 marks](b)

    Determine the magnitude of each of the two charges.

    (Determine)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Explain why, with both charges positive, the electric potential cannot be zero anywhere on the $x$-axis, and state where it comes closest to being zero.

    (Explain)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    The charge $q_{1}$ is now replaced by a charge of the same magnitude but negative sign, so that $q_{1} = -2.00 \times 10^{-7}\ {\rm C}$ and $q_{2} = +8.00 \times 10^{-7}\ {\rm C}$. Determine the position on the $x$-axis at which the resultant field strength is zero, and determine every position on the $x$-axis at which the electric potential is zero.

    (Determine)
  5. [3 marks](e)

    Explain why there is one position at which the field strength is zero but two at which the potential is zero, referring explicitly to the fact that one quantity is added as a vector and the other as a scalar.

    (Explain)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Determine the position on the $x$-axis at which the resultant field strength is zero, and determine **every** position on the $x$-axis at which the electric potential is zero. [4 marks] (Determine) (e) Explain why there is one position at which the field strength is zero but two at which the potential is zero, referring explicitly to the fact that one quantity is added as a vector and the other as a scalar. [3 marks] (Explain)
Reveal the answer

(a) At $x = 0.20\ {\rm m}$ the distances to the two charges are $0.20\ {\rm m}$ and $0.60 - 0.20 = 0.40\ {\rm m}$. Between two positive charges the field strengths oppose each other, so for the resultant to vanish their magnitudes must be equal (M1):
$$\frac{kq_{1}}{(0.20)^{2}} = \frac{kq_{2}}{(0.40)^{2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{q_{1}}{0.040} = \frac{q_{2}}{0.160} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q_{2} = 4q_{1} \;(A1).$$
(b) The potential at that point is the scalar sum of the two contributions:
$$V = \frac{kq_{1}}{0.20} + \frac{kq_{2}}{0.40} = k\left(\frac{q_{1}}{0.20} + \frac{4q_{1}}{0.40}\right) = k(5q_{1} + 10q_{1}) = 15kq_{1} \;(M1),$$
using the relation from part (a) before any substitution. Hence
$$q_{1} = \frac{2.70 \times 10^{4}}{15 \times 8.99 \times 10^{9}} = 2.00 \times 10^{-7}\ {\rm C} \;(A1),$$
and therefore $q_{2} = 4q_{1} = 8.00 \times 10^{-7}\ {\rm C}$ (A1). Both are positive, as stated. Note that using part (a) first is what made this possible: had the candidate instead written the two potential contributions as two separate unknowns, the single potential reading would have been one equation in two unknowns.
(c) With both charges positive, the potential at a point is $V = k q_{1}/r_{1} + k q_{2}/r_{2}$, and since every $q$ and every $r$ is positive, both terms are positive everywhere on the axis (M1). A sum of two positive terms cannot be zero, so there is no point on the $x$-axis at which $V = 0$ (A1). The potential falls monotonically as a point moves outwards from either charge towards infinity, so $V \to 0$ asymptotically; the zero is reached only in the limit of infinite distance (A1). Note the contrast with the field strength: there, two opposing contributions can cancel at a finite point, and did.
(d) With unlike charges, between them the field strengths point in the same direction -- away from the positive charge and towards the negative one, both along $-x$ for a point between them -- so no cancellation is possible there. Cancellation must therefore occur outside the pair, on the side of the smaller magnitude, that is at $x < 0$. Equating magnitudes (M1),
$$\frac{k|q_{1}|}{x^{2}} = \frac{kq_{2}}{(0.60 - x)^{2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{0.60 - x}{|x|} = \sqrt{\frac{8.00}{2.00}} = 2 ,$$
and with $x < 0$ this gives $0.60 - x = -2x$, so $x = -0.60\ {\rm m}$ (A1). For the potential, each region must be treated separately because $|x|$ and $|x - 0.60|$ change form:
$$V = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{2.00 \times 10^{-7}}{|x|} = \frac{8.00 \times 10^{-7}}{|x - 0.60|} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |x - 0.60| = 4|x| \;(M1).$$
Between the charges, $0 < x < 0.60$: $0.60 - x = 4x$, so $x = 0.12\ {\rm m}$ (A1).
To the left of both charges, $x < 0$: $0.60 - x = -4x$, so $x = -0.20\ {\rm m}$ (A1).
To the right of both charges, $x > 0.60$: $x - 0.60 = 4x$ gives $x = -0.20$, which contradicts the assumption, so that region contributes nothing. There are therefore two positions, $x = 0.12\ {\rm m}$ and $x = -0.20\ {\rm m}$, at which the potential vanishes, against one where the field strength vanishes. Checking: at $x = 0.12$, $k(8.00 \times 10^{-7}/0.48 - 2.00 \times 10^{-7}/0.12) = k(1.667 \times 10^{-6} - 1.667 \times 10^{-6}) = 0$; at $x = -0.20$, $k(8.00 \times 10^{-7}/0.80 - 2.00 \times 10^{-7}/0.20) = k(1.00 \times 10^{-6} - 1.00 \times 10^{-6}) = 0$.
(e) Field strength is a vector: at each point the two contributions are added with their directions, so a zero requires two contributions of equal magnitude and opposite direction (M1). With unlike charges that happens only outside the pair, on the side of the smaller charge, giving exactly one place on the line (A1). Potential is a scalar: contributions are added as signed numbers, so a zero requires only that the positive and negative amounts happen to cancel in size (M1). The scalar $V$ changes sign on crossing either charge, so there is a zero between them, and there is a second zero outside on the side of the smaller magnitude where the inverse-distance contributions can again balance (A1). One fewer piece of information -- direction -- is being matched, which is precisely why there is more than one place it can happen (A1).

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for noting that between two positive charges the field strengths oppose and therefore equating magnitudes, (A1) for $q_{2} = 4q_{1}$. A candidate who equates $kq_{1}/0.20^{2} = kq_{2}/0.40^{2}$ without any statement about direction scores (M1) but loses (A1) if the distances are taken as $0.20$ and $0.60$.
(b) (M1) for forming the potential sum and substituting $q_{2} = 4q_{1}$ so that it collapses to one unknown, (A1) for $q_{1} = 2.00 \times 10^{-7}\ {\rm C}$, (A1) for $q_{2} = 8.00 \times 10^{-7}\ {\rm C}$. Accept $0.200\ \mu{\rm C}$ and $0.800\ \mu{\rm C}$. Follow-through from part (a) is available. Note that a potential of exactly $2.70 \times 10^{4}\ {\rm V}$ corresponds to $15kq_{1} = 2.697 \times 10^{4}\ {\rm V}$; rounding of the stated value is condoned and the charges are round numbers, which is the intended reading.
(c) (M1) for noting both terms in the potential sum are positive, (A1) for concluding there is no zero, (A1) for locating the limit at infinity and noting the contrast with the field strength. A candidate who says 'the potential approaches zero at infinity' without explaining why no finite point works loses the first two marks.
(d) (M1) for identifying that cancellation must be outside the pair and, for the potential, writing the scalar equation with modulus signs, (A1) for $x = -0.60\ {\rm m}$, (A1) for $x = 0.12\ {\rm m}$, (A1) for $x = -0.20\ {\rm m}$. Four marks total; candidates who treat only one region for the potential lose marks for the missing answer, and it is expected that most will find the inner zero and not the outer one. Candidates who obtain an $x$ inside the region they assumed should be credited only if they check their answer against the assumption. Note especially that any answer giving two zeros of the field strength, or one zero of the potential, should be treated as a failure to carry out the case analysis rather than an arithmetic slip.
(e) (M1) for naming field strength as a vector addition, (A1) for the conclusion that a zero needs opposition and occurs in one place, (M1) for naming potential as a scalar addition, (A1) for the two resulting regions and the sign change across each charge, (A1) for the summary that less information is being matched. Five fields; credit three. Accept any argument reaching 'vector versus scalar' as the reason.

Why this question is hard

Every part of this item is decided by whether a quantity carries direction. That is one sentence of physics and most of a syllabus of confusion, because the two quantities are introduced together, have almost identical-looking formulas, and are usually asked about in contexts where the distinction has no consequence.

Here it has consequences. Two like charges put a point between them where the fields oppose and cancel, so there is a finite position at which $E = 0$ while no such position exists for $V$. Reverse one charge and the situation inverts almost completely: now there is exactly one position where $E = 0$, sitting outside the pair, but two positions where $V = 0$, one between the charges and one outside on the side of the smaller magnitude. The counts differ, and no amount of arithmetic makes them agree.

The recovery in parts (a) and (b) has a specific order that has to be respected. The field-strength observation is not about the charges directly; it is about the ratio of two squared distances, $0.16/0.04 = 4$, which converts into a relation between the charges. Only after that relation exists does the potential reading become usable, because the potential expression then collapses from two unknowns to one and gives $15kq_{1}$. A candidate who skips part (a) and writes the potential equation directly has one equation in two unknowns and an answer that looks like it ought to work.

The case analysis in part (d) is the part that actually separates candidates, and it is worth being explicit about why. The equation $|x - 0.60| = 4|x|$ is genuinely three equations wearing one coat: each region between and outside the two charges resolves the modulus signs differently. Two of them give valid answers and the third gives $x = -0.20$, which lies outside the region assumed and must be discarded -- though it is not garbage, since $-0.20$ is one of the two genuine answers, reached by a route that assumed the wrong region. Handling that kind of near-miss correctly requires writing down the assumption before solving, which is exactly the habit the part rewards.

Finally, part (e) is not asking for restatement. A candidate can complete every previous part arithmetically and still be unable to say why one answer is single and the other doubled. Being a vector means two quantities have to be equal and opposite, which restricts location as well as size; being a scalar means only size counts, with more places to satisfy it.

Challenge lever
Two unknowns are being recovered from two readings, but the first reading cannot be used until it has been converted: the position at which the field strength vanishes carries a ratio of distances squared, and only once that ratio has been inverted into a relation between the charges does the potential reading have anything to say. The candidate also has to keep field strength and potential as different objects throughout -- one vector, one scalar -- because the second half asks where each vanishes and the answers are not the same in number, and the reason they differ is the whole point.

Adaptation
The standard question gives two charges and asks for the field or the potential at a named point, which is a substitution. Here the arrangement is inverted twice over: the charges are unknown and recovered from a zero-field position plus one potential reading, and then one charge changes sign and the question becomes how many zeros there are and where, for each of the two quantities. The comparison of the counts -- one zero of E against two of V -- with an explicit demand for the vector-versus-scalar explanation, is not present in the source. All figures are original and were chosen so that the charge ratio is exactly four, the recovered charges are round at 0.200 and 0.800 microcoulombs, and the two potential zeros sit at clean positions 0.12 m and -0.20 m.
Verification
The ratio was checked as 0.160/0.040 = 4. The potential was recomputed as 15 * 8.99e9 * 2.00e-7 = 26970 V, which rounds to the stated 2.70e4 V. The field-zero position for the unlike-charge case was solved as (0.60 - x)/|x| = 2 giving x = -0.60 m and verified by evaluating both magnitudes: k*2e-7/0.36 against k*8e-7/1.44, whose ratio is (8/2)*(0.36/1.44) = 4*0.25 = 1. Both potential zeros were substituted back and give exactly zero to floating-point precision.
