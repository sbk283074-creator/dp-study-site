The descent that only works because the modulus is three

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL · MATH-AHL1.6-001

SL 1.6 (simple deductive proof, numerical and algebraic; proof by contradiction and by counterexample)P1 · Section B15 marksdifficulty 5technology: not allowedShow thatDeduceHenceExplain

Question

The task is to prove that there are no positive integers $a$, $b$ and $c$ satisfying the equation $a^{2} + b^{2} = 3c^{2}$.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that the square of every integer leaves remainder $0$ or $1$ when divided by $3$.

    (Show that)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Deduce that if $a^{2} + b^{2} = 3c^{2}$ for integers $a$, $b$ and $c$, then $3$ divides both $a$ and $b$.

    (Deduce)
  3. [5 marks](c)

    Hence show that there are no positive integers $a$, $b$ and $c$ with $a^{2} + b^{2} = 3c^{2}$.

    (Hence)
  4. [3 marks](d)

    Explain why the same argument with $3$ replaced by $2$ does not force $a$ and $b$ to be even, and give a solution of $a^{2} + b^{2} = 2c^{2}$ showing that the corresponding conclusion is false.

    (Explain)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Every integer is of the form $3k$, $3k+1$ or $3k+2$. (M1) Squaring these gives $9k^{2}$, $9k^{2} + 6k + 1$ and $9k^{2} + 12k + 4$. (M1) The first is divisible by $3$, while the second and third leave remainder $1$, so every square leaves remainder $0$ or $1$. (A1)

(b) By part (a), $a^{2}$ and $b^{2}$ each leave remainder $0$ or $1$ modulo $3$, so $a^{2} + b^{2}$ leaves remainder $0$, $1$ or $2$. (M1) The right-hand side $3c^{2}$ is divisible by $3$, so the left-hand side must leave remainder $0$. (M1) Remainder $0$ forces $0 + 0$, so $a^{2}$ and $b^{2}$ are both divisible by $3$. (A1) A square divisible by $3$ forces its root to be divisible by $3$, so $3 \mid a$ and $3 \mid b$. (A1)

(c) From part (b), write $a = 3a_1$ and $b = 3b_1$. (M1) Substituting gives $9a_1^{2} + 9b_1^{2} = 3c^{2}$, so $3(a_1^{2} + b_1^{2}) = c^{2}$. (M1) Therefore $3 \mid c^{2}$ and hence $3 \mid c$. (A1) Writing $c = 3c_1$ and dividing by $3$ gives $a_1^{2} + b_1^{2} = 3c_1^{2}$. (M1) So $(a_1, b_1, c_1)$ is another positive integer solution with $a_1 < a$. (A1) Repeating without end produces a strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers, which is impossible, so no such triple exists. (R1)

(d) Modulo $2$, every square leaves remainder $0$ or $1$. (M1) Both $0 + 0$ and $1 + 1$ leave remainder $0$ modulo $2$, so the congruence does not force $a$ and $b$ even. (A1) Indeed $a = b = c = 1$ gives $1 + 1 = 2 = 2 \times 1$, a genuine solution. (R1)

Markscheme notes

Part (a): the two (M1) marks are for the residue classes and for squaring them, and the (A1) for the conclusion. A candidate may tabulate the three cases instead of using algebra. Part (b): the first (M1) is for listing the possible remainders of $a^{2} + b^{2}$ and the second for matching the remainder $0$ forced by the right-hand side. The first (A1) is for concluding $3 \mid a$ and $3 \mid b$ from the remainders, and the second (A1) is for the step that a square divisible by $3$ forces its root to be divisible by $3$; this must be justified, not merely asserted. Part (c): the (M1) marks are for the substitutions $a = 3a_1$ and $b = 3b_1$ and for reaching $c^{2} = 3(a_1^{2} + b_1^{2})$. The (A1) is for $3 \mid c$. The final (M1)(A1) are for producing the smaller solution and identifying it as smaller, and the (R1) is for naming the impossibility of an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers. Part (d): the (M1) is for the two residue classes modulo $2$, the (A1) for the observation that $1 + 1$ is congruent to $0$, and the (R1) for an explicit counterexample such as $a = b = c = 1$.

Why this question is hard

This is a proof by infinite descent, and the whole argument hinges on the modulus being three. Modulo $3$ the squares are only $0$ and $1$, so a sum of two squares can take remainder $0$, $1$ or $2$. The right-hand side $3c^{2}$ is always divisible by $3$, so the remainder must be $0$, and the only way two residues drawn from $0$ and $1$ sum to $0$ modulo $3$ is $0 + 0$. Both $a^{2}$ and $b^{2}$ are therefore divisible by $3$, which forces both $a$ and $b$ to be divisible by $3$.

Part (c) turns this into a descent. Writing $a = 3a_1$ and $b = 3b_1$ and substituting gives $c^{2} = 3(a_1^{2} + b_1^{2})$, so $c$ is also divisible by $3$. Dividing through by $3$ produces a smaller solution $(a_1, b_1, c_1)$ of the same equation. If a solution existed, this process could be repeated without end, generating a strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers, and no such sequence can exist. That contradiction proves the equation has no positive integer solutions.

Part (d) is the discriminating step. The same style of argument with $3$ replaced by $2$ fails, because modulo $2$ the squares are again $0$ and $1$ but now $1 + 1$ is also congruent to $0$, just as $0 + 0$ is. The congruence therefore does not force $a$ and $b$ to be even, and indeed $a = b = c = 1$ satisfies $a^{2} + b^{2} = 2c^{2}$. The classic errors are asserting $3 \mid a$ without justifying that a square divisible by $3$ forces its root to be divisible by $3$, and stopping the descent after one step without naming the impossibility.
