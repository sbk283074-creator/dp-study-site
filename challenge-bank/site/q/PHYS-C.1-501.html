C.1 Simple harmonic motion: a vertical mass-spring oscillator whose equilibrium position is not the unstretched length

Physics HL · PHYS-C.1-501

Theme C: Wave behaviourC.1 (Simple harmonic motion: the defining equation $a = -\omega^{2}x$, the period of a mass-spring system, amplitude, the relation between speed and displacement, and oscillations about an equilibrium position)P1 · Section A5 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateDetermine

Question

The figure shows a light spring hanging from a fixed support. On the left the spring is drawn at its unstretched length of 0.500 m. On the right the same spring carries a mass of 0.600 kg and hangs at rest in equilibrium at a length of 0.750 m, so the static extension is 0.250 m. The mass is then pulled down a further 0.050 m below its equilibrium position and released from rest, so that it oscillates vertically about the equilibrium position with an amplitude of 0.050 m. Take $g = 9.81$ m s$^{-2}$, take the spring to be massless and to obey Hooke's law at all times, and ignore air resistance. Questions (a) to (e) all refer to this oscillation. Each is worth one mark and numerical answers should be given to three significant figures.

  1. [1 mark](a)

    Calculate the period of the vertical oscillation of the mass.

    (Calculate)
  2. [1 mark](b)

    Calculate the greatest speed reached by the mass during the oscillation.

    (Calculate)
  3. [1 mark](c)

    Calculate the speed of the mass at the instant when it is 0.030 m below its equilibrium position.

    (Calculate)
  4. [1 mark](d)

    Determine the greatest length of the spring at any point during the oscillation.

    (Determine)
  5. [1 mark](e)

    Calculate the magnitude of the net force on the mass when it is at the lowest point of the oscillation.

    (Calculate)

Reveal the answer

(a) B. In equilibrium the spring force balances the weight, so
$mg = ke$ and $k = (0.600)(9.81)/0.250 = 23.5$ N m$^{-1}$. Then
$T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.600/23.5} = 2\pi(0.1597) = 1.00$ s. Substituting
$k = mg/e$ first gives the neater form $T = 2\pi\sqrt{e/g}$, in which the mass has
cancelled: the period depends only on how far the spring hangs down under its own
load, and not on the load or on how far it is then pulled. (A1)

(b) D. The angular frequency is $\omega = 2\pi/T = 6.26$ rad s$^{-1}$ and the
greatest speed is reached at the equilibrium position, where
$v_{\max} = \omega A = 6.26 \times 0.050 = 0.313$ m s$^{-1}$. The average speed over
a quarter cycle is only 0.199 m s$^{-1}$, because the mass is slow near the ends.
(A1)

(c) A. Speed and displacement are related by $v^{2} = \omega^{2}(A^{2} - x^{2})$,
so $v = 6.26\sqrt{0.050^{2} - 0.030^{2}} = 6.26\sqrt{0.0016} = 6.26 \times 0.0400 =
0.251$ m s$^{-1}$. The displacement enters squared, so the speed at three fifths of
the amplitude is still four fifths of the maximum, not two fifths of it. (A1)

(d) C. Simple harmonic motion is motion *about the equilibrium position*, and the
amplitude is measured from there. The length of the spring therefore oscillates
between $0.750 - 0.050 = 0.700$ m and $0.750 + 0.050 = 0.800$ m. The unstretched
length of 0.500 m sets the equilibrium position but plays no part in the excursion.
(A1)

(e) D. The net force in simple harmonic motion is $F = -m\omega^{2}x$ measured
from equilibrium, and at the lowest point $x = +A = 0.050$ m, so
$|F| = kA = 23.5 \times 0.050 = 1.18$ N upwards. It is worth checking this against the
forces directly: the spring is then stretched by $0.250 + 0.050 = 0.300$ m, so it
pulls up with $23.5 \times 0.300 = 7.06$ N against a weight of 5.89 N, leaving 1.18 N.
(A1)

Markscheme notes

One mark per question, no negative marking, and no method marks: the key is the answer.

(a) Accept 1.00 s. Do not accept 1.42 s, which is a pendulum period built from the
0.500 m unstretched length. An answer of 0.449 s shows the amplitude being used as a
length in the same formula; the period of a simple harmonic oscillator does not
depend on the amplitude.

(b) Accept 0.313 m s⁻¹. An answer of 0.199 m s⁻¹ is the mean speed over a quarter
cycle and is the commonest wrong answer; it is worth drawing the distinction
explicitly if a candidate raises it.

(c) Accept 0.251 m s⁻¹. An answer of 0.125 m s⁻¹ shows a linear rule being used in
place of the quadrature one, which is the specific error this question tests.

(d) Accept 0.800 m. Do not accept 1.000 m. That value is right for a different
experiment — release from the natural length gives a greatest extension of twice the
static extension — and a candidate who offers it has substituted a remembered result
for the situation described.

(e) Accept 1.18 N. An answer of zero confuses a turning point with an equilibrium
point, and the confusion is worth naming: the mass is at rest for an instant precisely
because the net force is at its greatest there. An answer of 7.06 N has computed the
spring force rather than the resultant.

Why this question is hard

A vertical mass–spring system is the standard place where a candidate's two mental
pictures of an oscillator come apart. One picture is of the spring: it has a natural
length, and pulling it down stretches it further. The other is of the oscillation: it
is centred on the equilibrium position, and everything — displacement, speed,
restoring force — is measured from there. The figure draws the two pictures side by
side because the questions only come out right if the second one is used.

Part (a) is where the wrong picture costs most. Reaching for $2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ is
natural, because it is the formula most candidates have used most often, and with the
0.500 m unstretched length it produces a plausible 1.42 s. But that is the period of a
pendulum, whose restoring force comes from gravity acting on a fixed length. Here the
restoring force comes from the spring, and the period is $2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$. The useful
step is to eliminate $k$ using the static equilibrium condition $mg = ke$, which
collapses the whole thing to $T = 2\pi\sqrt{e/g}$: the period is decided by the
0.250 m the spring sags under its load, and neither the 0.600 kg nor the 0.050 m pull
appears in it at all. That is why a candidate who uses the amplitude as the length gets
0.449 s, and why both wrong answers are wrong for the same reason — they put a length
into the formula that the oscillator's period does not depend on.

Part (d) is the same trap in a different coat. The result that a mass released from a
spring's natural length falls to twice the static extension is real and well taught;
applied here it gives 1.000 m. But that experiment starts at the natural length with
the mass at rest and no oscillation centred anywhere. This one starts 0.050 m below
equilibrium, so the amplitude is 0.050 m and the longest length is 0.800 m. A correct
result from another setup is the most convincing kind of wrong answer, which is exactly
why it is offered.

Part (c) and part (e) close on the two distinctions that survive once the geometry is
right. Speed does not fall linearly with displacement — the relation
$v^{2} = \omega^{2}(A^{2} - x^{2})$ is a quadrature one, so at three fifths of the
amplitude the mass still has four fifths of its maximum speed. And a turning point is
not an equilibrium point: at the lowest point the velocity is zero because the net
force is largest, 1.18 N upwards, which is what sends the mass back.
