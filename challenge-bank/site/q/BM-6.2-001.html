Two matrices, one product, opposite instructions

Business management SL · BM-6.2-001

Unit 6: 6.2 Ansoff matrix and 6.4 Boston Consulting Group (BCG) matrix (the SL Toolkit). P1 Section A.P1 · Section A15 marksdifficulty 4CalculateExplainEvaluate

Brackenwood Pet Foods

Brackenwood Pet Foods is a family-owned manufacturer of dry dog food, based in one factory and selling through pet shops and a small number of garden centres. The directors use the BCG matrix each year to decide where to invest, measuring market share within the premium dog-food segment, and they use the Ansoff matrix whenever they consider a new product or a new market.

The premium segment is worth EUR 150 million a year. Supermarket own-label dog food, which Brackenwood does not supply, is a separate value segment three times larger. Against the whole dog-food market, Brackenwood's best-selling product, Adult Complete, has a share of 11%, while the value-segment leader has 13%.

The directors treat market growth of 10% or more a year as high, and a relative market share of 1.0 or more as high. The table gives the four main products.

Question

The directors of Brackenwood Pet Foods must decide where to invest over the next three years.

  1. [5 marks](a)

    Using the table, calculate the relative market share of each of the four products and classify each one using the BCG matrix. Show your working.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Explain how the Ansoff matrix would classify two growth options open to Brackenwood, using examples from the case.

    (Explain)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    Evaluate whether Brackenwood should follow the BCG recommendation to harvest Adult Complete. Justify your answer with reference to the Ansoff matrix and to the market data.

    (Evaluate)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) Relative market share = own share divided by the largest rival's share. Puppy Complete 22/20 = 1.10; Adult Complete 34/18 = 1.89; Senior Care 7/26 = 0.27; Grain-Free Fresh 9/24 = 0.38. (M1)(A1)(A1)

Growth: Puppy Complete 14% (high), Adult Complete 3% (low), Senior Care 2% (low), Grain-Free Fresh 16% (high).

Classification: Puppy Complete is a star, with high growth and high relative share. Adult Complete is a cash cow, with low growth and high relative share. Senior Care is a dog, with low growth and low relative share. Grain-Free Fresh is a question mark, with high growth and low relative share. (A1)(A1)

(b) Ansoff classifies growth by whether the product and the market are existing or new. Market development means an existing product sold into a new market: Adult Complete sold through the supermarket value channel, which Brackenwood does not currently supply. The product already exists; only the market is new. (A1)(A1)

Market penetration means more of an existing product sold into the existing market: winning more shelf space for Adult Complete in the pet shops and garden centres it already uses. (A1)

Also creditable: product development, such as new Grain-Free recipes for existing premium customers, and diversification, a new product for a new market, which is the riskiest quadrant. (A1)

(c) Indicative evaluation. The BCG reading is clear. Adult Complete has a relative market share of 1.89 and market growth of only 3%, so it is a classic cash cow: the matrix says take its cash and invest it in the star, Puppy Complete. On the premium-segment numbers this is textbook, and harvesting means withdrawing promotion and product development from Adult Complete rather than reinvesting in it.

Ansoff contradicts that instruction. The lowest-risk growth available to Brackenwood is market development: taking the existing Adult Complete product into the supermarket value segment. That is a new market for an existing product, the second-least-risky quadrant, and it needs investment, which is exactly what harvesting removes. So the BCG instruction to take cash out of Adult Complete would close the one low-risk growth option the firm has.

The data make the clash worse. The classification depends on how the market is defined. Measured against the premium segment, Adult Complete has a relative share of 1.89; measured against the whole dog-food market, its share is 11% against the value leader's 13%, a relative share of 0.85, which is a dog, not a cash cow. The same product carries opposite BCG labels. The tool cannot settle the question until the directors decide which market they intend to compete in, and that is a strategic choice, not a calculation.

A defensible answer can still harvest: the firm is small, Puppy Complete is the star, and cash is scarce. The strongest answers notice that harvesting Adult Complete would destroy the Ansoff option the same product makes possible, and recommend funding market development of the cash cow instead. Credit any supported conclusion. (A1)(A1)(A1)(A1)

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for the relative-share method and (A1)(A1) for the four correct figures. Award (A1)(A1) for the four correct classifications; a candidate who misclassifies one product but applies the 10% and 1.0 rules consistently earns one of the two. Accept 0.38 or 0.375 for Grain-Free Fresh and 0.27 for Senior Care. A candidate who computes relative share as the largest rival divided by the firm's own share reverses the matrix and cannot earn the classification marks.

(b) Two marks for market development correctly identified and applied to the supermarket channel; one mark for a correctly applied market penetration example; one further mark for either product development or diversification correctly applied. Do not credit new product, new market as market development.

(c) The discriminating marks are for seeing that the Ansoff market-development option requires the very investment that BCG harvesting removes, and for noticing that the cash-cow label depends on the premium-segment definition. A candidate who simply repeats the BCG classification and recommends harvesting cannot earn more than two marks. Credit a conclusion that harvests only if it is justified by the firm's lack of funds, not by the claim that Adult Complete has no growth left.

Why this question is hard

The item is built so that two accepted tools give opposite instructions about the same product. On the premium-segment data, Adult Complete is a textbook cash cow: 3% growth and a relative share of 1.89, five times the 0.38 of the question mark. The BCG logic is to take the cash it generates and put it into the star, Puppy Complete. A student who does the classification correctly and stops there will recommend harvesting.

Ansoff changes the answer, because it asks a different question. BCG asks where the cash is; Ansoff asks how risky growth would be. The least risky growth Brackenwood has is market development, the existing Adult Complete product sold into the supermarket value segment, which is precisely the product BCG has told the firm to stop investing in. Harvesting the cash cow and pursuing the Ansoff option are mutually exclusive, and the case does not say which the directors should prefer.

The lever is that the BCG classification is not a fact but a consequence of a choice about the market. Premium segment: relative share 1.89, cash cow. Whole market: 11% against 13%, relative share 0.85, dog. The matrix is often taught as though market share were given; here it is defined by the directors, and changing the definition changes the instruction. That is the point the strongest answers reach, and it is what makes the question a decision rather than an exercise in plotting.

Classic wrong turns: treating the BCG label as objective; recommending investment in the star without noticing that the firm has no cash because the cash cow is being harvested; placing market development in the diversification quadrant, which overstates its risk; and forgetting that the same product can be a cash cow and a dog at the same time.
