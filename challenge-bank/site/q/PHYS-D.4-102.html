D.4 A conducting rod sliding on rails in a uniform magnetic field

Physics HL · PHYS-D.4-102

Theme D: FieldsD.4 Induction (motional e.m.f., magnetic force on a current-carrying conductor)P215 marksdifficulty 5technology: permittedShow thatCalculateDetermineExplain

Question

Two long parallel conducting rails are a distance $l = 0.50$ m apart in a horizontal plane and are joined at one end by a resistor. A straight conducting rod of mass $m = 0.20$ kg rests across the rails, perpendicular to them, and can slide along them without friction. A uniform magnetic field of magnitude $B = 0.80$ T is directed vertically downwards, perpendicular to the plane of the rails.

The rod is given an initial speed $u = 4.0$ m/s along the rails. The total resistance of the circuit, including the rod and the rails, is $R = 2.0\ \Omega$. Air resistance is negligible.

  1. [3 marks](a)

    Show that, at an instant when the speed of the rod is $v$, the induced current is $I = \dfrac{Blv}{R}$ and hence that the rod experiences a retarding force $F = \dfrac{B^2 l^2 v}{R}$.

    (Show that)
  2. [5 marks](b)

    Using $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = v\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}$, show that the speed of the rod decreases linearly with distance, and hence determine the total distance $d$ travelled before the rod comes to rest.

    (Show that)
  3. [3 marks](c)

    Determine the total charge $Q$ that flows through the resistor before the rod comes to rest, without finding the current as a function of time.

    (Determine)
  4. [4 marks](d)

    A student states: *halving the resistance halves the stopping distance, so it must also halve the charge that flows.* Determine whether each half of this statement is correct, and account for the total energy dissipated in the resistor using the velocity-distance graph from part (b).

    (Explain)

Total: 15 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a) The e.m.f. induced across the rod is $\varepsilon = Blv$ (A1), so the current is $I = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{R} = \dfrac{Blv}{R}$ (A1). The force on a current-carrying conductor perpendicular to the field is $F = BIl$ (M1), and Lenz's law makes it oppose the motion, so $F = BIl = \dfrac{B^2l^2v}{R}$ (AG).

(b) Newton's second law along the rails gives $m\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\dfrac{B^2l^2}{R}v$ (A1). Substituting $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = v\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}$ gives $mv\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x} = -\dfrac{B^2l^2}{R}v$, so $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x} = -\dfrac{B^2l^2}{mR}$, a constant (M1)(A1). The speed therefore falls linearly with distance, from $u$ to $0$, and the distance is $d = \dfrac{muR}{B^2l^2}$ (M1). Numerically $d = \dfrac{0.20(4.0)(2.0)}{(0.80)^2(0.50)^2} = \dfrac{1.60}{0.160} = 10.0$ m (A1).

(c) The impulse of the magnetic force equals the change in momentum: $\int F\,\mathrm{d}t = m(u - 0)$, and since $F = BIl$ and $\int I\,\mathrm{d}t = Q$ we get $Bl\,Q = mu$ (M1)(A1), so $Q = \dfrac{mu}{Bl} = \dfrac{0.20(4.0)}{0.80(0.50)} = 2.0$ C (A1). Note that $R$ does not appear.

(d) First half: correct. From (b), $d = \dfrac{muR}{B^2l^2}$, so halving $R$ to $1.0\ \Omega$ gives $d = 5.0$ m, half of $10.0$ m (A1). Second half: incorrect. From (c), $Q = \dfrac{mu}{Bl}$ is independent of $R$: at $R = 1.0\ \Omega$ the current is twice as large at every instant but flows for half the time, and the two effects cancel exactly, so $Q = 2.0$ C again (A1). Energy: the graph of $v$ against $x$ is a straight line from $(0, u)$ to $(d, 0)$, so the work done against the retarding force is $W = \int_0^d F\,\mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{B^2l^2}{R}\int_0^d v\,\mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{B^2l^2}{R}\left(\tfrac12 u d\right)$ (M1) $= \dfrac{(0.80)^2(0.50)^2}{2.0}\times\tfrac12(4.0)(10.0) = 0.080 \times 20 = 1.6$ J, which equals the initial kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mu^2 = \tfrac12(0.20)(4.0)^2 = 1.6$ J (A1). All of the initial kinetic energy is dissipated as thermal energy in the circuit, whatever the value of $R$.

Markscheme notes

(a) (A1) for the motional e.m.f. expression, (A1) for the current, (M1) for F = BIl with the direction established by Lenz's law. A candidate who writes F = BIl but never substitutes the current earns (M1) only.

(b) (A1) for the equation of motion, (M1) for the chain-rule substitution, (A1) for recognising that the resulting gradient is constant and therefore that v falls linearly with x, (M1) for setting v = 0 to obtain d, (A1) for 10.0 m. Candidates who instead solve the differential equation in time to obtain v = u*exp(-t/tau) and integrate should be credited fully for d, and should additionally be credited for noting that the rod approaches rest asymptotically in time while covering a finite distance. Do not award the linearity mark to a candidate who assumes it.

(c) The question deliberately forbids finding I(t); award (M1) for the impulse-momentum statement, (A1) for the substitution F = BIl and the identification of the integral of I as Q, (A1) for 2.0 C. A candidate who integrates an exponential current and reaches the same number earns the marks, but only if the integral is evaluated correctly.

(d) One mark for each half of the student's statement, with the reasoning; (M1) for using the area under the v-x graph as the work integral; (A1) for 1.6 J and the identification with the initial kinetic energy. Condone g-free arithmetic here — no weight appears. Accept the equivalent route through integral of I squared R dt if the exponential form is used correctly.

Why this question is hard

This is a question about two quantities that look as though they must move together and do not. The stopping distance is proportional to $R$; the total charge is independent of it. Everything else in the item is arranged so that a candidate who tries to compute the charge by first finding the current as a function of time has to do a hard integral, while a candidate who steps back and uses impulse gets it in one line — and, more importantly, sees immediately why $R$ cancels.

The second idea is the substitution $\frac{dv}{dt} = v\frac{dv}{dx}$. Applied here it converts an exponential decay in time into a straight line in distance, which is much easier to work with and which makes the energy audit a matter of reading the area of a triangle. That is the whole point of the velocity-distance graph: $\int F\,dx$ becomes $\frac{B^2l^2}{R}$ times the area, and the area is $\tfrac12 u d$.

The classic wrong turn is to treat the motion as uniformly decelerated. The force is proportional to $v$, so the deceleration is not constant; assuming constant deceleration gives $d = \tfrac12 u t$ with a time obtained from an incorrect constant force, and every downstream number is then wrong. A second wrong turn is to conclude that because the rod never reaches rest in finite *time*, the distance is infinite — the distance is finite ($10$ m) and is reached asymptotically.

There is a third trap that is easy to miss even after a correct calculation. The three answers — $10$ m, $2.0$ C and $1.6$ J — sit next to each other on the page and invite the reading that they are three versions of the same quantity. They are not. Only the distance depends on the resistance; only the charge is independent of it; and the energy is independent of both $R$ and $B$, being simply the whole of the initial kinetic energy. Checking which parameters each result actually contains is the cheapest available check on whether the reasoning that produced it was the right reasoning, and it is what part (d) is testing.

Finally, note what the item does *not* require: at no point is the current written as a function of time. That is deliberate. The exponential form $v = ue^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = mR/(B^2l^2)$ is available and gives the same answers, but a candidate who reaches for it first has to integrate twice and is far more likely to make an arithmetic slip than one who uses impulse for the charge and the chain rule for the distance. Choosing the representation in which the answer is shortest is itself part of what is being examined.

Challenge lever
The two quantities a candidate would naturally quote together — how far the rod travels and how much charge flows — scale in opposite ways with the one parameter they would both be assumed to share. The distance is proportional to R and the charge is independent of it, because halving the resistance doubles the current at every instant while halving the time for which it flows. Reaching the result requires refusing one integration entirely: the impulse relation Bl*Q = m*delta(v) gives the charge without ever writing the current as a function of time, and the substitution dv/dt = v*dv/dx turns the distance into a straight line on a velocity-distance graph.
