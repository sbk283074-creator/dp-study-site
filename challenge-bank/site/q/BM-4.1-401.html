4.1 Market share rising in both regions while the overall share falls

Business management SL · BM-4.1-401

Unit 4: MarketingUnit 4: 4.1 The role of marketing (market share and market growth); 4.2 Marketing planning; 6.7 Descriptive statistics (weighted averages and the comparison of groups)P2 · Section B11 marksdifficulty 4technology: permittedCalculateExplainEvaluate

Question

The figure shows a firm's sales in two regions in two consecutive years. The overall market is 1 000 units in each year, so the total market has not grown or shrunk, but the two regions have changed size. In year 1 the North market was 400 units and the firm sold 80 of them; the South market was 600 units and the firm sold 210. In year 2 the North market was 700 units and the firm sold 147; the South market was 300 units and the firm sold 111. The figure prints the firm's share of each regional market.

The managing director tells the board that the firm has lost market share in a year in which it performed better in every single region, and asks the marketing department both to explain how that can have happened and to say what the marketing plan should do about it.

(a) Calculate the firm's overall market share in each of the two years, and the percentage growth or decline of each regional market between the two years.

(b) The firm's share of the North market and its share of the South market both rise, yet its overall share falls. Explain how both statements can be true, using the figures.

(c) Evaluate the managing director's claim that the firm lost market share because it performed worse, and recommend, with supporting figures, where the marketing plan for year 3 should concentrate its effort.

Reveal the answer

(a) In year 1 the firm sells 80 + 210 = 290 units out of a market of 1 000, so its overall share is 290 / 1 000 = 29.0 per cent. In year 2 it sells 147 + 111 = 258 units, so its overall share is 258 / 1 000 = 25.8 per cent (M1)(A1). The North market grew from 400 to 700 units, a growth of (700 - 400) / 400 = 75 per cent. The South market fell from 600 to 300 units, a decline of (600 - 300) / 600 = 50 per cent (A1).

(b) An overall market share is not an average of the two regional shares taken equally; it is a weighted average in which the weight of each region is the size of that region's market. In year 1 the weights are 400 and 600, so the overall share is (400 x 0.20 + 600 x 0.35) / 1 000 = (80 + 210) / 1 000 = 0.290. In year 2 the weights are 700 and 300, giving (700 x 0.21 + 300 x 0.37) / 1 000 = (147 + 111) / 1 000 = 0.258 (M1)(A1). Both regional shares did rise, from 20.0 to 21.0 per cent in the North and from 35.0 to 37.0 per cent in the South, so the firm is right about its own performance in each region (A1). But the weight moved at the same time and moved against the firm: the North, where it holds only about a fifth of the market, grew by 75 per cent and now carries 70 per cent of the overall market, while the South, where it holds more than a third, halved. The gain from performing better in each region is more than cancelled by the loss from being under-represented in the region that grew, and the two effects together leave the overall share 3.2 percentage points lower (A1).

(c) The claim is half right, and the half that is wrong matters. It is wrong to say the firm performed worse: its share rose in both regions, from 20.0 to 21.0 per cent and from 35.0 to 37.0 per cent, and a rising share in a market is the clearest available evidence of competitive performance (A1). But it is true that the year was a setback, and the reason is not only the change in the mix. The firm's own volume fell from 290 units to 258, a decline of 11 per cent, in a market whose total size did not change at all; the mixture effect explains why the share fell faster than the volume did, but it does not explain the fall in volume, and a firm that sells fewer units in a static market has lost ground by any commercial measure (A1). The mixture effect also understates the danger, because the region that shrank is the region where the firm is strong: if the South keeps contracting at 50 per cent a year, the firm's strongest position disappears on its own, whatever it does about its share (M1). The marketing plan should therefore concentrate on the North. At 700 units and growing at 75 per cent, the North is where the market is, and the firm holds only 21 per cent of it. Lifting the North share to the level already achieved in the South, 37 per cent, would mean selling 259 units there instead of 147, an extra 112 units, and would raise the overall share to (259 + 111) / 1 000 = 37.0 per cent, well above the 29.0 per cent of year 1 (A1). Chasing share in the South would be harder work for less: the market there is halving, so gaining share in it means winning customers in a shrinking pool while the firm's absolute volume still falls. The recommendation is a North-led plan with a target share stated as a number, and the South treated as a market to defend rather than to grow.

Markscheme notes

(a) (M1) for summing the firm's units in each year; (A1) for 29.0 and 25.8 per cent; (A1) for +75 per cent in the North and -50 per cent in the South. Accept the shares given as 290/1 000 and 258/1 000 without conversion to percentages.

(b) (M1) for identifying the overall share as a weighted average with the regional market sizes as weights; (A1) for the two weighted calculations producing 0.290 and 0.258; (A1) for stating the two regional shares as rising, 20.0 to 21.0 and 35.0 to 37.0; (A1) for the explanation that the weight moved towards the region where the firm is weak, with the 400-to-700 and 600-to-300 change cited. A candidate who calls the result impossible, or who says one of the figures must be wrong, scores nothing: the part is testing whether the arithmetic of a changing mixture has been understood. Do not accept a purely verbal answer that does not produce the two weighted sums.

(c) (A1) for rejecting the claim that performance fell, with the two regional increases cited; (A1) for the separate observation that the firm's own volume fell from 290 to 258 in an unchanged market, which is a real loss and not a mixing effect; (M1) for the strategic risk that the strong region is the shrinking one; (A1) for a quantified recommendation. Accept any quantified target in the North, such as matching the South's 37 per cent share, or restoring the 29 per cent overall share, provided the calculation is shown. Do not accept 'increase advertising' or 'improve the product' without a figure and a region.

Why this question is hard

The arithmetic in this item is four divisions and a subtraction, and none of it is harder than a share calculation in any other question. The difficulty is that the firm's share rises in every component and falls in the total, which looks like an error until the candidate notices that the components carry different weights and that the weights have moved. The effect has a name in statistics and appears under several names in marketing, and it is worth having because it reverses conclusions in exactly this way: a firm, a school, a hospital or a sales team can improve in every group it is measured on and still deteriorate in aggregate, provided the groups have changed size.

The figures are chosen so that neither of the two easy explanations survives. The first easy explanation is that the market grew and the firm did not keep up, which is ruled out by fixing the total market at 1 000 units in both years. The second is that the whole movement is an artefact and the firm has nothing to worry about, which is ruled out by the fall in the firm's own volume from 290 units to 258 in a market of constant size. So the candidate is left with the mixture effect as the explanation of why the share fell faster than the volume, and the volume fall as a separate and more serious fact that the mixture does not explain.

The recommendation is where the item earns its marks, because the two obvious answers are both wrong. Defending the South is defending the region where the firm is strongest, which feels prudent and is not: the market there is halving, so holding share means holding a shrinking base, and the firm's strongest position dissolves on its own. Attacking the North sounds like the harder task, because the firm holds only 21 per cent there, but the market is growing at 75 per cent and the firm already holds more than a third of the neighbouring region, so the capability exists. Growing a share in a growing market also costs less per unit than growing one in a shrinking market, because part of the growth arrives with the market rather than having to be won.

There is a general lesson about which average to quote, and it is the same one that appears in the decision about whether to report a company-wide figure or a segment-by-segment one. The overall share is what the board and the investors see, so it cannot be dismissed; the regional shares are what the business can act on, so they cannot be ignored. The mistake is to treat either as the whole truth, and the managing director's error is to read the aggregate as a verdict on performance when it is really a statement about the market the firm happens to be in.

Challenge lever
A share is an average, and an average over a changing mixture can move against every one of its components. Here the firm's share rises in both regions and falls overall, because the region where it is weak is the region that grew: the North doubled in importance while the South halved. The change in the weighting more than cancels the improvement in each region, and the candidate has to say so with numbers rather than describe it as a paradox. The item then refuses to let the explanation stand alone, because the firm's own volume fell from 290 units to 258 in a market of fixed total size, so the loss is not purely a mixing artefact and the managing director's complaint is half right. Saying which half is the point of the final part.

The source presents two groups whose rates move in one direction while the combined rate moves the other, and asks for the paradox to be identified. Here the setting is a firm's market share in two regions and the numbers are chosen so that the total market is constant, which removes market growth as an explanation and forces the change in composition to carry the whole effect; the firm's own volume is also made to fall, so the candidate cannot call the result a pure artefact. The final part asks for a marketing recommendation with a quantified target, which converts an exercise in statistics into a business decision, and the recommended region is the one where the firm is weakest, which the source material gives no reason to consider.
Verification
Each regional share was recomputed from the units and the market sizes, and the overall shares were computed both directly from the totals and as weighted averages of the regional shares, agreeing at 0.290 and 0.258. The regional growth rates were computed as +75 per cent and -50 per cent. The North target was computed as 37 per cent of 700 units, giving 259 units against an actual 147, an increase of 112 units, and the resulting overall share was confirmed as 370/1 000. The direction of the three movements was checked explicitly: both regional shares up, the overall share down.
