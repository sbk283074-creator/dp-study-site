Harbour Bikes Ltd

Harbour Bikes Ltd assembles and sells bicycles from a single site. Extracts from its final accounts for the year ended 31 December 2026 are given below, together with a summary cash-flow statement for the same year.

The company's main supplier has written to say that from 1 March it will require payment within 14 days instead of the current 45 days. Trade payables at the year end were €132 000, representing approximately 45 days of purchases.

Income statement extracts (€000)

| Revenue | 1 840 |
|---|---|
| Cost of sales | 1 104 |
| Gross profit | 736 |
| Expenses | 496 |
| Net profit | 240 |

Balance sheet extracts (€000)

| Inventory | 210 |
|---|---|
| Trade receivables | 165 |
| Cash | 48 |
| Trade payables | 132 |

Cash-flow summary (€000)

| Net cash flow from operating activities | 186 |
|---|---|
| Net cash flow from investing activities | (140) |
| Net cash flow from financing activities | (60) |
| Opening cash balance | 62 |
| Closing cash balance | 48 |

Question

The finance director must decide whether the business can meet the supplier's new 14-day terms.

  1. [4 marks](a)

    Calculate the gross profit margin and the net profit margin for the year ended 31 December 2026.

    (Calculate)
  2. [4 marks](b)

    Calculate the current ratio and the acid test (liquid) ratio at 31 December 2026.

    (Calculate)
  3. [6 marks](c)

    Using your calculations in (a) and (b) together with the cash-flow data, analyse whether Harbour Bikes Ltd can meet the supplier's new 14-day payment terms.

    (Analyse)

Total: 14 marks. Try the question before revealing anything below.

Reveal the answer

(a)

$$\text{Gross profit margin}=\frac{\text{gross profit}}{\text{revenue}}\times100=\frac{736}{1840}\times100=40.0\%$$ (M1)(A1)

$$\text{Net profit margin}=\frac{\text{net profit}}{\text{revenue}}\times100=\frac{240}{1840}\times100=13.0\%$$ (M1)(A1)

(b)

Current assets $=210+165+48=423$ (€000); current liabilities $=132$ (€000).

$$\text{Current ratio}=\frac{423}{132}=3.20\ (\text{or }3.20:1)$$ (M1)(A1)

$$\text{Acid test ratio}=\frac{\text{current assets}-\text{inventory}}{\text{current liabilities}}=\frac{423-210}{132}=\frac{213}{132}=1.61$$ (M1)(A1)

(c) Indicative analysis:

The profitability and liquidity figures look strong. A gross margin of 40% and a net margin of 13% show a healthy business, and a current ratio of 3.20 with an acid test of 1.61 is well above the textbook benchmarks of 2:1 and 1:1. On these figures alone, the business looks able to absorb a change in payment terms. (A1)

But the ratios measure a point in time, and the trend is the problem. The cash-flow summary shows net cash from operations of €186 000 being more than consumed by investing (€140 000) and financing (€60 000) outflows, so cash *fell* by €14 000 over the year, from €62 000 to €48 000. (A1)(A1) A business whose cash balance is falling while it is profitable is growing faster than its working capital can support.

The 14-day terms are the real test. Payables of €132 000 currently represent 45 days of purchases; requiring payment in 14 days means settling the same purchases roughly 31 days earlier. The cash tied up is about

$$132\times\frac{31}{45}=\€91\ 000,$$

which must be found immediately, as a one-off, on top of ongoing trading. (A1)(A1) Against a closing cash balance of €48 000, that cannot be paid from cash. Even if the transition itself were a one-off, the change permanently raises the working capital requirement: the business would in future pay roughly 31 days earlier on every order, so the €91 000 is not recovered once the change has been absorbed. Meeting the terms therefore requires a permanent source of finance, not merely a temporary adjustment.

So where does that leave the ratios? The acid test of 1.61 looks reassuring, but €165 000 of the liquid assets are trade receivables — cash that has not yet arrived — and the inventory of €210 000 cannot be used to pay a supplier at all. The current ratio of 3.20 is flattered by that inventory. (A1)

Conclusion. The business is profitable but illiquid in the short term, and it cannot meet 14-day terms from its own resources without first slowing its investment spending, negotiating a longer period to phase in the change, accelerating receivables collection, or arranging an overdraft. The ratios in (a) and (b) describe the business it has been; the cash-flow statement describes what is happening to it now — and for this decision the cash flow is the relevant evidence.

Markscheme notes

(a) Award (M1) for each correct formula, (A1) for each correct percentage. Accept 40% and 13.0% (or 13.04%).

(b) Award (M1) for identifying current assets correctly (inventory + receivables + cash = 423) and (A1) for 3.20; (M1) for excluding inventory and (A1) for 1.61. Candidates who include inventory in the acid test (giving 3.20 again) forfeit the second pair of marks.

(c) Six marks. Award: (i) one for noting that the ratios look healthy against benchmarks; (ii) two for extracting the cash *trend* from the statement (net decrease of €14 000) and linking it to investment exceeding operating cash generation; (iii) two for quantifying the one-off cash requirement of roughly €91 000 and comparing it with the €48 000 available; (iv) one for the quality-of-liquidity point (receivables are not cash; inventory cannot pay suppliers). Follow through on the candidate's own figures.

A conclusion is not separately marked but the analysis must point somewhere. SL boundary: do not credit inventory turnover or debtor days calculations as *required* content (efficiency ratios are HL only), but credit them if used correctly as additional analysis — the question can be answered fully without them.

Why this question is hard

This question is built around a genuine and very common business situation: a profitable company that runs out of cash. The trap is that every ratio the student has been taught says the business is fine.

A 40% gross margin, a 13% net margin, a current ratio of 3.2 and an acid test of 1.61 — all above benchmark. A student who stops there concludes that Harbour Bikes can easily absorb the change. The evidence that says otherwise is in the cash-flow statement, which is the one document the ratios do not summarise: operations generated €186 000, but investing and financing consumed €200 000, so cash *fell* by €14 000 over the year.

The second step is quantifying the shock. Payables of €132 000 represent 45 days; paying in 14 means finding about 31 days' worth of purchases early — roughly €91 000, as a one-off, immediately. Against €48 000 of cash, that is not feasible, and the ratios cannot help because the assets they count are not spendable: €210 000 of inventory cannot be handed to a supplier, and €165 000 of receivables is money customers have not yet paid.

The generalisable lesson — and the reason this is worth six marks — is that ratios are a snapshot and cash flow is a film. For any decision about *timing* (can we pay in 14 days rather than 45?), the snapshot is the wrong evidence, however healthy it looks. That is also why the acid test is the more informative of the two liquidity ratios here but is still not sufficient: it correctly strips out inventory, but it still treats receivables as if they were cash.

Classic wrong turns: including inventory in the acid test; concluding from the 40% gross margin that the business is 'profitable so it can pay'; and treating the €48 000 closing balance as if it were available in full when it is already committed to ongoing trading.
