Mathematics AA HL · Internal assessment

The exploration

The exploration is 20% of your grade, marked by your own teacher and moderated by the IB. It is a piece of mathematics you choose, pursue, and write up in roughly 12–20 pages. It rewards depth over breadth, honesty over polish, and — the part students find hardest — your thinking being visible on the page. This page tells you exactly what a level 7 looks like, criterion by criterion, and how to build one.

20 marks · 20% of the grade Criteria A–E: 4 + 4 + 3 + 3 + 6 Coursework · Moderated

The five criteria at a glance

CriterionMarksThe one-sentence question the examiner is asking
A — Presentation4Is this a coherent, well-structured piece of mathematical writing that a reader can follow?
B — Mathematical communication4Is the mathematics correct, well-notated, and explained in appropriate language?
C — Personal engagement3Is this genuinely the student's own exploration, with evidence of independent thinking?
D — Reflection3Does the student evaluate their work, discuss limitations, and consider what would come next?
E — Use of mathematics6Is the mathematics correct, sophisticated, at the right level, and actually used to get somewhere?
Why this exists

The exploration is the only part of the diploma where you do mathematics rather than answer questions about it. The IB wants to see whether you can formulate a question, choose a method, notice that your first method is inadequate, and say so. That is why reflection and personal engagement have their own criteria: a technically perfect piece with no evidence of thinking caps out around 13/20.

Note on the rules you must check

Confirm the current requirements — page expectations, the declaration of academic honesty, and the exact wording of the criteria — against the current Mathematics: analysis and approaches guide and the handbook of procedures for your session. Your school's internal deadlines and any page or word guidance come from your teacher, not from this page.

Criterion A — Presentation

A level 7 (4/4) looks like

  • A clear rationale in the first half page: what you are investigating, why it interests you, and what the aim is.
  • Logical structure with headings and subheadings that match the actual journey of the maths.
  • Every diagram, table and graph is numbered, captioned and referred to in the text — never dropped in decoratively.
  • Equations typeset properly (use an equation editor); variables defined at first use.
  • Reads like a paper: introduction, development, conclusion, bibliography, appendices.

A level 3 (2/4) looks like

  • Opens with a dictionary definition ("Mathematics is the study of…") and no stated aim.
  • One long unbroken document with no signposting; the reader cannot tell where the argument is going.
  • Screenshots pasted in with no caption, axes unlabelled, units missing.
  • Handwritten expressions photographed, or maths typed as plain text (x^2 instead of \(x^2\)).
  • No bibliography, or a bibliography with sources never cited in the text.
Exam technique — the eight-minute read test

Give your draft to a friend who is not doing HL maths and give them eight minutes. If they cannot tell you (a) what question you were trying to answer and (b) what your answer was, your presentation is not yet at a 4. This single test catches most presentation problems.

Criterion B — Mathematical communication

A level 7 (4/4) looks like

  • Correct notation throughout: \(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\), \(\sum_{r=1}^{n}\), \(\int_a^b\), \(\equiv\) where identity is meant.
  • Definitions stated before use: "\(\sigma\) will denote the standard deviation throughout".
  • Results justified: "since \(\Delta<0\), the quadratic has no real roots, so the model predicts the ball never reaches the ground — which is why I rejected it."
  • Consistent, appropriate precision (3 s.f. unless stated), with units on every physical quantity.
  • Key results interpreted in words, not just numbers: what the answer means for the original question.

A level 3 (2/4) looks like

  • Uses "=" where "\(\approx\)" is meant; drops the "\(\mathrm{d}x\)" from integrals.
  • Variables introduced without definition, or reused with two different meanings.
  • Calculator output reproduced with no explanation of what was entered.
  • Inconsistent rounding (2 d.p. in one table, 5 s.f. in another).
  • Correct answers with no interpretation — the reader cannot tell why the number matters.
Common trap

Confusing "communication" with "length". Explaining a routine step in three paragraphs adds words, not marks. Conversely, writing "it is obvious that" before a non-obvious step loses a mark you could have had for one clause. Communicate the decisions, not the arithmetic.

Criterion C — Personal engagement

A level 7 (3/3) looks like

  • The topic is connected to something genuinely yours: a sport you play, an instrument, a family business, a game you coded.
  • You collect your own data (filming a throw, timing a kettle, running a simulation you wrote).
  • Independent choices are visible and justified: "I tried a linear model first; the residual plot showed curvature, so…"
  • You go beyond the textbook: an extension, a different case, a question the syllabus does not answer.
  • Your voice is present: "This surprised me because…", "I had assumed… but the data showed…".

A level 3 (1–2/3) looks like

  • A standard textbook investigation copied with different numbers.
  • Data downloaded from the internet with no reason given for choosing it.
  • No choices: the method is the only method anyone would have used.
  • Personal engagement asserted rather than shown: "I have always been passionate about mathematics."
  • Written in the passive voice throughout, with no first person anywhere.

How much personal engagement is enough?

How to use it

You need roughly three to five genuine decision points distributed through the piece, each with one or two sentences of justification. That is it. Concretely:

  1. Choose your own question, not one from a list of IA titles.
  2. Generate at least one dataset yourself (measurement, experiment, simulation).
  3. Make one methodological choice where a different choice was available, and say why.
  4. Explore one "what if" that the syllabus does not require.
  5. Write in the first person when describing those choices — "I chose", "I noticed", "I decided". The first person here is a strength, not informality.

Criterion D — Reflection

A level 7 (3/3) looks like

  • Reflection appears throughout, not only in a final paragraph.
  • You discuss the limitations of your model with specific causes: air resistance ignored, sample of 20, linear assumption at large \(x\).
  • You evaluate why a method failed, not just that it did.
  • You state what you would do next with more time or better data, and why that would help.
  • You connect the result back to the rationale: did you answer the question you started with?

A level 3 (1–2/3) looks like

  • A single paragraph at the end beginning "In conclusion, I have learnt a lot."
  • Generic limitations with no link to the actual mathematics ("there may have been human error").
  • No mention of what would be done differently.
  • Results restated but never evaluated.

How to write genuine reflection

How to use it — four sentence stems that score
  1. Surprise: "I expected \(X\), but the model predicted \(Y\); the reason is that …, which shows my assumption about … was wrong."
  2. Limitation with mechanism: "The model overestimates after \(t=40\) s because it assumes a constant cooling rate, whereas Newton's law makes the rate proportional to the temperature difference."
  3. Comparison: "The quadratic fit has \(R^2=0.98\) but predicts negative heights after \(t=6\) s, so the cubic is preferable despite the lower \(R^2\) on this range."
  4. Forward: "With a longer time window I would fit \(k\) by least squares rather than using two data points, which would reduce the sensitivity to measurement error in the first reading."

Place one of these after each major stage. Four or five well-placed reflective sentences across the piece is a 3; one paragraph at the end is a 1.

Criterion E — Use of mathematics

This is 6 of the 20 marks and the criterion that separates a 14 from a 19. The mathematics must be:

QualityWhat it meansLevel 7 vs level 3
RelevantThe maths actually answers the question posed7: every technique earns its place. 3: techniques listed for their own sake (a \(\chi^2\) test with no hypothesis).
Commensurate with the courseAt HL level, ideally using HL-only content7: uses HL techniques (integration by parts, differential equations, Maclaurin, complex numbers, hypothesis testing) meaningfully. 3: GCSE-level content, or HL content used wrongly.
CorrectNo errors that undermine the argument7: error-free, or any slip is minor and does not affect the conclusion. 3: an error that invalidates the result.
Thorough and preciseAppropriate precision, units, definitions7: consistent 3 s.f., units, defined variables. 3: inconsistent rounding, missing units.
With insightConnections made, structure noticed7: "the same recurrence appears in the loan model and the cooling model because both are geometric decay". 3: no connections drawn.
Common trap — sophistication is not difficulty

Dropping a Fourier transform or a contour integral into an exploration does not impress anyone; using it wrongly actively loses marks. The top band rewards mathematics you have genuinely understood and applied to a question you care about. A beautifully executed optimisation using calculus, with a real data set and a thoughtful error analysis, beats a mangled attempt at something from a second-year university course.

How to choose a topic: the 60-second viability test

How to use it

Answer these six questions out loud. Five yeses means start writing tomorrow; three or fewer means keep looking.

  1. Can I state the question in one sentence? If you cannot name the specific quantity you will find, you do not have a topic yet.
  2. Do I have (or can I collect) data or a definite setup within one week? No data, no exploration.
  3. Is at least one HL technique genuinely needed? Name it. If the answer is "not really", the ceiling is a 4 on criterion E.
  4. Is there an obvious way to make it harder after the first result? You need 3–4 escalations, so there must be somewhere to go.
  5. Does it connect to something I actually care about? You will spend 30+ hours on this.
  6. Will it still be interesting when the first model fails? The failure is where the marks are, so you need to want to fix it.

The ideal structure

How to use it — section by section
  1. Title page. Title, your candidate details, word/page count.
  2. Rationale (½–1 page). What is the question, why it matters to you, what you will do. End with a one-sentence aim starting "The aim of this exploration is to…".
  3. Background and definitions (½ page). Only what the reader needs; define every variable.
  4. Stage 1 — the simplest useful model. Get a result, however crude. Reflect.
  5. Stage 2 — relax an assumption. Add air resistance, use a measured parameter, allow a non-constant rate. Compare with Stage 1. Reflect.
  6. Stage 3 — a second method or a validation. Solve numerically and compare with the analytic answer; test the model against data you did not use to build it. Reflect.
  7. Stage 4 — extension. A different case, a sensitivity analysis, a "what if the parameter doubles?" This is where criterion C is won.
  8. Conclusion (½–1 page). Answer the original question with numbers and units. Then limitations with mechanisms, then what you would do next.
  9. Bibliography. Every source cited in the text, in a consistent format.
  10. Appendix (optional). Raw data, long tables, code. Keep the main text clean.

Marking traps — what actually costs you marks

Common trap
  1. No stated aim. Everything downstream depends on it: relevance, reflection, conclusion.
  2. Mathematics above the level of the course, used incorrectly. This is worse than simple mathematics done well.
  3. Descriptive statistics alone. A mean, a standard deviation and a scatter plot is not HL mathematics. If you use statistics, you need inference: hypothesis tests, confidence intervals, distributions.
  4. Copying a method without understanding it. Examiners ask "why this step?" — if the working cannot be explained, criterion B collapses.
  5. Reflection only in the conclusion. Drops you to 1–2 on criterion D.
  6. Results with no units or absurd precision. "The height is 3.4285719 m" from data measured with a ruler.
  7. Plagiarism of structure. Following a published exploration step by step is academic dishonesty even if the numbers are yours.
  8. Too long. A 40-page exploration is usually padded, and padding dilutes the good mathematics. Ask your teacher what length they expect.

Practical advice

Start in the long holiday before Y13 if you can — you will have covered enough HL content by the end of Y12 for almost every topic above, and starting early removes the single biggest risk to the exploration: running out of time while the mathematics is still interesting.

Academic honesty

The exploration must be your own work. Specifically:

  • Using a source for a formula, a dataset or a method is fine — cite it, both in the text and in the bibliography.
  • Copying text, diagrams or worked solutions from a website, a textbook, a friend or an AI tool is not fine. Paraphrasing without acknowledgement is also misconduct.
  • Using AI or a tutor to explain something you then write up yourself is legitimate; submitting generated text or solutions as your own is not. When in doubt, declare the assistance you received.
  • Keep your raw data, drafts and calculation files. If the IB moderates your work, being able to show your process protects you.
  • Your teacher must be able to confirm the work is yours; you will sign a declaration to that effect.
The honest test

If a classmate read your exploration and then read your sources, would they be able to tell which sentences are yours? If not, rewrite it. The strongest explorations sound like one specific person thinking, and that voice is also what criterion C rewards.

Submission checklist

Worked mini-exemplar — OUTLINE ONLY

This is an outline, not a finished exploration

What follows is a plan for one idea, written to show how a single topic can be designed to hit all five criteria. There is no data, no full working and no conclusion here — do not submit anything like this. Use it as a template for your own planning.

Title: "How long does my coffee actually stay drinkable? From Newton's law to a changing room temperature"

Rationale (personal). I drink two cups of coffee every morning in a mug my grandmother gave me, and I always misjudge how long I have before it goes cold. I want a model that predicts the time at which the coffee falls below 60°C, and I want to know how much it matters that the room itself warms up while the coffee cools.

Aim. To model the cooling of a mug of coffee using Newton's law of cooling, to fit the cooling constant from my own measurements, and to compare that model with one in which the ambient temperature is itself changing.

Stage 1 — the textbook model

Separate and solve \(\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = -k(T-T_a)\) with constant \(T_a\): \(T(t) = T_a + (T_0-T_a)\mathrm{e}^{-kt}\). Take \(T_a = 21^\circ\)C, \(T_0 = 88^\circ\)C, estimate \(k\) from two of my measurements, and predict the drinkable window. Mathematics: separable differential equation, exponential model — HL Topic 5.

Planned reflection: using only two data points makes \(k\) very sensitive to measurement error; a least-squares fit on \(\ln(T-T_a)\) against \(t\) would use all the data, and I will do that in Stage 3.

Stage 2 — better estimation of \(k\)

Take logs: \(\ln(T-T_a) = \ln(T_0-T_a) - kt\), a straight line in \(t\) with gradient \(-k\). Use linear regression on 12 measurements, quote \(r\), and comment on the residuals. Mathematics: logs, regression, correlation — Topics 1 and 4.

Planned reflection: the residual plot is curved, not random, which suggests the constant-\(T_a\) assumption is wrong — probably because my radiator switched on during the experiment.

Stage 3 — a changing ambient temperature

Model \(T_a(t) = 21 + 0.02t\) and solve \(\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = -k(T - T_a(t))\) using an integrating factor (or Euler's method with a table if the analytic form gets messy). Compare the predicted drinkable window with Stage 2. Mathematics: linear first-order ODE, numerical solution, comparison of models.

Planned reflection: the two models differ by less than 40 seconds over the relevant range, so the simpler model is adequate for my purposes — an honest "the harder model was not worth it" is a legitimate and valuable conclusion.

Stage 4 — extension: mug shape and surface area

Repeat the experiment with a lidded travel mug and a wide bowl, and relate the fitted \(k\) to the exposed surface area of the liquid. Predict \(k\) for a third container and test it. Mathematics: modelling, proportional reasoning, validation against unused data.

How this outline maps to the criteria

CriterionWhere this design earns the marks
A — PresentationClear aim, four numbered stages, one captioned graph per stage, a data appendix.
B — CommunicationVariables defined (\(T\), \(T_a\), \(k\), \(t\)), units on every temperature, consistent 3 s.f., each result interpreted ("the coffee reaches 60°C after 11.4 minutes").
C — Personal engagementMy own mug, my own measurements, my own "what if the room warms up" question, plus a fourth-stage idea (surface area) that no textbook asked for.
D — ReflectionA reflective paragraph after each of the four stages, including a limitation with a mechanism (residual curvature from the radiator) and a forward statement (least-squares fit).
E — MathematicsHL differential equations, regression, logarithms, and a genuine comparison of two models — all relevant to the question, none decorative.

Notice what makes this work: the mathematics is serviceable (a good HL student can do all of it), the data is mine, and there is a real reason to go further at each stage. That is the whole design problem of the exploration.

Practice — self-assessment

  1. A draft opens: "Mathematics is everywhere in our daily lives. In this exploration I will investigate calculus." Identify three specific problems and rewrite the opening in one sentence.

    Show answer

    Problems: (1) a generic statement of fact rather than a rationale; (2) "investigate calculus" is a topic, not a question — no quantity is named, so relevance cannot be judged; (3) no personal connection, so criterion C starts at zero.

    Rewrite: "I play as a goalkeeper and want to know how much the angle of a penalty kick changes the chance of a save, so the aim of this exploration is to determine, using trigonometric and probabilistic models, the shot placement that minimises the probability of a goal."

    Answer: one sentence containing a personal hook, a named quantity, and the methods. That is the whole rationale.

  2. A student's exploration uses only the mean, median, standard deviation and a scatter graph of 30 data points. What is the likely ceiling on criterion E, and what should they add?

    Show answer

    Descriptive statistics alone is below the level of the course, so criterion E would be capped around 3–4/6. They should add inferential mathematics: state a hypothesis and test it (a \(t\)-test or \(\chi^2\) test), build a confidence interval, or model the data with a distribution and test the fit. If the data is a time series, a regression with residual analysis plus a hypothesis test on the gradient is a natural upgrade.

    Answer: add inference. "Description" is GCSE; "inference" is HL.

  3. Write a reflection sentence that would earn a mark, for a model that predicts a ball's height going negative after 6 seconds.

    Show answer

    Model answer: "The quadratic model predicts \(h<0\) for \(t>6\) s, which is physically impossible; this happens because the model ignores air resistance and because I extrapolated beyond the range of my data (0–5 s), so the prediction should not be used outside that interval."

    Answer: the mark comes from naming the mechanism (constant acceleration, extrapolation) rather than saying "the model is not accurate".

  4. How many decimal places should you report for a length measured with a school ruler, and why does over-reporting cost marks?

    Show answer

    A school ruler reads to about 0.5 mm, so report to the nearest millimetre (or 3 s.f. if the value is large). Reporting 3.4285719 m claims a precision of micrometres, which is false precision: the data cannot support it, it undermines criterion B ("thorough and precise"), and it suggests to the examiner that the relationship between data and calculation has not been thought about.

    Answer: match the precision of your measurements; consistency is itself a mark.

  5. Your first model fails completely. Is this a disaster?

    Show answer

    No — it is the best thing that can happen, provided you write it up. Criteria C and D reward the response to failure: explain why it failed, what assumption was wrong, and what you did next. The exploration that goes right first time has nothing to reflect on and typically caps at 2/3 on criterion D. Keep the failed attempt in the final piece.

    Answer: a documented failure with a diagnosed cause is worth more than an unexamined success.

  6. You found a complete worked solution to your exact question online. What is the honest way to use it?

    Show answer

    Do not submit it or follow its structure. You may use it to check whether your own answer is plausible, and you may cite it as a source for a standard result or a dataset — but the derivation, the write-up and the choices must be yours, in your own words. If you used it to learn a technique, say so in your acknowledgements. Changing the numbers is not enough to make copied work yours.

    Answer: cite ideas, never submit text or structure; when in doubt, declare the assistance.

  7. Give two ways to add a genuine escalation to an optimisation exploration that has already found the answer.

    Show answer

    (1) Sensitivity: vary each parameter by ±10% and report how much the optimum moves — this shows whether the answer is robust and uses calculus meaningfully. (2) Change the objective or the constraint: minimise material with a fixed volume, then minimise cost with different material prices for the base and the side, and compare the two optima with an explanation of the difference.

    Answer: sensitivity analysis and a changed constraint. Both are "further mathematics", not padding.

  8. Your conclusion says: "In conclusion, I found the answer and learned a lot about mathematics." Rewrite it to reach the top of criterion D.

    Show answer

    Model answer: "The model predicts a drinkable window of 11.4 minutes, compared with 10.9 minutes under the simpler constant-ambient model — a difference too small to matter in practice, so the simpler model is adequate for my purposes. The main limitation is that the cooling constant \(k\) was fitted to a single mug in a single room; because \(k\) depends on the exposed surface area, the value cannot be transferred to a different container without repeating the experiment. With more time I would repeat the measurement across five containers and test whether \(k\) is proportional to surface area, which would turn the fitted constant into a prediction."

    Answer: a number, a comparison, a limitation with a mechanism, and a specific next step. That is criterion D at its top.
