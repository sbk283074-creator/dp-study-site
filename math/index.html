Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL

AA HL is the course where you are expected to know why, not just know how. Every result you use — the derivative of \(\sin x\), De Moivre's theorem, the standard error of a mean — is something you should be able to rebuild from a definition under pressure. This page is the map: what the course contains, how it is examined, what your calculator can and cannot do for you, and, most importantly, how the five topics are really one subject wearing five different hats.

240 teaching hours (210 content + 30 exploration) HL only: complex numbers, proof, Maclaurin, differential equations, inference Paper 1 · Paper 2 · Paper 3 · IA

What AA HL actually is

AA means analysis and approaches. Read that as: the mathematics is studied for its own structure. You prove things. You manipulate symbols. You meet a problem you have never seen and you build the tool you need, rather than selecting it from a menu. That is a different skill from AI (applications and interpretation), where the mathematics is mainly a language for modelling messy data with a calculator doing the heavy lifting.

Why this exists

The IB splits maths into two routes because "being good at algebra" and "being good at modelling" are genuinely different talents. AA HL is the route for mathematicians, physicists, engineers and computer scientists who will meet rigorous proof again at university. Roughly half of an AA HL exam tests technique; the other half tests whether you can adapt that technique to an unfamiliar setting — which is exactly what Paper 3 is built to do.

Consequences for you: because Paper 1 is calculator-free, your arithmetic and algebra have to be automatic. Exact trigonometric values, log laws, the derivative of \(\mathrm{e}^{2x}\), the factorisation of \(x^3-8\) — all of it must come from memory and by hand. Students who "understand but are slow" lose more marks in AA HL than students who memorise properly and understand later.

What the weights mean for you

Marks per minute: Paper 1 ≈ 0.92, Paper 2 ≈ 0.92, Paper 3 ≈ 0.73. Paper 3 gives you more time per mark because it expects you to think, not just execute. In Papers 1 and 2 you have roughly one minute per mark plus 10 minutes; if a 6-mark question is still fighting you after 8 minutes, write down your best partial method and move on — markschemes award method marks generously and follow-through marks are real.

The formula booklet: what you are given, what you must own

You receive a clean copy of the Mathematics: analysis and approaches formula booklet for every exam. It is roughly 12 pages. Knowing precisely what is in it saves you hours of pointless memorising — and knowing what is not in it saves you from discovering that fact in the exam hall.

How to use it

In the first week of Y12, print the booklet and go through it with three highlighters: green for "I already know this", amber for "in the booklet — do not memorise, but practise finding it fast", pink for "must memorise". Then re-highlight at the end of Y12. Time yourself locating five formulas (Maclaurin for \(\ln(1+x)\), \(\chi^2\) statistic, volume of revolution, sum of a geometric series, compound-angle identity for \(\cos(A-B)\)) — under 60 seconds total is expert level.

Typically given — do NOT memorise

  • Areas and volumes: parallelogram, triangle, trapezium, circle, sphere, cone, pyramid, cylinder
  • Arithmetic & geometric sequence/series formulas, \(S_\infty = \dfrac{u_1}{1-r}\)
  • Compound interest: \(FV = PV\left(1+\dfrac{r}{100k}\right)^{kn}\)
  • Binomial coefficient \(\displaystyle\binom{n}{r}=\dfrac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}\)
  • Quadratic formula, discriminant, sum & product of roots
  • Arc length \(l=r\theta\), sector area \(A=\tfrac12 r^2\theta\) (radians)
  • Sine rule, cosine rule, \(A=\tfrac12 ab\sin C\)
  • \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\), \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\), double-angle identities
  • Sum & product of roots of a polynomial
  • Maclaurin series for \(\mathrm{e}^x,\ \sin x,\ \cos x,\ \ln(1+x),\ (1+x)^p\)
  • \(E(X)=\sum x\,P(X=x)\), \(Var(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2\); binomial & Poisson mean/variance
  • Linear-combination rules \(E(aX+bY)\), \(Var(aX\pm bY)\); \(\chi^2=\sum\dfrac{(f_o-f_e)^2}{f_e}\)

Typically NOT given — memorise or derive

  • Laws of logarithms and change of base \(\log_a b=\dfrac{\ln b}{\ln a}\)
  • All differentiation & integration rules: chain, product, quotient, \(\sin,\cos,\tan,\mathrm{e}^x,\ln x\)
  • Integration by parts \(\int u\,\mathrm{d}v = uv-\int v\,\mathrm{d}u\) and the tabular shortcut
  • Compound-angle identities (if absent from your copy) and all Pythagorean identities beyond \(\sin^2+\cos^2=1\)
  • De Moivre's theorem and the \(n\)th-root formula \(z^{1/n}=r^{1/n}\mathrm{cis}\!\left(\dfrac{\theta+2k\pi}{n}\right)\)
  • L'Hôpital's rule statement and its conditions
  • The 6-step hypothesis-test structure and the critical-value language
  • Variance/standard deviation formulas for grouped and ungrouped data
  • Exact values \(\sin 30^\circ,\cos 45^\circ,\tan 60^\circ,\dots\) (check your copy — many print them)
  • Bayes' theorem, the CLT statement, confidence interval structure
Common trap

Three booklet mistakes cost marks every session. (1) Wrong-substitution booklets: students use the \(\cos 2\theta\) form they like rather than the one that cancels; check all three forms before choosing. (2) Unit mismatch: the booklet's arc/sector formulas assume radians and the compound-interest formula assumes a percentage rate \(r\), not a decimal. (3) Over-trust: the booklet gives you \(\binom{n}{r}\) but not the general term of \((a+b)^n\); you still have to know that the term in \(x^r\) comes from choosing \(r\) of the \(x\)-brackets.

Verify against your own copy

Booklet contents have been reissued with small changes. The lists above reflect the current analysis-and-approaches booklet; open the copy your school gives you and tick each item off yourself. Spend 20 minutes on this once and you will never again waste exam time hunting for a formula that was never there.

GDC skills that actually earn marks

A graphics display calculator is not an answer machine; it is a checking machine and a number cruncher. The markschemes award marks for the method you write down, so a calculator answer with no visible method scores zero on a "show that" and often only the final accuracy mark elsewhere.

How to use it

Adopt one habit: write the mathematics, then use the GDC to check it, then write "GDC check" beside it. If your calculator disagrees with your algebra, you have found an error while you still have time to fix it. The reverse habit — calculator first, algebra never — is how students score 40% on Paper 2.

Common trap

Radian mode. Degree mode. Check it before Paper 2 starts, and check it again after every reset. Second trap: rounding in the middle of a question — store full-precision values in calculator memory (STO→A) and round only the final answer, otherwise a 3 s.f. answer can drift outside the tolerance. Third trap: your GDC is only as good as the window. A parabola that "looks like it has one root" often has two; use a table of values to confirm.

How the five topics interlock

Here is the part that turns five separate revision lists into one mental model. Every arrow below is a specific technique that crosses a topic boundary, because that is exactly what the IB examines in Paper 3 and in the last part of every Section B question.

Topic 2 Functions→ Topic 5 Calculus→ Topic 3 Trigonometry→ Topic 1 Algebra
Connections — the eight links that matter most
  • Functions ↔ calculus ↔ trig identities ↔ substitution. The concrete crossing point is integration by substitution. To evaluate \(\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{1+x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x\) you substitute \(x=\tan\theta\), then use \(1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\) (Topic 3) to kill the denominator, integrate \(\int 1\,\mathrm{d}\theta\), and substitute back using the function/inverse-function relationship (Topic 2). One integral, three topics. Same chain runs in reverse for \(\int \sin^2 x\,\mathrm{d}x\): use \(\cos 2x = 1-2\sin^2 x\) to turn a square into a linear trig function you can integrate.
  • Series ↔ Maclaurin ↔ limits. The Maclaurin coefficients \(\frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}\) are pure Topic 1 (factorials, general term, binomial expansion of \((1+x)^p\)) applied to Topic 5 derivatives. Their main use is evaluating limits: \(\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x - x}{x^3}\) is impossible by substitution and painful by L'Hôpital (three rounds), but immediate from \(\sin x = x - \frac{x^3}{3!}+\cdots\): the answer is \(-\frac16\).
  • Complex numbers ↔ De Moivre ↔ trig ↔ roots of unity. De Moivre gives \(\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta\) in three lines — a Topic 3 identity proved with Topic 1 machinery. The \(n\)th roots of unity are equally spaced on the unit circle, which makes them the vertices of a regular \(n\)-gon (Topic 3 geometry), and they sum to zero (Topic 1 geometric series with ratio a root). Paper 3 loves this cluster.
  • Statistics ↔ calculus via continuous probability. A pdf \(f\) must satisfy \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 1\) (Topic 5 integration, often by parts or substitution), the mean is \(E(X)=\int x f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\), the median \(m\) solves \(\int_{-\infty}^{m} f = \tfrac12\), and the cdf is \(F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x} f\). Every one of those is a calculus question wearing a statistics hat — and it is a guaranteed Topic 4 HL question type.
  • Vectors ↔ kinematics ↔ calculus. A position vector \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}2t\\ t^2\end{pmatrix}\) differentiates component-wise to velocity \(\mathbf{v}(t)\) and acceleration \(\mathbf{a}(t)\) (Topic 5); speed is \(|\mathbf{v}|\) (Topic 3 modulus); the time of closest approach between two moving particles minimises \(|\mathbf{r}_A-\mathbf{r}_B|^2\) using the scalar product (Topic 3) and a stationary-point argument (Topic 5). This is the single most common Paper 3 kinematics setup.
  • Transformations ↔ sinusoidal graphs ↔ differential equations. Writing \(f(x)=3\sin(2x-1)+4\) as amplitude 3, period \(\pi\), phase shift \(\frac12\), centreline 4 is Topic 2 transformation language applied to a Topic 3 graph — and it is the natural solution of \(\frac{\mathrm{d}^2x}{\mathrm{d}t^2}=-\omega^2 x\) in Topic 5.
  • Proof ↔ everything. Induction proves summation results (Topic 1), divisibility, derivatives of \(x^n\) (Topic 5) and De Moivre's theorem (Topic 1). Contradiction proves irrationality and the infinitude of primes. Counterexample kills a false conjecture in one line. The IB does not have a "proof topic"; it has a proof habit that can be examined anywhere.
  • Logs & exponentials ↔ calculus ↔ statistics. \(\mathrm{e}\) is defined by \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\mathrm{e}^x=\mathrm{e}^x\); the normal pdf contains \(\mathrm{e}^{-x^2/2}\); exponential models are linearised by taking logs so that regression (Topic 4) applies. Change of base is what lets your calculator evaluate \(\log_2 7\).
Connections to your other subjects

Physics HL: kinematics, SHM, exponential decay, uncertainty and the normal distribution, and vector geometry of fields — you will meet the same mathematics one term later, so treat Physics as extra calculus practice. Computer Science HL: recursion is a sequence, algorithm complexity is logs, and floating-point standard form is Topic 1; the Java/C++ integer division you use there is literally the division algorithm in the proof that \(\sqrt2\) is irrational. English A Lang & Lit SL: the exploration's criterion B (mathematical communication) is graded on the same principle as a good essay — audience, structure, precision.

How to use it

Paper 3: how to survive it

Paper 3 is 75 minutes for about 55 marks across two extended problems. Each problem is a chain of 6–10 parts that wanders across topic boundaries. The content is always within the syllabus; the combination is deliberately unfamiliar. Students who prepare by memorising question types panic here; students who prepare by understanding mechanisms do well.

Why this exists

Paper 3 exists to test whether you can do what mathematicians actually do: meet an unfamiliar object, explore it with the tools you have, and notice a pattern. That is why the first parts are usually gentle ("verify that…", "find, in terms of \(n\),…") — the IB is teaching you a new object through parts (a), (b), (c) and then asking you to use it in (f).

How to use it
  1. Spend 5 minutes reading both problems in full before writing anything. Choose the one whose first three parts you can definitely do. Do not start at question 1 by reflex.
  2. Do part (a) whatever it takes. It is nearly always accessible and it is the foundation for follow-through marks.
  3. Treat every "show that" as a gift. It tells you the destination, so you can check your working as you go, and the answer is printed — you cannot be wrong if your algebra is honest.
  4. When stuck, specialise. Test the general statement with \(n=1,2,3\); compute a concrete example with actual numbers; draw the Argand diagram, the slope field, the table of values. Pattern first, proof second. This is legitimate and it is how the marks are found.
  5. Use follow-through deliberately. If you cannot get a value, invent a plausible one, write "let \(k=1.7\) (from part (c))" and continue. Later parts are marked on method using your value.
  6. Answer in the language of the question. If it says "hence", use the previous result. If it says "deduce", a one-line justification from an earlier part is enough.
  7. Leave the last 6 minutes for structure. Every part should have a visible conclusion with units and, where relevant, a sentence of interpretation.
Common trap

The biggest Paper 3 error is abandoning a problem after one hard part. Because the parts are chained, later parts are often easier than the part you just failed — and they carry follow-through marks. Second error: treating Paper 3 like Paper 2 and rushing. You have 35 minutes per problem; use the reading time. Third: refusing to use your GDC to explore. In Paper 3 the calculator is a laboratory — generate 20 terms of the sequence, plot the family of curves, evaluate the integral numerically, then prove what you observed.

Check yourself

Diagnostic practice

  1. A bank pays 4.2% nominal annual interest, compounded monthly. Write down the expression for the value of a \$5\,000 deposit after 8 years, and calculate it.

    Show answer
    1. Formula (booklet): \(FV = PV\left(1+\dfrac{r}{100k}\right)^{kn}\) with \(PV=5000\), \(r=4.2\), \(k=12\), \(n=8\).
    2. Substitute: \(FV = 5000\left(1+\dfrac{4.2}{1200}\right)^{96} = 5000(1.0035)^{96}\).
    3. Evaluate: \((1.0035)^{96} = 1.39817\ldots\), so \(FV = 6990.86\).
    Answer: \$6\,991 (nearest dollar). Method mark for the correct substituted expression; accuracy mark for the value. Sanity check: 4.2% for 8 years simple would give \$6\,680, and compounding must give slightly more — it does.

  2. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x - x}{x^3}\) using a Maclaurin series.

    Show answer
    1. \(\sin x = x - \dfrac{x^3}{3!} + \dfrac{x^5}{5!} - \cdots\).
    2. So \(\sin x - x = -\dfrac{x^3}{6} + \dfrac{x^5}{120}-\cdots\).
    3. Divide by \(x^3\): \(-\dfrac16 + \dfrac{x^2}{120}-\cdots \to -\dfrac16\) as \(x\to0\).
    Answer: \(-\dfrac16\). One mark for quoting the series, one for subtracting \(x\), one for the limit. Sanity check: for \(x=0.1\), \((\sin 0.1-0.1)/0.001 = -0.16658\), close to \(-1/6\).

  3. Use De Moivre's theorem to show that \(\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta\).

    Show answer
    1. \(\cos 3\theta + i\sin 3\theta = (\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^3\).
    2. Expand: \(= \cos^3\theta + 3i\cos^2\theta\sin\theta - 3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta - i\sin^3\theta\).
    3. Real parts: \(\cos3\theta = \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta = \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta(1-\cos^2\theta)\).
    4. \(= \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta + 3\cos^3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta\).
    Answer: shown. Comparing real parts is the whole trick; the \(i\sin 3\theta\) side simultaneously gives \(\sin3\theta = 3\sin\theta-4\sin^3\theta\).

  4. A continuous random variable has pdf \(f(x)=kx(4-x)\) for \(0\le x\le 4\), zero otherwise. Find \(k\), then find \(E(X)\).

    Show answer
    1. Normalise: \(\int_0^4 kx(4-x)\,\mathrm{d}x = k\left[2x^2-\dfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^4 = k\left(32-\dfrac{64}{3}\right)=\dfrac{32k}{3}=1\), so \(k=\dfrac{3}{32}\).
    2. \(E(X)=\dfrac{3}{32}\int_0^4 x^2(4-x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{3}{32}\left[\dfrac{4x^3}{3}-\dfrac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^4 = \dfrac{3}{32}\left(\dfrac{256}{3}-64\right)\).
    3. \(\dfrac{256}{3}-64=\dfrac{64}{3}\), so \(E(X)=\dfrac{3}{32}\cdot\dfrac{64}{3}=2\).
    Answer: \(k=\dfrac{3}{32}\), \(E(X)=2\). Sanity check: the pdf is symmetric about \(x=2\), so the mean must be 2 — the fastest possible check, and it confirms the arithmetic.

  5. Two particles have position vectors \(\mathbf{r}_A = \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix} + t\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{r}_B = \begin{pmatrix}10\\1\end{pmatrix} + t\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\end{pmatrix}\), \(t\ge0\). Find the time at which they are closest, and the minimum distance.

    Show answer
    1. Relative position: \(\mathbf{r}_A-\mathbf{r}_B = \begin{pmatrix}-9\\1\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}4\\-1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-9+4t\\1-t\end{pmatrix}\).
    2. Squared distance: \(D(t)=(-9+4t)^2+(1-t)^2 = 82 - 74t + 17t^2\).
    3. Minimise: \(D'(t) = -74+34t = 0 \Rightarrow t = \dfrac{37}{17}=2.176\).
    4. \(D_{\min}= 82 - 74(37/17) + 17(37/17)^2 = 82 - \dfrac{2738}{17} + \dfrac{1369}{17} = 82 - \dfrac{1369}{17} = \dfrac{25}{17}\).
    5. Distance \(=\sqrt{25/17}=1.21\).
    Answer: \(t=2.18\) s, distance \(1.21\) m. Minimising the square of the distance avoids a square root in the derivative — a standard exam-efficient move.

  6. Explain, in one sentence each, why a "show that" question cannot be answered with a calculator in Paper 1, and what "hence" obliges you to do.

    Show answer

    A "show that" credits the visible algebra that leads to the printed result — a calculator output shows no method, so it scores zero even when correct. "Hence" obliges you to build on the immediately preceding result (usually a "show that" answer); if you solve the later part by a different route you lose the "hence" mark even if your answer is right.

    Answer: as above. This pair of facts is worth several marks per paper.
