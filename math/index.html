Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL

Analysis and approaches HL is the course where the question is never really "do you know the method?" but "can you rebuild the method when the setting is new?" Every result you use — the derivative of sin x, De Moivre's theorem, the standard error of a mean — is something you should be able to reconstruct from a definition under a clock. That is a different skill from being good at exercises, and it is why the grade is decided less by how much you have covered than by how you have covered it. This page is the hub: what the five topics contain, how the four components are examined, what Paper 3 actually asks of you, how markschemes award marks, how to use a calculator without losing marks to it, and how to train for all of it. Each topic has its own page — this one gives you the map, the exam mechanics, and enough worked mathematics to make the connections concrete.

Why this page is worth reading before the topic pages

There are two ways to be a strong AA HL student. The first is to know five topics separately: a sequences list, a functions list, a trigonometry list, a statistics list, a calculus list. The second is to know one subject in which the same handful of moves keep reappearing — complete the square, substitute, compare coefficients, differentiate and set to zero, take logs, look for a pattern and then prove it. Students who learn the first way find Paper 3 incomprehensible. Students who learn the second way find it hard but fair, because Paper 3 is built almost entirely from those moves placed in an unfamiliar order.

There is a second, blunter reason. Roughly half of an AA HL exam tests technique you have drilled; the other half tests whether you can adapt it. The adaptation half is where the grade boundary sits, and almost nobody revises for it deliberately, because it does not feel like a topic. Sections 4, 6 and 7 below are about that half.

1. What AA HL actually is — and how it differs from AI

Analysis and approaches means the mathematics is studied for its own structure. You manipulate symbols, you prove statements, you work with exact values, and you meet problems you have not been shown how to solve. Applications and interpretation (AI) is the other route: the mathematics there is mainly a language for modelling messy, real data, with a calculator doing the heavy lifting and much more emphasis on statistics, networks and numerical methods. Both are rigorous; they are rigorous about different things.

Three consequences follow, and they shape how you should study. First, because Paper 1 is calculator-free, your arithmetic and algebra have to be automatic: exact trigonometric values, the log laws, the derivative of e2x, the factorisation of x3 − 8. Students who "understand but are slow" lose more marks in AA HL than students who memorise properly and understand afterwards. Second, because the course is algebraic, most of your errors will be sign errors, not idea errors — which is why an error log (section 11) works so well here. Third, because proof is examinable everywhere, you need a written routine for each of the three proof types, not a vague sense of what they are. Section 8 gives you all three with full write-ups.

Core concepts you must know

Below are the foundational objects of the course, each in one sentence you could write in an exam. They are grouped roughly by the topic page where the full treatment — intuition, formal statement, proof, worked examples and practice — already exists; this section is the map, not the territory. Do not read it as vocabulary to memorise. In AA HL almost every lost mark traces back to a definition that was nearly right: a domain you forgot to restrict, a probability read as conditional when it is not, a derivative evaluated at the wrong point. Precision here is worth more than speed.

number systems — ℕ ⊂ ℤ ⊂ ℚ ⊂ ℝ ⊂ ℂ: naturals, integers, rationals, reals, complex. Say which set your answer lives in whenever a question asks for "all solutions".
standard form and SI prefixes — a number written as a × 10k with 1 ≤ |a| < 10; significant figures count digits that carry information, decimal places count digits after the point.
sequence vs series — a sequence is an ordered list of terms; a series is the sum of them. Arithmetic has a constant difference, geometric a constant ratio.
sigma notation — Σ is a compact instruction to add: the limits tell you the first and last index, so they also tell you how many terms there are.
infinite geometric series — converges only when |r| < 1, with sum to infinity a1/(1 − r); otherwise it diverges and has no sum.
exponent and logarithm laws — logs turn multiplication into addition and powers into multiples; they are the inverses of exponentials, so alogax = x.
e and the natural logarithm — e is the base whose rate of growth equals its current value; ln is log base e, and it is the logarithm every calculus result about growth uses.
binomial theorem — an expansion of (a + b)n whose general term is C(n, r) an−r br; permutations count arrangements where order matters, combinations where it does not.
partial fractions — splitting one rational expression into a sum of simpler ones; the tool that turns an impossible integral into two easy ones and makes series telescope.
complex number — z = a + bi with i2 = −1; modulus is distance from the origin, argument is direction, Euler form is reiθ, and De Moivre turns powers and nth roots into arithmetic on the argument.
function — a rule giving exactly one output for each input; domain is the allowed inputs, range the outputs produced, the inverse undoes it (only if one-to-one), a composite applies one rule after another.
asymptote — a line a graph approaches but never reaches: vertical where a denominator is zero, horizontal from a limit at infinity, oblique (HL) from a polynomial division.
polynomial — a sum of powers of x; its roots are its x-intercepts, the factor and remainder theorems link factors to roots, and the discriminant counts real roots of a quadratic.
radian and the unit circle — an angle measured as arc length over radius; the exact trig values come from the circle, which is why Paper 1 can ask for them with no calculator.
trigonometric identity — a statement true for every angle — Pythagorean, compound-angle, double-angle — used to rewrite an expression into a form you can solve (HL expects the proofs).
vector — a quantity with magnitude and direction; the scalar (dot) product gives a projection or an angle, the vector (cross) product (HL) gives a perpendicular direction and an area.
limit — the value a function approaches as its input approaches a point; the idea both continuity and the derivative are built on, and the reason L'Hôpital's rule works (HL).
derivative — instantaneous rate of change and gradient of the tangent, defined from first principles as the limit of a difference quotient; it is zero at a stationary point.
integral — the limit of a sum of strips: a definite integral is signed area between curve and axis, an indefinite integral is the family of antiderivatives plus c.
differential equation — an equation containing a derivative; solving it means recovering the function, by separating variables or (HL) by Euler's method step by step.
Maclaurin series (HL) — writing a function as an infinite polynomial whose coefficients are its derivatives at zero; accuracy improves with more terms and with x nearer zero.
random variable — a quantity whose value is decided by chance: discrete if you count the outcomes, continuous if you measure them.
probability distribution — the rule assigning probabilities to outcomes: binomial for a fixed number of trials with constant p, Poisson for events in an interval, normal for the bell curve set by mean and standard deviation.
expectation and variance — E(X) is the long-run average; Var(X) is the average squared deviation from it, so it measures spread in squared units.
correlation and regression — r measures the strength of linear association only, never causation; the regression line predicts y from x, and only inside the range of the data.
hypothesis test — assume H0, measure how unlikely the data would be if it were true (the p-value), and reject H0 when p is below the significance level; Type I rejects a true H0, Type II keeps a false one.
central limit theorem (HL) — for large n the distribution of sample means is approximately normal whatever the parent distribution, which is why normal methods work so widely.
proof — induction for statements indexed by an integer, contradiction by assuming the negation and reaching an impossibility, counterexample by producing one case that kills a universal claim.

How the five topics hold together

The five topics are not five subjects. Topic 1 supplies the objects (numbers, sequences, complex numbers) and the habit of proof that the rest assumes. Topic 2 supplies the language: every model in the course is a function, and domain, range, inverse and transformation are the questions you ask of any graph the exam shows you. Topic 5 is Topic 2 applied to change: the derivative is a limit of a gradient, the integral is a limit of a sum, and both are defined on functions you met in Topic 2. Topic 3 is the same algebra in space — vectors are ordered triples obeying arithmetic, and the unit circle is why trig identities exist at all — and it is the topic where HL adds the most. Topic 4 is the one place the course stops being deterministic: you model randomness with a distribution and then test whether the model survives the data.

Definitions that carry marks on their own

A handful of questions each session ask for nothing else: state the condition for convergence of a geometric series; write the definition of the derivative from first principles; state what a p-value is; give the condition for SHM-like behaviour in a recurrence; define a one-to-one function. These are one or two marks each and they are entirely memorisable — a student who can recite them picks up marks other students never attempt. Write them on one side of a card, in the exact wording, and read it weekly.

Three confusions that cost the most marks

Sequence versus series. "Find the sum of the first ten terms" is a series question; using the nth-term formula answers a different question and scores zero. A p-value is not the probability that H0 is true. It is the probability of data at least this extreme, calculated assuming H0 is true. An inverse is not a reciprocal. f−1(x) undoes the function; 1/f(x) divides one by it. Confusing them is the single most common error in Topic 2, and it is never a slip — it costs every method mark after it.

What the weights mean for your timing

Marks per minute are almost identical across the three papers: Paper 1 ≈ 0.92 (110 marks in 120 min), Paper 2 ≈ 0.92 (110 in 120), Paper 3 ≈ 0.92 (55 in 60). So Paper 3 is not a slower paper in clock terms — it only feels slower because every mark costs more thought. That is the trap: the nominal pace is the same as Paper 2, but the questions are unfamiliar and chained, so you must deliberately spend the first few minutes of each problem understanding it before you write. In Papers 1 and 2 you have roughly one minute per mark plus ten minutes of slack; if a 6-mark question is still fighting you after eight minutes, write down the best partial method you have and move on. Markschemes award method marks generously, and follow-through marks are real.

The other number worth internalising: Paper 1 is 30% of your grade and is the only paper where nobody can help you. No calculator, no checking, no numerical safety net. Every hour you spend making exact arithmetic automatic is worth more than an hour spent learning one more technique you can do on a calculator anyway.

4. What Paper 3 is really like

Paper 3 is one hour for about 55 marks across two extended problems. Each problem is a chain of six to ten parts that wanders across topic boundaries. The content is always within the syllabus; the combination is deliberately unfamiliar. Students who prepare by memorising question types panic here. Students who prepare by understanding mechanisms do well, because the mechanisms are the same ones you have used all year.

Why this exists

Paper 3 exists to test whether you can do what mathematicians actually do: meet an unfamiliar object, explore it with the tools you already have, notice a pattern, and then justify the pattern. That is why the early parts are nearly always gentle — "verify that…", "write down…", "find, in terms of n…". The paper is teaching you a new object through parts (a), (b) and (c), and then asking you to use it in part (f). The marks are there; what is being tested is whether you read far enough ahead to see where the chain is going.

4.1 The six question families

Almost every Paper 3 problem belongs to one of these. Learn the first move for each and you have removed most of the unfamiliarity.

FamilyWhat it looks likeYour first move
A recurrence you have never seen u1 given, un+1 = f(un) for some function f Compute the first four or five terms on your GDC. Then look for a transformation that makes the recurrence linear — a reciprocal, a log, or a shift.
A family of curves with a parameter y = xn e−x, or a curve depending on a Sketch three members on your GDC (three values of the parameter). Note what is invariant — often an area or an x-intercept — then prove it.
A function defined by an integral F(x) = ∫0x f(t) dt Use the fundamental theorem immediately: F′(x) = f(x). The whole question is usually about F′, not about F.
A transformation or matrix in the plane A 2 × 2 matrix applied repeatedly, or a geometric transformation Apply it to a general point (x, y) once, then twice, and look for the pattern before reaching for eigenvalues.
A differential equation with a twist Separable equation plus a slope field, or Euler's method with a comparison Solve it exactly first if you can; the numerical part is then a comparison, and the comparison is where the marks are.
A distribution defined by a new pdf f(x) = k · g(x) on an interval Normalise first (integrate and set equal to 1, giving k), then note any symmetry — a symmetric pdf has its mean and median at the line of symmetry for free.
Worked example 1 Paper 3 · 16 marks · GDC allowed · an unfamiliar-situation chain

Question. A function f is defined by f(x) = x/(1 + x) for x ≥ 0. A sequence is defined by u1 = 1 and un+1 = f(un) for n ≥ 1.

(a) Write down u2 and u3.
(b) Show that if un > 0 then un+1 > 0.
(c) Hence state, with a reason, whether every term of the sequence is positive.
(d) Show that 1/un+1 = 1/un + 1.
(e) Hence find un in terms of n.
(f) Deduce the value of limn→∞ un.

  1. (a) Specialise first — this is the whole point of part (a). u2 = f(1) = 1/2. Then u3 = f(½) = (½)/(3/2) = 1/3. Mark: 2 × (A1) for the two values.
  2. (b) Positivity. Suppose un > 0. Then 1 + un > 1 > 0, and un+1 = un/(1 + un) is a positive number divided by a positive number, hence positive. Mark: (M1) for the division of positives, (A1) for the conclusion. Note that you need the sentence, not just the inequality.
  3. (c) The "hence" is doing work. u1 = 1 > 0, and by part (b) positivity propagates: u1 > 0 ⇒ u2 > 0 ⇒ u3 > 0 and so on, so every term is positive. Mark: (A1) for stating it follows from the base case together with the implication — an induction-flavoured sentence, even though the question did not say "induction".
  4. (d) The key move — invert the recurrence. un+1 = un/(1 + un), so 1/un+1 = (1 + un)/un = 1/un + 1. Mark: (M1) for inverting, (A1) for the simplification. This is the moment the problem turns from "unfamiliar" into "an arithmetic sequence".
  5. (e) Recognise what you now have. Let vn = 1/un. Then vn+1 = vn + 1, so v is an arithmetic sequence with first term v1 = 1/u1 = 1 and common difference 1. Hence vn = 1 + (n − 1)(1) = n, and therefore un = 1/n. Marks: (M1) for the substitution, (A1) for vn = n, (A1) for un = 1/n.
  6. (f) Deduce — one line, from the closed form. limn→∞ 1/n = 0, so the limit is 0. Mark: (A1), awarded for one line because part (e) did the work. This is what "deduce" means in practice.
  7. Sanity check, which you should always do. From (a), u2 = ½ and u3 = ⅓; the formula un = 1/n gives exactly those. And the terms are positive and decreasing towards 0, which is consistent with f(x) < x for x > 0.
Answer: u2 = ½, u3 = ⅓; every term is positive; un = 1/n; the limit is 0. The transferable lesson: when a recurrence will not behave, try transforming it — reciprocal, logarithm, or shift by a constant. Three candidates, and one of them nearly always works. That single habit is worth more on Paper 3 than any amount of past-paper memorising.
How to use it — a Paper 3 routine
  1. Spend five minutes reading both problems in full before writing anything. Choose the one whose first three parts you can definitely do. Do not start at question 1 by reflex.
  2. Do part (a) whatever it takes. It is nearly always accessible, and it is the foundation for every follow-through mark that follows.
  3. Treat every "show that" as a gift. It tells you the destination, so you can check your own working as you go, and the answer is printed — you cannot be "wrong" if your algebra is honest.
  4. When stuck, specialise. Test the general statement with n = 1, 2, 3; compute a concrete example with actual numbers; draw the Argand diagram, the slope field, the table of values. Pattern first, proof second. This is legitimate, and it is how the marks are found.
  5. Use follow-through deliberately. If you cannot get a value, invent a plausible one, write "let k = 1.7 (from part (c))" and continue. Later parts are marked on method using your value.
  6. Write in the language of the question. If it says "hence", use the previous result. If it says "deduce", a one-line justification from an earlier part is enough. If it says "justify", give the reason, not the answer.
  7. Leave the last six minutes for structure. Every part should have a visible conclusion with units and, where relevant, one sentence of interpretation.
Common trap

The biggest Paper 3 error is abandoning a problem after one hard part. Because the parts are chained, later parts are frequently easier than the part you just failed — and they carry follow-through marks using your own (wrong) number. The second error is treating Paper 3 like Paper 2 and rushing: you have about 35 minutes per problem, and five of those should be reading. The third is refusing to use the GDC as a laboratory — generate twenty terms of the sequence, plot the family of curves, evaluate the integral numerically, and then prove what you observed.

5. The calculator: what your GDC can and cannot do for you

A graphics display calculator is not an answer machine; it is a checking machine and a number cruncher. Markschemes award marks for the method you write down. A calculator answer with no visible method scores zero on a "show that" and often only the final accuracy mark everywhere else — which means a student who writes nothing but x = 2.37 can be one mark better off than a student who writes nothing.

How to use it

Adopt one habit and it will save you marks in every paper: write the mathematics first, then use the GDC to check it, then write "GDC check" beside it. If the calculator disagrees with your algebra, you have found an error while there is still time to fix it. The reverse habit — calculator first, algebra never — is how capable students score 40% on Paper 2, because the paper is not asking for numbers, it is asking for the route to them.

5.1 What it can do, what it cannot, and what it should never do alone

Linear systems (rref) Topic 1 and Topic 3 planes Write the augmented matrix or the three equations before quoting any solution, and say what a row of zeros means.
Sequence mode and the finance solver Topic 1 loans and annuities State n, r, PV, PMT, FV with their signs. The sign convention is where this mark dies.
Slope fields and the ODE solver Topic 5 differential equations Draw at least three tangent segments by hand in your answer booklet and copy the shape of the field.
Graph sketching: window, zoom, trace, table Everywhere A small rough sketch proves you understand the shape, and catches the "no solution" cases that a single screenshot hides.
Worked example 2 IB-style · 5 marks · GDC allowed · the same question answered two ways

Question. Solve e2x = 5 − x.

Version A — calculator only (0 to 1 mark out of 5).

Student writes: "x = 0.717". There is no equation, no rearrangement, no indication of which function was entered, and no evidence that the student noticed there is only one root. On a markscheme of M1 for a valid rearrangement or a stated intersection, A1 for the value, A1 for 3 s.f., plus marks for justifying uniqueness, this script scores at most the accuracy mark — and if the value were wrong, it would score zero, because there is nothing else on the page.

Version B — method first (5 marks).

  1. Write the equation as a single function: e2x + x − 5 = 0. (M1)
  2. Justify uniqueness: d/dx (e2x + x − 5) = 2e2x + 1 > 0 for all x, so the function is strictly increasing and has at most one root. (R1)
  3. Locate it: at x = 0 the expression is 1 − 5 = −4; at x = 1 it is e2 − 4 ≈ 3.39. Sign change, so there is a root in (0, 1). (M1)
  4. Solve numerically: GDC check — x = 0.71637…
  5. Answer to 3 s.f.: x = 0.716. (A1)(A1)
Answer: x = 0.716. Both students had the same number. One wrote one line and the other wrote five. The difference is four marks — about a grade boundary over a paper. Rule: the calculator output is the last line of a solution, never the first.
Worked example 3 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed · what the machine cannot write for you

Question. A machine is set to fill bags with 500 g of coffee. The mass delivered is normally distributed with standard deviation 12 g. A quality controller suspects the setting has drifted and takes a random sample of 40 bags, obtaining a mean of 496.3 g. Test at the 5% level whether the mean has changed.

  1. Hypotheses, in words and symbols. Let μ be the population mean mass. H0: μ = 500; H1: μ ≠ 500. Two-tailed, because "has changed" does not specify a direction. (A1)(A1) — one mark for each hypothesis, and the two-tailed alternative is the difference between them.
  2. Test statistic. Standard error = 12/√40 = 1.8974. z = (496.3 − 500)/1.8974 = −1.9500. (M1)(A1)
  3. Compare. Critical values are ±1.96 at 5% two-tailed. Since |−1.9500| < 1.96, the statistic does not lie in the rejection region. Equivalently p = 2 × P(Z < −1.95) = 0.0512 > 0.05. (M1)(A1)
  4. Conclusion in context. There is insufficient evidence at the 5% level to conclude that the mean mass has changed; we do not reject H0. (R1) — this sentence is a mark of its own, and it must say "do not reject", never "accept".
  5. The point of the example. This result is within a hair of the boundary. If you had rounded the standard error to 1.90 early you would get z = −1.947, still not significant; if you had rounded to 1.9 you would get −1.95 and a wrong-looking comparison. Store full precision and round once.
Answer: z = −1.95, p = 0.0512; do not reject H0. Four of the seven marks here are words your calculator cannot produce: two hypotheses, a comparison, and a conclusion. Rule: in statistics the GDC supplies the number and you supply everything else.
Common trap

Four calculator errors account for a remarkable share of lost Paper 2 marks. (1) Radian mode. Check before the paper starts and again after any reset. Every trig derivative, integral and arc-length calculation silently breaks in degree mode. (2) Rounding early. Store values in memory and round once at the end. (3) Trusting the window. A parabola that "looks like it has one root" often has two; confirm with a table of values. (4) The wrong standard deviation. Your GDC offers both sn and sn−1; the IB expects you to know which is the unbiased estimate of the population variance.

6. The command terms and what they cost

The same answer can score 0 or full marks depending on the verb in front of it. This is the cheapest set of marks in the entire course: it costs you nothing to learn, and it is examined in every paper. The IB publishes definitions of these terms; the versions below are the working translations that matter at the moment of writing.

TermWhat the examiner wantsWhat it costs you if you ignore it
State / write down The answer only. No working is needed, and no working is credited. Nothing directly — but students waste two minutes writing out a derivation for one mark, then run out of time later.
Calculate / find / determine Working and answer. "Determine" additionally wants you to resolve any ambiguity (which root, which case). A bare answer scores 0 if wrong, and sometimes only the final accuracy mark if right.
Show that Every algebraic line, visible, leading to the printed result. Everything. A calculator cannot answer it; a jump cannot answer it; the answer is already printed so only the route has value.
Hence You must use the immediately preceding result. One mark, even with a correct answer obtained another way. This is the cruellest term in the course.
Hence or otherwise Using the previous result is safe but optional. Nothing — but "hence" is usually faster, so ignoring it costs time rather than marks.
Deduce A short argument from something already established. No re-derivation. Time, and sometimes the mark if your re-derivation is incomplete.
Justify A reason attached to an answer: a sign, a second derivative, a comparison. The accuracy mark may stand but the reasoning mark goes.
Prove A complete general argument. Examples are not proof. Almost everything, if you respond with verification instead of proof.
Evaluate Weigh something up and reach a judgement supported by reasons or numbers. One to two marks: "it is good" is not an evaluation.
Verify Substitute and check. Do not solve again from scratch. Time, and the mark if you verify the wrong thing.
Sketch Shape, intercepts, asymptotes and a labelled scale. Not a plot. Accuracy marks, usually for missing an asymptote or an intercept.

6.1 Weak versus strong, side by side

"Show that" — 4 marks

Question: Show that (1 − cos 2x)/sin 2x = tan x, for sin x ≠ 0.

Weak — 1/4

"Using the identities 1 − cos 2x = 2sin2x and sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x, we get tan x."

Why it scores 1: the two correct identities earn one method mark, and the substitution that would earn the rest never appears on the page. The examiner cannot award accuracy for a result that was printed in the question. In Paper 1 this answer is worth less than it looks, because there is no calculator to fall back on and the student has demonstrated only recall, not manipulation.

Strong — 4/4

  1. 1 − cos 2x = 1 − (1 − 2sin2x) = 2sin2x. (M1)(A1)
  2. sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x. (A1)
  3. So (1 − cos 2x)/sin 2x = 2sin2x/(2 sin x cos x) = sin x/cos x = tan x. (A1)

Why it scores 4: every line is visible, the double-angle identity is derived rather than quoted (which is what HL expects of cos 2x), and the final cancellation is shown. Nothing is left for the examiner to infer.

"Hence" — 3 marks after a "show that"

Question: (a) Show that x3 − 3x2 + 4 = (x − 2)2(x + 1). (b) Hence solve x3 − 3x2 + 4 = 0.

Weak — 1/3

Student answers (b) by trying values or using the GDC polynomial solver and writing "x = 2 or x = −1".

Why it scores 1: the answer is entirely correct and earns the accuracy mark, but the "hence" mark is forfeited because the previous result was not used, and the reasoning mark for identifying x = 2 as a double root is also gone. Note what the student has lost: the fact that the graph touches the axis at x = 2, which is usually the next part of the question.

Strong — 3/3

  1. Using (a), (x − 2)2(x + 1) = 0. (M1) — the substitution of the factorisation is the "hence".
  2. So x = 2 (a repeated root) or x = −1. (A1)(A1)

Why it scores 3: the previous result is used explicitly, both roots are given, and the repeated root is named — which is exactly the observation the examiner will ask about in part (c).

"Justify" — 2 marks

Question: The point (3, −23) is a stationary point of f. Justify that it is a minimum.

Weak — 0/2

"It is a minimum because it is the lowest point on the graph."

Why it scores 0: this restates the claim. There is no evidence, no calculation and no criterion. It would score nothing even if the point genuinely is the global minimum, because the command term asks for the reason, and this gives a synonym.

Strong — 2/2

"f″(x) = 6x − 6, so f″(3) = 12 > 0; since the second derivative is positive at the stationary point, it is a local minimum." (M1)(A1)

Why it scores 2: a stated test, a computed value, and the sign used to reach the conclusion. Two marks, two ingredients. (A sign diagram of f′(x) with "− 0 +" would earn the same two marks.)

"Evaluate" — 3 marks

Question: A population is modelled by P(t) = 1000 e0.03t, where t is years from now. Evaluate this model.

Weak — 0/3

"The model is good because it is exponential and populations grow exponentially."

Why it scores 0: circular. It asserts that the model's assumption is true and calls that an evaluation. No number, no limitation, no comparison, no judgement about range of validity.

Strong — 3/3

"Over the range the data covers (t = 0 to 10) the model predicts growth from 1000 to 1350, which matches the observed trend, and the 3% rate is plausible. But unrestricted exponential growth is implausible beyond that: the model predicts about 20 100 at t = 100 and about 404 000 at t = 200, with no carrying capacity, no resource constraint and no migration. It is therefore reasonable for short-term planning and misleading for long-term projection — a logistic model with a carrying capacity would be more realistic."

Why it scores 3: it names a strength with numbers, a weakness with numbers, and reaches a qualified judgement that says where the model is and is not usable. That last move — specifying the range of validity — is what separates a 2 from a 3.

7. How to set out a solution: the markscheme logic

Markschemes are not built from answers; they are built from moves. Understanding the four kinds of mark changes how you write, because it tells you what to put on the page when you are stuck.

MarkWhat it rewardsHow to make sure you get it
M — method A correct step, even if the arithmetic is wrong: setting a derivative to zero, writing the correct substitution, applying the correct rule. Never skip the setup line. "f′(x) = 0" before solving is worth a mark on its own.
A — accuracy The right number or expression following from a correct method. Round only at the end, to 3 s.f. unless told otherwise, and include units where the question has any.
R — reasoning A justification: a sign, a comparison, a conclusion in context. If the question says "justify", "explain", "evaluate" or "interpret", there is an R mark and it wants a sentence, not a number.
FT — follow through Correct work built on your earlier wrong answer. Keep going with your value. Do not restart and do not erase. Write "using k = 4.2 from (b)" so the examiner can follow you.
AG — answer given The result is printed in the question (a "show that"). Every line must be shown. There is no credit for the destination, only for the route.

Confirm these abbreviations against a real markscheme when your teacher gives you one — they are the IB's own, but seeing them attached to a question you have attempted is what makes them stick.

Worked example 4 IB-style · 8 marks · No GDC · where the marks are won and lost

Question. Let f(x) = x3 − 3x2 − 9x + 4, for x ∈ ℝ.

(a) Find f′(x). [2]
(b) Find the coordinates of the two stationary points. [4]
(c) Determine the nature of each stationary point. [2]

  1. (a) f′(x) = 3x2 − 6x − 9 = 3(x2 − 2x − 3) = 3(x − 3)(x + 1). M1 for attempting to differentiate (any term with a correct power drop); A1 for the fully correct expression. The factorised form is not required here but will save you a mark of time in (b).
  2. (b) Stationary points satisfy f′(x) = 0, so 3(x − 3)(x + 1) = 0 giving x = 3 or x = −1. M1 for setting the derivative to zero; A1 for both x-values (follow-through allowed from your own derivative).
  3. (b) continued — the step everyone forgets. f(3) = 27 − 27 − 27 + 4 = −23, so (3, −23). f(−1) = −1 − 3 + 9 + 4 = 9, so (−1, 9). M1 for substituting back into f; A1 for both y-values.
  4. (c) f″(x) = 6x − 6. Since f″(3) = 12 > 0, (3, −23) is a local minimum; since f″(−1) = −12 < 0, (−1, 9) is a local maximum. M1 for finding the second derivative; A1 for both natures correctly identified with the sign used as the reason.
  5. Where the eight marks are actually lost, in order of frequency:
    • Substituting into f′ instead of f in (b) — the classic slip. Loses one A1 and possibly the M1.
    • Giving only the x-values in (b). "Stationary points at x = 3 and x = −1" loses the final A1: a point is a pair.
    • Writing "minimum at x = 3" with no sign of f″. Loses the A1 in (c) even though the answer is right.
    • An arithmetic slip in f(−1): −1 − 3 + 9 + 4 is 9, and students who write 7 or 11 lose the A1 but keep everything else if the working is visible.
  6. Now the follow-through demonstration. Suppose you differentiated wrongly and got f′(x) = 3x2 − 6x − 6. You lose the A1 in (a). You then solve your quadratic correctly and substitute back into f — both of those are follow-through marks and you keep them. You then find f″ correctly and classify your points — also kept. You have lost one mark out of eight for a wrong derivative. If instead you had spotted the error and panicked, erased everything and left part (b) blank, you would have lost six.
Answer: f′(x) = 3x2 − 6x − 9; (3, −23) minimum, (−1, 9) maximum. The layout rules this demonstrates: write the setup line before the result; give coordinates, not values; state the test and the sign when asked to determine; never erase — cross out once and continue with your value.
Six layout habits that are worth marks
  1. Write the formula, then substitute, then evaluate. Three lines, and the first one is often a method mark.
  2. Never round mid-question. Keep full precision in the calculator memory.
  3. Give 3 significant figures unless the question asks for exact or for 2 decimal places. And match the accuracy asked for — "to the nearest integer" means no decimal places.
  4. Include units whenever the question has any, and put them in the final sentence, not in every line.
  5. Answer the question that was asked. If it asks for a maximum value, give the value; if it asks when, give the time. Both, if you are unsure: one sentence each.
  6. Use the answer line. If the paper has one, put your final answer there, whatever mess is above it. Examiners mark what they can find.

8. Proof and reasoning — what HL actually expects

Proof is not a topic in this course; it is a habit that can be examined anywhere. That is precisely why it is under-revised: it never appears as a block. There are three forms you must be able to produce on demand, and each has a fixed skeleton. Learn the skeleton and the hardest part disappears.

Why proof is examined at all

A proof is the difference between "this is true for the cases I checked" and "this is true, and I can say why". In AA HL the examiners are testing whether you can tell those apart, because that distinction is what the course is for. It also happens to be the most reliably mark-dense thing in the syllabus: a complete induction proof is four or five marks for about ninety seconds of writing, once the routine is automatic.

8.1 Proof by induction

Use it when a statement is indexed by a positive integer n: a summation formula, a divisibility claim, a derivative formula, De Moivre's theorem. Four obligatory parts:

  1. Base case. Prove the statement for the first value of n (usually n = 1). Write it out explicitly — this is a mark.
  2. Assumption. "Assume the statement is true for n = k." Write out what that assumption actually says.
  3. Inductive step. Prove the statement for n = k + 1, using the assumption. If your proof of the k + 1 case never mentions the assumption, it is not an induction proof.
  4. Conclusion. "Since the statement is true for n = 1, and truth for n = k implies truth for n = k + 1, it is true for all n ∈ ℤ+ by induction." Missing this sentence loses a mark every session.
Worked example 5 IB-style · 6 marks · No GDC · induction on a summation

Question. Prove by induction that Σr=1n r(r + 1) = n(n + 1)(n + 2)/3 for all n ∈ ℤ+.

  1. Base case, n = 1. LHS = 1 × 2 = 2. RHS = 1 × 2 × 3/3 = 2. Equal, so the statement holds for n = 1. (A1)
  2. Assumption. Assume that for some k ≥ 1, Σr=1k r(r + 1) = k(k + 1)(k + 2)/3. (M1)
  3. Inductive step. Consider n = k + 1: Σr=1k+1 r(r + 1) = [Σr=1k r(r + 1)] + (k + 1)(k + 2). (M1) for splitting the sum — this line is the structural heart of every summation induction.
  4. Use the assumption. = k(k + 1)(k + 2)/3 + (k + 1)(k + 2).
  5. Factorise — do not expand. = (k + 1)(k + 2)[k/3 + 1] = (k + 1)(k + 2)(k + 3)/3. (A1)(A1)
  6. Identify the target. This is exactly the stated formula with n replaced by k + 1, since (k + 1)((k + 1) + 1)((k + 1) + 2)/3 = (k + 1)(k + 2)(k + 3)/3.
  7. Conclusion. True for n = 1, and truth for n = k implies truth for n = k + 1, so true for all positive integers n by induction. (R1)
  8. The trap. Students who expand k(k + 1)(k + 2) into a cubic and then try to re-factorise it waste two minutes and usually make a sign error. Factorising out the common (k + 1)(k + 2) at step 5 is the move that makes this question quick.
Answer: as proved above. Transferable rule: in a summation induction, always replace the k + 1 sum by "the k sum plus the last term". That one line is worth two marks and it is identical in every version of the question.

8.2 Proof by contradiction

Use it when direct proof is awkward and the negation of the claim gives you something to work with — irrationality, infinitude, "there is no…". The skeleton: assume the opposite, derive something impossible, conclude the opposite is false.

Worked example 6 IB-style · 6 marks · No GDC · irrationality of √2

Question. Prove that √2 is irrational.

  1. Assume the negation. Suppose √2 is rational. Then √2 = p/q where p and q are positive integers with no common factor (the fraction is in lowest terms). (A1) — the "no common factor" clause is the whole engine; omit it and the proof collapses.
  2. Square and rearrange. 2 = p2/q2, so p2 = 2q2. (M1)
  3. Deduce p is even. p2 is even, and the square of an odd number is odd, so p must be even. Write p = 2m. (M1)(A1)
  4. Substitute back. (2m)2 = 2q2, so 4m2 = 2q2 and therefore q2 = 2m2.
  5. Deduce q is even by the same argument. (A1)
  6. Contradiction. Both p and q are even, so they share a factor of 2 — contradicting the assumption that the fraction was in lowest terms.
  7. Conclusion. The assumption that √2 is rational leads to a contradiction, so √2 is irrational. (R1)
  8. Why the lowest-terms clause matters. Without it, "both are even" is not a contradiction — 4/2 has both parts even and is perfectly rational. The contradiction is only against the specific representation you assumed existed.
Answer: as proved above. Exam habit: in any contradiction proof, the last two lines are formulaic — "this contradicts…" then "therefore the assumption is false and…". Write them in full; both carry marks.

8.3 Counterexample

A universally quantified claim — "for all n", "if n is prime then…" — is destroyed by one exception. A counterexample is a complete disproof, and it is answerable in a single line, which is why it is worth knowing cold.

Worked example 7 IB-style · 3 marks · No GDC · disproof by counterexample

Question. A student claims: "If n is prime, then 2n − 1 is prime." Disprove this claim.

  1. Test small primes first — that is the method. n = 2: 3, prime. n = 3: 7, prime. n = 5: 31, prime. n = 7: 127, prime. The claim looks good, which is exactly why it is a good exam question.
  2. Keep going. n = 11: 211 − 1 = 2048 − 1 = 2047.
  3. Factorise. 2047 = 23 × 89. (M1)(A1)
  4. State the conclusion explicitly. Since 11 is prime but 211 − 1 = 2047 is composite, the claim is false. (R1)
  5. What earns no marks: writing "the claim is false" with no example, or giving an example that does not actually satisfy the hypothesis (e.g. n = 1, which is not prime, or n = 9, which is not prime). The counterexample must meet the if part and fail the then part.
Answer: n = 11 gives 2047 = 23 × 89, so the claim is false. Why it matters: this is the cheapest three marks in the syllabus, and it tests the one logical distinction students blur constantly — that any number of supporting examples proves nothing, while a single counterexample disproves everything.
The four proof errors examiners see most
  1. Verifying instead of proving. Checking n = 1, 2, 3 and concluding. This is the single most common zero on an induction question.
  2. A circular argument. Using the result you were asked to prove inside its own proof — for instance using d/dx (sin x) = cos x to prove the limit that establishes it.
  3. Assuming what you need. In a contradiction proof, assuming the negation but then reasoning as if the original claim were already known.
  4. Missing the closing sentence. Induction proofs that simply stop after the algebra. The conclusion names the principle; without it the examiner cannot tell whether you know why the argument works.

9. The exploration (IA)

Twenty marks, 20% of your grade, marked by your teacher and moderated by the IB. It is the only component you can draft, be told what is wrong with, and redraft — and it is the component students start latest. It is also, quietly, the most improvable 20% you own.

Why the exploration is graded the way it is

The IB is not looking for a research paper. It is looking for evidence that you can do mathematics rather than answer questions about it: formulate a question, choose a method, notice that your first method is inadequate, and say so. That is why reflection and personal engagement have their own criteria — a technically flawless piece with no visible thinking caps out around 13/20, which is a 5, not a 7.

9.1 How to choose a topic: the escalation test

Run any candidate topic through these four questions. If you cannot answer yes to at least three, keep looking.

  1. Is there a parameter? A topic with a number you can vary (n, k, p, an angle, a coefficient) gives you a family to explore rather than a single calculation. No parameter usually means no mathematics beyond the first page.
  2. Can you go deeper if it turns out to be easy? This is the most important one. The ideal topic has an obvious first approach and a less obvious second one. If your first approach works immediately and there is nowhere to go, you cannot score well on criterion E.
  3. Is the mathematics at HL level? Criterion E rewards mathematics that is "commensurate with the level of the course". Modelling a quadratic with three data points is not. Optimising a three-variable packaging problem using calculus and then checking with a numerical method is.
  4. Will there be genuine dead ends? Dead ends are good. Reaching one, explaining why it happened, and changing approach is the most natural possible evidence for criterion D (reflection) and criterion C (personal engagement).
Topic familyWhy it works at AA HLThe danger
Optimisation with a real constraint Calculus, a parameter to vary, a clear check (does the second derivative confirm it?), and a natural extension to a different shape or constraint. Stopping at the first answer. Extend to a family, or compare two methods.
A recurrence or iterative scheme Sequences, limits, convergence criteria, and a natural numerical experiment before the algebra. Describing what the calculator did instead of explaining why it happens.
Approximating something with a series Maclaurin series, error bounds, and a concrete accuracy question you can actually answer. Tabulating values with no analysis of the error.
A probability or game-theoretic model Real probability, simulation to check the theory, and a clean way to generalise. One simulation and a screenshot. You need the theory to explain the simulation.
A pure-mathematics investigation Proof, pattern-seeking, generalisation — the most natural fit for criteria C, D and E. Choosing something far beyond the syllabus and then quoting results you cannot justify. That damages criterion E rather than helping it.
Traps specific to the exploration

Choosing a topic because it sounds impressive. A clear, complete investigation of something modest beats an abandoned attempt at something famous, every time. Outsourcing the mathematics. Using a formula, a proof or a method from beyond the course without justifying it is penalised under criterion E — if you need it, derive it, or at minimum explain it in your own words. Waiting for inspiration. Set a deadline for choosing, and pick the best of your three ideas on that date. Leaving reflection to the last paragraph. Reflection earns its marks when it happens at the moment you hit the problem, and it reads that way.

10. How AA HL connects to physics and computer science

You are taking physics and computer science alongside this course. That is not a coincidence of timetabling; roughly a third of what you do in those subjects is this mathematics wearing a different notation. Treating the overlap deliberately is the single most efficient revision decision available to you: same idea, two exposures, one set of notes.

Calculus→ Kinematics & SHM (Physics)→ Differential equations→ Numerical methods (CS)

10.1 Physics: the calculus is the same calculus

Displacement, velocity and acceleration are a function and its first two derivatives; that is the entire content of kinematics. Simple harmonic motion is the differential equation d2x/dt2 = −ω2x. Exponential decay is dN/dt = −λN. Field geometry is vector geometry. And uncertainty and error propagation in the physics IA is the same standard-deviation machinery you use in Topic 4.

Worked example 8 IB-style · 7 marks · No GDC · the shared kinematics move

Question. A particle moves in a straight line with velocity v = 3t2 − 8t + 4 m s−1 for 0 ≤ t ≤ 3. Find the total distance travelled in the first three seconds.

  1. Recognise the trap. Total distance is not displacement. Distance is ∫|v| dt, so you must know where the particle turns round.
  2. Find when v = 0. 3t2 − 8t + 4 = 0 gives t = (8 ± √(64 − 48))/6 = (8 ± 4)/6, so t = ⅔ or t = 2. Both lie in [0, 3]. (M1)(A1)
  3. Integrate to get displacement. s = t3 − 4t2 + 4t + C, and C = 0 if s = 0 at t = 0. (M1)(A1)
  4. Evaluate at the four endpoints. s(0) = 0; s(⅔) = 8/27 − 16/9 + 8/3 = (8 − 48 + 72)/27 = 32/27 ≈ 1.185; s(2) = 8 − 16 + 8 = 0; s(3) = 27 − 36 + 12 = 3. (A1)
  5. Add the absolute changes. |1.185 − 0| + |0 − 1.185| + |3 − 0| = 1.185 + 1.185 + 3 = 5.37. (M1)(A1)
  6. Sanity check. The displacement after 3 seconds is 3 m but the distance is 5.37 m — larger, as it must be, because the particle moved forwards, back to where it started, and then forwards again.
Answer: 5.37 m. Why it belongs in both subjects: in physics this is a kinematics question; in maths it is "integrate the modulus, so find the zeros first". Same three steps, same trap, two grade opportunities. See space, time and motion for the physics framing.

10.2 Computer science: sequences, logarithms and numerical methods

Three overlaps are worth exploiting. Recursion is a recurrence relation. Any recursive function with T(n) = T(n/2) + 1 is the same object as an arithmetic sequence in disguise. Complexity is logarithms. "How many times can I halve n before reaching 1?" is log2n, and the change-of-base rule is what lets you compare log2 with any other base. Numerical methods are loops. Euler's method in Topic 5 is a for loop with a step size; writing it in code is the fastest way to understand the step-size error, and vice versa.

Worked example 9 IB-style · 5 marks · No GDC · a recurrence from a halving algorithm

Question. An algorithm on an input of size n halves the input and does one unit of work, repeating until the input has size 1. Let T(n) be the number of units of work, with T(1) = 0 and T(n) = T(n/2) + 1 for n > 1. Find T(2k) and hence state the complexity of the algorithm.

  1. Specialise — the Paper 3 move again. T(2) = T(1) + 1 = 1; T(4) = T(2) + 1 = 2; T(8) = T(4) + 1 = 3. The pattern is T(2k) = k. (M1)(A1)
  2. Prove it properly. Substitute n = 2k and let U(k) = T(2k). Then U(k) = T(2k−1) + 1 = U(k − 1) + 1 with U(0) = T(1) = 0. So U is an arithmetic sequence with common difference 1, giving U(k) = k. (M1)(A1)
  3. Translate back. Since k = log2n, T(n) = log2n, and the algorithm is O(log n). (R1)
  4. The point. Nothing here is outside the maths syllabus. It is a recurrence, a substitution, an arithmetic sequence and a logarithm. Recognising that in a computer science question is free marks in programming and free understanding in Topic 5.
Answer: T(2k) = k, so T(n) = log2n and the algorithm is O(log n). Why it is worth knowing: the substitution U(k) = T(2k) is exactly the "transform the recurrence until it is linear" move from Worked example 1. One technique, two subjects.
Where the overlap pays twice

Physics HL: kinematics and SHM are differential equations; fields and forces are vector geometry; uncertainty and error bars are standard deviation and the normal distribution. Revise the maths once and use it in both — see space, time and motion and fields. Computer Science HL: recursion is a recurrence relation, complexity is logarithms, floating-point representation is standard form and binary, and Euler's method is a loop — see computational thinking and programming. English A: criterion B of the exploration is graded on mathematical communication, which is the same discipline as a well-built paragraph: define your terms, signpost, conclude.

11. How to revise for mathematics

Mathematics revision fails in a specific way: students re-read notes and recognise the worked examples, which produces the feeling of knowing without the ability to perform. The fix is not more hours; it is changing the activity. Three principles.

Deliberate practice

Attempt the question before looking at any solution, including your notes. Struggling for four minutes and then failing teaches you more than reading a perfect solution, because the failure tells you exactly which move you did not have. Reading solutions feels productive and is nearly worthless.

Interleaving

Do not practise Topic 5 for a week. Mix topics within every session, because the exam does, and because the hardest part of an exam question is choosing the method — a skill you only train on mixed sets. A topic block is for first learning; after that, everything is mixed.

Spacing

Revisit a question you got wrong after three days, then a week, then three weeks. One hour spread over three days beats three hours in one evening for anything you want to retain until May.

11.1 The error log

Keep one document. Every time you lose a mark on anything — homework, a class test, a past paper — add one row. Four columns, and the fourth is the one that matters.

At the end of every month, count them. If careless and command term together are more than a third of your losses, more content revision will not help you — you need to change how you write, which is what sections 6 and 7 are for.

How to mark your own paper
  1. Mark it the next day, or get someone else to mark it. Marking your own work immediately after writing it is unreliable — you mark what you meant, not what you wrote.
  2. Use the real markscheme, not the answer key. Award method marks generously to yourself (the examiner will), but be ruthless about accuracy and reasoning marks.
  3. Log every lost mark with a category before you look at the total. The total is the least useful number on the page.
  4. Separate "I could not do it" from "I did not finish it". The first is a content problem; the second is a pacing problem; they have completely different fixes.
  5. Re-attempt, from blank paper, every question you scored below half marks on. Do it within a week, before familiarity fades into false confidence.

12. Diagnostic practice

Eight questions, one per core technique, graded from straightforward to hard. Attempt each one fully before you open the answer — the value is in the attempt, not the checking.

  1. Solve 2 log3x − log3(x − 2) = 2. SL / HL 4 marks · No GDC

    Show the answer
    1. Domain first. Need x > 0 and x − 2 > 0, so x > 2. Writing this down is a mark in most markschemes and costs four seconds.
    2. Combine using the log laws. 2 log3x = log3(x2), so the left side is log3(x2/(x − 2)).
    3. Exponentiate. x2/(x − 2) = 32 = 9.
    4. Solve. x2 = 9x − 18, so x2 − 9x + 18 = 0 and (x − 3)(x − 6) = 0, giving x = 3 or x = 6.
    5. Check against the domain. Both are greater than 2, so both are valid. (Had one been 1, it would be rejected — a trap that costs a mark every session.)
    Answer: x = 3 or x = 6. Why this is worth knowing: combining logs, exponentiating and then checking the domain is the standard four-move routine for every log equation in the course, and step 5 is the move students omit.

  2. The function f is defined by f(x) = (2x − 1)/(x + 3) for x ≠ −3. Find f−1(x) and state its domain and range. SL / HL 6 marks · No GDC

    Show the answer
    1. Set y = f(x) and swap. y = (2x − 1)/(x + 3). For the inverse, swap x and y: x = (2y − 1)/(y + 3).
    2. Solve for y. x(y + 3) = 2y − 1, so xy + 3x = 2y − 1.
    3. Collect the y terms. xy − 2y = −1 − 3x, so y(x − 2) = −1 − 3x and y = (−1 − 3x)/(x − 2) = (3x + 1)/(2 − x).
    4. State the inverse. f−1(x) = (3x + 1)/(2 − x).
    5. Domain and range — the swap rule. The domain of f−1 is the range of f. Since f(x) = 2 − 7/(x + 3), the range of f is y ≠ 2. So: domain of f−1 is x ≠ 2; range of f−1 is y ≠ −3 (the domain of f).
    6. Check. f(0) = −1/3, and f−1(−1/3) = (−1 + 1)/(2 + ⅓) = 0. Round trip confirmed.
    Answer: f−1(x) = (3x + 1)/(2 − x), domain x ≠ 2, range y ≠ −3. Why this is worth knowing: "domain of the inverse = range of the original" is examined constantly and is the single most reliable way to get the last two marks of this question.

  3. Show that (1 − cos 2x)/sin 2x = tan x for all x where both sides are defined. HL only 4 marks · No GDC

    Show the answer
    1. Start from the double-angle identities, not from the answer. cos 2x = 1 − 2sin2x and sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x.
    2. Numerator. 1 − cos 2x = 1 − (1 − 2sin2x) = 2sin2x.
    3. Divide. (1 − cos 2x)/sin 2x = 2sin2x/(2 sin x cos x).
    4. Cancel and recognise. = sin x/cos x = tan x, as required.
    5. Domain note. This requires sin 2x ≠ 0 and cos x ≠ 0; both fail at the same points, so the identity holds wherever either side is defined.
    Answer: shown. Why this is worth knowing: this is the canonical "show that" — the identities are in the formula booklet, the cancellation is not. It also appears inside integrals in Topic 5, where ∫ tan x dx is done by rewriting the integrand this way round.

  4. Prove by induction that 32n − 1 is divisible by 8 for all n ∈ ℤ+. HL only 6 marks · No GDC

    Show the answer
    1. Base case, n = 1. 32 − 1 = 8, which is divisible by 8. (A1)
    2. Assumption. Assume that for some k ≥ 1, 32k − 1 = 8m for some integer m, i.e. 32k = 8m + 1. (M1)
    3. Inductive step. Consider n = k + 1: 32(k+1) − 1 = 32k+2 − 1 = 9 · 32k − 1. (M1) for the index law.
    4. Use the assumption. = 9(8m + 1) − 1 = 72m + 8 = 8(9m + 1). (A1)(A1)
    5. Conclude. Since 9m + 1 is an integer, 32(k+1) − 1 is divisible by 8.
    6. Final sentence. The statement is true for n = 1, and truth for n = k implies truth for n = k + 1, so by induction it is true for all positive integers n. (R1)
    7. The trick worth remembering: rewriting 32k = 8m + 1 at the assumption stage turns the step into one substitution. Students who leave the assumption as "is divisible by 8" usually get stuck at step 4.
    Answer: as proved. Why this is worth knowing: divisibility induction is the version that appears most often, and the manoeuvre "express the assumption as an equation with an integer parameter m" converts every one of them into the same two lines.

  5. Evaluate ∫0π/2 x sin 2x dx, giving your answer in an exact form. HL only 6 marks · No GDC

    Show the answer
    1. Integration by parts. Let u = x and dv = sin 2x dx. Then du = dx and v = −½ cos 2x. (M1)(A1)
    2. Apply the formula. ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du, so the integral is [−½x cos 2x]0π/2 + ½∫0π/2 cos 2x dx. (M1)
    3. First term. At x = π/2: −½ · (π/2) · cos π = −½ · (π/2) · (−1) = π/4. At x = 0: 0. So the boundary term is π/4.
    4. Second term. ½[½ sin 2x]0π/2 = ¼(sin π − sin 0) = 0. (A1)
    5. Combine. The value is π/4 + 0 = π/4. (A1)
    6. Sanity check. x sin 2x is non-negative on [0, π/2] (both factors are), so the integral must be positive — and π/4 ≈ 0.785 is. A GDC check gives 0.785398, confirming it.
    Answer: π/4. Why this is worth knowing: choosing u = x — the polynomial factor — is the rule that makes integration by parts terminate. Differentiate the polynomial, integrate the trig or exponential, and the second integral is always simpler.

  6. Find the three cube roots of −8i, giving your answers in the form a + bi. HL only 7 marks · GDC allowed

    Show the answer
    1. Write in modulus–argument form. −8i has modulus 8 and argument −π/2 (equivalently 3π/2). Use 3π/2 to keep the angles positive: −8i = 8 cis(3π/2). (M1)(A1)
    2. Apply De Moivre for roots. The cube roots are 81/3 cis((3π/2 + 2kπ)/3) = 2 cis(π/2 + 2kπ/3) for k = 0, 1, 2. (M1)(A1)
    3. List the three arguments. π/2, π/2 + 2π/3 = 7π/6, and π/2 + 4π/3 = 11π/6.
    4. Convert to Cartesian form. 2 cis(π/2) = 2i; 2 cis(7π/6) = 2(−√3/2 − ½i) = −√3 − i; 2 cis(11π/6) = 2(√3/2 − ½i) = √3 − i. (A1)(A1)
    5. Check by cubing. (2i)3 = 8i3 = −8i, correct.
    6. Check the sum. 2i + (−√3 − i) + (√3 − i) = 0. The nth roots of any complex number always sum to zero, which is the fastest possible check on this question type. Geometrically they are the vertices of an equilateral triangle on the circle of radius 2.
    Answer: 2i, −√3 − i, √3 − i. Why this is worth knowing: adding 2kπ before dividing by n is the step that produces all the roots; forgetting it is why students find one root instead of three.

  7. A machine fills bags of rice. The mass delivered is normally distributed with standard deviation 12 g. The target mean is 500 g. A random sample of 40 bags has mean 496.3 g. (a) State suitable hypotheses. (b) Calculate the test statistic. (c) State the p-value. (d) Write a conclusion in context at the 5% significance level. HL only 7 marks · GDC allowed

    Show the answer
    1. (a) Hypotheses. Let μ be the population mean mass of a bag. H0: μ = 500; H1: μ ≠ 500. Two-tailed, because the question asks whether the mean differs, not whether it is lower. (A1)(A1)
    2. (b) Test statistic. The standard error is 12/√40 = 1.8974. Then z = (496.3 − 500)/1.8974 = −3.7/1.8974 = −1.9500. (M1)(A1)
    3. (c) p-value. p = 2 × P(Z < −1.95) = 2 × 0.02559 = 0.0512. (A1) from the GDC or tables.
    4. (d) Conclusion. Since p = 0.0512 > 0.05 (equivalently |−1.95| < 1.96), there is insufficient evidence at the 5% level to reject H0. We cannot conclude that the mean has changed. (R1)
    5. The two sentences that lose the mark. "Accept H0" — never write this; a hypothesis test either rejects or fails to reject. "The mean is 500 g" — the test does not establish that either.
    6. Interpretation, if asked. The result is very close to the boundary; a larger sample would likely resolve it. That observation, when the question invites comment, is worth a reasoning mark.
    Answer: H0: μ = 500, H1: μ ≠ 500; z = −1.95; p = 0.0512; do not reject H0 at the 5% level. Why this is worth knowing: four of the seven marks are the words around the number. The GDC gives you the number in four seconds and cannot give you anything else.

  8. Solve the differential equation dy/dx = y cos x, given that y = 2 when x = 0. Hence find the value of y when x = π/2. HL only 6 marks · GDC allowed

    Show the answer
    1. Separate the variables. (1/y) dy = cos x dx. (M1)
    2. Integrate both sides. ∫ (1/y) dy = ∫ cos x dx, so ln|y| = sin x + C. (A1)(A1)
    3. Exponentiate. y = Aesin x, where A = ±eC. (M1)
    4. Apply the condition. At x = 0: 2 = Aesin 0 = A, so A = 2 and y = 2esin x. (A1)
    5. Hence. At x = π/2: sin(π/2) = 1, so y = 2e ≈ 5.44. (A1)
    6. Sanity check. sin x ranges over [−1, 1], so y ranges over [2e−1, 2e] ≈ [0.736, 5.44]. The solution is periodic and never changes sign, which is consistent with an exponential-model equation in which y = 0 is itself a solution.
    Answer: y = 2esin x, and y = 2e ≈ 5.44 at x = π/2. Why this is worth knowing: separating, integrating, exponentiating and then applying the condition is one routine that handles every separable equation in the syllabus — the same routine as exponential growth and Newton's law of cooling.

show that — a command term where the result is printed; only the visible working is credited.
hence — a command term obliging you to use the immediately preceding result.
deduce — reach a conclusion by a short argument from something already established.
justify — give the reason behind an answer, not the answer itself.
evaluate — weigh something up, with reasons and numbers, and reach a qualified judgement.
verify — substitute and check a given result; do not re-solve.
method mark (M) — awarded for a correct step even when the arithmetic that follows is wrong.
accuracy mark (A) — awarded for a correct value or expression following a correct method.
reasoning mark (R) — awarded for a justification, comparison or conclusion in context.
follow through (FT) — credit for correct work built on your own earlier incorrect value.
answer given (AG) — a result printed in the question, as in "show that".
markscheme — the published marking instructions; the nearest thing to a statement of what the IB actually rewards.
proof by induction — prove a base case, then prove that truth for n = k forces truth for n = k + 1.

counterexample — a single case that satisfies a claim's hypothesis but not its conclusion, disproving it.
lowest terms — a fraction whose numerator and denominator share no common factor; the engine of the √2 proof.
exact value — an answer in surds, fractions, π or e, not a decimal approximation.

radians — the angle measure required by every calculus formula involving trig functions.

p-value — the probability of a result at least as extreme as the one observed, if H0 is true.
critical value — the boundary of the rejection region; 1.96 for a two-tailed 5% normal test.
Type I error — rejecting H0 when it is true.
Type II error — failing to reject H0 when it is false.
follow-through chain — the linked parts of a Paper 3 problem, where a later part is marked using your earlier answer.
formula booklet — the booklet of formulas provided in every exam; knowing its contents is a skill.
exploration — the internally assessed 20-mark investigation; also called the IA.
deliberate practice — practising the specific move you cannot yet do, at the edge of your ability.
interleaving — mixing topics within a session so that method selection is practised, not just execution.
