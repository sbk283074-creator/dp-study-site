Geometry & trigonometry

Two halves that turn out to be one subject. The geometry half is about measuring space: distances and angles in 3-D, areas, volumes. The trigonometry half is about periodic behaviour, and it is the single most transferable skill in the course — trig identities are the engine of Topic 5 integration, and vectors are the language of kinematics. At HL you also prove the identities rather than just quote them.

3.1 Three-dimensional geometry

SL / HL

Intuition: in 3-D you very rarely need new formulas — you need to find the right right-angled triangle inside the solid. Every 3-D problem is: identify a right triangle, apply Pythagoras or SOHCAHTOA, then interpret. The skill is visual, and it is trainable: always redraw the relevant triangle flat, in 2-D, beside the solid.

Formal statements:

  • Distance between \((x_1,y_1,z_1)\) and \((x_2,y_2,z_2)\): \(d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2+(z_2-z_1)^2}\).
  • Angle between a line and a plane: the angle between the line and its projection onto the plane. Method: pick two points on the line, drop perpendiculars to the plane, and the projection is the segment joining the feet.
  • Angle between two lines: the angle between lines drawn parallel to them that intersect.
  • Angle between two planes HL only: the angle between lines in each plane that are both perpendicular to the line of intersection.

Worked example 1 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. A cuboid has base \(ABCD\) with \(AB=8\), \(BC=6\), and height \(AE=5\). (a) Find the length of the space diagonal \(AG\). (b) Find the angle between \(AG\) and the base plane \(ABCD\).

  1. Base diagonal \(AC = \sqrt{8^2+6^2} = 10\) (the 6-8-10 triangle — memorise it, it recurs).
  2. Space diagonal \(AG = \sqrt{AC^2+AE^2} = \sqrt{100+25} = \sqrt{125} = 5\sqrt5 \approx 11.2\).
  3. The projection of \(AG\) onto the base plane is \(AC\). So the required angle is \(\angle GAC\), in the right triangle \(ACG\) (right angle at \(C\), since \(CG\) is vertical).
  4. \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{CG}{AC} = \dfrac{5}{10} = 0.5 \Rightarrow \theta = 26.6^\circ\).
Answer: \(AG=5\sqrt5\approx11.2\); angle \(26.6^\circ\). Marks: one for \(AC\), one for \(AG\), two for identifying the projection as \(AC\), two for the angle. Sanity check: the angle must be less than the angle between \(AG\) and any line in the base through \(A\); and \(5/10\) giving about 27° is sensible.
Worked example 2 IB-style · 9 marks · GDC allowed

Question. A solid cone has base radius 6 cm and vertical height 8 cm. (a) Find its volume and total surface area. (b) The cone is melted and recast into a sphere. Find the radius of the sphere. (c) Find the angle between the slant edge and the base plane.

  1. Slant height \(l = \sqrt{6^2+8^2} = 10\).
  2. Volume \(=\frac13\pi r^2h = \frac13\pi(36)(8) = 96\pi \approx 302\) cm³.
  3. Surface area \(= \pi r^2 + \pi r l = 36\pi + 60\pi = 96\pi \approx 302\) cm². (Coincidence of numbers here, not a rule.)
  4. Sphere: \(\frac43\pi R^3 = 96\pi \Rightarrow R^3 = 72 \Rightarrow R = 4.16\) cm.
  5. Angle between slant edge and base: the slant edge runs from the apex to a point on the rim; its projection on the base is the radius to that point. So in the right triangle with legs 8 (height) and 6 (radius): \(\tan\theta = \frac{8}{6} \Rightarrow \theta = 53.1^\circ\).
Answer: \(V=96\pi\) cm³, \(A=96\pi\) cm², \(R=4.16\) cm, angle \(53.1^\circ\). The volume-conservation step (b) is a standard IB twist: equate volumes, cancel \(\pi\), solve. The angle in (c) uses the axial cross-section triangle, not the surface.
Common trap

(1) Using the wrong triangle: the angle between a line and a plane is not the angle between the line and any random line in the plane — it is the angle with the projection. (2) Confusing slant height \(l\) with vertical height \(h\) in \(\pi r l\). (3) Forgetting that a pyramid's volume has the factor \(\frac13\): the IB gives it in the booklet, but students reach for \(\frac12\) under pressure. (4) Rounding before the final step in multi-part volume questions — keep \(\pi\) symbolic as long as possible.

3.2 Radian measure, arc length and sector area

SL / HL

Intuition: degrees are arbitrary; someone chose 360. Radians are forced by the geometry: one radian is the angle whose arc equals the radius. That makes radians the natural unit for calculus — which is why \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\sin x = \cos x\) is only true in radians, and why every trig question in Topic 5 assumes them.

\[\pi\text{ radians} = 180^\circ,\qquad l = r\theta,\qquad A_{\text{sector}} = \tfrac12 r^2\theta,\qquad A_{\text{segment}} = \tfrac12 r^2(\theta-\sin\theta)\quad(\theta\text{ in radians}).\]
Worked example 3 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. A sector of a circle of radius 12 cm has area 160 cm². (a) Find the angle in radians. (b) Find the perimeter of the sector. (c) The sector is folded into a cone. Find the base radius of the cone.

  1. \(\frac12 r^2\theta = 160 \Rightarrow \frac12(144)\theta = 160 \Rightarrow \theta = \frac{320}{144} = \frac{20}{9} = 2.22\) rad.
  2. Arc length \(l = r\theta = 12\cdot\frac{20}{9} = \frac{80}{3} = 26.7\) cm. Perimeter \(= l + 2r = 26.7+24 = 50.7\) cm.
  3. When folded, the arc becomes the circumference of the cone's base: \(2\pi R = \frac{80}{3}\Rightarrow R = \frac{40}{3\pi} = 4.24\) cm.
Answer: (a) 2.22 rad; (b) 50.7 cm; (c) 4.24 cm. The folding step is a favourite: "arc length becomes circumference" is the one idea you need. Sanity check: \(R=4.24 < 12\), as it must be.

3.3 The unit circle and exact values

SL / HL

Intuition: put a circle of radius 1 at the origin. For a point \(P\) at angle \(\theta\) measured anticlockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis, the coordinates are literally \((\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\). That is not a formula to memorise; it is the definition of sine and cosine for any angle. Everything else — signs in quadrants, exact values, identities — comes from this picture.

How to use it

Memorise the \(\sin\) row as \(\frac{\sqrt0}{2},\frac{\sqrt1}{2},\frac{\sqrt2}{2},\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\frac{\sqrt4}{2}\) and the \(\cos\) row is the same list backwards. Then \(\tan = \sin/\cos\). For any other angle, use the quadrant: find the related acute angle (the "reference angle") and attach the sign. ASTC — All positive in quadrant 1, Sin positive in 2, Tan in 3, Cos in 4.

3.4 Graphs of the trigonometric functions

SL / HL

\(y=\sin x\): period \(2\pi\), amplitude 1, passes through the origin, zeros at multiples of \(\pi\). \(y=\cos x\): the same curve shifted left by \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) — zeros at odd multiples of \(\frac{\pi}{2}\). \(y=\tan x\): period \(\pi\), vertical asymptotes at \(x=\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi\), range \(\mathbb{R}\). In the form \(y=a\sin(b(x-c))+d\): amplitude \(|a|\), period \(\frac{2\pi}{|b|}\), shift \(\begin{pmatrix}c\\d\end{pmatrix}\).

Common trap

Confusing \(\sin 2x\) (period \(\pi\), two cycles in \(2\pi\)) with \(2\sin x\) (period \(2\pi\), double height). And with \(y=\sin(x+\frac{\pi}{3})\) the graph moves left, not right — the sign inside the bracket is always the opposite of your instinct.

3.5 Solving trigonometric equations

SL / HL
How to use it — the five-step method
  1. Isolate one trig function (or factorise to get products equal to zero).
  2. Substitue if disguised: \(\sin^2x\) or \(\cos 2x\) may let you turn the equation into a quadratic in \(\sin x\) — use \(\cos^2x = 1-\sin^2x\).
  3. Find the principal value with your GDC (or exact values), and note the interval and whether it is degrees or radians.
  4. Find all solutions in the interval using symmetry: for \(\sin x = k\), the second solution is \(\pi - \theta\); for \(\cos x = k\), it is \(-\theta\) (i.e. \(2\pi-\theta\)); for \(\tan x=k\), add \(\pi\).
  5. Check the endpoints of the interval, and count solutions against a sketch.
Worked example 4 IB-style · 7 marks · No GDC

Question. Solve \(2\sin^2x + 3\cos x = 0\) for \(0\le x \le 2\pi\).

  1. Convert to one function using \(\sin^2x = 1-\cos^2x\): \(2(1-\cos^2x)+3\cos x = 0\).
  2. \(2-2\cos^2x+3\cos x = 0 \Rightarrow 2\cos^2x - 3\cos x - 2 = 0\).
  3. Factorise: \((2\cos x + 1)(\cos x - 2) = 0\).
  4. \(\cos x = -\frac12\) or \(\cos x = 2\) (reject — cosine lies in \([-1,1]\)).
  5. \(\cos x = -\frac12\): principal value \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\); by symmetry the other solution in \([0,2\pi]\) is \(2\pi - \frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac{4\pi}{3}\).
Answer: \(x=\dfrac{2\pi}{3},\ \dfrac{4\pi}{3}\). Rejecting \(\cos x=2\) is a mark. Sanity check: \(2\sin^2(120°)+3\cos120° = 2(\frac34)+3(-\frac12) = 1.5-1.5=0\). ✓
Worked example 5 IB-style · 8 marks · GDC allowed

Question. Solve \(\sin 2x = \sin x\) for \(0\le x\le 2\pi\), and hence find the total area enclosed between \(y=\sin 2x - \sin x\) and the \(x\)-axis on \([0,\pi]\).

  1. Use \(\sin 2x = 2\sin x\cos x\): \(2\sin x\cos x - \sin x = 0 \Rightarrow \sin x(2\cos x - 1)=0\).
  2. \(\sin x = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0,\ \pi,\ 2\pi\); \(\cos x = \frac12 \Rightarrow x = \frac{\pi}{3},\ \frac{5\pi}{3}\).
  3. On \([0,\pi]\): the curve crosses at \(x=0,\ \frac{\pi}{3},\ \pi\). Test \(x=\frac{\pi}{6}\): \(\sin\frac{\pi}{3}-\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = 0.866-0.5>0\). Test \(x=\frac{2\pi}{3}\): \(\sin\frac{4\pi}{3}-\sin\frac{2\pi}{3} = -0.866-0.866<0\).
  4. Area \(= \left|\int_0^{\pi/3}(\sin2x-\sin x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right| + \left|\int_{\pi/3}^{\pi}(\sin2x-\sin x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\).
  5. Antiderivative: \(-\frac12\cos2x+\cos x\). First piece: \(\left[-\frac12\cos 2x+\cos x\right]_0^{\pi/3} = \left(\frac14+\frac12\right) - \left(-\frac12+1\right) = \frac34-\frac12 = \frac14\).
  6. Second piece: \(\left[-\frac12\cos2x+\cos x\right]_{\pi/3}^{\pi} = \left(-\frac12 - 1\right) - \frac34 = -\frac94\); absolute value \(\frac94\).
  7. Total \(= \frac14+\frac94 = \frac52 = 2.5\).
Answer: solutions \(0,\frac{\pi}{3},\pi,\frac{5\pi}{3},2\pi\); area \(2.5\). This question is a perfect example of Topics 3 and 5 fused: the trig equation supplies the limits of integration, and "total area" forces you to split at the crossing and take absolute values.

3.6 Sine rule, cosine rule, area of a triangle

SL / HL \[\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C},\qquad c^2 = a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C \quad\left(\text{i.e. }\cos C = \frac{a^2+b^2-c^2}{2ab}\right),\qquad \text{Area} = \frac12 ab\sin C.\]
Proof Area formula, sine rule (from area) and cosine rule (from coordinates)

Area: drop the perpendicular from \(B\) to side \(b\); its length is \(c\sin A\). So area \(=\frac12\times b \times c\sin A\). Same argument from any vertex. ■

Sine rule: all three area expressions are equal: \(\frac12 bc\sin A = \frac12 ac\sin B = \frac12 ab\sin C\). Divide by \(\frac12 abc\): \(\frac{\sin A}{a} = \frac{\sin B}{b} = \frac{\sin C}{c}\). Invert. ■

Cosine rule: place \(C\) at the origin, \(A\) at \((b,0)\) and \(B\) at \((a\cos C,\ a\sin C)\). Then \(c^2 = (a\cos C - b)^2 + (a\sin C)^2 = a^2\cos^2C - 2ab\cos C + b^2 + a^2\sin^2C = a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C\). ■

Meaning: the cosine rule is Pythagoras with a correction term \(-2ab\cos C\); when \(C=90^\circ\) the correction vanishes, which is why Pythagoras is a special case. Wrong tool when: you know two angles and a side — use the sine rule; using the cosine rule there wastes two minutes.

The ambiguous case (SSA)

When you know two sides and a non-included angle, there may be two triangles, one, or none. The test: given \(a,\ b,\ A\), compute \(\sin B = \frac{b\sin A}{a}\). If \(\sin B>1\): no triangle. If \(\sin B = 1\): one right triangle. If \(\sin B<1\): two candidate angles, \(B_1=\arcsin(\cdot)\) and \(B_2 = 180^\circ - B_1\); keep \(B_2\) only if \(A + B_2 < 180^\circ\).

Worked example 6 IB-style · 8 marks · GDC allowed

Question. In triangle \(ABC\), \(A=35^\circ\), \(a=7\), \(b=10\). Find all possible values of \(B\), \(C\) and \(c\).

  1. \(\dfrac{\sin B}{10} = \dfrac{\sin 35^\circ}{7} \Rightarrow \sin B = \dfrac{10\sin35^\circ}{7} = \dfrac{10(0.5736)}{7} = 0.8194\).
  2. \(B_1 = \arcsin(0.8194) = 55.0^\circ\). Then \(C_1 = 180-35-55.0 = 90.0^\circ\) and \(c_1 = \dfrac{7\sin90^\circ}{\sin35^\circ} = \dfrac{7}{0.5736} = 12.2\).
  3. Second candidate: \(B_2 = 180-55.0 = 125.0^\circ\). Check \(A+B_2 = 160^\circ < 180^\circ\) ✓, so a second triangle exists.
  4. \(C_2 = 180-35-125.0 = 20.0^\circ\); \(c_2 = \dfrac{7\sin20^\circ}{\sin35^\circ} = \dfrac{7(0.3420)}{0.5736} = 4.17\).
Answer: two triangles: \((B,C,c) = (55.0^\circ, 90.0^\circ, 12.2)\) and \((125.0^\circ, 20.0^\circ, 4.17)\). The mark scheme always allocates a mark for stating that there are two triangles. Sketch the SSA configuration to see it: side \(a\) swinging about \(C\) can cut the ray from \(A\) twice.
Worked example 7 IB-style · 6 marks · GDC allowed

Question. Two ships leave port \(P\). Ship A sails 12 km on a bearing of \(040^\circ\); ship B sails 15 km on a bearing of \(105^\circ\). Find the distance between them and the bearing of B from A.

  1. The angle at \(P\) between the two paths is \(105^\circ-40^\circ = 65^\circ\).
  2. Cosine rule: \(d^2 = 12^2+15^2-2(12)(15)\cos65^\circ = 144+225-360(0.4226) = 369-152.1 = 216.9\).
  3. \(d = 14.7\) km.
  4. Angle at A: \(\dfrac{\sin(\angle A)}{15} = \dfrac{\sin 65^\circ}{14.73} \Rightarrow \sin(\angle A) = \dfrac{15(0.9063)}{14.73} = 0.9229\), so \(\angle A = 67.3^\circ\).
  5. Bearing of B from A: the bearing of A from P is \(040^\circ\), so the bearing of P from A is \(220^\circ\). The angle at A between AP and AB is \(67.3^\circ\), and B lies "to the south-east" of A, so bearing \(= 220^\circ - 67.3^\circ = 152.7^\circ\).
Answer: 14.7 km; bearing \(153^\circ\). Bearing questions are geometry questions in disguise — always sketch a compass rose with north lines. Get the reverse bearing right (add/subtract 180°) before touching the internal angle.

3.7 Trigonometric identities

SL / HL

\(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\) and \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta = 1\) are given in the formula booklet and are SL/HL. The rest of this section is HL.

Proof of \(\sin^2+\cos^2=1\): on the unit circle the point \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) is at distance 1 from the origin, so by Pythagoras \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta = 1\). Divide by \(\cos^2\theta\) to get \(1+\tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta\); divide by \(\sin^2\theta\) to get \(1+\cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta\).

HL: reciprocal ratios and the Pythagorean identities

HL only \[\sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta},\qquad \csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta},\qquad \cot\theta = \frac{1}{\tan\theta}=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}.\] \[1+\tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta,\qquad 1+\cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta.\]

HL: compound-angle identities, with proof

HL only \[\sin(A\pm B) = \sin A\cos B \pm \cos A\sin B\] \[\cos(A\pm B) = \cos A\cos B \mp \sin A\sin B\] \[\tan(A\pm B) = \frac{\tan A \pm \tan B}{1 \mp \tan A\tan B}\]
Proof \(\cos(A-B)=\cos A\cos B+\sin A\sin B\), and all the others from it
  1. Take \(P(\cos A,\sin A)\) and \(Q(\cos B,\sin B)\) on the unit circle. Both are distance 1 from \(O\).
  2. By the distance formula: \(PQ^2 = (\cos A-\cos B)^2+(\sin A-\sin B)^2 = \cos^2A+\cos^2B-2\cos A\cos B + \sin^2A+\sin^2B-2\sin A\sin B = 2 - 2(\cos A\cos B+\sin A\sin B)\).
  3. By the cosine rule in triangle \(POQ\), where \(\angle POQ = A-B\): \(PQ^2 = 1^2+1^2-2(1)(1)\cos(A-B) = 2-2\cos(A-B)\).
  4. Equate: \(2-2\cos(A-B) = 2-2(\cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B)\), so \(\cos(A-B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B\). ■
  5. Replace \(B\) by \(-B\), using \(\cos(-B)=\cos B\) and \(\sin(-B)=-\sin B\): \(\cos(A+B) = \cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B\).
  6. For sine: \(\sin(A+B) = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-(A+B)\right) = \cos\!\left(\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-A\right)-B\right) = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-A\right)\cos B + \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-A\right)\sin B = \sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B\).
  7. Replace \(B\) by \(-B\) for \(\sin(A-B)\). For tangent, divide \(\sin(A+B)\) by \(\cos(A+B)\) and then divide top and bottom by \(\cos A\cos B\): \(\tan(A+B) = \dfrac{\tan A+\tan B}{1-\tan A\tan B}\).

Meaning: one picture generates six identities. If you remember the construction, you never need to memorise the list. Wrong tool when: you are solving an equation numerically — identities are for exact work and for integration, not for a GDC solve.

HL: double-angle identities

HL only \[\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta,\qquad \cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta-1 = 1-2\sin^2\theta,\qquad \tan 2\theta = \frac{2\tan\theta}{1-\tan^2\theta}.\]
Proof Double-angle identities from the compound-angle ones (set \(B=A\))
  1. \(\sin(A+A) = \sin A\cos A + \cos A\sin A = 2\sin A\cos A\).
  2. \(\cos(A+A) = \cos A\cos A - \sin A\sin A = \cos^2A - \sin^2A\).
  3. Using \(\sin^2A = 1-\cos^2A\): \(\cos 2A = \cos^2A-(1-\cos^2A) = 2\cos^2A-1\).
  4. Using \(\cos^2A = 1-\sin^2A\): \(\cos 2A = (1-\sin^2A)-\sin^2A = 1-2\sin^2A\).
  5. \(\tan 2A = \dfrac{\tan A+\tan A}{1-\tan A\tan A} = \dfrac{2\tan A}{1-\tan^2A}\).

Meaning: the three forms of \(\cos 2\theta\) are the same fact dressed for three jobs: use \(1-2\sin^2\theta\) when the rest of the expression is in sines, \(2\cos^2\theta-1\) when it is in cosines, and \(\cos^2-\sin^2\) when both appear. Choosing the right form is the exam skill — this is exactly what \(\int\sin^2x\,\mathrm{d}x\) requires in Topic 5.

Worked example 8 IB-style · 7 marks · No GDC

Question. Given \(\sin\theta = \dfrac35\) with \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}<\theta<\pi\), find the exact values of (a) \(\cos\theta\); (b) \(\sin 2\theta\); (c) \(\cos 2\theta\); (d) \(\tan 2\theta\).

  1. \(\theta\) is in quadrant 2, so \(\cos\theta<0\): \(\cos\theta = -\sqrt{1-\frac{9}{25}} = -\frac45\).
  2. \(\sin2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = 2\cdot\frac35\cdot\left(-\frac45\right) = -\frac{24}{25}\).
  3. \(\cos 2\theta = 1-2\sin^2\theta = 1-2\cdot\frac{9}{25} = \frac{7}{25}\).
  4. \(\tan 2\theta = \dfrac{\sin2\theta}{\cos2\theta} = \dfrac{-24/25}{7/25} = -\dfrac{24}{7}\).
  5. Check: \(\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta<\pi\) means \(\pi<2\theta<2\pi\), so \(2\theta\) is in quadrants 3 or 4. With \(\sin2\theta<0\) and \(\cos2\theta>0\), \(2\theta\) is in quadrant 4. ✓
Answer: \(-\frac45,\ -\frac{24}{25},\ \frac{7}{25},\ -\frac{24}{7}\). The quadrant check in step 5 is what turns a correct answer into a justified one.
Worked example 9 IB-style · 8 marks · No GDC

Question. (a) Show that \(\dfrac{\sin 2x}{1+\cos 2x} = \tan x\). (b) Hence solve \(\tan x = 3\cos x\) for \(0\le x\le 2\pi\).

  1. \(\sin 2x = 2\sin x\cos x\) and \(1+\cos 2x = 1 + (2\cos^2x-1) = 2\cos^2x\).
  2. So \(\dfrac{\sin2x}{1+\cos2x} = \dfrac{2\sin x\cos x}{2\cos^2x} = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \tan x\). ■
  3. For (b): \(3\cos x = \tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} \Rightarrow 3\cos^2x = \sin x\).
  4. \(3(1-\sin^2x) = \sin x \Rightarrow 3\sin^2x + \sin x - 3 = 0\).
  5. \(\sin x = \dfrac{-1\pm\sqrt{1+36}}{6} = \dfrac{-1\pm\sqrt{37}}{6}\). \(\frac{-1+\sqrt{37}}{6} = 0.8471\); \(\frac{-1-\sqrt{37}}{6} = -1.180\) (reject, outside \([-1,1]\)).
  6. \(x = \arcsin(0.8471) = 1.012\) or \(x = \pi - 1.012 = 2.130\).
  7. Check: both satisfy \(\cos x>0\) for the first and \(\cos x<0\) for the second? At \(x=2.130\), \(\cos x = -0.531\) and \(\tan x = -1.594\), while \(3\cos x = -1.594\). ✓ Both valid.
Answer: \(x = 1.01\) or \(2.13\) (radians). The "hence" in (b) is a hint to rewrite \(\tan x\) using the identity from (a) — although here the direct \(\tan=\sin/\cos\) route is equivalent. Always check both solutions in the original equation: squaring can introduce extras.

3.8 HL: inverse trigonometric functions

HL only

Intuition: \(\sin x\) is not one-to-one (infinitely many angles share a sine), so it cannot have an inverse unless we restrict its domain. The IB uses principal values:

FunctionDomainRange (principal values)
\(y=\arcsin x\)\(-1\le x\le1\)\(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\le y\le\dfrac{\pi}{2}\)
\(y=\arccos x\)\(-1\le x\le1\)\(0\le y\le\pi\)
\(y=\arctan x\)\(\mathbb{R}\)\(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}< y<\dfrac{\pi}{2}\)

The graphs are the reflections of the restricted trig graphs in \(y=x\). Two consequences you will be tested on: \(\arcsin(\sin x)\) is not always \(x\) (only when \(x\) is in the principal range), and \(\arcsin x + \arccos x = \frac{\pi}{2}\).

Worked example 10 IB-style · 6 marks · No GDC

Question. Find the exact value of (a) \(\arcsin\!\left(\sin\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\); (b) \(\arctan 1 + \arccos\!\left(-\frac12\right)\); (c) \(\tan\!\left(\arcsin\frac35\right)\).

  1. (a) \(\sin\frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac12\), and \(\arcsin\frac12 = \frac{\pi}{6}\), not \(\frac{5\pi}{6}\) — because \(\frac{5\pi}{6}\) is outside the principal range \([-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]\).
  2. (b) \(\arctan 1 = \frac{\pi}{4}\); \(\arccos(-\frac12) = \frac{2\pi}{3}\). Sum \(= \frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{2\pi}{3}=\frac{11\pi}{12}\).
  3. (c) Let \(\theta = \arcsin\frac35\), so \(\sin\theta=\frac35\) and \(\theta\) is acute. Then \(\cos\theta = \frac45\), so \(\tan\theta = \frac{3/5}{4/5} = \frac34\).
Answer: (a) \(\frac{\pi}{6}\); (b) \(\frac{11\pi}{12}\); (c) \(\frac34\). Part (a) is the single most common inverse-trig error in the whole course: arcsin returns the principal value, always.

3.9 HL: vectors in three dimensions

HL only

Intuition: a vector is an arrow: it has length and direction, but no fixed position. You add them tip-to-tail; you scale them by stretching. The scalar (dot) product is a machine that turns two vectors into a number, and that number is zero exactly when the vectors are perpendicular — which makes it the fastest perpendicularity test in mathematics.

\[\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3 = |\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta,\qquad |\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2}.\]
Proof Why \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta\)
  1. Place the tails of \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) together; the third side of the triangle is \(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\).
  2. Cosine rule: \(|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}|^2 = |\mathbf{a}|^2+|\mathbf{b}|^2-2|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta\).
  3. Expand the left side in components: \(|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}|^2 = (b_1-a_1)^2+(b_2-a_2)^2+(b_3-a_3)^2 = |\mathbf{a}|^2+|\mathbf{b}|^2-2(a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3)\).
  4. Compare the two expressions: \(a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3 = |\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta\). ■

Meaning: the dot product measures "how much of \(\mathbf{a}\) points along \(\mathbf{b}\)". If \(\theta=90^\circ\) it is zero; if the vectors point the same way it is \(|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\). Wrong tool when: you need a vector perpendicular to two given vectors — that is the vector (cross) product, which is the subject of section 3.10. The fallback that always works: solve \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{b}=0\) for a normal \(\mathbf{n}\).

Lines: \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b}\)

The line through point \(\mathbf{a}\) in direction \(\mathbf{b}\) is \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+\lambda\mathbf{b}\), \(\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\). In coordinates: \(\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\\a_3\end{pmatrix} + \lambda\begin{pmatrix}b_1\\b_2\\b_3\end{pmatrix}\), giving the parametric equations \(x=a_1+\lambda b_1\), etc. Eliminating \(\lambda\) gives the Cartesian form \(\frac{x-a_1}{b_1}=\frac{y-a_2}{b_2}=\frac{z-a_3}{b_3}\).

How to use it — classifying two lines
  1. Parallel: direction vectors are scalar multiples (\(\mathbf{b}_1 = k\mathbf{b}_2\)).
  2. Coincident: parallel and they share a point (check whether a point on one satisfies the other's equation).
  3. Intersecting: set the two parametric forms equal, giving three equations in two unknowns (\(\lambda,\mu\)); solve two, then check the third. Consistent → intersect.
  4. Skew: not parallel and the system is inconsistent — the lines miss each other in space. This is the case that only exists in 3-D, and the IB loves it.
  5. Angle between lines: \(\cos\theta = \dfrac{|\mathbf{b}_1\cdot\mathbf{b}_2|}{|\mathbf{b}_1||\mathbf{b}_2|}\) — the modulus gives the acute angle.
Worked example 11 IB-style · 9 marks · GDC allowed

Question. Line \(L_1\): \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix}\). Line \(L_2\): \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}+\mu\begin{pmatrix}-1\\3\\2\end{pmatrix}\). (a) Show that the lines intersect and find the point of intersection. (b) Find the angle between them.

  1. Set equal: \(1+2\lambda = 4-\mu\); \(2-\lambda = 1+3\mu\); \(3+\lambda = 2\mu\).
  2. From the third: \(\lambda = 2\mu - 3\). Substitute into the second: \(2-(2\mu-3) = 1+3\mu \Rightarrow 5-2\mu = 1+3\mu \Rightarrow \mu = \frac45\), so \(\lambda = \frac85-3 = -\frac75\).
  3. Check the first equation: LHS \(= 1+2(-\frac75) = -\frac95\); RHS \(= 4-\frac45 = \frac{16}{5}\). Not equal!
  4. So the three equations are inconsistent: the lines do not intersect. They are not parallel (direction vectors are not multiples), so they are skew.
  5. Angle: \(\mathbf{b}_1\cdot\mathbf{b}_2 = (2)(-1)+(-1)(3)+(1)(2) = -2-3+2 = -3\); \(|\mathbf{b}_1|=\sqrt6\), \(|\mathbf{b}_2|=\sqrt{14}\).
  6. \(\cos\theta = \dfrac{|-3|}{\sqrt6\sqrt{14}} = \dfrac{3}{\sqrt{84}} = 0.3273 \Rightarrow \theta = 70.9^\circ\).
Answer: the lines are skew; the angle between them is \(70.9^\circ\). The trap is assuming intersection: you must solve two equations and then test the third. Two of three equations agreeing means nothing.
Worked example 12 IB-style · 9 marks · GDC allowed

Question. Two particles move with position vectors (metres) at time \(t\) (seconds): \(\mathbf{r}_A = \begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{r}_B = \begin{pmatrix}14\\5\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix}\). (a) Find the velocity of each particle and the speed of A. (b) Find the time when they are closest and the minimum distance between them.

  1. Velocities are the coefficients of \(t\): \(\mathbf{v}_A = \begin{pmatrix}3\\4\end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{v}_B=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix}\) m s−1. Speed of A \(= \sqrt{9+16}=5\) m s−1.
  2. Relative position: \(\mathbf{r}_A-\mathbf{r}_B = \begin{pmatrix}-12\\-6\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}2\\6\end{pmatrix}\).
  3. Squared distance: \(D(t) = (-12+2t)^2+(-6+6t)^2 = 144-48t+4t^2+36-72t+36t^2 = 180-120t+40t^2\).
  4. Minimise: \(D'(t) = -120+80t = 0 \Rightarrow t = 1.5\) s.
  5. \(D(1.5) = 180-180+40(2.25) = 90\), so minimum distance \(= \sqrt{90} = 9.49\) m.
  6. Alternative (vector) check: at closest approach the relative velocity is perpendicular to the relative position: \((\mathbf{r}_A-\mathbf{r}_B)\cdot(\mathbf{v}_A-\mathbf{v}_B)=0\). Relative velocity \(=\begin{pmatrix}2\\6\end{pmatrix}\); relative position at \(t=1.5\) is \(\begin{pmatrix}-9\\3\end{pmatrix}\); dot product \(=-18+18=0\). ✓
Answer: speeds 5 and \(\sqrt5\) m s−1; closest at \(t=1.5\) s with distance 9.49 m. The dot-product test in step 6 is a beautiful cross-check that uses two topics at once, and it is exactly the kind of reasoning Paper 3 rewards.
Common trap

(1) Forgetting the modulus in \(\cos\theta=\frac{|\mathbf{b}_1\cdot\mathbf{b}_2|}{|\mathbf{b}_1||\mathbf{b}_2|}\): without it you get the obtuse angle, and the IB asks for the acute one. (2) Concluding "intersect" from two of three equations. (3) Using position vectors instead of direction vectors for the angle between lines. (4) In kinematics, differentiating a position vector term by term but forgetting that a constant vector's derivative is zero.

3.10 HL: the vector (cross) product

HL only

Intuition: the dot product takes two vectors and returns a number — it answers "how much do these point the same way?". The cross product takes two vectors and returns a vector, and that vector is perpendicular to both of them. That single property is why the cross product is the tool for anything involving a plane, a normal direction, or an area in three dimensions.

\[\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\\ a_1&a_2&a_3\\ b_1&b_2&b_3\end{vmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}a_2b_3-a_3b_2\\ a_3b_1-a_1b_3\\ a_1b_2-a_2b_1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}| = |\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta.\]
Proof Why \(|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|\) is the area of a parallelogram
  1. The parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) has base \(|\mathbf{a}|\).
  2. Its perpendicular height is \(|\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between them.
  3. So the area is \(|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta\) — which is exactly \(|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|\).
  4. Halve it: the triangle with two sides \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) has area \(\tfrac12|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|\).

Meaning: the direction of \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\) carries the normal information; its magnitude carries an area. One object doing two jobs is why the IB so often asks for "the area of triangle \(ABC\)" immediately after asking for a cross product. Wrong tool when: the vectors are parallel (then \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}\)), or when the question is about an angle — use the dot product for that.

How to use it — the four properties that get tested
  1. Direction: perpendicular to both inputs, given by the right-hand rule. Always check it: dot your answer with each input vector — both results must be 0.
  2. Anti-commutative: \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}=-(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a})\). Order matters here, unlike with the dot product.
  3. Parallel test: \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}\) exactly when the vectors are parallel (or one is zero).
  4. Area: parallelogram \(=|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|\); triangle \(=\tfrac12|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|\).
Worked example 13 IB-style · 8 marks · cross product and area

Question. Let \(\mathbf{a}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\3\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}\). (a) Find \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\). (b) Hence find a unit vector perpendicular to both. (c) Find the area of the triangle with vertices \(O(0,0,0)\), \(A(1,-2,3)\) and \(B(4,0,-1)\).

  1. Component formula: \(\bigl((-2)(-1)-(3)(0),\ (3)(4)-(1)(-1),\ (1)(0)-(-2)(4)\bigr)\).
  2. \(= (2-0,\ 12+1,\ 0+8) = \begin{pmatrix}2\\13\\8\end{pmatrix}\).
  3. Verify: \((2,13,8)\cdot(1,-2,3) = 2-26+24 = 0\) ✓ and \((2,13,8)\cdot(4,0,-1) = 8+0-8 = 0\) ✓.
  4. Magnitude: \(|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}| = \sqrt{4+169+64} = \sqrt{237}\).
  5. Unit normal: \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{237}}\begin{pmatrix}2\\13\\8\end{pmatrix}\). Its negative is also perpendicular — the question asks for a unit vector, so either sign is accepted.
  6. Area of triangle \(OAB\) \(= \tfrac12|\overrightarrow{OA}\times\overrightarrow{OB}| = \tfrac12\sqrt{237} = 7.70\).
Answer: \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}=(2,13,8)\); unit normal \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{237}}(2,13,8)\); area \(=7.70\). The check in step 3 costs ten seconds and catches the single most common arithmetic slip — a sign error in the middle component.

3.11 HL: the equation of a plane

HL only

Intuition: a line needs a point and one direction. A plane needs a point and two non-parallel directions — or, more usefully, a point and a normal. The normal is the direction the plane does not go in, and it is far more powerful than the two directions, because "everything in the plane is perpendicular to the normal" is a single equation.

Three equivalent forms — you must be able to move between all three:

\[\underbrace{\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+\lambda\mathbf{b}+\mu\mathbf{c}}_{\text{point + two directions}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n}}_{\text{point + normal}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{ax+by+cz=d}_{\text{Cartesian, where }\mathbf{n}=\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\\c\end{pmatrix}}\]
How to use it — finding the equation of a plane
  1. Given a point \(\mathbf{a}\) and a normal \(\mathbf{n}\): write \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n}\), then expand to \(ax+by+cz=d\).
  2. Given three points \(A,B,C\): form two vectors in the plane, \(\mathbf{b}=\overrightarrow{AB}\) and \(\mathbf{c}=\overrightarrow{AC}\), then the normal is \(\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}\). Now use step 1.
  3. Given the Cartesian form \(ax+by+cz=d\): read the normal straight off as \(\mathbf{n}=(a,b,c)\). No working needed — this is why the IB often gives it in this form.
  4. Any multiple is the same plane: \(2x+4y-6z=8\) and \(x+2y-3z=4\) describe the same plane. Scale to taste, but keep it consistent within one question.
Worked example 14 IB-style · 9 marks · plane through three points

Question. Find the Cartesian equation of the plane through \(A(1,2,3)\), \(B(3,-1,4)\) and \(C(0,2,-1)\).

  1. Two vectors in the plane: \(\overrightarrow{AB}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-3\\1\end{pmatrix}\), \(\overrightarrow{AC}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\\-4\end{pmatrix}\).
  2. Normal \(= \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = \bigl((-3)(-4)-(1)(0),\ (1)(-1)-(2)(-4),\ (2)(0)-(-3)(-1)\bigr) = \begin{pmatrix}12\\7\\-3\end{pmatrix}\).
  3. Check: \((12,7,-3)\cdot(2,-3,1) = 24-21-3 = 0\) ✓; \((12,7,-3)\cdot(-1,0,-4) = -12+0+12 = 0\) ✓.
  4. Use \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n}\) with \(\mathbf{a}=A\): \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n} = 12(1)+7(2)-3(3) = 12+14-9 = 17\).
  5. So the plane is \(12x+7y-3z = 17\).
  6. Verify with the two unused points: \(B\): \(12(3)+7(-1)-3(4) = 36-7-12 = 17\) ✓; \(C\): \(12(0)+7(2)-3(-1) = 0+14+3 = 17\) ✓.
Answer: \(12x+7y-3z=17\). Substituting the third point back in is not paranoia — it is the only way to catch a cross-product slip before you spend ten more marks on a wrong normal.

3.12 HL: intersections and angles with planes

HL only

Everything in this section reduces to one idea: the normal does the work. Angles with a plane are angles with the normal (with a complement); intersecting a line with a plane is substituting a parametric line into one Cartesian equation.

Why the formulas look "swapped"

The angle between a line and a plane is the angle between the line and its projection onto the plane. That is the complement of the angle between the line and the normal — so the formula uses \(\sin\), not \(\cos\):

\[\sin\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{n}|}{|\mathbf{b}||\mathbf{n}|}\quad\text{(line vs plane)}, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2|}{|\mathbf{n}_1||\mathbf{n}_2|}\quad\text{(plane vs plane)}.\]

Two planes meet at the same angle as their normals — no complement needed there, because the normals are both in the "perpendicular" role.

How to use it — the three intersection cases
  1. Line \(\cap\) plane: substitute the parametric line \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+\lambda\mathbf{b}\) into \(ax+by+cz=d\), giving one equation in \(\lambda\). Solve, then substitute \(\lambda\) back into the line to get the point. If the coefficient of \(\lambda\) is zero: the line is either parallel to the plane (no solution) or lies in it (infinitely many).
  2. Plane \(\cap\) plane: a line. Solve the two Cartesian equations together — put one variable equal to \(t\) and express the other two in terms of it.
  3. Three planes: solve the \(3\times3\) system. Four outcomes — a unique point (the normals are independent); a common line (the planes form a "book"); no common point (two are parallel, or they form a triangular prism); or all three coincide.
Worked example 15 IB-style · 11 marks · line meets plane, and the angle

Question. The plane \(\Pi\) has equation \(2x-y+3z=11\). The line \(L\) has equation \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\4\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\). (a) Find the point where \(L\) meets \(\Pi\). (b) Find the angle between \(L\) and \(\Pi\).

  1. Write \(L\) in components: \(x=1+2\lambda\), \(y=\lambda\), \(z=4-\lambda\).
  2. Substitute into \(\Pi\): \(2(1+2\lambda) - (\lambda) + 3(4-\lambda) = 11\).
  3. Simplify: \(2+4\lambda-\lambda+12-3\lambda = 11 \Rightarrow 14 + 0\lambda = 11\).
  4. The \(\lambda\) terms cancel and the statement is false — so \(L\) is parallel to \(\Pi\) and never meets it. (Had it read \(14=14\), the line would lie in the plane.)
  5. Angle: \(\mathbf{b}=(2,1,-1)\), \(\mathbf{n}=(2,-1,3)\).
  6. \(\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{n} = 4-1-3 = 0\). So \(\mathbf{b}\perp\mathbf{n}\): the direction of the line is perpendicular to the normal, meaning the line runs along the plane. The angle is \(\theta = \arcsin\frac{0}{\sqrt6\sqrt{14}} = 0^\circ\), consistent with step 4.
Answer: no point of intersection — \(L\) is parallel to \(\Pi\); the angle is \(0^\circ\). Note how the dot product being zero explains the algebra: "coefficient of \(\lambda\) is zero" and "direction \(\perp\) normal" are the same fact wearing two different hats.
Worked example 16 IB-style · 12 marks · three planes

Question. Determine the nature of the intersection of \(\Pi_1: x+y+z=6\), \(\Pi_2: 2x-y+z=3\), \(\Pi_3: 3x+2z=9\).

  1. From \(\Pi_3\): \(x = \dfrac{9-2z}{3}\).
  2. Substitute into \(\Pi_1\): \(\dfrac{9-2z}{3}+y+z = 6 \Rightarrow y = 6 - z - \dfrac{9-2z}{3} = \dfrac{18-3z-9+2z}{3} = \dfrac{9-z}{3}\).
  3. Substitute both into \(\Pi_2\): \(2\cdot\dfrac{9-2z}{3} - \dfrac{9-z}{3} + z = 3\).
  4. Multiply by 3: \(18-4z-9+z+3z = 9 \Rightarrow 9 + 0z = 9\). Always true.
  5. So \(z\) is free: the three planes share a line. Put \(z=t\): \(x = \dfrac{9-2t}{3}\), \(y = \dfrac{9-t}{3}\), \(z=t\).
  6. Check \(t=0\): \((3,3,0)\) — \(\Pi_1\): 6 ✓, \(\Pi_2\): \(6-3+0=3\) ✓, \(\Pi_3\): 9 ✓.
Answer: the planes meet in the line \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\\0\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac23\\-\tfrac13\\1\end{pmatrix}\). The give-away is the row that reduces to \(0=0\) — one free variable means a line, two free variables means the planes coincide, and a row like \(0=5\) means no common point.
Common trap

(1) Using \(\cos\) instead of \(\sin\) for the line–plane angle. If your answer looks suspiciously large, you have found the angle with the normal. (2) Forgetting the modulus — angles are acute unless stated otherwise. (3) Reporting the value of \(\lambda\) as the answer; the question asks for the point, so you must substitute back. (4) Concluding "no intersection" when the \(\lambda\) terms cancel: check whether the remaining statement is true (line lies in the plane) or false (parallel, no intersection).

Practice

  1. Convert \(225^\circ\) to radians, and \(\dfrac{7\pi}{12}\) to degrees.

    Show answer

    \(225^\circ \times \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{5\pi}{4}\) rad. \(\frac{7\pi}{12}\times\frac{180}{\pi} = 105^\circ\).

    Answer: \(\frac{5\pi}{4}\) and \(105^\circ\). Multiply by \(\frac{\pi}{180}\) to go to radians, by \(\frac{180}{\pi}\) to go back.

  2. A sector has perimeter 30 cm and radius 8 cm. Find its angle in radians and its area.

    Show answer
    1. Perimeter \(= 2r + l = 16 + l = 30 \Rightarrow l = 14\) cm.
    2. \(\theta = \frac{l}{r} = \frac{14}{8} = 1.75\) rad.
    3. Area \(= \frac12 r^2\theta = \frac12(64)(1.75) = 56\) cm².
    Answer: 1.75 rad, 56 cm². Equivalently \(A=\frac12 r l = \frac12(8)(14)=56\) — a useful shortcut.

  3. Solve \(\sqrt3\tan x + 1 = 0\) for \(-\pi \le x \le \pi\).

    Show answer
    1. \(\tan x = -\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\). Principal value: \(-\frac{\pi}{6}\).
    2. Tangent has period \(\pi\), so add \(\pi\): \(-\frac{\pi}{6}+\pi = \frac{5\pi}{6}\).
    3. In \([-\pi,\pi]\): \(-\frac{\pi}{6}\) and \(\frac{5\pi}{6}\). (Adding \(\pi\) again exceeds \(\pi\).)
    Answer: \(x=-\dfrac{\pi}{6},\ \dfrac{5\pi}{6}\). Tangent's period is \(\pi\), not \(2\pi\) — one extra solution pair is the usual error here.

  4. In triangle \(ABC\), \(b=9\), \(c=6\), \(A=52^\circ\). Find \(a\) and the area of the triangle.

    Show answer
    1. Cosine rule: \(a^2 = 81+36-2(9)(6)\cos52^\circ = 117-108(0.6157) = 117-66.5 = 50.5\).
    2. \(a = 7.11\).
    3. Area \(=\frac12 bc\sin A = \frac12(9)(6)\sin52^\circ = 27(0.7880) = 21.3\).
    Answer: \(a=7.11\), area \(=21.3\). Two sides and the included angle (SAS) is cosine rule then area — never the sine rule.

  5. Prove that \(\dfrac{1-\cos 2x}{\sin 2x} = \tan x\), and hence find the exact value of \(\tan 22.5^\circ\).

    Show answer
    1. \(1-\cos2x = 1-(1-2\sin^2x)=2\sin^2x\); \(\sin2x = 2\sin x\cos x\).
    2. Ratio \(= \dfrac{2\sin^2x}{2\sin x\cos x} = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \tan x\). ■
    3. Put \(x = 22.5^\circ\), so \(2x = 45^\circ\): \(\tan 22.5^\circ = \dfrac{1-\cos45^\circ}{\sin45^\circ} = \dfrac{1-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{\frac{\sqrt2}{2}} = \dfrac{2-\sqrt2}{\sqrt2} = \sqrt2-1\).
    Answer: \(\sqrt2-1 \approx 0.414\). This "half-angle" trick (use a double-angle identity with \(2x\) equal to a nice angle) produces exact values for \(15^\circ\), \(22.5^\circ\), \(75^\circ\) and so on.

  6. Find a vector of magnitude 5 in the direction of \(\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\2\end{pmatrix}\).

    Show answer
    1. \(|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{1+4+4} = 3\).
    2. Unit vector: \(\frac13\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\2\end{pmatrix}\).
    3. Scale to magnitude 5: \(5\cdot\frac13\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}5/3\\-10/3\\10/3\end{pmatrix}\).
    Answer: \(\begin{pmatrix}5/3\\-10/3\\10/3\end{pmatrix}\). Always divide by the magnitude first, then scale — a unit vector is the bridge.

  7. Show that the lines \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\2\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\\0\end{pmatrix}+\mu\begin{pmatrix}2\\4\\-2\end{pmatrix}\) are parallel, and determine whether they are coincident.

    Show answer
    1. Direction vectors: \(\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix}2\\4\\-2\end{pmatrix} = 2\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix}\). Parallel. ✓
    2. Test whether \((3,4,0)\) lies on the first line: \(1+\lambda=3\Rightarrow\lambda=2\); then \(y=0+2(2)=4\) ✓; \(z=2+2(-1)=0\) ✓.
    3. All three agree, so the point is shared and the lines are coincident — the same line written twice.
    Answer: coincident. Parallel plus one shared point is the definition; if the \(z\)-check had failed, they would be distinct parallel lines.

  8. A particle has velocity \(\mathbf{v}(t) = \begin{pmatrix}2t\\3t^2-4\end{pmatrix}\) and initial position \(\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}\). Find its position vector, and its distance from the origin at \(t=2\).

    Show answer
    1. Integrate: \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \begin{pmatrix}t^2+c_1\\ t^3-4t+c_2\end{pmatrix}\); use \(\mathbf{r}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}\) to get \(c_1=1,\ c_2=2\).
    2. \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \begin{pmatrix}t^2+1\\ t^3-4t+2\end{pmatrix}\).
    3. At \(t=2\): \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}5\\2\end{pmatrix}\); distance \(=\sqrt{25+4}=\sqrt{29}=5.39\) m.
    Answer: \(\sqrt{29}\approx5.39\) m. Integration of a vector is component-wise — the reverse of the differentiation in Worked example 12. This is why Topic 5 and Topic 3 must be revised together.

  9. Let \(\mathbf{a}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\4\\2\end{pmatrix}\). (a) Find \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\). (b) Verify your answer is perpendicular to both. (c) Find the area of the triangle with vertices \(O(0,0,0)\), \(A(2,-1,3)\), \(B(-1,4,2)\).

    Show answer
    1. \(\bigl((-1)(2)-(3)(4),\ (3)(-1)-(2)(2),\ (2)(4)-(-1)(-1)\bigr) = (-2-12,\ -3-4,\ 8-1) = (-14,-7,7)\).
    2. Check: \((-14,-7,7)\cdot(2,-1,3) = -28+7+21 = 0\) ✓; \((-14,-7,7)\cdot(-1,4,2) = 14-28+14 = 0\) ✓.
    3. \(|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}| = \sqrt{196+49+49} = \sqrt{294}\).
    4. Area \(= \tfrac12\sqrt{294} = 8.57\).
    Answer: \((-14,-7,7)\); area \(=8.57\). Note that \((14,7,-7)\) is equally valid as a normal — the sign only records which way the right-hand rule points.

  10. Find the Cartesian equation of the plane through \(A(2,0,1)\), \(B(1,3,-1)\) and \(C(4,-2,2)\).

    Show answer
    1. \(\overrightarrow{AB}=(-1,3,-2)\), \(\overrightarrow{AC}=(2,-2,1)\).
    2. Normal \(= \bigl(3(1)-(-2)(-2),\ (-2)(2)-(-1)(1),\ (-1)(-2)-3(2)\bigr) = (-1,-3,-4)\).
    3. \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n} = (2,0,1)\cdot(-1,-3,-4) = -2+0-4 = -6\).
    4. Plane: \(-x-3y-4z=-6\), i.e. \(x+3y+4z=6\).
    5. Check \(B\): \(1+9-4=6\) ✓; check \(C\): \(4-6+8=6\) ✓.
    Answer: \(x+3y+4z=6\). Multiplying the whole equation by \(-1\) is the same plane — pick whichever makes the leading coefficient positive, then never change it mid-question.

  11. The plane \(\Pi\) has equation \(x-2y+z=5\). The line \(L\) has equation \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\2\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}\). (a) Find the coordinates of the point where \(L\) meets \(\Pi\). (b) Find the angle between \(L\) and \(\Pi\).

    Show answer
    1. Components: \(x=\lambda\), \(y=1-\lambda\), \(z=2+3\lambda\).
    2. Substitute: \(\lambda-2(1-\lambda)+(2+3\lambda)=5 \Rightarrow \lambda-2+2\lambda+2+3\lambda=5 \Rightarrow 6\lambda=5\), so \(\lambda=\tfrac56\).
    3. Point: \(\bigl(\tfrac56,\ 1-\tfrac56,\ 2+\tfrac{15}{6}\bigr) = \bigl(\tfrac56,\ \tfrac16,\ \tfrac92\bigr)\).
    4. Check: \(\tfrac56-\tfrac13+\tfrac92 = \tfrac{5-2+27}{6} = \tfrac{30}{6}=5\) ✓.
    5. Angle: \(\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{n} = (1,-1,3)\cdot(1,-2,1) = 1+2+3 = 6\); \(|\mathbf{b}|=\sqrt{11}\), \(|\mathbf{n}|=\sqrt6\).
    6. \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{6}{\sqrt{11}\sqrt6} = \dfrac{6}{\sqrt{66}} = 0.7385 \Rightarrow \theta = 47.6^\circ\).
    Answer: \(\bigl(\tfrac56,\tfrac16,\tfrac92\bigr)\); \(47.6^\circ\). If you used \(\cos\) here you would get \(42.4^\circ\) — the angle with the normal, and the single most common way to lose these marks.

  12. The planes \(\Pi_1: 2x+y-z=4\) and \(\Pi_2: x-y+2z=1\) intersect in a line. Find a vector equation of that line.

    Show answer
    1. Add the equations: \(3x+z=5\), so \(z=5-3x\).
    2. Put \(x=t\); then \(z=5-3t\).
    3. From \(\Pi_1\): \(2t+y-(5-3t)=4 \Rightarrow y = 9-5t\).
    4. So \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\9\\5\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}1\\-5\\-3\end{pmatrix}\).
    5. Check \(t=0\), the point \((0,9,5)\): \(\Pi_1\): \(0+9-5=4\) ✓; \(\Pi_2\): \(0-9+10=1\) ✓.
    Answer: \(\mathbf{r}=(0,9,5)+t(1,-5,-3)\). Infinitely many correct forms exist — any point on the line and any non-zero multiple of the direction will do. You can also get the direction as \(\mathbf{n}_1\times\mathbf{n}_2\), which is a good cross-check.
