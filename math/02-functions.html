Mathematics AA HL · Topic 2

Functions

A function is a machine: you feed it an input, it returns exactly one output. Everything in this topic — quadratics, exponentials, inverses, modulus, asymptotes — is a different machine, and the exam tests whether you can predict what a machine does from its equation, and write the equation of a machine from its graph. Topic 2 is also the language of Topic 5: you cannot differentiate what you cannot sketch.

32 teaching hours (HL) HL only: polynomials, oblique asymptotes, odd/even, inequalities, 1/f(x) Paper 1 · Paper 2 · Paper 3

2.1 What a function is: domain, range, and the vertical line test

SL / HL

Intuition: a function is a rule that never hesitates. For each allowed input there is exactly one output. \(y=\sqrt{x}\) is a function; \(y=\pm\sqrt{x}\) is not, because \(x=4\) would produce two answers. The domain is the set of legal inputs, the range is the set of outputs you actually get.

Formal statement: \(f:A\to B\) is a function if every \(a\in A\) is assigned exactly one \(b\in B\). We write \(f(x)\) for the output, and \(f:A\mapsto B\). A function is one-to-one (injective) if different inputs always give different outputs: \(f(a)=f(b)\Rightarrow a=b\). It is onto (surjective) if every element of \(B\) is hit.

How to use it — finding domains and ranges

Domain restrictions come from exactly four sources, and you check them in this order: (1) denominators cannot be zero; (2) square roots (and any even root) need a non-negative argument; (3) logarithms need a strictly positive argument; (4) an applied context may restrict further (time \(t\ge0\), a length \(>0\)). For the range, either read it from a sketch, or find the range of the "inner" function and push it through the outer one. To find the range algebraically, solve \(y=f(x)\) for \(x\) and see which \(y\) values admit a real solution.

Worked example 1 IB-style · 5 marks · No GDC

Question. Let \(f(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4-x}}\) and \(g(x)=\ln(x-1)\). State the largest possible domain of each, and the range of \(f\).

  1. For \(f\): need \(4-x>0\) (strictly, because it is in a denominator and under a root), so \(x<4\). Domain: \((-\infty,4)\).
  2. For \(g\): need \(x-1>0\), so \(x>1\). Domain: \((1,\infty)\).
  3. Range of \(f\): as \(x\to4^{-}\), \(\sqrt{4-x}\to0^{+}\), so \(f(x)\to\infty\); as \(x\to-\infty\), \(f(x)\to0^{+}\).
  4. \(f\) is continuous and increasing on its domain, so the range is \((0,\infty)\).
Answer: \(\text{dom}(f)=(-\infty,4)\), \(\text{range}(f)=(0,\infty)\); \(\text{dom}(g)=(1,\infty)\). The strict inequality \(4-x>0\) is the mark: \(4-x\ge0\) would allow division by zero at \(x=4\).

2.2 Linear and quadratic functions

SL / HL

Intuition: a quadratic \(ax^2+bx+c\) is a parabola. The sign of \(a\) decides whether it opens up or down; the vertex is its turning point; the discriminant \(\Delta=b^2-4ac\) counts how many times it crosses the \(x\)-axis. Everything you are ever asked — maximum height of a projectile, minimum cost, "for what values of \(k\) does this have two solutions?" — is one of those three facts.

Completing the square and vertex form

\[ax^2+bx+c = a\left[\left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \frac{b^2}{4a^2}\right]+c = a\left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \frac{\Delta}{4a}\] so the vertex is at \(\left(-\dfrac{b}{2a},\ -\dfrac{\Delta}{4a}\right)\) and the axis of symmetry is \(x=-\dfrac{b}{2a}\).
Proof The quadratic formula by completing the square
  1. Start: \(ax^2+bx+c=0\) with \(a\neq0\). Divide by \(a\): \(x^2+\dfrac{b}{a}x+\dfrac{c}{a}=0\).
  2. Move the constant: \(x^2+\dfrac{b}{a}x = -\dfrac{c}{a}\).
  3. Complete the square — add \(\left(\dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2\) to both sides: \(\left(x+\dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}-\dfrac{c}{a} = \dfrac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}\).
  4. Take square roots: \(x+\dfrac{b}{2a} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\).
  5. \(x = \dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\). ■

Meaning: the formula is not a separate fact to memorise — it is completing the square, done once and for all with letters. The axis of symmetry \(x=-\frac{b}{2a}\) falls out of step 3 before you even take the square root. Wrong tool when: the question says "find the turning point" — completing the square gives it directly, while the formula gives roots and requires an extra step.

The discriminant and sum/product of roots

\[\Delta = b^2-4ac:\quad \Delta>0 \Rightarrow \text{two distinct real roots};\quad \Delta=0 \Rightarrow \text{one (repeated)};\quad \Delta<0 \Rightarrow \text{no real roots (a complex conjugate pair)}.\] \[\alpha+\beta = -\frac{b}{a},\qquad \alpha\beta = \frac{c}{a}\quad\text{(Vieta's formulas)}\]
Proof Why \(\alpha+\beta=-\frac{b}{a}\) and \(\alpha\beta=\frac{c}{a}\)
  1. If the roots are \(\alpha,\beta\), then \(ax^2+bx+c = a(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)\).
  2. Expand the right side: \(a\bigl(x^2-(\alpha+\beta)x+\alpha\beta\bigr) = ax^2 - a(\alpha+\beta)x + a\alpha\beta\).
  3. Compare coefficients with \(ax^2+bx+c\): coefficient of \(x\) gives \(-a(\alpha+\beta)=b\), so \(\alpha+\beta=-\frac{b}{a}\).
  4. Constant term: \(a\alpha\beta = c\), so \(\alpha\beta = \frac{c}{a}\). ■

Meaning: the coefficients of a polynomial are symmetric functions of its roots — which is why you can answer questions about roots without ever finding them. This generalises to cubics in section 2.7. Wrong tool when: you actually need the roots themselves; then use the formula or factorise.

Worked example 2 IB-style · 7 marks · No GDC

Question. Let \(f(x)=2x^2-8x+5\). (a) Write \(f(x)\) in the form \(a(x-h)^2+k\). (b) Hence write down the coordinates of the vertex and the equation of the axis of symmetry. (c) State the range of \(f\). (d) Find the values of \(p\) for which \(f(x)=p\) has no real solutions.

  1. \(2x^2-8x+5 = 2(x^2-4x)+5 = 2\bigl[(x-2)^2-4\bigr]+5 = 2(x-2)^2-8+5 = 2(x-2)^2-3\).
  2. Vertex: \((2,-3)\); axis of symmetry: \(x=2\).
  3. Since \(a=2>0\) the parabola opens upward with minimum \(-3\), so range \(=[-3,\infty)\).
  4. \(f(x)=p\) has no real solutions when the horizontal line \(y=p\) misses the parabola, i.e. \(p<-3\).
Answer: (a) \(2(x-2)^2-3\); (b) \((2,-3)\), \(x=2\); (c) \([-3,\infty)\); (d) \(p<-3\). Every part follows from the vertex form — that is why "express in the form \(a(x-h)^2+k\)" is such a common opening instruction. The "hence" in (b) means use your answer to (a), so do not use \(-\frac{b}{2a}\) instead.
Worked example 3 IB-style · 7 marks · No GDC

Question. The equation \((k+1)x^2 + 4x + (k-2) = 0\) has two distinct real roots. Find the set of possible values of \(k\).

  1. Two distinct real roots \(\Rightarrow \Delta > 0\) (and the equation must genuinely be quadratic, so \(k\neq-1\)).
  2. \(\Delta = 16 - 4(k+1)(k-2) = 16 - 4(k^2-k-2) = 16-4k^2+4k+8 = 24+4k-4k^2\).
  3. \(-4k^2+4k+24 > 0 \Rightarrow k^2-k-6 < 0\) (divide by \(-4\), flip the sign).
  4. \((k-3)(k+2)<0 \Rightarrow -2<k<3\).
  5. Exclude \(k=-1\) (then the equation becomes \(4x-3=0\), with only one root).
Answer: \(-2<k<3,\ k\neq-1\). The exclusion is the mark most students miss: a "quadratic" with \(a=0\) is not quadratic. Sanity check: \(k=0\) gives \(x^2+4x-2=0\), \(\Delta=16+8=24>0\). ✓
Common trap

(1) Forgetting to flip the inequality when dividing by a negative — step 3 above. (2) Writing \(x=-\frac{b}{2a}\) when asked for the vertex: that is only the \(x\)-coordinate; you must substitute back. (3) \(\Delta=0\) means "one repeated root", not "no roots" — the IB marks this distinction. (4) Assuming \(a>0\): always check the sign of \(a\) before deciding whether the vertex is a maximum or a minimum.

2.3 Rational functions and asymptotes

SL / HL

Intuition: a rational function is one polynomial divided by another: \(f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\). Where \(Q(x)=0\) the function blows up — a vertical asymptote (unless the factor cancels, which gives a hole). Far out to the right, the highest powers dominate, so the function settles towards a horizontal asymptote (or, if the numerator's degree is one higher, an oblique straight line).

Formal statement: for \(f(x)=\dfrac{ax+b}{cx+d}\):

  • vertical asymptote: \(x=-\dfrac{d}{c}\);
  • horizontal asymptote: \(y=\dfrac{a}{c}\);
  • \(y\)-intercept \(=\dfrac{b}{d}\), \(x\)-intercept \(=-\dfrac{b}{a}\).
Proof Why the horizontal asymptote is the ratio of leading coefficients
  1. Take \(f(x)=\dfrac{ax+b}{cx+d}\) and divide top and bottom by \(x\): \(f(x) = \dfrac{a+\frac{b}{x}}{c+\frac{d}{x}}\).
  2. As \(x\to\pm\infty\), \(\frac{b}{x}\to0\) and \(\frac{d}{x}\to0\), so \(f(x)\to\dfrac{a}{c}\).
  3. Same argument for any rational function: divide by the highest power of \(x\) in the denominator. If \(\deg P = \deg Q\), the limit is the ratio of leading coefficients; if \(\deg P < \deg Q\), the limit is 0 (so \(y=0\)); if \(\deg P > \deg Q\), there is no horizontal asymptote.

Meaning: asymptotes are limits, which is why this topic sits on the border of Topic 5 — the IB loves asking for \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)\) in Paper 1. Wrong tool when: the factor causing the zero also appears in the numerator: \(\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=x+1\) for \(x\neq1\) has a hole at \(x=1\), not an asymptote.

HL: oblique (slant) asymptotes

HL only

When \(\deg P = \deg Q + 1\), divide: \(P(x) = Q(x)(mx+c) + R(x)\) with \(\deg R < \deg Q\). Then \(f(x) = mx+c+\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)}\), and since the fraction tends to 0, the oblique asymptote is \(y=mx+c\).

Worked example 4 IB-style · 9 marks · GDC allowed

Question. Let \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^2+3x+1}{x-1}\). (a) Find the equations of the two asymptotes. (b) Find the coordinates of the turning points. (c) Sketch the graph, labelling intercepts and asymptotes.

  1. Vertical asymptote: denominator zero at \(x=1\).
  2. Divide: \(x^2+3x+1 \div (x-1)\). \(x^2 \div x = x\); \(x(x-1)=x^2-x\); subtract: \(4x+1\); \(4x\div x = 4\); \(4(x-1)=4x-4\); subtract: \(5\). So \(f(x) = x+4+\dfrac{5}{x-1}\).
  3. Oblique asymptote: \(y=x+4\).
  4. Intercepts: \(f(0)=\frac{1}{-1}=-1\), so \((0,-1)\). \(x\)-intercepts: \(x^2+3x+1=0\Rightarrow x=\frac{-3\pm\sqrt5}{2}\), i.e. \(-2.618\) and \(-0.382\).
  5. Turning points: \(f'(x) = 1 - \dfrac{5}{(x-1)^2} = 0 \Rightarrow (x-1)^2=5 \Rightarrow x = 1\pm\sqrt5\), i.e. \(x=-1.236\) and \(x=3.236\).
  6. \(f(-1.236) = -1.236+4+\frac{5}{-2.236} = 2.764-2.236 = 0.528\) (local max on the left branch); \(f(3.236) = 7.236+\frac{5}{2.236}=7.236+2.236 = 9.472\) (local min on the right branch).
Answer: asymptotes \(x=1\) and \(y=x+4\); turning points \((-1.24,\,0.53)\) and \((3.24,\,9.47)\). Every mark here is method: the division for the oblique asymptote, the derivative for the turning points, and the labelled sketch. Sanity check: \(\frac{5}{x-1}>0\) on the right branch, so the curve sits above the asymptote there — and 9.47 > 7.24. ✓
Worked example 5 IB-style · 6 marks · GDC allowed

Question. Find the asymptotes of \(g(x)=\dfrac{2x^2-5x+3}{x^2-4}\) and hence describe its behaviour as \(x\to\pm\infty\).

  1. Denominator: \(x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2)\), so vertical asymptotes at \(x=2\) and \(x=-2\).
  2. Numerator at those points: \(2(4)-10+3=1\neq0\) (at \(x=2\)), so no cancellation — genuine asymptotes.
  3. Degrees equal, so horizontal asymptote \(y=\frac{2}{1}=2\).
  4. Confirm by division: \(2x^2-5x+3 = 2(x^2-4) + (-5x+11)\), so \(g(x) = 2 + \dfrac{-5x+11}{x^2-4}\to2\).
Answer: \(x=\pm2\) and \(y=2\). As \(x\to\infty\), \(g(x)\to 2^{-}\) (the remainder is negative for large \(x\)); as \(x\to-\infty\), \(g(x)\to2^{+}\). Stating whether the approach is from above or below is what distinguishes a good sketch from a mediocre one.

2.4 Exponential and logarithmic graphs

SL / HL

Intuition: \(y=a^x\) grows by equal factors in equal time; \(y=\ln x\) grows ever more slowly. They are mirror images of each other in the line \(y=x\). Every exponential curve passes through \((0,1)\); every log curve passes through \((1,0)\). Adding \(k\) shifts the horizontal asymptote of an exponential to \(y=k\), which is the single most useful modelling feature in the course.

\(f(x)=a^x\ (a>1)\)\(f(x)=\log_a x\ (a>1)\)
Domain\(\mathbb{R}\)\((0,\infty)\)
Range\((0,\infty)\)\(\mathbb{R}\)
Key point\((0,1)\)\((1,0)\)
Asymptote\(y=0\) (horizontal)\(x=0\) (vertical)
Inverse\(\log_a x\)\(a^x\)
Worked example 6 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. The temperature of a cup of coffee is modelled by \(T(t) = 20 + 70\mathrm{e}^{-0.08t}\), where \(T\) is in °C and \(t\) in minutes. (a) State the room temperature and the initial temperature. (b) Find the time when the coffee reaches 60°C. (c) Explain why the model predicts the coffee never reaches 20°C.

  1. As \(t\to\infty\), \(\mathrm{e}^{-0.08t}\to0\), so \(T\to20\): room temperature is 20°C.
  2. \(T(0)=20+70=90\)°C.
  3. \(60 = 20+70\mathrm{e}^{-0.08t} \Rightarrow \mathrm{e}^{-0.08t} = \frac{40}{70} = \frac47\).
  4. \(-0.08t = \ln\frac47 = -0.5596 \Rightarrow t = 6.99\) minutes.
  5. \(\mathrm{e}^{-0.08t}>0\) for all finite \(t\), so \(T(t) > 20\) always; 20 is a horizontal asymptote, approached but never attained.
Answer: 20°C and 90°C; \(t=7.0\) min; \(y=20\) is an asymptote because \(\mathrm{e}^{-0.08t}\) is strictly positive. Part (c) wants the word asymptote and the reason — "it gets closer and closer" alone scores nothing.
Worked example 7 IB-style · 8 marks · No GDC

Question. The curve \(y = \ln(x-2)+1\) meets its inverse at a point on the line \(y=x\). Find that point.

  1. Inverse: swap \(x\) and \(y\): \(x = \ln(y-2)+1 \Rightarrow \ln(y-2) = x-1 \Rightarrow y = \mathrm{e}^{x-1}+2\). So \(f^{-1}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{x-1}+2\).
  2. A function and its inverse are reflections in \(y=x\), so any intersection on \(y=x\) satisfies \(f(x)=x\). (Intersections off the line are possible but this question specifies on it.)
  3. Solve \(\ln(x-2)+1 = x\), i.e. \(\ln(x-2) = x-1\), or \(x-2 = \mathrm{e}^{x-1}\).
  4. This cannot be solved algebraically. With a GDC: solve \(h(x)=\ln(x-2)+1-x=0\). Try \(x=2.5\): \(\ln0.5+1-2.5 = -2.19\); \(x=3\): \(0+1-3=-2\); \(x=2.2\): \(\ln0.2+1-2.2=-2.81\). The function stays negative, so there is no solution on \(y=x\).
  5. Check the derivative: \(h'(x)=\frac{1}{x-2}-1\), zero at \(x=3\), where \(h=-2\) — a maximum, and it is still negative. So the curve never meets the line \(y=x\).
Answer: no such point exists. This example is deliberately uncomfortable: the standard exam shortcut (\(f(x)=f^{-1}(x)\Rightarrow f(x)=x\)) is only valid when you already know an intersection exists. Verify with a sketch or a sign check before using it.

2.5 Sinusoidal graphs and transformations

SL / HL

The general sinusoid is \(f(x)=a\sin(b(x-c))+d\): \(|a|\) = amplitude, period \(=\frac{2\pi}{|b|}\), \(c\) = horizontal translation (phase shift), \(d\) = the centreline (vertical translation). Topic 3 treats the trigonometry; the point here is that reading these four numbers off an equation is a transformation skill.

Why order matters

Transformations are replacements, and replacements do not commute. Compare \(y=f(2x+6)\) with \(y=f(2(x+3))\): they are the same thing. Now compare \(y=f(2x)+6\) with \(y=f(2x+6)\): the first stretches horizontally by \(\frac12\) then shifts up 6; the second stretches horizontally by \(\frac12\) then shifts left 3. The rule that never fails: factorise the argument first (\(f(bx+c)=f(b(x+\frac{c}{b}))\)), then apply transformations in the order horizontal translation → horizontal stretch → reflection → vertical stretch → vertical translation.

How to use it
\(y=f(x)+d\)translate \(\begin{pmatrix}0\\d\end{pmatrix}\)
\(y=f(x-c)\)translate \(\begin{pmatrix}c\\0\end{pmatrix}\) — note the minus
\(y=pf(x)\)vertical stretch, scale factor \(p\)
\(y=f(qx)\)horizontal stretch, scale factor \(\frac1q\)
\(y=-f(x)\)reflection in the \(x\)-axis
\(y=f(-x)\)reflection in the \(y\)-axis

To find the image of a point \((x_0,y_0)\) under \(y=af(b(x-c))+d\): the new point is \(\left(\frac{x_0}{b}+c,\ ay_0+d\right)\). Learn this formula and every transformation question becomes substitution.

Worked example 8 IB-style · 6 marks · No GDC

Question. The graph of \(y=x^2\) is transformed to \(y=3(x-2)^2+5\). Describe fully the transformation, and state the image of the point \((1,1)\).

  1. Write in the form \(a\,f(b(x-c))+d\): \(y = 3f(x-2)+5\) with \(f(x)=x^2\), so \(a=3,\ b=1,\ c=2,\ d=5\).
  2. Description: translation by \(\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\end{pmatrix}\), then a vertical stretch of scale factor 3, then a translation by \(\begin{pmatrix}0\\5\end{pmatrix}\).
  3. Image of \((1,1)\): \(\left(\frac11+2,\ 3(1)+5\right) = (3,8)\).
  4. Check: on the new curve, \(x=3\) gives \(3(3-2)^2+5 = 8\). ✓
Answer: as above; image \((3,8)\). Marks: one per transformation described correctly, one for the image. Saying "stretch then translate" in the wrong order loses the description marks — do the horizontal work first.
Worked example 9 IB-style · 7 marks · No GDC

Question. A sinusoid has maximum value 7 at \(x=1\) and minimum value \(-1\) at \(x=5\). Find a formula of the form \(f(x)=a\sin(b(x-c))+d\).

  1. Centreline: \(d = \frac{7+(-1)}{2} = 3\). Amplitude: \(|a| = \frac{7-(-1)}{2}=4\).
  2. Half a period separates a maximum from the next minimum: \(\frac{T}{2} = 5-1 = 4\), so \(T=8\) and \(b = \frac{2\pi}{8}=\frac{\pi}{4}\).
  3. For a sine curve, the maximum occurs a quarter-period after the point where it crosses the centreline going up, i.e. at \(c+\frac{T}{4}\). So \(1 = c + 2 \Rightarrow c = -1\).
  4. Formula: \(f(x) = 4\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}(x+1)\right)+3\).
  5. Check at \(x=5\): \(\frac{\pi}{4}(6) = \frac{3\pi}{2}\), \(\sin\frac{3\pi}{2}=-1\), giving \(-4+3=-1\). ✓
Answer: \(f(x)=4\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}(x+1)\right)+3\). Equivalent answers exist (e.g. a cosine with a different shift) — the IB accepts any correct form. Sanity-check your formula at both given points; that is a free mark.

2.6 Composite and inverse functions

SL / HL

Intuition: a composite \(g\circ f\) means "do \(f\), then do \(g\)" — and the order is written right-to-left, like Russian dolls. An inverse \(f^{-1}\) means "undo \(f\)". The catch: only one-to-one functions can be undone. If both \(x=2\) and \(x=-2\) map to 4, then \(f^{-1}(4)\) cannot decide which to return.

Formal statement: \((g\circ f)(x) = g(f(x))\). The inverse satisfies \(f(f^{-1}(x))=f^{-1}(f(x))=x\), exists only when \(f\) is one-to-one, and \(\text{dom}(f^{-1}) = \text{range}(f)\), \(\text{range}(f^{-1}) = \text{dom}(f)\).

Proof Why the graph of \(f^{-1}\) is the reflection of \(f\) in \(y=x\)
  1. By definition, \(y=f(x) \iff x = f^{-1}(y)\).
  2. So the point \((a,b)\) lies on \(y=f(x)\) exactly when \((b,a)\) lies on \(y=f^{-1}(x)\).
  3. The map \((a,b)\mapsto(b,a)\) is precisely reflection in the line \(y=x\) (the line \(y=x\) is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining \((a,b)\) to \((b,a)\)).
  4. Hence the two graphs are mirror images in \(y=x\). ■

Meaning: this gives you a two-step procedure for finding an inverse: swap \(x\) and \(y\), then make \(y\) the subject. It also explains why \(f\) and \(f^{-1}\) meet on \(y=x\) if they meet at all. Wrong tool when: \(f\) is not one-to-one on its natural domain — you must first restrict the domain (e.g. \(x\ge0\) for \(x^2\)), which is exactly what the IB asks when it introduces \(\arcsin\) in Topic 3.

Worked example 10 IB-style · 7 marks · No GDC

Question. Let \(f(x)=\dfrac{2x+1}{x-3}\), \(x\neq3\). (a) Find \(f^{-1}(x)\) and state its domain and range. (b) Show that \(f(f^{-1}(x))=x\).

  1. Let \(y=\dfrac{2x+1}{x-3}\). Swap: \(x = \dfrac{2y+1}{y-3}\).
  2. \(x(y-3) = 2y+1 \Rightarrow xy-3x = 2y+1 \Rightarrow y(x-2) = 3x+1 \Rightarrow y = \dfrac{3x+1}{x-2}\).
  3. So \(f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{3x+1}{x-2}\), domain \(x\neq2\), range \(y\neq3\).
  4. Verify: \(f(f^{-1}(x)) = \dfrac{2\left(\frac{3x+1}{x-2}\right)+1}{\left(\frac{3x+1}{x-2}\right)-3} = \dfrac{\frac{6x+2+x-2}{x-2}}{\frac{3x+1-3x+6}{x-2}} = \dfrac{7x}{7} = x\). ■
Answer: \(f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{3x+1}{x-2}\). Note the swap: the forbidden input of \(f\) is 3 and its forbidden output is 2; for \(f^{-1}\) they exchange. Spotting that this function is self-inverse-like in structure (a Möbius map) explains why the algebra collapses so neatly.
Worked example 11 IB-style · 6 marks · No GDC

Question. Let \(f(x)=x^2-4x+5\) for \(x\ge k\). Find the smallest value of \(k\) for which \(f^{-1}\) exists, and find \(f^{-1}\) in that case.

  1. \(f(x)=(x-2)^2+1\): a parabola with vertex \((2,1)\). It is not one-to-one on \(\mathbb{R}\).
  2. Restricting to one side of the axis of symmetry \(x=2\) makes it one-to-one. Smallest such \(k\) with the standard convention \(x\ge k\) is \(k=2\).
  3. Then \(y=(x-2)^2+1 \Rightarrow (x-2)^2 = y-1 \Rightarrow x = 2+\sqrt{y-1}\) (positive root because \(x\ge2\)).
  4. So \(f^{-1}(x) = 2+\sqrt{x-1}\), domain \(x\ge1\), range \(y\ge2\).
Answer: \(k=2\); \(f^{-1}(x)=2+\sqrt{x-1}\). Choosing the positive root is the whole point of the restriction — write a sentence justifying it, because that justification is a mark.
Common trap

(1) Confusing \(f^{-1}(x)\) with \(\frac{1}{f(x)}\) — the inverse is not the reciprocal (except by coincidence for some functions). The reciprocal is written \((f(x))^{-1}\). (2) Forgetting to swap domain and range. (3) Assuming \((g\circ f)^{-1} = g^{-1}\circ f^{-1}\); the correct rule is \((g\circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}\) — reverse the order, like taking off shoes before socks. (4) Believing \(f(x)=f^{-1}(x)\) always implies \(f(x)=x\); see Worked example 7.

2.7 HL: polynomial functions

HL only

The remainder theorem: when \(P(x)\) is divided by \((x-a)\), the remainder is \(P(a)\). The factor theorem: \((x-a)\) is a factor of \(P(x)\) if and only if \(P(a)=0\).

Proof The remainder theorem
  1. Division with a linear divisor gives \(P(x) = (x-a)Q(x) + R\), where the remainder \(R\) has degree less than 1 — so \(R\) is a constant.
  2. Substitute \(x=a\): \(P(a) = (a-a)Q(a) + R = 0 + R = R\). ■
  3. Factor theorem: if \(P(a)=0\) then \(R=0\), so \(P(x)=(x-a)Q(x)\) and \((x-a)\) is a factor. Conversely if \((x-a)\) is a factor then \(P(a) = (a-a)Q(a) = 0\). ■

Meaning: evaluating a polynomial at one number tells you whether a linear expression divides it exactly — no long division needed. Wrong tool when: you are dividing by a quadratic; then the remainder has the form \(ax+b\) and you must compare coefficients.

Vieta's formulas for a cubic \(ax^3+bx^2+cx+d\) with roots \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\):

\[\alpha+\beta+\gamma = -\frac{b}{a},\qquad \alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha = \frac{c}{a},\qquad \alpha\beta\gamma = -\frac{d}{a}.\] For a quartic \(ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e\): sum of roots \(=-\frac{b}{a}\), sum of products in pairs \(=\frac{c}{a}\), sum of products in threes \(=-\frac{d}{a}\), product \(=\frac{e}{a}\). The signs alternate.
Worked example 12 IB-style · 9 marks · No GDC

Question. \(P(x)=2x^3-5x^2+kx-12\). When \(P(x)\) is divided by \((x-2)\) the remainder is 6. (a) Find \(k\). (b) Hence factorise \(P(x)\) completely. (c) State the sum and product of the roots.

  1. Remainder theorem: \(P(2)=6\). \(P(2)=2(8)-5(4)+2k-12 = 16-20+2k-12 = 2k-16\).
  2. \(2k-16=6 \Rightarrow k=11\).
  3. \(P(x)=2x^3-5x^2+11x-12\). Try \(x=1\): \(2-5+11-12=-4\); \(x=\frac32\): \(6.75-11.25+16.5-12=0\). So \((2x-3)\) is a factor.
  4. Divide: \(2x^3-5x^2+11x-12 = (2x-3)(x^2-x+4)\). Check the quadratic's discriminant: \(1-16=-15<0\), so it does not factorise further over the reals.
  5. Sum of roots \(=-\frac{b}{a}=\frac52\); product \(=-\frac{d}{a}=\frac{12}{2}=6\).
  6. Check: the real root is \(\frac32\), and the complex pair from \(x^2-x+4\) has sum 1 and product 4. Total: \(\frac32+1=\frac52\) ✓; \(\frac32\times4 = 6\) ✓.
Answer: \(k=11\); \(P(x)=(2x-3)(x^2-x+4)\); sum \(\frac52\), product 6. The check in step 6 uses Vieta to confirm the factorisation — do it, it catches arithmetic slips instantly.
Worked example 13 IB-style · 8 marks · No GDC

Question. The equation \(x^3-3x^2-4x+12=0\) has roots \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\). (a) Find \(\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2\). (b) Find a cubic equation with roots \(\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{1}{\beta},\frac{1}{\gamma}\).

  1. Vieta: \(\alpha+\beta+\gamma=3\), \(\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha=-4\), \(\alpha\beta\gamma=-12\).
  2. Use \((\alpha+\beta+\gamma)^2 = \alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2 + 2(\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha)\).
  3. \(9 = \alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2 + 2(-4) \Rightarrow \alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2 = 17\).
  4. New roots: sum \(=\dfrac{\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha+\alpha\beta}{\alpha\beta\gamma} = \dfrac{-4}{-12}=\dfrac13\).
  5. Sum of pairwise products \(=\dfrac{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}{\alpha\beta\gamma} = \dfrac{3}{-12} = -\dfrac14\); product \(=\dfrac{1}{\alpha\beta\gamma}=-\dfrac{1}{12}\).
  6. Cubic: \(x^3 - \frac13 x^2 - \frac14 x + \frac{1}{12} = 0\), or \(12x^3-4x^2-3x+1=0\).
Answer: (a) 17; (b) \(12x^3-4x^2-3x+1=0\). The identity in step 2 (square of a sum) is the standard bridge between "sum of roots" and "sum of squares"; memorise it. Sanity check on (b): the original factors as \((x-2)(x+2)(x-3)\), roots \(2,-2,3\); reciprocals \(\frac12,-\frac12,\frac13\) sum to \(\frac13\) ✓.

2.8 Modulus, reciprocal graphs and inequalities

HL only

Intuition: \(|x|\) means "distance from zero", so it is never negative. The graph of \(y=|f(x)|\) takes everything below the \(x\)-axis and flips it up; the graph of \(y=f(|x|)\) throws away the left half and mirrors the right half across the \(y\)-axis. The graph of \(y=\frac{1}{f(x)}\) turns zeros into vertical asymptotes and sends large values to zero.

How to use it — the three derived graphs
  1. \(y=|f(x)|\): sketch \(f\); reflect the parts below the axis in the \(x\)-axis; delete the original below-axis pieces. \(x\)-intercepts are unchanged; they become sharp corners (cusps).
  2. \(y=f(|x|)\): keep the part of \(f\) with \(x\ge0\); reflect it in the \(y\)-axis. The result is even: symmetric about the \(y\)-axis.
  3. \(y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}\): where \(f=0\), draw a vertical asymptote. Where \(f\) has a local max, \(\frac1f\) has a local min (and vice versa, for positive \(f\)). Where \(f>1\), \(\frac1f\in(0,1)\); where \(0<f<1\), \(\frac1f>1\). Points where \(f=\pm1\) are fixed.
Worked example 14 IB-style · 7 marks · No GDC

Question. Solve \(|2x-1| = |x+3|\).

  1. Method 1 — square both sides (valid because both sides are non-negative): \((2x-1)^2 = (x+3)^2\).
  2. \(4x^2-4x+1 = x^2+6x+9 \Rightarrow 3x^2-10x-8 = 0\).
  3. \((3x+2)(x-4)=0 \Rightarrow x=-\frac23\) or \(x=4\).
  4. Method 2 — cases, split at \(x=\frac12\) and \(x=-3\): for \(x\ge\frac12\): \(2x-1=x+3\Rightarrow x=4\) ✓; for \(-3\le x<\frac12\): \(1-2x=x+3\Rightarrow x=-\frac23\) ✓; for \(x<-3\): \(1-2x=-(x+3)\Rightarrow x=4\), rejected (not in this region).
Answer: \(x=-\frac23\) or \(x=4\). Check: \(x=4\): \(|7|=|7|\) ✓; \(x=-\frac23\): \(|-\frac73| = |\frac73|\) ✓. Squaring is faster and safe here; the case method is required when the equation mixes a modulus with a non-modulus term (e.g. \(|x-1| = 2x\)).
Worked example 15 IB-style · 8 marks · GDC allowed

Question. Solve \(\dfrac{x-1}{x+2} \ge 0\), and hence solve \(\dfrac{x-1}{x+2} \ge \dfrac{1}{2}\).

  1. Critical values: numerator zero at \(x=1\); denominator zero at \(x=-2\) (excluded from the domain).
  2. Sign chart on the three intervals: \(x<-2\): e.g. \(x=-3\) gives \(\frac{-4}{-1}=4>0\) ✓; \(-2<x<1\): \(x=0\) gives \(-\frac12<0\) ✗; \(x>1\): \(x=2\) gives \(\frac14>0\) ✓.
  3. Solution: \(x<-2\) or \(x\ge1\). (Include \(x=1\) because the inequality is "≥"; never include \(x=-2\).)
  4. For the second: \(\dfrac{x-1}{x+2}-\dfrac12 \ge 0 \Rightarrow \dfrac{2x-2-x-2}{2(x+2)}\ge0 \Rightarrow \dfrac{x-4}{x+2}\ge0\).
  5. Same method: critical values \(x=4\) (include) and \(x=-2\) (exclude). Solution: \(x<-2\) or \(x\ge4\).
Answer: \(x<-2\) or \(x\ge1\); then \(x<-2\) or \(x\ge4\). The single most common error is multiplying through by \((x+2)\) without knowing its sign — that flips the inequality for half the domain. Always bring everything to one side and use a sign chart.

HL: odd and even functions

HL only

\(f\) is even if \(f(-x)=f(x)\) for all \(x\) (symmetry in the \(y\)-axis: \(x^2,\ \cos x,\ |x|\)). \(f\) is odd if \(f(-x)=-f(x)\) (rotational symmetry of order 2 about the origin: \(x^3,\ \sin x,\ \frac1x\)). Most functions are neither: to prove "neither", give one value of \(x\) where both conditions fail.

Worked example 16 IB-style · 5 marks · No GDC

Question. Determine whether \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^3}{x^2+1}\) and \(g(x)=x^3+x^2\) are odd, even or neither.

  1. \(f(-x) = \dfrac{(-x)^3}{(-x)^2+1} = \dfrac{-x^3}{x^2+1} = -f(x)\), so \(f\) is odd.
  2. \(g(-x) = -x^3+x^2\). Compare: \(g(x)=x^3+x^2\) (not equal) and \(-g(x)=-x^3-x^2\) (not equal, the \(x^2\) term differs in sign). At \(x=1\): \(g(-1)=0\) while \(g(1)=2\) and \(-g(1)=-2\).
  3. So \(g\) is neither.
Answer: \(f\) odd, \(g\) neither. One counterexample suffices for "neither" — you do not need to test every \(x\). In questions about definite integrals on \([-a,a]\), an odd function integrates to zero; that shortcut returns in Topic 5.

Check yourself

Practice

  1. Express \(3x^2+12x+7\) in the form \(a(x+h)^2+k\), and state the minimum value and where it occurs.

    Show answer
    1. \(3(x^2+4x)+7 = 3[(x+2)^2-4]+7 = 3(x+2)^2-12+7 = 3(x+2)^2-5\).
    2. Minimum value \(-5\) at \(x=-2\).
    Answer: \(3(x+2)^2-5\); minimum \(-5\) at \(x=-2\). Check: \(3(-2)^2+12(-2)+7 = 12-24+7=-5\). ✓

  2. Find \(k\) if the quadratic \(kx^2-6x+9=0\) has exactly one real root.

    Show answer
    1. Case 1: \(k=0\) gives \(-6x+9=0\), a linear equation with exactly one root. So \(k=0\) works.
    2. Case 2: \(k\neq0\), require \(\Delta=0\): \(36-4(k)(9)=0 \Rightarrow 36-36k=0 \Rightarrow k=1\).
    Answer: \(k=0\) or \(k=1\). Missing the \(k=0\) linear case is the classic omission — the word "quadratic" was not used, so a linear equation is allowed.

  3. Find the asymptotes and intercepts of \(y=\dfrac{3x-1}{x+2}\), and hence state its range.

    Show answer
    1. Vertical: \(x=-2\). Horizontal: \(y=3\).
    2. \(x\)-intercept: \(3x-1=0\Rightarrow x=\frac13\). \(y\)-intercept: \(y=\frac{-1}{2}=-\frac12\).
    3. Range: \(y\neq3\) (the horizontal asymptote is never reached: \(\frac{3x-1}{x+2}=3\) would require \(-1=6\)).
    Answer: \(x=-2\), \(y=3\); intercepts \((\frac13,0)\) and \((0,-\frac12)\); range \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{3\}\).

  4. Describe the transformation mapping \(y=f(x)\) to \(y=-2f(3x-6)+1\), and give the image of \((4,5)\).

    Show answer
    1. Factorise the argument: \(3x-6 = 3(x-2)\). So \(a=-2,\ b=3,\ c=2,\ d=1\).
    2. Order: translate right 2; horizontal stretch scale \(\frac13\); vertical stretch scale 2 with reflection in the \(x\)-axis; translate up 1.
    3. Image: \(\left(\frac43+2,\ -2(5)+1\right) = \left(\frac{10}{3},\ -9\right)\).
    Answer: as above; image \((\frac{10}{3},-9)\). Note \(3x-6\) must be rewritten as \(3(x-2)\) before the shift is seen to be 2, not 6.

  5. Let \(f(x)=\dfrac{x}{x+1}\). Find \(f^{-1}(x)\), state its domain, and verify that \(f^{-1}(f(x))=x\).

    Show answer
    1. \(y=\dfrac{x}{x+1}\); swap: \(x=\dfrac{y}{y+1} \Rightarrow xy+x=y \Rightarrow y(1-x)=x \Rightarrow y=\dfrac{x}{1-x}\).
    2. \(f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{x}{1-x}\), domain \(x\neq1\).
    3. \(f^{-1}(f(x)) = \dfrac{\frac{x}{x+1}}{1-\frac{x}{x+1}} = \dfrac{\frac{x}{x+1}}{\frac{1}{x+1}} = x\). ✓
    Answer: \(f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{x}{1-x}\). This function is self-inverse in structure: the same algebra appears twice.

  6. Given that \((x+2)\) is a factor of \(x^3+ax^2-7x+b\), and that the remainder on division by \((x-1)\) is \(-6\), find \(a\) and \(b\).

    Show answer
    1. \(P(-2)=0\): \(-8+4a+14+b=0 \Rightarrow 4a+b=-6\).
    2. \(P(1)=-6\): \(1+a-7+b=-6 \Rightarrow a+b=0\).
    3. Subtract: \(3a = -6 \Rightarrow a=-2\), then \(b=2\).
    Answer: \(a=-2,\ b=2\). Two conditions, two equations — the factor theorem and the remainder theorem used together is the standard pairing.

  7. Solve \(\dfrac{2}{x-1} \le 1\).

    Show answer
    1. \(\dfrac{2}{x-1}-1 \le 0 \Rightarrow \dfrac{2-(x-1)}{x-1}\le0 \Rightarrow \dfrac{3-x}{x-1}\le0\).
    2. Critical values: \(x=3\) (include), \(x=1\) (exclude).
    3. Sign chart: \(x<1\): \(\frac{+}{-}<0\) ✓; \(1<x<3\): \(\frac{+}{+}>0\) ✗; \(x>3\): \(\frac{-}{+}<0\) ✓.
    Answer: \(x<1\) or \(x\ge3\). Test \(x=0\): \(\frac{2}{-1}=-2\le1\) ✓. Test \(x=2\): \(\frac21=2\le1\) ✗. Consistent.

  8. Sketch \(y=|x^2-4|\) and state the range. Where is the function not differentiable?

    Show answer
    1. \(x^2-4\) is a parabola with zeros at \(x=\pm2\) and vertex \((0,-4)\).
    2. Reflect the part below the axis: for \(|x|<2\), \(y=4-x^2\); for \(|x|\ge2\), \(y=x^2-4\).
    3. Range: \([0,\infty)\).
    4. Not differentiable at \(x=\pm2\) — the sharp corners (cusps) where the reflected piece meets the original.
    Answer: range \([0,\infty)\); corners at \(x=\pm2\). The "corners" answer is pure Topic 5: differentiability fails where the left and right gradients disagree.
