Statistics & probability

Topic 4 is the topic where AA HL students lose the most marks per minute, because it feels "easy" right up to the point where it isn't. The descriptive half (mean, spread, correlation) is quick. The inference half (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, χ²) is where a mark depends on one sentence you either write or don't. The fix is procedural: learn one hypothesis-testing routine and run it identically for every variant.

4.1 Data: types, diagrams and summaries

SL / HL

Intuition: statistics begins by describing what you have and ends by guessing what you do not have. The first half of this section is description; the whole of the second half of the topic is guessing — with an honest measure of how wrong you might be.

ConceptMeaningWatch out for
PopulationEvery individual you care aboutOften too large to measure
SampleThe subset you actually measureMust be representative; a biased sample ruins everything downstream
Discrete dataCountable values (number of siblings)Bar charts, gaps between bars
Continuous dataMeasured on a scale (height, time)Histograms, no gaps, frequency density not frequency
Grouped dataBinned into intervalsUse mid-interval values; results are estimates
How to use it — histograms and grouped estimates

In a histogram with unequal class widths, the area of each bar represents frequency, so the vertical axis is frequency density \(=\dfrac{\text{frequency}}{\text{class width}}\). To estimate the mean from grouped data use \(\bar x = \dfrac{\sum f x}{\sum f}\) where \(x\) is the mid-interval value. To estimate the standard deviation use \(s_n = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sum f x^2}{\sum f} - \bar x^2}\). Your GDC's statistics mode does this in seconds — but you must enter mid-interval values into one list and frequencies into another, and you must know which standard deviation it is reporting.

Common trap

\(s_n\) versus \(s_{n-1}\). Your GDC displays both. \(s_n\) (dividing by \(n\)) describes this sample; \(s_{n-1}\) (dividing by \(n-1\)) is the unbiased estimate of the population standard deviation, and it is the one you use in a \(t\)-test or a confidence interval. If a question says "estimate the population standard deviation", it wants \(s_{n-1}\). Writing the wrong one loses the accuracy mark and can flip a conclusion.

Cumulative frequency, box plots and outliers

A cumulative frequency curve plots the running total against the upper class boundary; the median is at half the total frequency, the quartiles at one-quarter and three-quarters. A box and whisker plot shows five numbers: minimum, \(Q_1\), median, \(Q_3\), maximum. The interquartile range is \(\text{IQR} = Q_3-Q_1\), and an outlier is any value below \(Q_1-1.5\times\text{IQR}\) or above \(Q_3+1.5\times\text{IQR}\).

Exam technique

When comparing two data sets, never just quote numbers. Use the sentence frame: "The median of A is ___ compared with ___ for B, so on average A is ___; however the IQR of A is ___ compared with ___ for B, so A is more/less spread." One comparative sentence about centre and one about spread, each with numbers, is a full-marks comparison. Adding a comment on skew or outliers is what pushes a good answer to the top band.

4.2 Mean, median, mode, variance, standard deviation

SL / HL \[\bar x = \frac{\sum x}{n},\qquad s_n^2 = \frac{\sum x^2}{n}-\bar x^2 = \frac{\sum (x-\bar x)^2}{n},\qquad s_n = \sqrt{s_n^2},\qquad s_{n-1}^2 = \frac{n}{n-1}s_n^2.\]
Proof The computational formula for variance, and why \(n-1\) is unbiased

Computational form:

  1. \(\sum (x_i-\bar x)^2 = \sum(x_i^2 - 2x_i\bar x + \bar x^2) = \sum x_i^2 - 2\bar x\sum x_i + n\bar x^2\).
  2. Since \(\sum x_i = n\bar x\), this is \(\sum x_i^2 - 2n\bar x^2 + n\bar x^2 = \sum x_i^2 - n\bar x^2\).
  3. Divide by \(n\): \(\dfrac{\sum x_i^2}{n}-\bar x^2\). ■

Why \(n-1\): \(\bar x\) is computed from the same data, so the deviations \((x_i-\bar x)\) are constrained to sum to zero — you have "used up" one piece of information, leaving \(n-1\) independent deviations. Dividing by \(n\) therefore systematically underestimates the true variance; multiplying by \(\frac{n}{n-1}\) corrects it exactly. This is Bessel's correction, and it is why the estimator is called unbiased: \(E(S_{n-1}^2) = \sigma^2\).

Wrong tool when: the data are heavily skewed or contain outliers — then the mean and standard deviation are poor summaries and you should report the median and IQR.

Worked example 1 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. The numbers of minutes 12 students spent on homework were: 15, 22, 22, 25, 28, 30, 31, 33, 35, 40, 42, 95. (a) Find the mean and standard deviation. (b) Identify any outliers. (c) state which of the mean or median is the better measure of centre, and justify your answer.

  1. \(\sum x = 418\), so \(\bar x = \dfrac{418}{12} = 34.8\) minutes.
  2. \(\sum x^2 = 19\,586\) (GDC), so \(s_n^2 = \dfrac{19586}{12} - 34.8^2 = 1632.2 - 1213.4 = 418.8\), and \(s_n = 20.5\) minutes.
  3. Median \(=\frac{30+31}{2} = 30.5\). \(Q_1 = 23.5\), \(Q_3 = 37.5\), so IQR \(= 14\).
  4. Upper fence: \(37.5 + 1.5(14) = 58.5\). The value 95 is above it, so 95 is an outlier.
  5. The median (30.5) is the better measure of centre: the outlier 95 drags the mean up to 34.8, above nine of the twelve values.
Answer: mean 34.8, \(s_n=20.5\), outlier 95, median preferred. Sanity check: the mean being larger than the median indicates right skew — consistent with a single extreme value at the top.
Worked example 2 IB-style · 6 marks · GDC allowed

Question. The table shows the masses (kg) of 50 parcels.

Mass (kg)0–22–55–1010–20
Frequency1218146

Estimate the mean and standard deviation. Explain why a histogram of this data must use frequency density.

  1. Mid-interval values: 1, 3.5, 7.5, 15.
  2. \(\bar x = \dfrac{12(1)+18(3.5)+14(7.5)+6(15)}{50} = \dfrac{12+63+105+90}{50} = \dfrac{270}{50} = 5.4\) kg.
  3. \(\dfrac{\sum fx^2}{n} = \dfrac{12(1)+18(12.25)+14(56.25)+6(225)}{50} = \dfrac{12+220.5+787.5+1350}{50} = \dfrac{2370}{50} = 47.4\).
  4. \(s_n^2 = 47.4 - 5.4^2 = 47.4-29.16 = 18.24 \Rightarrow s_n = 4.27\) kg.
  5. Class widths are unequal (2, 3, 5, 10), so plotting raw frequency would make wide classes look artificially large; frequency density \(=\frac{f}{\text{width}}\) makes the area proportional to frequency.
Answer: mean 5.4 kg, \(s_n = 4.27\) kg. This is an estimate — say so, that word is often a mark.

4.3 Correlation and linear regression

SL / HL

Intuition: Pearson's \(r\) measures how close the points are to a straight line: \(r=1\) perfect positive, \(r=-1\) perfect negative, \(r\approx0\) no linear relationship. The regression line of \(y\) on \(x\) is the straight line that best predicts \(y\) from \(x\), and it always passes through the point \((\bar x,\ \bar y)\).

Common trap

Three killers. (1) Correlation is not causation: ice-cream sales and drowning deaths correlate because both rise in summer. (2) Extrapolation: using the regression line outside the range of the data is unjustified — the relationship may change completely. (3) \(r\) only measures linear association: a perfect parabola can give \(r\approx0\). Also note \(r\) is unchanged by linear scaling, but the regression line's equation does change if you swap units.

Worked example 3 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. Data for 8 students gave \(\sum x = 52\), \(\sum y = 96\), \(\sum x^2 = 396\), \(\sum y^2 = 1264\), \(\sum xy = 692\). (a) Find \(r\). (b) Find the regression line of \(y\) on \(x\). (c) Predict \(y\) when \(x=9\), and comment on the reliability.

  1. \(r = \dfrac{n\sum xy - \sum x\sum y}{\sqrt{\left(n\sum x^2-(\sum x)^2\right)\left(n\sum y^2-(\sum y)^2\right)}} = \dfrac{8(692)-52(96)}{\sqrt{(8(396)-52^2)(8(1264)-96^2)}} = \dfrac{5536-4992}{\sqrt{(3168-2704)(10112-9216)}} = \dfrac{544}{\sqrt{464\times896}}\).
  2. \(\sqrt{415\,744} = 644.8\), so \(r = 0.844\) — a strong positive correlation.
  3. Gradient \(b = \dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \dfrac{544/8}{464/8} = \dfrac{68}{58} = 1.172\); \(\bar x = 6.5,\ \bar y=12\), so \(a = 12 - 1.172(6.5) = 4.379\).
  4. Line: \(y = 1.17x + 4.38\). At \(x=9\): \(y = 1.172(9)+4.379 = 14.93\).
  5. \(x\) ranges from about 1 to 12 in this data set (since \(\bar x=6.5\)), so \(x=9\) is within range: the prediction is reasonably reliable.
Answer: \(r=0.844\); \(y=1.17x+4.38\); prediction 14.9, reliable (interpolation). Note \(r^2 = 0.712\), meaning about 71% of the variation in \(y\) is explained by the linear model — a sentence like that is what "interpret \(r^2\)" wants.

4.4 Probability

SL / HL

Intuition: probability is a measure of how much of the possibility space an event occupies. Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot both happen (\(P(A\cap B)=0\)); they are independent if one happening tells you nothing about the other (\(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\), equivalently \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\)). These are different ideas and students confuse them constantly.

How to use it — the four rules you need
  • Addition: \(P(A\cup B) = P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\); if mutually exclusive, the last term is 0.
  • Multiplication: \(P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B\mid A)\); if independent, \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\).
  • Conditional: \(P(A\mid B) = \dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\).
  • Complement: \(P(A') = 1-P(A)\).

Venn diagrams for overlapping categories; tree diagrams for sequences of events (multiply along branches, add across outcomes).

Worked example 4 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. In a class of 30, 18 study Physics, 14 study Chemistry, and 7 study neither. (a) Find the number studying both. (b) A student is chosen at random. Given that they study Chemistry, find the probability they study Physics. (c) Show that the events "studies Physics" and "studies Chemistry" are not independent.

  1. Studying at least one: \(30-7 = 23\). By inclusion–exclusion: \(23 = 18+14 - n(P\cap C)\), so \(n(P\cap C) = 9\).
  2. \(P(P\mid C) = \dfrac{n(P\cap C)}{n(C)} = \dfrac{9}{14} = 0.643\).
  3. For independence we need \(P(P\mid C) = P(P)\). But \(P(P) = \frac{18}{30} = 0.6 \neq 0.643\).
  4. Equivalently: \(P(P)P(C) = 0.6\times\frac{14}{30} = 0.28\), while \(P(P\cap C) = \frac{9}{30} = 0.3\). Not equal.
Answer: 9; \(0.643\); not independent. Always quote both numbers in an independence test and compare them explicitly — writing "they are different" without the numbers scores nothing.
Worked example 5 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. A test for a disease is 95% accurate when the disease is present and 97% accurate when it is absent. In the population, 1 in 200 people have the disease. A randomly chosen person tests positive. Find the probability they actually have the disease.

  1. Tree: \(P(D) = 0.005\), \(P(D')=0.995\); \(P(+\mid D)=0.95\), \(P(+\mid D')=0.03\).
  2. \(P(D\cap +) = 0.005\times0.95 = 0.00475\); \(P(D'\cap +) = 0.995\times0.03 = 0.02985\).
  3. \(P(+) = 0.00475+0.02985 = 0.0346\).
  4. \(P(D\mid +) = \dfrac{0.00475}{0.0346} = 0.137\).
Answer: 0.137 (about 14%). The result shocks everyone, and it is the point: with a rare disease, most positive tests are false positives. This is Bayes' theorem in action — and it is HL-examinable in its formal version (section 4.8).

4.5 Discrete random variables

SL / HL

Intuition: a random variable is a number whose value is decided by chance. Its distribution tells you the probability of each value. The expected value \(E(X)\) is the long-run average — not "the value I expect", but "what I'd average over many repetitions". The variance measures how spread out the values are around \(E(X)\).

\[E(X) = \sum x\,P(X=x),\qquad E(g(X)) = \sum g(x)P(X=x),\qquad Var(X) = E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2.\]
Proof \(Var(X) = E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2\)
  1. By definition \(Var(X) = E\!\left[(X-\mu)^2\right]\) where \(\mu = E(X)\).
  2. Expand inside the expectation: \(E\!\left[(X-\mu)^2\right] = E\!\left[X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2\right]\).
  3. Expectation is linear: \(= E(X^2) - 2\mu E(X) + \mu^2 = E(X^2) - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2 = E(X^2)-\mu^2\). ■

Meaning: "mean of the squares minus the square of the mean". This is the same identity as the computational variance formula in section 4.2 — data and distributions share the algebra. Wrong tool when: \(X\) is continuous — then replace sums with integrals (section 4.7).

The binomial distribution

SL / HL

Conditions: fixed number \(n\) of independent trials, two outcomes, constant probability \(p\) of "success". Then \(X\sim B(n,p)\) with \(P(X=x) = \binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x}\), \(E(X)=np\), \(Var(X)=np(1-p)\).

Worked example 6 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. A fair die is rolled 8 times. Let \(X\) be the number of sixes. (a) Find \(P(X=2)\). (b) Find \(P(X\ge 2)\). (c) Find \(E(X)\) and \(Var(X)\). (d) The die is now suspected of bias. In 60 rolls, 17 sixes appear. Is this evidence of bias at the 5% level?

  1. \(X\sim B(8,\frac16)\). \(P(X=2) = \binom{8}{2}\left(\frac16\right)^2\left(\frac56\right)^6 = 28\times0.02778\times0.3349 = 0.2605\).
  2. \(P(X\ge2) = 1-P(X\le1) = 1-[0.2326+0.3721] = 0.3953\) (GDC binomial CDF).
  3. \(E(X) = 8\times\frac16 = \frac43\); \(Var(X) = 8\times\frac16\times\frac56 = \frac{10}{9}\).
  4. Hypothesis test: \(H_0: p=\frac16\), \(H_1: p\neq\frac16\). Under \(H_0\), \(Y\sim B(60,\frac16)\) and \(P(Y\ge 17) = 0.0144\). Two-tailed: \(p\)-value \(=2\times0.0144 = 0.0288 < 0.05\).
  5. Reject \(H_0\): there is evidence at the 5% level that the die is biased.
Answer: (a) 0.261; (b) 0.395; (c) \(\frac43\), \(\frac{10}{9}\); (d) reject \(H_0\) (\(p=0.0288\)). Part (d) is the HL binomial test — note the two-tailed doubling and the conclusion phrased "evidence of bias", never "the die is biased".
Worked example 7 IB-style · 8 marks · GDC allowed

Question. A game costs \$5 to play. You roll two dice and win \$\(W\) where \(W\) is the sum of the dice, in dollars, if the sum exceeds 8; otherwise you win nothing. (a) Tabulate the distribution of your profit \(P\). (b) Find the expected profit. (c) Is the game fair?

  1. Sums exceeding 8: 9, 10, 11, 12 with probabilities \(\frac{4}{36},\frac{3}{36},\frac{2}{36},\frac{1}{36}\). Total \(\frac{10}{36}=\frac{5}{18}\).
  2. Profit \(= W-5\) if you win, and \(-5\) if you lose. So: \(P(\text{profit}=4)=\frac{4}{36}\), \(5:\frac{3}{36}\), \(6:\frac{2}{36}\), \(7:\frac{1}{36}\), \(-5:\frac{26}{36}\).
  3. \(E(\text{profit}) = \frac{4(4)+5(3)+6(2)+7(1)+(-5)(26)}{36} = \frac{16+15+12+7-130}{36} = \frac{-80}{36} = -2.22\).
  4. Not fair: the expected profit is \(-\$2.22\) per game, so the player loses on average.
Answer: \(E = -\$2.22\); not fair (a fair game has \(E=0\)). Careful with the profit variable: a classic error is to compute \(E(W)\) and forget to subtract the stake.

4.6 The normal distribution

SL / HL

Intuition: the bell curve. It is symmetric about its mean \(\mu\), and \(\sigma\) controls how wide it is. About 68% of the data lies within \(1\sigma\) of the mean, 95% within \(2\sigma\), 99.7% within \(3\sigma\). It matters because (section 4.11) sample means are approximately normal whatever the population looks like.

\[X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2),\qquad Z = \frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1).\]
Worked example 8 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. The masses of apples are normally distributed with mean 150 g and standard deviation 22 g. (a) Find the probability that a randomly chosen apple has mass between 140 g and 170 g. (b) 10% of apples have mass greater than \(m\) grams. Find \(m\).

  1. Standardise: \(z_1 = \frac{140-150}{22} = -0.4545\); \(z_2 = \frac{170-150}{22} = 0.9091\).
  2. \(P(-0.4545<Z<0.9091) = P(Z<0.9091) - P(Z<-0.4545) = 0.8183-0.3247 = 0.4936\).
  3. For (b): \(P(X>m) = 0.10 \Rightarrow P\left(Z>\frac{m-150}{22}\right) = 0.10 \Rightarrow \frac{m-150}{22} = 1.2816\) (inverse normal).
  4. \(m = 150 + 22(1.2816) = 178.2\) g.
Answer: (a) 0.494; (b) 178 g. Always sketch and shade: it tells you whether to use the upper or lower tail and catches sign errors in \(z\).

4.7 HL: continuous random variables

HL only

Intuition: for a continuous variable, \(P(X=x)=0\) for every single value — only intervals have probability, and probability is area under the pdf. This is where Topic 5 walks straight into Topic 4: every probability is a definite integral.

\[P(a\le X\le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 1,\qquad F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.\] \[E(X) = \int x\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad E(g(X)) = \int g(x) f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad Var(X) = E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2.\] \[\text{median } m:\ \int_{-\infty}^{m} f = \tfrac12;\qquad \text{lower quartile}:\ \int_{-\infty}^{q_1} f = \tfrac14.\]
How to use it
  1. If a constant \(k\) is unknown, normalise first: integrate \(f\) over its support, set equal to 1, solve for \(k\).
  2. For \(E(X)\), multiply the integrand by \(x\) and integrate over the support — the limits come from the support, not from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\), if \(f\) is piecewise.
  3. For the median, set the cdf equal to \(\frac12\) and solve (you may need to decide which piece of a piecewise cdf applies — check that your \(m\) lies in the interval you used).
  4. Use your GDC's numerical integration to check every one of these integrals. A 20-second check is worth more than a re-derivation.
Worked example 9 IB-style · 10 marks · GDC allowed

Question. A continuous random variable \(X\) has pdf \(f(x) = \begin{cases} kx(3-x), & 0\le x\le 3 \\ 0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\) (a) Show that \(k=\dfrac{2}{9}\). (b) Find \(E(X)\). (c) Find the median of \(X\). (d) Find \(P(X>2)\).

  1. Normalise: \(\int_0^3 kx(3-x)\,\mathrm{d}x = k\left[\frac{3x^2}{2}-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^3 = k\left(\frac{27}{2}-9\right) = k\cdot\frac{9}{2} = 1 \Rightarrow k = \frac29\). ■
  2. \(E(X) = \frac29\int_0^3 x^2(3-x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac29\left[x^3-\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^3 = \frac29\left(27-\frac{81}{4}\right) = \frac29\cdot\frac{27}{4} = \frac32\).
  3. Median \(m\): \(\frac29\int_0^m x(3-x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac12 \Rightarrow \frac29\left(\frac{3m^2}{2}-\frac{m^3}{3}\right)=\frac12\).
  4. Multiply: \(\frac{3m^2}{2}-\frac{m^3}{3} = \frac94 \Rightarrow 18m^2 - 4m^3 = 27 \Rightarrow 4m^3-18m^2+27=0\).
  5. Solve on the GDC: \(m = 1.50\) (the other roots lie outside \([0,3]\)). Indeed \(m=1.5\) gives \(4(3.375)-18(2.25)+27 = 13.5-40.5+27=0\). ✓
  6. \(P(X>2) = \frac29\int_2^3 x(3-x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac29\left[\frac{3x^2}{2}-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_2^3 = \frac29\left(\frac92 - \left(6-\frac83\right)\right) = \frac29\left(4.5-\frac{10}{3}\right) = \frac29\cdot\frac76 = \frac{7}{27}\).
Answer: \(k=\frac29\); \(E(X)=1.5\); median 1.5; \(P(X>2)=\frac{7}{27}=0.259\). Note that the mean equals the median here because the pdf is symmetric about \(x=1.5\) — spotting that saves the entire cubic in parts (b) and (c).
Worked example 10 IB-style · 9 marks · GDC allowed

Question. The time \(T\) (hours) a phone battery lasts has pdf \(f(t) = \dfrac{1}{8}\mathrm{e}^{-t/8}\) for \(t\ge0\). (a) Verify this is a valid pdf. (b) Find \(E(T)\). (c) Find the probability the battery lasts more than 10 hours.

  1. \(\int_0^\infty \frac18\mathrm{e}^{-t/8}\,\mathrm{d}t = \frac18\left[-8\mathrm{e}^{-t/8}\right]_0^\infty = -[0-1] = 1\). ✓
  2. \(E(T) = \frac18\int_0^\infty t\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/8}\,\mathrm{d}t\). Integrate by parts with \(u=t\), \(\mathrm{d}v=\mathrm{e}^{-t/8}\mathrm{d}t\): \(= \frac18\left(\left[-8t\mathrm{e}^{-t/8}\right]_0^\infty + 8\int_0^\infty \mathrm{e}^{-t/8}\,\mathrm{d}t\right) = \frac18\left(0 + 64\right) = 8\) hours.
  3. \(P(T>10) = \int_{10}^\infty \frac18\mathrm{e}^{-t/8}\mathrm{d}t = \left[-\mathrm{e}^{-t/8}\right]_{10}^\infty = \mathrm{e}^{-10/8} = 0.287\).
Answer: \(E(T)=8\) hours, \(P(T>10)=0.287\). This is the exponential distribution — the continuous model for waiting times, and the continuous analogue of the geometric distribution. The integration by parts in (b) is exactly the Topic 5 technique from section 5.8.

4.8 HL: Poisson distribution and Bayes' theorem

HL only

Poisson intuition: count how many times something happens in a fixed interval when events occur independently at a constant average rate and there is no upper limit. Emails per hour, flaws per metre of cable, decays per second. One parameter only: the mean rate \(m\).

\[X\sim Po(m):\quad P(X=x) = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-m}m^x}{x!},\qquad E(X)=Var(X)=m.\]
How to use it — choosing between binomial and Poisson

Ask: is there a fixed number of trials \(n\)? Yes → binomial. No, just an average rate over a continuous interval → Poisson. Watch the interval: if the rate is 3 per hour, then over 2 hours the parameter is \(m=6\); over 20 minutes it is \(m=1\). Rescaling the interval is the single most common error.

Worked example 11 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. Calls arrive at a switchboard at an average rate of 4 per 10 minutes. (a) Find the probability of exactly 2 calls in 10 minutes. (b) Find the probability of at least 5 calls in 30 minutes. (c) Find the probability of no calls in 5 minutes.

  1. (a) \(X\sim Po(4)\): \(P(X=2) = \dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{-4}4^2}{2!} = \dfrac{0.01832\times16}{2} = 0.1465\).
  2. (b) 30 minutes is three intervals, so \(m = 12\). \(P(X\ge5) = 1-P(X\le4) = 1-0.0076 = 0.9924\) (GDC).
  3. (c) 5 minutes is half, so \(m=2\). \(P(X=0) = \mathrm{e}^{-2} = 0.1353\).
Answer: (a) 0.147; (b) 0.992; (c) 0.135. Every part requires rescaling the rate to match the interval — write "\(m=12\)" explicitly so the examiner can see you did.

Bayes' theorem

Intuition: you observe an effect and want to revise your belief about the cause. Bayes' theorem is the rule for flipping a conditional probability: \(P(A\mid B)\) from \(P(B\mid A)\).

\[P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(A)P(B\mid A)}{P(A)P(B\mid A)+P(A')P(B\mid A')}\] More generally, for a partition \(A_1,\dots,A_k\): \(P(A_i\mid B) = \dfrac{P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)}{\sum_j P(A_j)P(B\mid A_j)}\).
Proof Bayes' theorem from the definition of conditional probability
  1. By definition: \(P(A\mid B) = \dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\) and \(P(B\mid A) = \dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}\).
  2. From the second, \(P(A\cap B) = P(B\mid A)P(A)\).
  3. By the law of total probability, \(P(B) = P(A\cap B) + P(A'\cap B) = P(B\mid A)P(A)+P(B\mid A')P(A')\).
  4. Substitute both into step 1: \(P(A\mid B) = \dfrac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B\mid A)P(A)+P(B\mid A')P(A')}\). ■

Meaning: the posterior \(= \dfrac{\text{prior}\times\text{likelihood}}{\text{total probability of the evidence}}\). Wrong tool when: the \(A_i\) do not form a partition (they must be exhaustive and mutually exclusive).

Worked example 12 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. Machine A makes 60% of items and has a 2% defect rate; machine B makes 40% and has a 5% defect rate. An item is chosen at random and is defective. Find the probability it came from machine A.

  1. \(P(A)=0.6,\ P(B)=0.4,\ P(D\mid A)=0.02,\ P(D\mid B)=0.05\).
  2. \(P(A\mid D) = \dfrac{P(A)P(D\mid A)}{P(A)P(D\mid A)+P(B)P(D\mid B)} = \dfrac{0.6(0.02)}{0.6(0.02)+0.4(0.05)} = \dfrac{0.012}{0.012+0.020}\).
  3. \(= \dfrac{0.012}{0.032} = 0.375\).
Answer: 0.375. Even though machine A makes most items, a defective item is more likely to come from B (62.5%) because B's defect rate is much higher. Drawing a tree first, then applying Bayes, is the most reliable route.

4.9 HL: linear combinations of random variables & unbiased estimates

HL only \[E(aX+b) = aE(X)+b,\qquad Var(aX+b) = a^2Var(X),\] \[E(aX\pm bY) = aE(X)\pm bE(Y),\qquad Var(aX\pm bY) = a^2Var(X)+b^2Var(Y)\ \ (\text{independent }X,Y).\] For \(n\) independent identically distributed variables: \(E(\bar X)=\mu\), \(Var(\bar X)=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\).
Common trap

Variance always uses squared coefficients and always adds for independent variables — including the minus case: \(Var(X-Y) = Var(X)+Var(Y)\), not \(Var(X)-Var(Y)\). And \(Var(2X+3Y)=4Var(X)+9Var(Y)\): students write \(2Var(X)+3Var(Y)\) every single session.

Worked example 13 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. Independent random variables have \(E(X)=10,\ Var(X)=4\) and \(E(Y)=3,\ Var(Y)=9\). Find the mean and standard deviation of (a) \(3X-2Y\); (b) \(X+5\).

  1. (a) \(E(3X-2Y) = 3(10)-2(3) = 24\).
  2. \(Var(3X-2Y) = 9(4)+4(9) = 36+36 = 72\), so sd \(=\sqrt{72} = 8.49\).
  3. (b) \(E(X+5) = 15\); \(Var(X+5) = 1^2\cdot4 = 4\), so sd \(=2\).
Answer: (a) mean 24, sd 8.49; (b) mean 15, sd 2. Adding a constant shifts the mean but does not change the spread — that is the whole content of \(Var(aX+b)=a^2Var(X)\).

4.10 HL: the central limit theorem

HL only

Statement: if \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are independent identically distributed random variables with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), then for large \(n\) (usually \(n\ge30\) is taken as sufficient) the sample mean is approximately normal:

\[\bar X \sim N\!\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\ \text{approximately},\qquad\text{equivalently}\quad \sum X_i \sim N(n\mu,\ n\sigma^2).\]
Why this exists

The CLT is the reason statistics works. You almost never know the population distribution — but you rarely need to, because averages are normal regardless. Its practical content is the \(\sqrt n\): the standard deviation of the mean is \(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\), so to halve your uncertainty you need four times the data.

Worked example 14 IB-style · 7 marks · GDC allowed

Question. The mass of a suitcase has mean 18 kg and standard deviation 5 kg. (a) Find the probability that 40 randomly chosen suitcases have total mass exceeding 760 kg. (b) State the theorem you used and why it applies.

  1. \(S = \sum X_i\) has \(E(S)=40\times18 = 720\) and \(Var(S) = 40\times25 = 1000\), so sd \(=\sqrt{1000}=31.62\).
  2. By the CLT, \(S\approx N(720, 1000)\).
  3. \(P(S>760) = P\!\left(Z>\dfrac{760-720}{31.62}\right) = P(Z>1.265) = 0.103\).
  4. The CLT applies because \(n=40\ge30\), the suitcases are independent, and they are identically distributed.
Answer: 0.103. Writing the CLT conditions (large \(n\), independence, identical distribution) is usually a mark — and it is the difference between a statistical argument and a guess.

4.11 HL: confidence intervals

HL only

Intuition: a confidence interval takes a sample mean and builds a range around it that usually contains the true population mean. A 95% confidence interval means: if you repeated the whole procedure many times, 95% of the intervals you built would contain \(\mu\). It does not mean "there is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) is in this interval" — \(\mu\) is fixed; the interval is random.

\[\text{CI for }\mu:\quad \bar x \pm z\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \ (\sigma\text{ known}),\qquad \bar x \pm t_{n-1}\frac{s_{n-1}}{\sqrt n}\ \ (\sigma\text{ unknown, normal population}).\]
How to use it
  1. Identify: is \(\sigma\) (population sd) given? Yes → \(z\)-interval. Only \(s_{n-1}\) (sample sd) → \(t\)-interval with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.
  2. Write the formula, substitute, and give the interval with units.
  3. Interpret in context: "We are 95% confident that the true mean ___ lies between ___ and ___."
  4. If asked how to halve the width: quadruple \(n\) (width \(\propto \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\)).
  5. Link to hypothesis tests: a 95% CI contains exactly those values of \(\mu_0\) that would not be rejected by a two-tailed test at the 5% level.
Worked example 15 IB-style · 9 marks · GDC allowed

Question. A sample of 25 batteries has mean lifetime 52.3 hours. The population standard deviation is known to be 6 hours. (a) Find a 95% confidence interval for the mean lifetime. (b) Find a 99% confidence interval. (c) The manufacturer claims the mean is 55 hours. Comment.

  1. \(\sigma\) known → \(z\)-interval: \(52.3 \pm 1.96\cdot\dfrac{6}{\sqrt{25}} = 52.3 \pm 1.96(1.2) = 52.3\pm2.352\).
  2. 95% CI: \((49.9,\ 54.7)\) hours.
  3. 99%: \(z=2.576\): \(52.3\pm 2.576(1.2) = 52.3 \pm 3.091\), so \((49.2,\ 55.4)\) hours.
  4. The 95% interval does not contain 55, so at the 5% level there is evidence against the claim. The 99% interval does contain 55, so at the 1% level there is not sufficient evidence.
Answer: (49.9, 54.7) and (49.2, 55.4). Higher confidence → wider interval: that relationship is worth stating explicitly for a mark. Part (c) shows the CI/test duality that Paper 3 loves.

4.12 HL: hypothesis testing — one procedure, every variant

HL only

Every IB hypothesis test — \(z\)-test, \(t\)-test, binomial test, Poisson test, \(\chi^2\) goodness-of-fit, \(\chi^2\) independence — is the same six steps. Learn the routine once and you have learned six question types.

The 6-step procedure (use this every time)
  1. Hypotheses. Write \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) in symbols and in words, defining the parameter (e.g. "\(p\) is the probability that a randomly chosen bulb is defective"). \(H_0\) always contains the equality; \(H_1\) determines whether the test is one- or two-tailed.
  2. Model and conditions. Name the distribution of the test statistic under \(H_0\) (e.g. \(X\sim B(20,0.4)\), or \(\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\) by the CLT with \(n\ge30\)), and state the significance level \(\alpha\) (default 5% if not given).
  3. Test statistic. Compute it with a formula and a visible substitution: \(z=\frac{\bar x-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\), \(t=\frac{\bar x-\mu}{s/\sqrt n}\), \(\chi^2=\sum\frac{(f_o-f_e)^2}{f_e}\), or a direct \(p\)-value from a binomial/Poisson tail.
  4. \(p\)-value or critical region. Either find \(p\) and compare with \(\alpha\), or find the critical value and check whether the statistic falls in the rejection region. Write the comparison explicitly: "\(0.032 < 0.05\)".
  5. Decision. "Reject \(H_0\)" or "fail to reject \(H_0\)" — never "accept \(H_0\)".
  6. Conclusion in context. One sentence, in the language of the question, that a non-mathematician could understand: "There is sufficient evidence at the 5% level to conclude that the mean lifetime is less than 55 hours."
Common trap

(1) Writing \(H_1\) with an equals sign. (2) Forgetting to double the \(p\)-value for a two-tailed test. (3) Saying "accept \(H_0\)" — you can only fail to reject it. (4) Concluding with no reference to the significance level or the context. (5) Using \(s_n\) instead of \(s_{n-1}\) in a \(t\)-test. (6) For \(\chi^2\), using percentages instead of counts, or forgetting that expected frequencies must be at least 5 (otherwise combine categories).

Variant 1: \(z\)-test (\(\sigma\) known) and Variant 2: \(t\)-test (\(\sigma\) unknown)

Worked example 16 IB-style · 9 marks · GDC allowed

Question. The labelled mass of a packet of rice is 500 g. A sample of 15 packets has mean mass 496.2 g and standard deviation 5.1 g. Test at the 5% level whether the mean mass differs from 500 g, assuming masses are normally distributed.

  1. Let \(\mu\) be the population mean mass. \(H_0: \mu = 500\); \(H_1: \mu \neq 500\) (two-tailed).
  2. \(\sigma\) unknown, population normal, \(n=15\) → \(t\)-test with 14 degrees of freedom. \(\alpha=0.05\).
  3. \(t = \dfrac{\bar x - \mu}{s_{n-1}/\sqrt n} = \dfrac{496.2-500}{5.1/\sqrt{15}} = \dfrac{-3.8}{1.3166} = -2.886\).
  4. \(p\)-value (two-tailed, df 14) \(= 2P(T_{14} < -2.886) = 0.0118\).
  5. \(0.0118 < 0.05\), so reject \(H_0\).
  6. There is sufficient evidence at the 5% level to conclude that the mean mass of a packet is different from 500 g (the sample suggests it is under-filled).
Answer: reject \(H_0\), \(p=0.0118\). Using \(s_{n-1}=5.1\) is essential; if 5.1 were \(s_n\), then \(s_{n-1} = 5.1\sqrt{\frac{15}{14}} = 5.28\) and the conclusion could change. Always check which one the question gives you.

Variant 3: binomial test; Variant 4: Poisson test

Worked example 17 IB-style · 8 marks · GDC allowed

Question. A hospital records an average of 3.0 admissions per night. After a local factory closes, the hospital records 7 admissions on a particular night. Test at the 5% level whether the mean number of admissions per night has decreased or increased.

  1. Let \(\lambda\) be the mean number of admissions per night. \(H_0: \lambda = 3\); \(H_1: \lambda \neq 3\).
  2. Under \(H_0\), \(X\sim Po(3)\).
  3. \(P(X\ge 7) = 1 - P(X\le 6) = 1 - 0.9665 = 0.0335\).
  4. Two-tailed: since the Poisson is skewed, the standard IB approach is to double this tail: \(p = 2(0.0335) = 0.0670\). (Some markschemes accept comparing the single tail with 0.025.)
  5. \(0.0670 > 0.05\), so we fail to reject \(H_0\).
  6. There is insufficient evidence at the 5% level that the mean number of admissions has changed.
Answer: do not reject \(H_0\). Note the judgement call: the observed value 7 is in the upper tail, so the appropriate one-tailed \(p\) is 0.0335; whether to double depends on whether \(H_1\) is two-tailed. State your choice — examiners credit a justified method.

Variant 5: \(\chi^2\) goodness of fit

Test statistic \(\chi^2 = \sum\dfrac{(f_o-f_e)^2}{f_e}\) with degrees of freedom \(\nu = k-1\) (minus one more for each parameter estimated from the data). Reject \(H_0\) if \(\chi^2_{\text{calc}} > \chi^2_{\text{crit}}\) or if \(p < \alpha\).

Worked example 18 IB-style · 10 marks · GDC allowed

Question. A die is rolled 120 times with these results: score 1: 14, 2: 22, 3: 18, 4: 25, 5: 17, 6: 24. Test at the 5% level whether the die is fair.

  1. \(H_0\): the die is fair (each score has probability \(\frac16\)); \(H_1\): it is not fair.
  2. Expected frequency for each score \(= \frac{120}{6} = 20\).
  3. \(\chi^2 = \dfrac{(14-20)^2}{20}+\dfrac{(22-20)^2}{20}+\dfrac{(18-20)^2}{20}+\dfrac{(25-20)^2}{20}+\dfrac{(17-20)^2}{20}+\dfrac{(24-20)^2}{20}\).
  4. \(= \dfrac{36+4+4+25+9+16}{20} = \dfrac{94}{20} = 4.7\).
  5. Degrees of freedom \(= 6-1 = 5\). Critical value at 5%: \(\chi^2_{5,0.95} = 11.07\).
  6. \(4.7 < 11.07\) (equivalently \(p = 0.454 > 0.05\)), so fail to reject \(H_0\).
  7. There is no evidence at the 5% level that the die is unfair.
Answer: \(\chi^2=4.7\), \(\nu=5\), \(p=0.454\); do not reject \(H_0\). All expected frequencies are 20 ≥ 5, so no categories need combining. Quote \(\nu\) — it is a mark on its own.

Variant 6: \(\chi^2\) test for independence

Expected frequency \(= \dfrac{\text{row total}\times\text{column total}}{\text{grand total}}\); degrees of freedom \(\nu = (r-1)(c-1)\).

Worked example 19 IB-style · 11 marks · GDC allowed

Question. 200 voters were classified by age group and party preference:

Party XParty YTotal
Under 30483280
30 and over5466120
Total10298200

Test at the 5% level whether age group and party preference are independent.

  1. \(H_0\): age group and party preference are independent; \(H_1\): they are not independent.
  2. Expected counts: under-30/X \(=\frac{80\times102}{200} = 40.8\); under-30/Y \(=39.2\); 30+/X \(=61.2\); 30+/Y \(=58.8\).
  3. \(\chi^2 = \dfrac{(48-40.8)^2}{40.8}+\dfrac{(32-39.2)^2}{39.2}+\dfrac{(54-61.2)^2}{61.2}+\dfrac{(66-58.8)^2}{58.8}\).
  4. \(= \dfrac{51.84}{40.8}+\dfrac{51.84}{39.2}+\dfrac{51.84}{61.2}+\dfrac{51.84}{58.8} = 1.271+1.322+0.847+0.882 = 4.322\).
  5. \(\nu = (2-1)(2-1) = 1\). Critical value at 5% with 1 df: 3.841.
  6. \(4.322 > 3.841\) (equivalently \(p = 0.0377 < 0.05\)), so reject \(H_0\).
  7. There is evidence at the 5% level that age group and party preference are associated — younger voters in this sample favour Party X.
Answer: \(\chi^2 = 4.32\), \(\nu=1\), \(p=0.0377\); reject \(H_0\). Note the Yates correction is not required in this syllabus. Always add the interpretive sentence identifying which cell drove the result.

Type I and Type II errors

\(H_0\) true\(H_0\) false
Reject \(H_0\)Type I error — probability \(=\alpha\) (the significance level)Correct decision — probability \(=1-\beta\), the power
Fail to reject \(H_0\)Correct decision — probability \(=1-\alpha\)Type II error — probability \(=\beta\)

The only way to reduce both simultaneously is to increase the sample size. Decreasing \(\alpha\) makes Type I less likely but Type II more likely — that trade-off is the standard "discuss" question.

Worked example 20 IB-style · 6 marks · GDC allowed

Question. A test of \(H_0: \mu = 50\) against \(H_1: \mu > 50\) is carried out at the 5% level. Describe, in context, what a Type I error and a Type II error would be, and state which is more serious if \(H_0\) being true corresponds to a water supply being safe.

  1. Type I: concluding the mean exceeds 50 when in fact it equals 50 — declaring the water unsafe when it is safe.
  2. Type II: failing to detect that the mean exceeds 50 — declaring the water safe when it is not.
  3. Here a Type II error is more serious (unsafe water is supplied), so we would choose a larger \(\alpha\) (or a larger sample) to reduce \(\beta\) — accepting more false alarms to catch more real ones.
Answer: as above. \(P(\text{Type I}) = \alpha = 0.05\) by construction. The power \(1-\beta\) rises with \(n\) and with the true distance from \(\mu_0\).

Check yourself

Practice

  1. Data set: 4, 7, 7, 9, 10, 12, 13, 15, 40. Find the median, quartiles and any outliers.

    Show answer
    1. \(n=9\), median = 5th value = 10.
    2. Lower half: 4, 7, 7, 9 → \(Q_1 = 7\). Upper half: 12, 13, 15, 40 → \(Q_3 = 14\).
    3. IQR \(= 14-7 = 7\); upper fence \(= 14+1.5(7) = 24.5\); lower fence \(= 7-10.5 = -3.5\).
    4. 40 is above 24.5, so it is an outlier.
    Answer: median 10, \(Q_1=7\), \(Q_3=14\), outlier 40. Note the median is used as a fence-exclusion: with an odd \(n\), exclude the median when finding quartiles.

  2. \(X\sim B(12, 0.35)\). Find \(P(X=4)\) and \(P(X\ge 4)\).

    Show answer
    1. \(P(X=4) = \binom{12}{4}(0.35)^4(0.65)^8 = 495\times0.015006\times0.031864 = 0.2367\).
    2. \(P(X\ge4) = 1-P(X\le3) = 1-0.3467 = 0.6533\) (GDC binomial CDF).
    Answer: 0.237 and 0.653. Write \(X\sim B(12,0.35)\) before any computation — the distribution statement is itself a mark in hypothesis-testing questions.

  3. \(X\sim N(20, 9)\). Find \(P(17<X<26)\), and the value of \(a\) such that \(P(X<a)=0.90\).

    Show answer
    1. \(\sigma = 3\). \(z_1 = \frac{17-20}{3} = -1\), \(z_2 = \frac{26-20}{3} = 2\).
    2. \(P(-1<Z<2) = 0.9772-0.1587 = 0.8185\).
    3. \(P(X<a)=0.90 \Rightarrow z = 1.2816 \Rightarrow a = 20+3(1.2816) = 23.8\).
    Answer: 0.8185 and 23.8. Note \(N(20,9)\) means variance 9, so \(\sigma=3\) — the second parameter is the variance, and forgetting this is endemic.

  4. A continuous random variable has pdf \(f(x)=\dfrac{3}{8}x^2\) on \([0,2]\). Find \(E(X)\) and \(Var(X)\).

    Show answer
    1. Check: \(\frac38\int_0^2 x^2dx = \frac38\cdot\frac83 = 1\). ✓
    2. \(E(X) = \frac38\int_0^2 x^3dx = \frac38\cdot\frac{16}{4} = \frac38\cdot4 = 1.5\).
    3. \(E(X^2) = \frac38\int_0^2 x^4 dx = \frac38\cdot\frac{32}{5} = 2.4\).
    4. \(Var(X) = 2.4 - 2.25 = 0.15\).
    Answer: \(E(X)=1.5\), \(Var(X)=0.15\) (sd 0.387). The distribution is concentrated near 2, so the mean being 1.5 is sensible.

  5. Flaws in glass occur at 0.4 per m². Find the probability that a 5 m² sheet has (a) no flaws; (b) at least 3 flaws.

    Show answer
    1. Rate over 5 m²: \(m = 0.4\times5 = 2\). \(X\sim Po(2)\).
    2. (a) \(P(X=0)=\mathrm{e}^{-2}=0.1353\).
    3. (b) \(P(X\ge3) = 1-P(X\le2) = 1-(0.1353+0.2707+0.2707) = 1-0.6767 = 0.3233\).
    Answer: 0.135 and 0.323. Rescaling the rate to the given area is essential; using \(m=0.4\) scores zero.

  6. Independent variables: \(X\sim N(50, 25)\) and \(Y\sim N(30, 16)\). Find the distribution, mean and standard deviation of \(X-2Y\).

    Show answer
    1. Linear combinations of independent normal variables are normal.
    2. \(E(X-2Y) = 50-2(30) = -10\).
    3. \(Var(X-2Y) = 25 + 4(16) = 25+64 = 89\), so sd \(=9.43\).
    4. \(X-2Y \sim N(-10, 89)\).
    Answer: \(N(-10, 89)\), mean \(-10\), sd 9.43. The variance adds even though the combination subtracts — the trap of the decade.

  7. A sample of 36 has mean 18.4 and the population standard deviation is 3.6. Find a 90% confidence interval for the population mean.

    Show answer
    1. \(\sigma\) known → \(z\)-interval; \(z = 1.645\) for 90%.
    2. \(18.4 \pm 1.645\frac{3.6}{\sqrt{36}} = 18.4 \pm 1.645(0.6) = 18.4 \pm 0.987\).
    3. Interval: \((17.4,\ 19.4)\).
    Answer: (17.4, 19.4). To halve the width you need four times the sample: \(n=144\).

  8. Carry out a \(\chi^2\) goodness-of-fit test: 100 observations across 4 categories gave observed counts 32, 28, 22, 18, while the model predicts ratios 3:3:2:2. Test at the 10% level.

    Show answer
    1. \(H_0\): the data fit the 3:3:2:2 model; \(H_1\): they do not.
    2. Expected: \(30, 30, 20, 20\).
    3. \(\chi^2 = \frac{(32-30)^2}{30}+\frac{(28-30)^2}{30}+\frac{(22-20)^2}{20}+\frac{(18-20)^2}{20} = \frac{4}{30}+\frac{4}{30}+\frac{4}{20}+\frac{4}{20} = 0.1333+0.1333+0.2+0.2 = 0.6667\).
    4. \(\nu = 4-1 = 3\); critical value at 10% \(= 6.251\).
    5. \(0.667 < 6.251\) (\(p = 0.881 > 0.10\)), so fail to reject \(H_0\): the model fits well.
    Answer: \(\chi^2=0.667\), \(\nu=3\), do not reject \(H_0\). Convert ratios to expected counts using the total — never test ratios directly.
