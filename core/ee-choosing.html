Choosing your pathway and research question

The extended essay is decided in the first three weeks. Not by how well you write, but by whether you picked a question that can carry four thousand words of reasoning. A brilliant writer with a descriptive question caps at a C. An average writer with a genuinely arguable question, a defensible method and a supervisor they actually used, can reach an A. This page is about that first decision.

Pathways: subject-focused / interdisciplinary 30 marks · A 6 · B 6 · C 6 · D 8 · E 4 Class of 2028
What I verified, and what you must confirm

The official subject brief at ibo.org is behind bot protection, so I could not read the PDF directly. Everything below is cross-checked against the IB's own public curriculum-update page and against two independent school/university sources quoting the Extended essay guide (first assessment 2027).

  • Confirmed (IB's own wording): the essay is "presented as a formal piece of academic writing containing no more than 4,000 words". Two pathways — subject-focused or interdisciplinary. Five criteria: framework for the essay (6), knowledge and understanding (6), analysis and line of argument (6), discussion and evaluation (8), reflection (4) — 30 marks in total. Reflection focuses on the student's growth and the transferable skills learned. Categories removed from language A and language B.
  • Confirmed across multiple sources: three mandatory reflection sessions with your supervisor, the last of which is the concluding interview (viva voce); a single reflective statement of no more than 500 words, written on the Reflection and Progress Form (RPF) after that interview; roughly 40 hours of student work and 3–5 hours of supervision.
  • Confirm with your coordinator: whether an abstract is required in your session and whether it counts toward the 4,000; the exact list of prescribed interdisciplinary frameworks (reputable sources disagree on the names, and the IB states the frameworks are not directly assessed anyway); the word limit if you write in Chinese rather than English; and your school's own internal deadlines.

Two pathways, one question

You choose one of two research pathways. Everything else — supervision, word limit, criteria, grading — is identical. The pathway is not a difficulty setting. It is a statement about where your question lives.

Subject-focused pathway

You investigate a question through the concepts, theories and methods of one DP subject. One disciplinary lens, one set of conventions, one supervisor. This is the traditional route and it is the right one for most students, including you.

Interdisciplinary pathway

You integrate the concepts, methods or perspectives of two DP subjects to answer one focused question. The old "world studies" essay has been absorbed into this pathway, which is now broader: the integration must be essential to the question, but the two subjects do not have to be weighted equally.

The interdisciplinary trap

The interdisciplinary pathway sounds more impressive. That is exactly why it ruins essays. Here is the failure mode: you want to study, say, the mathematics of cryptography. You register it as Mathematics + Computer Science because it sounds richer. Then you write four thousand words of number theory, bolt on two paragraphs about Python, and the examiner — who is marking both subject guidances — sees a maths essay with decoration. The integration is not essential, so it reads as padding, and padding costs you in criteria A, C and D at once.

The test you must pass: write one sentence beginning "Computer science is necessary because …" where the ending is not "it provides context". If the second subject only provides context, motivation, or a real-world application, it is not part of the answer. Cut it.

What examiners actually reward here

The five criteria are applied identically to both pathways — there is no bonus for choosing the harder one. Criterion D (discussion and evaluation) is worth 8 of the 30 marks, more than a quarter of the whole essay, and it is where students most often lose their grade. A narrow, disciplined single-subject question gives you room to evaluate properly. A sprawling two-subject question forces you to spend words on coverage instead. Coverage does not earn marks; evaluation does.

How to choose — a six-step procedure
  1. Write the question you actually want to answer, in one sentence, without worrying about quality.
  2. Name the method you would use to answer it. Not the subject — the method. "Compare two published proofs", "run a simulation and compare against a theoretical bound", "close-read three passages".
  3. Ask: does that method belong entirely to one DP subject? If yes, you are subject-focused. Stop here.
  4. If no, write the one-sentence justification: "Subject X is necessary because …".
  5. Show that sentence to the teacher of the other subject and ask them to attack it. If they can delete their subject from the sentence and the question still stands, you are subject-focused.
  6. Only then register the pathway — and confirm with your coordinator that the school can supervise and register both subjects.

Your supervisor: what they are for

Your supervisor is a teacher in your school who guides the research process. Expect roughly three to five hours of their time in total across the whole essay, including the three mandatory reflection sessions. That is not much. Spend it well.

Your supervisor willYour supervisor will not

Discuss your method and warn you when it cannot answer the question.Design the method for you.

What to bring to every meeting
  1. One artefact. A draft paragraph, a source list, a results table, a section plan. Never arrive to "discuss progress" — that meeting produces nothing.
  2. Two or three specific questions written down in advance. "Is my method able to distinguish X from Y?" "Are these three sources enough for a literature-based essay?" "Does my conclusion over-claim?"
  3. Your research log (see the writing page) — every source, with one line on what you took from it.
  4. Your current word count and your current answer-in-one-sentence to the research question. If that sentence has not changed since last time, you have not been thinking.
  5. A next step you propose yourself. Leave with their corrections to your plan, not with a plan they invented.
Three ways students waste a supervisor
  1. Going silent for six weeks. Supervisors cannot help work they have not seen, and they are required to record the reflection sessions. Silence kills the process.
  2. Sending chapter-by-chapter for editing. They are permitted to review one full draft. If you send section 1, then section 2, then section 3, you have used your entitlement on fragments and nobody has seen the argument hang together.
  3. Arguing with feedback instead of testing it. You do not have to accept every suggestion. You do have to try it. Write the version they asked for, then compare. Often they are right; when they are not, you now have a much better reason than "I prefer mine".

Research ethics: when you need permission

Most EEs in maths, physics and computer science need no ethical approval: nobody is harmed by a simulation. The moment your research involves people or personal data, the rules change, and getting this wrong is an academic-honesty problem, not a technicality.

Your research involvesWhat you need
Only published sources, your own measurements on inanimate systems, code and public datasets.Nothing beyond normal citation. This should be your default.
Interviews, questionnaires, surveys, or classroom trials with identifiable people.Informed consent from every participant (and parental consent for anyone under 18), plus your supervisor's sign-off, before you collect anything.
Any data that can identify a person — names, usernames, school, location, screenshots of messages.Anonymise at the point of collection. Store the raw data separately and do not include it in the essay or appendices.
Analysis of a text or artwork (literature, film, Chinese A).No ethics approval — but you must cite the edition you used and any translations, and you must not present a critic's reading as your own.
Scraping websites, using an API, or collecting user-generated content.Check the site's terms of service and robots.txt. Use public, documented APIs where they exist. Do not collect personal data incidentally.
Animals, humans as experimental subjects, hazardous materials, or fieldwork with risk.Do not. An EE is not the place for it. Choose a question you can answer without it.
The ethics procedure, in order
  1. Before you collect anything, write down: who or what is the subject, what data you will record, how you will store it, how you will anonymise it, and when you will delete it.
  2. Show that note to your supervisor. Ask directly: "Does this need consent, and does the school need to approve it?"
  3. Get consent in writing if the answer is yes. A participant information line plus a signed agreement is enough at this level.
  4. State in the essay's method section that consent was obtained and data anonymised. One sentence. It reads as professional.
  5. If you cannot get consent, change the method — do not quietly proceed.
Connection to TOK

Ethics in research is a knowledge question in disguise: who decides what counts as acceptable means to a piece of knowledge? If you write a CS essay on scraping public data, or a physics essay using published datasets, you already have the raw material for a TOK essay on the relationship between method and justification. Keep your method notes — you will want them again.

The research question

Everything else in the extended essay is downstream of this sentence. Criterion A asks whether your research question, your research methods and your structural conventions "provide an effective framework for the essay". If the question is weak, the framework is weak, and criteria C and D — analysis, and discussion and evaluation — have nothing to attach to.

The five tests a good research question must pass

#TestThe question it killsHow to check it in ten seconds
1 Focused. Narrow enough to treat properly in 4,000 words. "The history of cryptography." Can you name the specific object, text, system, period or dataset you will examine? If not, it is a topic, not a question.
2 Arguable, not descriptive. A reasonable expert could disagree. "How does RSA encryption work?" Can someone answer "yes" or give one fact and be finished? Then there is nothing to argue. Rephrase with "to what extent", "how far", or two competing positions.
3 Answerable with sources you can actually access. "Are P = NP?" (Nice try.) Run the 20-minute source test below. If you cannot find material, the question is unanswerable for you — which is the same thing.
4 Not answerable with a single fact. "What is the time complexity of merge sort?" Does answering it require weighing evidence, comparing cases, or tracing a chain of reasoning? If a lookup table settles it, there is no essay.
5 You can sustain a line of argument through it. Any question whose answer is a list. Write your conclusion in one sentence now. If that sentence is not a claim someone could reject, the question has no argument in it.
The sentence shapes that work

Not every good question uses these words, but almost every weak one avoids them.

  • To what extent … — forces a judgement with degrees. Best all-rounder.
  • How far can X be explained by Y rather than Z? — forces two competing explanations.
  • Under what conditions does X fail? — forces you to test the boundary of a model. Excellent for maths, physics and CS.
  • How does A's treatment of B differ from C's, and what follows? — forces comparison with a consequence. Excellent for the language subjects.
  • Is X better understood as P or as Q? — forces a choice between framings.

Avoid: "Discuss …", "Explore …", "A study of …", "An investigation into …". These are topics with a verb attached, and they produce descriptive essays.

The narrowing funnel

Narrowing procedure — add one constraint at a time
  1. Add an object. Which text, system, algorithm, corpus, dataset, author, period?
  2. Add a comparison or a boundary. Compared with what? Up to what limit? Under what assumption?
  3. Add the method. How will you settle it — proof, simulation, close reading, secondary literature, measurement?
  4. Add the tension. What is the strongest thing someone could say against the answer you expect?
  5. Re-read it as an examiner. Could a competent student answer this in 300 words? If so, add another constraint.

Eight worked narrowings

Each of these starts as something a real student brought to a supervisor. Watch what changes at each step — and watch the two that get abandoned entirely, because abandoning a bad question early is the most valuable skill on this page.

Worked example 1 Physics — from a topic to a boundary condition
  1. Start: "Pendulums." Hopeless. A topic with no question.
  2. Add an object: "The motion of a simple pendulum." Still descriptive — this is a textbook chapter, not an essay.
  3. Add a tension: "How accurate is the simple pendulum model?" Better; now there is something to judge. But "how accurate" invites a single number.
  4. Add a boundary: "How accurate is the small-angle approximation for amplitudes up to 60°?" Now it is focused and testable — but still close to a lab report, because the answer is a measurement.
  5. Add the argument: "To what extent can the observed departure of a simple pendulum's period from \(T=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\) at amplitudes up to 60° be accounted for by the neglected terms of the small-angle series, rather than by systematic experimental error?"

Why this works: step 5 forces two competing explanations (theory vs apparatus) to be weighed against each other. That weighing is criterion D. Step 4 would have produced a physics IA; step 5 produces an argument.

Final RQ: "To what extent can the departure of a simple pendulum's period from \(T=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\) at amplitudes up to 60° be accounted for by the neglected terms of the small-angle expansion rather than by systematic experimental error?"
Worked example 2 Mathematics — from exposition to a question about a method's limits
  1. Start: "The Riemann zeta function." Far too large; a PhD topic.
  2. Narrow the object: "Euler's solution to the Basel problem." This is now writable — but as an exposition. It would be a beautiful piece of mathematical writing and a C-grade EE, because nothing is at stake.
  3. Add a comparison: "How does Euler's solution to the Basel problem compare with a modern rigorous proof?" Now there is a comparison, but "compare" tends to produce two descriptions side by side.
  4. Add the tension: "To what extent does Euler's 1735 evaluation of \(\sum 1/n^{2}\) depend on a step that is valid only because the sine product is entire, and how much of his argument survives when that step is made rigorous?"

Why this works: the question now asks you to judge a historical argument by modern standards — a genuine mathematical judgement, where the evidence is the proofs themselves.

Final RQ: "To what extent does Euler's evaluation of \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^{2}=\pi^{2}/6\) depend on an assumption about infinite products that was not justified until the nineteenth century?"
Worked example 3 Computer science — from a project to an evaluation
  1. Start: "Machine learning." Meaningless at this size.
  2. Add an object: "Neural networks for image classification." Still a survey.
  3. Add a method: "Comparing convolutional and fully connected networks on MNIST." Now it is an experiment — and an experiment is not an essay. This is the classic CS trap: a software project with no argument.
  4. Add the evaluation: "Do convolutional architectures outperform fully connected ones on MNIST because of translational invariance, or because of parameter count?"
  5. Add the boundary: "To what extent does the performance advantage of a convolutional network over a fully connected network of equal parameter count on MNIST depend on translational invariance rather than on the inductive bias of local connectivity?"

Why this works: controlling for parameter count turns a horse race into a causal question. The code is now evidence for a claim, not the deliverable.

Final RQ: "To what extent does the performance advantage of a small convolutional network over a parameter-matched fully connected network on MNIST depend on translational invariance rather than on local connectivity?"
Worked example 4 English A — from a theme to an authorial choice
  1. Start: "Dystopia in modern literature." Three books' worth of scope; you have 4,000 words.
  2. Add one text: "Dystopia in Nineteen Eighty-Four." Better, still a survey of theme.
  3. Add a mechanism: "How does Orwell create the sense of surveillance?" Now we are close-reading — but the answer is a list of techniques.
  4. Add the comparison and consequence: "To what extent does the shifting narrative perspective in the final section of Nineteen Eighty-Four undermine the novel's earlier presentation of Winston's interiority?"

Why this works: it identifies one formal feature (perspective), one location in the text (the final section), and one consequence (a retroactive change in how we read the whole). Critics disagree about this; you can take a position and weigh theirs.

Final RQ: "To what extent does the shift to third-person narration in the closing chapter of Nineteen Eighty-Four require the reader to reassess Winston's earlier first-person interiority?"

Worked example 6 Interdisciplinary done properly — physics + computer science
  1. Start: "Simulating chaotic systems." Vague, and probably physics alone.
  2. Add the object: "Numerical integration of the three-body problem." Still physics alone — the CS is decorative.
  3. Find the genuine second lens: chaotic systems are deterministic but unpredictable in practice. Why unpredictable? Physics says: sensitivity to initial conditions. Computer science says: floating-point representation makes the trajectory diverge from the exact one regardless of step size. These are two different explanations, and they can be separated.
  4. Add the tension: "To what extent does the observed unpredictability of double-precision simulations of the three-body problem reflect physical sensitivity to initial conditions rather than the accumulation of floating-point representation error?"

Why this works: delete either subject and the question collapses. Physics alone cannot characterise IEEE-754 error accumulation; CS alone has no reason to care about the three-body problem. The integration is essential, and the answer is a genuine judgement about which effect dominates at which time scale.

Final RQ: "To what extent does trajectory divergence in double-precision three-body simulations reflect physical sensitivity to initial conditions rather than floating-point error accumulation, and how does that balance change with integration time?"
Worked example 7 Abandoning a question — machine learning in medicine
  1. Start: "How effective are AI systems at diagnosing disease?"
  2. Apply test 3 (accessible sources). The literature is enormous and mostly behind paywalls; the good studies use proprietary datasets you cannot see.
  3. Apply test 2 (arguable). "Effective" has no fixed meaning without a chosen baseline — better than a radiologist? better than no screening? The question smuggles in the answer.
  4. Apply the ethics filter. Anything involving patient data requires consent and approval you will not get in the time available.
  5. Diagnosis: fail. Do not try to rescue it by adding words.

The salvage: move the question to where the evidence is public and the claim is checkable — "To what extent do published claims of superhuman performance in deep-learning dermatology classifiers survive when the test set is restricted to images from institutions not represented in the training data?" Same interest, answerable, and now genuinely critical.

Lesson: abandoning a question in week 2 costs you nothing. Abandoning it in week 12 costs you the essay.
Worked example 8 The trap of the "investigation" — and its repair
  1. Start: "An investigation into the efficiency of sorting algorithms." Note the word investigation. It signals a report, not an argument.
  2. Test 4 (not a single fact). Asymptotic complexity is known: merge sort is \(O(n\log n)\), quicksort is \(O(n^{2})\) worst case. A lookup settles it.
  3. The repair — make the known answer insufficient: asymptotic complexity says nothing about the range of \(n\) you will actually encounter, cache behaviour, or constant factors. Now the question has somewhere to go.
  4. Add the argument: "To what extent does asymptotic time complexity predict the observed relative performance of quicksort, merge sort and Timsort on arrays of size \(10^{3}\) to \(10^{6}\) in a single language runtime, and where does that prediction break down?"

Why this works: the answer is no longer in the textbook — it depends on measurement, and the interesting part is where the theory stops predicting the practice. That is criterion D material.

Final RQ: "To what extent does asymptotic complexity predict the relative wall-clock performance of quicksort, merge sort and Timsort on integer arrays of size 10³–10⁶ in Python, and at what input sizes does the prediction fail?"
The three questions that should never reach a supervisor's desk
  1. "How does X work?" — an explanation, not an argument. Rewrite as "how far does X's mechanism explain Y?"
  2. "What are the effects of X?" — produces a list, and lists have no line of argument. Rewrite as "which of X's effects is most significant for Y, and why?"
  3. "Is X good or bad?" — a moral verdict dressed as research. Rewrite as "by what criterion should X be judged, and how does it perform against that criterion?"

Two tests that settle it

The "so what?" test
  1. Write your research question on paper.
  2. Under it, write: "This matters because …" and finish the sentence with a specific consequence for the subject — a model that turns out to have a narrower domain than assumed, a reading that changes how a passage is understood, a method that fails in a named circumstance.
  3. If the honest completion is "because it is interesting" or "because nobody has written about it", you do not yet have a reason. Find the thing that would be different if your answer came out the other way.
  4. If nothing would be different, the question is decorative. Ask a different one.
The "can I find sources in 20 minutes?" test
  1. Set a timer for twenty minutes.
  2. Search an academic database (not a general search engine) using the nouns from your question.
  3. Your target: at least five sources you could plausibly use — journal articles, book chapters, a standard textbook, primary texts, or datasets.
  4. Check that you can actually open at least three of them today. Paywalled abstracts are not sources.
  5. If you finish with two, the question is too narrow, too new, or locked behind access you do not have. Adjust now — not after you have written 1,500 words.

Do this for your top two candidate questions before you commit. Twenty minutes each; it is the highest-yield forty minutes in the whole process.
