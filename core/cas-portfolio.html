Reflection, portfolio and the interviews

CAS has no marks, no grade and no examiner. The only thing that exists at the end of it is your portfolio — and inside the portfolio, the only thing with real weight is your reflection. This page is about writing reflections that evidence a learning outcome instead of narrating an afternoon, about assembling a portfolio that a coordinator can audit in twenty minutes, and about the three interviews that bookend the whole eighteen months.

Minimum three interviews: two in DP1, one in DP2 Reflection is the primary evidence Owns: reflection · portfolio architecture · interviews · the seven-outcome audit Seven learning outcomes · 18 months · one project
Where this page stops

Three pages split CAS between them. CAS — the requirement owns what CAS is: the three strands, the seven learning outcomes, the 18-month rule and the myths (including the 150-hour one, which is not real). CAS — the project owns choosing, testing, designing and running the project, and the evidence it generates. This page owns the reflection and the portfolio: how to write a reflection that evidences an outcome, what goes into the portfolio and how to organise it, the three interviews, and the seven-outcome audit. Where the strands appear here, they appear only as far as a reflection needs to name one.

1. Why most portfolios fail

Almost every CAS portfolio that causes a problem fails in the same way, and it is not the way students expect. The student did the CAS. They turned up, they were useful, they even enjoyed it. What they produced at the end was a narration: a record of what happened, written in the past tense, in the order it happened, with feelings attached to the end of each paragraph.

A narration is not evidence. "We went to the centre, we read with the children, it went well, I enjoyed it" contains no information that would not also be true of a different student doing a different activity on a different afternoon. Swap the nouns and the paragraph still works — which is the quickest test there is for whether a reflection is doing any work. If you can change the activity and the sentence survives, the sentence is not about the activity.

The second failure is chronological. Reflections written in the last three weeks of DP2, about experiences from DP1, are not reconstructions of learning; they are reconstructions of what the student now wishes they had learned. The specific moment that would have made an entry real — the thing the child said, the session that went wrong, the week the student nearly quit — is gone by then, and what replaces it is a generalisation. A coordinator reading thirty entries all written in the same fortnight, in the same voice, at the same level of generality, knows exactly what happened.

Why reflection is the evidence at all

CAS is not graded, so there is nobody to convince except your coordinator, and the coordinator was not at any of your activities. The only window they have onto what you did and what it did to you is what you wrote. The seven learning outcomes are internal states — you recognised a weakness, you persevered, you noticed an ethical dimension — and internal states cannot be photographed. A certificate proves you were in the room. Only your reflection can prove you noticed anything while you were there.

This is why the guide says reflection should be purposeful and "about quality rather than quantity", and why it warns that setting a quota of reflections per experience turns reflection "into an obligation, which is contrary to the purpose". There is no required number of reflections. There is a required amount of evidence, and the evidence is the reflection.

Trap — the four portfolio profiles that get flagged
  1. The diary. Thirty entries, all level 1, all ending "it was a good experience". Volume is mistaken for substance. A coordinator can read the first three and predict the rest.
  2. The retrospective. Everything written in the final month. Dated entries written months after the event are visible as such, because the detail is missing and the tone is uniform.
  3. The certificate collection. Evidence with no reflection attached. Proves presence, not engagement, and evidences outcome 4 and almost nothing else.
  4. The hole. Genuine, well-reflected CAS with one outcome — usually outcome 3 or outcome 7 — that no activity ever produced. This is the most common real failure and the reason the audit in section 9 exists.

2. What a reflection actually is

A reflection is not a report and it is not a feelings diary. It is an argument, backed by a specific moment, that something changed in how you think or what you can do. Three parts, and all three are load-bearing:

PartWhat it doesWhat happens if it is missing
The specific One concrete, dated thing that actually happened — a sentence someone said, a number, a decision, a failure. Not "the sessions went well" but "in the fourth session he refused to read aloud at all". The entry becomes interchangeable with any other entry. Nothing can be checked, and nothing is believable.
The change What you now think, what you now do differently, what you tried next — or, honestly, what you still cannot do. You have described an event. There is no learning in it, so there is nothing to evidence.
The cost What it cost you: a weakness admitted, a habit suppressed, a comfortable belief given up, an awkward conversation had. The entry reads as self-congratulation. Outcomes 1, 6 and 7 are almost impossible to evidence without this.

The guide describes reflection as moving through four kinds of thinking. They are cumulative — each one contains the ones below it — and the value rises sharply as you go up.

2.1 Weak and strong, side by side

Same activity, same student, same afternoon. The difference between these two passages is not eloquence, and it is not length. It is what the writer is willing to admit.

Weak — evidences nothing

Today we went to the community centre and helped with the children's reading session. It went well and the children were very enthusiastic. I enjoyed the experience and felt that it was worthwhile. I think this is important for my CAS service strand and I look forward to continuing next week.

Why it fails. Level 1 with one stock feeling attached. Nothing specific happened, nothing was difficult, nothing changed, no question is left open. There is no sentence here that a different student at a different placement could not have written. It evidences no outcome: not outcome 1 (no weakness named), not outcome 2 (no challenge), not outcome 4 (no perseverance, because nothing resisted).

Strong — evidences three outcomes

Third session. He can decode accurately but does not understand what he has read, so I stopped asking for more pages and started asking him to retell. Two sentences today where last week he managed none — but I am no longer sure the improvement is mine. He has also been attending a school intervention I did not know about, and I cannot separate the two effects. I keep catching myself preferring the version where I am the cause.

What I actually find difficult is the silence. Waiting thirty seconds for him to find a word feels unbearable and I fill it, and I think filling it is the thing that stops him learning. I have started counting to ten instead. I do not know whether being useful here needs a skill I do not have, or needs me to stop doing something I am good at.

Why it works. A specific, checkable observation; an approach changed in response to evidence; a threat to the writer's own narrative ("I keep catching myself preferring the version where I am the cause"); a named weakness; and an open question at the end. It evidences outcome 1 (a specific area for growth: tolerating silence), outcome 2 (developing a new skill by suppressing an old one), and it reaches level 4.

Six prompts that reliably produce level 3 and 4 material
  • What did I expect to happen, and what actually happened?
  • What did I find difficult — and was the difficulty in the task or in me?
  • If I were starting again tomorrow, what would I change first?
  • What did someone else do here that I could not have done?
  • What question does this raise that I cannot answer yet?
  • What am I tempted to write because it sounds good rather than because it is true?

That last one is the most productive item on the list. The gap between the version you would show a university and the version you would tell a friend is exactly where the learning is, and it is the part students delete.

3. The reflection cycle: plan, do, reflect, demonstrate

Reflection is not something you do at the end of an experience. It brackets it. The cycle has four stages, and the two that students skip are the first and the last — which is unfortunate, because those are the two that produce outcome 3 and outcome 5 evidence.

How to use the cycle — what to write at each stage
  1. Plan (before session one). Write the plan and date it. Include the goal in one sentence, who is involved, what could go wrong, and what you will do if it does. This single document is your outcome 3 evidence, and it is impossible to produce retrospectively.
  2. Do (during). Keep a session log: one line per session, dated, with anything unexpected noted while it is still unexpected. Two minutes, immediately after.
  3. Reflect (at three points, not one). Once early, once at the mid-point when you still have time to change something, and once at the end. The mid-point reflection is usually the best in the portfolio because it is the only one that can still change anything.
  4. Demonstrate (after). What exists now that did not before, who saw it, and what they said. A reflection that ends with an artefact and a recipient's response evidences outcome 5 in a way no amount of introspection can.
Worked example 1 One experience run through all four stages

Context. A student runs a six-week lunchtime robotics club for Year 7 students with two friends. Here is what each stage of the cycle produces in the portfolio.

Stage 1 — Plan (dated 12 September, before session one). "Goal: six Year 7 students build something that moves, by week six. Who: me (mechanical), Priya (wiring), Tom (sessions and sign-ups). Risks: nobody turns up after week two; the kits are too hard; we run out of glue. If sign-ups fall below four, we switch from building to taking things apart and rebuilding. First session: 24 September." — Evidences outcome 3. The date is the whole point; nothing else in the portfolio can do this job.

Stage 2 — Do (session log, one line each). "24 Sep — 11 signed up, 9 came. Two could not use a screwdriver. 1 Oct — 7 came. Kit 3 missing a motor; improvised. 8 Oct — 6 came. Two pairs finished and were bored; no plan for finishers. 15 Oct — 6 came. Added a 'break it and fix it' station for finishers. 22 Oct — 6. 29 Oct — 6, parents invited, 4 came." — Evidences outcome 4. The attrition from 9 to 6 is visible, so the commitment is real rather than asserted.

Stage 3 — Reflect (mid-point, 9 October). "Attendance has settled at six, which is better than I feared and worse than I told the coordinator. The problem I did not plan for is the finishers: two pairs completed the build in week three and spent week four dismantling each other's work. I assumed the difficulty would be the kit. It is not — the difficulty is that I plan for the slowest pair and the fastest pair then has nothing to do. Tom thinks we should have built one robot as a group. I think he is right and I am resisting because I designed the pair system. That is not a good reason." — Evidences outcome 1 (a specific weakness, named without hedging), outcome 2 (unlearning a design), and reaches level 4 through the sentence about resisting.

Stage 4 — Demonstrate (5 November). "Six robots, one of which moved more than a metre. Four parents came. Mrs Adeyemi asked whether we would run it again next term, and I said yes before checking with Priya and Tom, which was a mistake — Tom is doing the DP2 mocks then. The thing I would keep is the break-it station; the thing I would drop is the pairs." — Evidences outcome 5 (the collaboration changed the design, and the writer names the moment they over-committed the group).

Why the four stages matter together: no single entry here evidences more than two outcomes. The set evidences five, because each stage produces a different kind of artefact — a dated plan, a contemporaneous log, a mid-point reversal, and a consequence. One long final essay could not do this.

4. A procedure for writing a reflection in fifteen minutes

The reason portfolios end up retrospective is that students imagine a reflection as an essay. It is not. It is a short, structured response to something that just happened, and it is much easier to write badly than to write at all — so here is a mechanical procedure. Use it within twenty-four hours of a session, while the detail is still available.

How to use it — the seven-step reflection
  1. Date it and name the session. "Session 4 of 12, 8 October." Two seconds, and it is the difference between evidence and retrospection.
  2. Write one sentence of fact. The most concrete thing that happened. Not "it went well" — a thing. "He refused to read aloud." "Only six came." "The motor burned out."
  3. Write the expectation you had. "I expected…" One sentence. Without an expectation you cannot have a surprise, and without a surprise you have nothing to think about.
  4. Name the gap. What was different between the two sentences above, and was the cause in the situation or in you? Be specific about which.
  5. Do one thing about it. Literally one — a change you will make before the next session. If you cannot name one, say so and say why.
  6. Admit one cost. The weakness, the temptation, the thing you avoided, the credit you took. This is the sentence that evidences outcomes 1 and 7, and it is the one students omit.
  7. Leave one question open. Something this raised that you cannot answer yet. An unfinished reflection is a live one; a closed one is a report.

Then stop. A reflection that takes more than fifteen minutes is usually being inflated. Two hundred words of the above is worth more than eight hundred words of narration.

Trap — the five sentences that ruin an otherwise good entry
  • "This was a very valuable experience." Says nothing; delete or replace with what specifically was valuable.
  • "I learned the importance of teamwork." A conclusion with no evidence. Name what the team produced that you could not have.
  • "It was humbling." The single most overused word in CAS portfolios. If you were humbled, say what you were wrong about.
  • "This will look good on my university application." True, often, and fatal to the tone of the entry. Keep it out of the portfolio.
  • "Everyone benefited." Unverifiable and usually untrue. Say who benefited and how you know.

5. The seven learning outcomes: what actually proves each one

The seven outcomes are listed in full on CAS — the requirement. This table is the practical companion to that list: for each outcome, what evidence would genuinely prove it, what weak evidence looks like, and a sentence stem that reliably produces the strong version. The stems are not templates to fill in — they are shapes that force you to supply the specific thing.

LOWhat proves itWhat weak evidence looks likeModel sentence stem
1
Strengths and areas for growth		 A named weakness, identified before the activity, and evidence of you working on it. The profile section is where the baseline lives; without it there is no "growth" to point at. "I am good at working with people and I need to improve my time management" — a generic strength and a generic weakness, neither connected to anything you did. "I came into this assuming X was a strength. Session 4 showed it was not, because…"
2
Challenges, new skills		 A difficulty you chose, and a skill that did not previously exist. Best evidenced when the skill is describable — you can now do something on Tuesday you could not do in September. Continuing something you were already good at for two years. Or "it was challenging" with no account of the challenge. "The part I could not do at the start was…; by week N I could…; the thing that moved me was…"
3
Initiate and plan		 A dated plan written before the activity started, showing decisions you made — goal, roles, risks, contingency. Almost nothing else evidences this. A retrospective description of an activity you joined, with "I planned" retro-fitted. Coordinators recognise this instantly: there is no artefact predating the activity. "Before session one I decided X rather than Y because…; the risk I named was…; what actually went wrong was…"
4
Commitment and perseverance		 A dated log across months, ideally with the difficult weeks visible. Perseverance that was never tested is not perseverance, so the week you nearly stopped is the most valuable entry. "I attended every session." Attendance is not commitment if nothing ever competed with it. "The week I nearly stopped was…; what decided it was…; I was there for N of M sessions and the gaps were…"
5
Collaboration		 A specific outcome that solo work would not have produced, plus an honest account of a disagreement or a dependency. Collaboration that was frictionless was probably parallel work. "We worked well together and divided the tasks." That is coordination, not collaboration, and it evidences nothing about the benefit. "What the group produced that I could not have was…; the disagreement was about…; we resolved it by…"

Best evidenced when you have engaged with the issue beyond the activity — reading, a speaker, data. "This relates to poverty, which is a global issue." One clause, no mechanism, no engagement. Worse than leaving it out. "The local version of this is…; the larger pattern it sits inside is…; what I changed my mind about was…"

"It is important to respect other people's dignity." A slogan. Outcome 7 requires a dilemma, not a value. "The decision I was uncomfortable with was…; the reason I made it anyway was…; what I would decide differently is…"
The one-sentence version

You do not need seven activities for seven outcomes. One well-chosen project can evidence five or six of them. What you need is a portfolio in which every one of the seven can be pointed to — somewhere, in something, with a reflection behind it. The most common failure is not a missing outcome; it is an outcome that is genuinely visible in what you did but was never written down.

Why this works Why the "dated before" test is the whole game
  1. Evidence is credible in proportion to how hard it is to produce after the fact. A dated plan written before session one cannot be faked without deliberate dishonesty; a summary written at the end is trivially easy to write whether or not the thing happened.
  2. A dated plan is also falsifiable in a way a summary is not: it commits you to a goal, a sequence and a risk. When the project diverges, the divergence is itself the evidence.
  3. That divergence is called adaptation, and it is the richest reflection material in CAS. A plan that matches reality exactly is a plan that was not ambitious enough.
  4. When this is wrong: if you have already started and have no plan, do not write a fake one. Write a dated "plan as of now" note, say plainly that it is late, and reflect on why it was late. That is honest evidence for outcome 1, which is more than a backdated document gives you.

6. Six fully worked reflections

Six reflections across creativity, activity, service and the project, each annotated to show which outcome it hits and why the specific sentences do the work. Read them for the mechanics, not the prose — none of them is elegant, and none of them needs to be.

Worked example 2 Creativity — learning to finish (LO1, LO2)

The reflection. "I have started about fourteen pieces of music and finished two, so the goal for this term was one finished arrangement, not a good one. Week five: I have a complete draft and it is competent and completely forgettable, and I can hear exactly where I copied the harmonic shape of the piece I was listening to that week. The part I keep avoiding is the middle eight — every time I get there I start a new document instead. I think I have been using 'starting' as a way of never being judged, because an unfinished piece is not bad, it is just unfinished. Finishing it means finding out."

Which outcomes, and which sentences.

  • Outcome 1 — "I have started about fourteen pieces and finished two" is a quantified, unflattering baseline. "I have been using 'starting' as a way of never being judged" names the weakness as a motive rather than a skill gap, which is the harder and better version.
  • Outcome 2 — the goal was deliberately reframed ("one finished arrangement, not a good one"), so the challenge is finishing rather than quality. That is a real challenge for this student and a fake one for most, which is why the specificity matters.

Why it works. It contains a number, a self-diagnosis the writer would rather not publish, and a consequence ("finishing it means finding out"). It also fails to solve the problem, which is realistic and fine — a portfolio where every reflection ends in triumph reads as fiction.

The transferable move: the writer redefined success so that the thing they were bad at became the thing being measured. That is the cheapest way to turn an existing activity into outcome 2 evidence.
Worked example 3 Activity — the week it nearly ended (LO1, LO4)

The reflection. "Week eleven of training, and the week I nearly stopped. Not because of the running — because on Tuesday I did the session and then sat in the car park for twenty minutes working out whether to tell the coach that my knee had been sore for three weeks. I did not tell her, because I thought she would tell me to rest and I did not want to lose the place in the relay squad. On Thursday I told her. She took me out of the relay. That was the correct decision and I resented it completely for about a week, and the part I am not proud of is that I had already decided to tell her before I knew what she would say, which makes the honesty a calculation rather than a virtue."

Which outcomes, and which sentences.

  • Outcome 4 — the perseverance here is not the eleven weeks; it is the decision to continue after the consequence. Naming the week you nearly stopped is worth more than logging the weeks you did not.
  • Outcome 1 — "which makes the honesty a calculation rather than a virtue" is an unusually sharp piece of self-knowledge, and it is the sentence that lifts this from level 2 to level 4.

Why it works. It refuses the obvious moral. The student did the right thing and then interrogates their own motive for doing it, which is more convincing than any amount of "I learned the importance of listening to my body".

The transferable move: outcomes 1 and 4 are both evidenced by the bad week. If your log has no bad week in it, either nothing went wrong in eighteen months or you did not write it down.
Worked example 4 Service — the food-waste question (LO6, LO7)

The reflection. "Sixth Saturday at the community kitchen. We served 94 meals today, which is our highest, and I have stopped feeling good about that number. Roughly a third of what we hand out is food that a supermarket could not sell because of the date label or the shape of the vegetable, and the kitchen exists because that food has nowhere else to go. So the service I am doing is, in part, disposing of a surplus that someone else created and was paid not to deal with. I raised this with the coordinator and she said, fairly, that the people eating today are not abstract and the meal is real. I do not think she is wrong. I also do not think 'the meal is real' answers the question of why the surplus exists. I do not know what the honest version of helping looks like here — whether it is serving on Saturday, or writing to the council about surplus redistribution, or both, and whether doing both is a way of feeling better rather than doing better."

Which outcomes, and which sentences.

  • Outcome 6 — the global significance is not asserted ("food poverty is a global issue") but reasoned: the writer identifies a mechanism (surplus created by date labelling and cosmetic standards) connecting a local Saturday to a systemic pattern. That mechanism is what makes it count.
  • Outcome 7 — the ethical dimension is genuine because the writer's own participation is implicated. "The service I am doing is, in part, disposing of a surplus" threatens the activity the portfolio depends on, which is exactly why it is credible.

Why it works. It holds two true things at once — the meals are real, and the system is wrong — without resolving them. A reflection that resolves every tension reads as though the writer already knew the answer.

The transferable move: outcome 7 evidence almost always has this shape: the student discovers that their own helpfulness is entangled with something they would otherwise criticise. Write that down rather than protecting it.
Worked example 5 Project — the initiation entry (LO3, LO5)

The reflection. "Plan written 3 March, sessions start 21 March. The idea was mine and the design is not: I wanted to run a revision clinic for Year 11 and Priya pointed out that a clinic assumes people know what they need help with, which the students who need it most do not. So we changed it to diagnostic-first — twenty minutes of questions, then groups formed by what the questions revealed. That is a better design and it is not mine, and I notice I have written 'we' for the first time in this portfolio. The risk I have named is that nobody comes; the risk I have not named until now is that Priya and I disagree in front of thirty Year 11 students."

Which outcomes, and which sentences.

  • Outcome 3 — the dated plan preceding the first session, the stated risk, and a decision with a reason attached. "The idea was mine and the design is not" is the crucial admission: initiation does not mean authorship of every detail.
  • Outcome 5 — the collaboration is evidence because it changed the design. "I notice I have written 'we' for the first time in this portfolio" is a small, precise observation about the writer's own practice, which is what makes it believable.

Why it works. It is short, it is dated, and it contains a design decision the student did not make. Most false outcome 3 evidence claims total authorship; real initiation usually looks like this.

The transferable move: outcome 3 does not require you to have thought of everything. It requires a dated artefact showing you decided something before anyone did it.
Worked example 6 Project — the disagreement (LO5, LO2)

The reflection. "Mid-point. Tom has done almost nothing for two weeks and Priya wants to confront him and I do not, and I have been avoiding the conversation by doing his tasks for him, which is the same thing as lying about it. My reason is that I hate conflict; my better reason is that if I confront him and he leaves, the clinic collapses with two weeks to go. So I am protecting the project and calling it protecting him. We talked on Thursday. It turned out his grandmother has been ill and he assumed we knew, and the thing that actually happened in that conversation is that he had been avoiding us for the same reason we were avoiding him. The clinic is now running with three of us and a shortened session, which is worse than the plan and better than the alternative."

Which outcomes, and which sentences.

  • Outcome 5 — the strongest collaboration evidence in most portfolios is a disagreement recorded before the outcome is known. Note that the reflection was written before Thursday: the writer commits to a judgement ("protecting the project and calling it protecting him") that the resolution then complicates.
  • Outcome 2 — the new skill is having the conversation, and the writer is specific that the obstacle was internal rather than situational.

Why it works. Both members were avoiding each other for symmetrical reasons, and only the student's honesty about their own motive makes that visible. A version written afterwards would have been about Tom.

The transferable move: write the reflection while you are still wrong about something. Entries written after the resolution is known are always cleaner and always less convincing.
Worked example 7 Project — the end, and what was not achieved (LO7, LO1)

The reflection. "Final session, 11 May. Thirty-one students across five weeks, which is more than we planned for and fewer than we hoped. The honest evaluation is mixed: the diagnostic-first design worked, and the thing that did not work is that we never found out whether it helped. We collected no attainment data because I decided in week one that asking for it would be too bureaucratic, and that decision means the entire project rests on students saying they found it useful. They did say that. I do not think it is nothing, and I do not think it is evidence. If I ran it again I would ask the head of Year 11 for the mock results at the start and accept the three emails it cost. Separately: we are not continuing it, and I told Mrs Adeyemi we would try to. I would rather write that down than quietly drop it."

Which outcomes, and which sentences.

  • Outcome 7 — the ethical content here is about evidence and honesty towards the recipient: claiming an impact you did not measure is an ethical failure, and the writer names their own reason for not measuring it.
  • Outcome 1 — "I decided in week one that asking for it would be too bureaucratic" identifies a specific, recurring weakness (avoiding administrative friction) rather than a generic one.

Why it works as a final reflection. It evaluates against the plan, refuses the available credit, and ends with an unfulfilled commitment stated plainly. Portfolio endings that claim total success are the ones coordinators read twice.

The transferable move: "we never found out whether it helped" is the most useful sentence in any final reflection. Admitting an unmeasured outcome is stronger than asserting a measured one you cannot support.

7. Portfolio architecture

The guide does not prescribe a format — schools choose, and most use some version of three parts. What matters is not which platform you use but whether a coordinator can find, for each of the seven outcomes, the place where it is evidenced.

PartWhat goes thereWhat it is forHow students get it wrong
Profile Who you are at the start: your starting point, what you think your strengths are, what you think your weak spots are, and what you intend to get out of CAS. This is the baseline. Without it there is no visible change, so outcome 1 has nothing to point at. Written as a CV — strengths only. A profile with no weaknesses in it makes every later claim of growth unsupportable.
Experiences Each activity and the project, with dates, your role, who else was involved, and your reflections attached to the sessions they belong to. The substantive part. Dated, contemporaneous entries are worth far more than a retrospective summary. Reflections stored in a separate document from the activity they describe, so the connection has to be reconstructed at the final interview.
Evidence Plans, logs, emails, feedback, certificates, photographs with consent, the artefact itself, supervisor comments. Corroboration. Evidence without reflection proves nothing; reflection without evidence is assertion. A folder of certificates and photographs with nothing dated and nothing written — the portfolio looks full and evidences nothing.

7.1 How much is enough

This is the question students ask most and the guide answers least, because the honest answer depends on the quality of what you write. Here is the practical version.

Per ongoing experience

One reflection roughly every three to four sessions, plus one when something goes wrong. For a weekly activity running a term, that is four to six entries — not twelve. Twelve entries on the same activity is a quota, and quotas produce narration.

Per CAS project

Four or five: a dated plan before session one, an early reflection, a mid-point reflection written while you can still change something, a final evaluation, and one written after a follow-up visit. The mid-point and the follow-up are the two students skip.

Length

Between 120 and 300 words. Shorter than 120 and there is usually no specific moment in it; longer than 300 and it is usually narration with adjectives. A 1,500-word final reflection is almost always a worse document than four short ones.

Total

Across eighteen months, a strong portfolio typically holds fifteen to thirty reflections, a session log, one dated project plan, and a handful of artefacts. Fewer than ten reflections is thin. More than fifty is a warning sign, not an achievement.

How to organise it so an audit takes twenty minutes
  1. One page or entry per experience, with the dates at the top, your role stated in one line, and the reflections underneath in date order.
  2. Tag or label each reflection with the outcomes it evidences. Two minutes per entry, and it turns the final interview into a lookup rather than a search.
  3. Keep a single index document — the seven-outcome audit from section 9 — with links or page references to the evidence for each outcome. Update it each term.
  4. Put the dated project plan somewhere obvious, not buried in a folder of emails. It is the single most valuable document in the portfolio and the one most likely to be mislaid.
  5. Keep the raw material. The session log, the emails, the unedited supervisor comment. Do not tidy them away; contemporaneous messiness is a feature.
Connection

The tagging habit is the same one the TOK exhibition depends on: one specific object, one explicit reason it is there. And the "admit what did not work" habit is precisely what the reflection criterion in the extended essay rewards — both are asking you to write about a decision you reversed.

8. The three CAS interviews

The guide requires a minimum of three documented interviews with your CAS coordinator or adviser: at least two in the first year and one in the second. Feedback is recorded. They are not an examination — nobody fails an interview — but they are the mechanism by which problems become visible while they are still cheap.

InterviewWhenWhat it coversWhat to bringWhat good looks like
1 · Initial Near the start of the programme Your starting point, what you already do, what you want from CAS, and where the gaps are — strands with nothing in them, and outcomes your current activities will never produce. A written profile, including the parts you are not good at. You leave knowing which strand is empty and which two outcomes your current plan cannot reach. A coordinator who only hears your strengths cannot direct you.
2 · Progress Usually around the end of DP1 Progress so far, and the CAS project — its plan, its collaborators, its timing, and whether it has started. The dated project plan, the seven-outcome audit, and whatever is currently going wrong. The project exists on paper with a start date. This is the last interview before the summer and the last point at which problems are cheap.
3 · Final Before the programme ends, in DP2 Summative: you outline how you achieved the seven learning outcomes, and the coordinator makes the completion decision. The completed audit, with a specific moment and a specific artefact for each of the seven. Every claim is verifiable in about two minutes. You can name your weakest outcome and say what you did about it.

8.1 Typical questions, and what they are really asking

QuestionWhat is being checkedA weak answerA strong answer
"Which was your most meaningful experience?" Whether any of it actually landed, and whether you can say why without generalising. "All of them were meaningful in different ways." Names one, says what changed, and adds the part that went badly.
"What would you do differently?" Whether you evaluated anything, or just attended. "Nothing, it all went well." One specific design decision, with the cost of having made it the other way.
"How do you know you achieved outcome X?" Whether the portfolio contains evidence or only claims. "I definitely did, it was a big part of the project." "It is in the mid-point reflection for 9 October; the artefact is the revised plan."
"Which outcome was hardest for you?" Honesty, and whether you have done the audit. This is not a trap. "None, I think I covered them all." Names the weakest one, says when they noticed, and what they did about it.
"What did you contribute that nobody else could have?" Whether the collaboration was real or parallel. "I did my share of the work." Names a specific thing only they could do, and a specific thing only someone else could.
"Did anything not work?" Whether the portfolio is honest. A portfolio with no failures is not credible. "No, everything worked out in the end." Names the failure and what it cost, without immediately recovering from it.
Worked example 8 A final-interview answer, weak and strong

Question. "How do you know you showed commitment and perseverance?"

Weak. "I demonstrated commitment by attending regularly throughout the two years. I went to nearly all the sessions and I think that shows perseverance because sometimes I had a lot of homework and I still went."

Why it is weak. No numbers, no dates, no artefact, and the difficulty is hypothetical ("sometimes I had a lot of homework"). The coordinator cannot check any part of it.

Strong. "Commitment is in the session log — 34 of 38 sessions across eighteen months, and the four I missed are explained in the entry for 12 November. The week I nearly stopped is the week of the mocks, and the entry there says what changed my mind, which was honestly that Priya would have had to run the session alone. If you want the perseverance evidence specifically, it is not the attendance, it is the mid-point entry where we lost a collaborator and redesigned the sessions rather than cancelling them."

Why it is strong. Numbers, a date, a pointer to a specific entry, an admission that the motive was not noble, and a distinction between two outcomes the question conflated. It can be verified in about ninety seconds.

The structure to reuse for all seven: one sentence stating the outcome → the specific moment → where the evidence sits in the portfolio → what you would still change. Four sentences, seven outcomes, and you are finished.

Why this matters: the final interview is a verification exercise. Make every claim verifiable and it becomes a formality; leave claims unverifiable and the coordinator has to go looking, which is when gaps get found.
What the final interview is not

It is not a viva and it is not a test you can fail on the day. It is a check that the portfolio supports the claim that you met the seven outcomes. The students who struggle are not the ones with weak CAS — they are the ones whose CAS was fine but was never written down. If you can point to evidence, the interview is short, and the completion decision is made on the portfolio rather than on your performance in the room.

9. The seven-outcome audit

Do this twice: once before the end-of-DP1 interview, and once about a month before the final one. Twenty minutes each time. Its entire purpose is to surface a hole at a point where something can still be done about it.

How to use it — the audit procedure
  1. Make a seven-row table with columns: outcome / where is the evidence / is there a reflection / is it dated / gap?
  2. Fill it from the portfolio, not from memory. For each outcome, write the filename or page and the date of the entry that evidences it. If you cannot write a location, the row is empty — do not write "the project" as a location.
  3. Apply the two-artefact rule. An outcome counts only if there is both a reflection and a supporting artefact (plan, log, email, feedback, photograph with consent). One without the other is a half-row.
  4. Mark each row honestly: strong, thin, or empty. Thin means the evidence exists but is generic.
  5. For every thin or empty row, name one concrete action with a date. Not "do more service" but "ask Mrs Adeyemi on Tuesday whether the kitchen needs help with the surplus survey".
  6. Take the audit to the interview. It is the most useful document you can hand a coordinator, and it demonstrates the outcome 1 habit directly.
Worked example 9 Diagnosing a partially complete portfolio

The portfolio. A student in February of DP2 with: two years of school orchestra (creativity), a weekly football training commitment (activity), eighteen months at a community kitchen (service), and a CAS project — a Year 11 revision clinic — run the previous summer. Twenty-two reflections, a session log, and a folder of certificates.

The audit.

OutcomeEvidence locationReflection?Verdict
1 Strengths / growthProfile (Sept DP1); clinic mid-point, 9 JulYesStrong — baseline exists, so growth is visible.
2 Challenge, new skillsOrchestra entries, Oct & FebYesThin — both entries are about repertoire difficulty, not a new skill.
3 Initiate and planClinic plan, dated 3 Mar, before first session 21 MarYesStrong — the only dated pre-activity document, and it is enough.
4 CommitmentSession logs, all three activitiesPartlyStrong on evidence, thin on reflection — no entry names a week they nearly stopped.
5 CollaborationClinic entries, 3 Mar & 21 JulYesStrong — a live disagreement recorded in the mid-point entry.
6 Global significanceKitchen entry, 14 OctYesAdequate — one entry, but it reasons about a mechanism.

The diagnosis. Six of seven outcomes are pointed to. Outcome 7 is empty, and the reason is structural: every activity this student chose is one where they are unambiguously helpful. Orchestra, football and a revision clinic raise no ethical question for the person doing them. The kitchen raises one — the surplus food issue is visible in the 14 October entry — but the student wrote about it under outcome 6 and never returned to the question of their own participation in it.

What can still be done in February. Three options, in ascending order of effort:

  1. Write the reflection that already exists in their head. The 14 October entry is one paragraph away from outcome 7: what does it mean that the service they do disposes of a surplus someone else created? A follow-up entry, honestly dated as February, is legitimate evidence — it is a reflection on an experience that happened, not a fabrication.
  2. Ask the kitchen coordinator one direct question and reflect on the answer: what does the kitchen think about the surplus arrangement? A recorded conversation is a real artefact and takes a fortnight to arrange.
  3. Do the thing they identified — write to the council about surplus redistribution, or find out why the supermarket does not donate directly. Six weeks is enough for the attempt, and an attempt that fails is still outcome 7 evidence.

What must not be done. Rewriting the October entry to add ethical content that was not thought in October. That is fabrication in a portfolio whose entire purpose is honest self-reporting, and it is detectable because the entry's voice will not match the rest.

The lesson: this hole was visible in DP1 with a summer of lead time. The audit costs twenty minutes; discovering the same hole at the final interview costs the diploma.
The two fixes that do not work
  1. Retrofitting. Adding "I initiated this" to an activity you joined, or "I considered the ethics" to an experience where no ethical question arose. Both are false statements, and both are visible, because the rest of the entry is written in a different register.
  2. Bolting outcome 6 on. Appending "this relates to global poverty" to a local activity with no mechanism connecting them. Coordinators read dozens of these. If the genuine connection is thin, say so — "the global connection here is weak, and here is what I think it would take to make it real" is better evidence than a claim you cannot support.

10. Ethics, safeguarding and honesty in the portfolio

CAS is the only part of the diploma where you routinely interact with people outside the school, sometimes vulnerable people, largely unsupervised. That makes the portfolio an ethical document as well as an academic one.

Consent for photographs

Every identifiable person in every photograph needs consent, and for under-18s that means the organisation's or the parent's consent, not the child's smile. A photograph without consent should not be in the portfolio at all. The safer default: photograph hands, materials and the room, not faces. If your portfolio's evidence of service is a picture of a child you do not know, the ethical content of that is outcome 7 material whether or not you write about it.

Fabricated evidence

Inventing a session, a supervisor comment or a plan is academic misconduct and, unlike most misconduct, it is aimed at a document whose only purpose is to be true. The practical risk profile is bad too: a fabricated dated plan is the one document a coordinator is most likely to check, because it is the one that evidences outcome 3.

Plagiarism in reflection

Reflections are short and personal, which makes copied or AI-generated text obvious — the voice changes, the specifics disappear, and the weaknesses get described in generic terms. A reflection with no specific moment in it fails on its own terms before anyone checks where it came from.

Safeguarding and supervision

Work with under-18s or vulnerable adults happens under the host organisation's safeguarding arrangements, not yours. Do not be alone with a child in an unsupervised setting, do not give out personal contact details, and do not arrange sessions outside the placement. If something worries you, the route is your CAS coordinator and the organisation's safeguarding lead — not a reflection in the portfolio.

Anonymity in the writing

Use first names or initials for the people you work with, never full names, and do not include medical, immigration or family details that could identify someone even without a name. This matters because the portfolio is read by people outside the placement. "The boy I read with" is fine; "J., who is in foster care and has dyslexia" is not, however much detail it adds.

Why this is also a marks issue elsewhere

The honesty standard here is the same one the IB applies to the extended essay and to every internal assessment: the work must be yours and the account of it must be true. Students who get into the habit of writing the honest version in CAS — including the parts that go badly — write better reflections in the EE, because they have already learned that an admission costs nothing and a fabrication costs everything.

11. Failure modes and fixes

SymptomReal causeFix
Every entry ends "it was a really valuable experience". Reflecting on the activity as a whole rather than on a moment in it. Force one proper noun and one number into each entry: who, what, how many. Abstraction becomes impossible.
Twelve entries on one activity, none of them saying anything. A quota — either the school's or one you invented. Delete nothing, but write one more entry saying what the twelve failed to say, and stop writing until something happens.
The project has no plan dated before it started. The project was an experience relabelled afterwards, or genuinely was not planned. If it was not planned, write a dated "plan as of now" note and reflect honestly on the lateness. Better outcome 1 evidence than a backdated document.
No reflection mentions anything going wrong. Either nothing went wrong, or failures feel like admissions of failure. Write the worst session up first. It is nearly always the most useful entry, and it is the one that evidences outcomes 1, 4 and 7 at once.
The portfolio is mostly certificates and photographs. Evidence collected instead of engagement recorded. Rank your evidence by how hard it would be to produce retrospectively, and lead with those: the dated plan and the session log.
Outcome 6 is a single sentence bolted onto a local activity. The activity genuinely has no global dimension. Do something small that does — one article, one talk, one dataset — and write about what it changed. Or state plainly that the connection is thin and why.
Reflections all written in the same fortnight. Left to the end. Write within twenty-four hours of each session from now on, even one line. Contemporaneous fragments beat retrospective essays, and you can expand the good ones later.

12. A reflection timeline across the eighteen months

Reflection is not evenly distributed. Three moments in the eighteen months produce most of the useful material, and they are all early enough to act on.

Before DP1
Initial interview and the profile. Write the profile honestly, including the weaknesses — this is the baseline every later outcome 1 claim depends on. Set up the portfolio structure and start the session log habit before you have anything to log.
DP1 autumn
One reflection per activity, early. These are usually weak, and that is fine: they establish the baseline and the voice. Start the supervisor conversations — ask what doing this well looks like from their side, and what people usually get wrong.
DP1 spring
Write the dated project plan before the first session, not after. Then the second interview: bring the plan and the first seven-outcome audit. This is the single highest-value twenty minutes in the whole programme.
DP1 summer
The project runs — reflect during it, not after. Early entry, mid-point entry written while you can still change something, final evaluation. Ask for specific supervisor feedback on one thing you tried to improve.
DP2 autumn
Second audit, and fix the holes. Anything empty now still has a term to be filled. Write the follow-up reflection — is anyone still using what you made? That one visit produces more honest reflection than the whole final evaluation.
DP2 winter / spring
Final interview. Seven short answers, each pointing to a moment and an artefact. Tag every reflection with its outcomes. Nothing new started after this point.
The supervisor, used properly

Most students treat the supervisor as a signature. Instead ask two questions early: what does doing this well look like from your side? and what do people usually get wrong here? Both answers are reflection material and both make you better at the activity. Near the end, ask for specific feedback on one thing you tried to improve — a generic "he was very helpful" is worth little, but "she redesigned the sessions after the second week and attendance went up" is real evidence you can point at.

13. Practice

Each of these is a judgement you will actually have to make. Answer on paper before opening the answers.

  1. Diagnose the reflection. Three entries. For each, say which level it reaches, which outcome it could evidence, and what one sentence would lift it a level.

    • (a) "We trained for two hours. It was tiring but good preparation for the match on Saturday."
    • (b) "I have now attended every session for eight weeks. I have not missed one even when I had homework."
    • (c) "The woman running the centre thanked us for coming, which felt good, and I think it is valuable to give back to the community."
    Show the answer

    (a) Level 1–2. Description plus a stock feeling. Could evidence outcome 4 only weakly, and only as part of a longer pattern. Lifting sentence: "I have been avoiding the drills that expose my weaker foot, so I asked to be put on the left this week and it went badly — that is the specific thing I need to keep doing." Now it evidences outcomes 1 and 2.

    (b) Level 1 with a claim. Perfect attendance is evidence of commitment, so outcome 4 is genuinely supported — but there is no reflection, only an assertion. Lifting sentence: name the week you almost did not go and what decided it. Perseverance that was never tested is not perseverance.

    (c) Level 2, and the weakest of the three ethically. "Give back to the community" and the thank-you as the emotional payoff are exactly the posture outcome 7 asks you to examine: the service is framed as something the writer bestows. Lifting sentence: "I am not certain we were useful — the centre reorganised the room after we left, and I cannot tell whether that means we helped or got in the way." That one sentence turns a self-congratulatory entry into genuine outcome 7 evidence.

    Why this is worth knowing: every lift comes from naming something specific that went against the writer — a weakness, a temptation, a doubt. Reflections improve when the writer stops protecting their own narrative.

  2. Evidence or not? Rank these five from strongest to weakest and say why: a certificate of participation; a dated plan you wrote before the project started; a supervisor's generic letter; a session log with dates and one line each; a photograph of you at the activity.

    Show the answer
    1. The dated plan. Contemporaneous, yours, and the only item that evidences outcome 3. Nothing else on the list can be backdated without dishonesty.
    2. The session log. Contemporaneous and dated, which is what makes it credible — one line per session beats a polished retrospective essay.
    3. The certificate. Proves attendance: real but thin, evidencing outcome 4 and little else.
    4. The generic supervisor letter. Confirms you turned up and were pleasant. Almost no outcome content unless it names something specific you changed.
    5. The photograph. Weakest, and the only one with an ethical condition attached. It proves presence, not engagement, and without consent it should not be in the portfolio at all.
    Why this is worth knowing: evidence is strong in proportion to how hard it is to produce retrospectively. A portfolio of certificates and photographs looks like participation; the dated plan and the log are what make it look like a project.

  3. The gap you cannot backfill. It is March of DP2. You have a full record of activity and service, plenty of evidence, and your audit shows outcome 3 (initiate and plan) is empty — everything you did was something you joined. What is the honest position, and what can still be done?

    Show the answer

    You cannot manufacture outcome 3 retrospectively. Writing "I planned this" about an activity you joined, months after the fact, is a false statement in a portfolio whose entire purpose is honest self-reporting. Do not do it, and do not dress up a minor logistical task as initiation.

    What can still be done: start something small, now, and let it be small. A three-session initiative you genuinely designed is real evidence; a rewritten history is not. Examples that fit in six weeks: a single revision clinic for younger students on a topic you know well; a short lunchtime club run with two other people; one fundraising or awareness event for a cause you are already involved with.

    Then be straight about it at the final interview. "Outcome 3 was the gap; here is when I noticed and what I did about it in March" is a far stronger position than a portfolio claiming everything was fine. The coordinator is looking for evidence of the outcomes, not for a flawless record.

    Why this is worth knowing: the audit exists precisely so that this problem surfaces when it is cheap to fix. Doing it once, late, costs you the diploma; doing it twice costs forty minutes.

  4. Rewrite it. Here is a level 1 entry: "Today we painted the mural. It took about three hours and it looks good. Everyone worked well together and I think the school will like it." Rewrite it so that it evidences outcomes 5 and 7, in under 120 words.

    Show the answer

    "Day two on the mural. Wei and I disagreed about the face — he wanted it accurate, I wanted it to read from the far end of the corridor, and we spent forty minutes going nowhere until he sketched both and we held them up from thirty metres. He was right about accuracy and I was right about distance, and the version we painted is a compromise neither of us would have chosen alone, which I think is the argument for not doing this by myself.

    The part I am less comfortable with: the mural is on a wall in a corridor the art department chose, and nobody asked the students who walk past it. We are painting what we think the school should look like. I do not know whether that is decoration or imposition, and I notice I would rather not ask now that it is half finished."

    Outcome 5 is evidenced by the specific disagreement and the artefact it produced (two sketches, held up). Outcome 7 by the imposition question, which implicates the writer rather than somebody else — and by the admission that they would rather not ask.

    Why this is worth knowing: the rewrite did not add facts about the activity. It added a disagreement and a doubt, both of which were almost certainly already true of the original afternoon.

  5. Audit a claim. A student says: "My portfolio has 40 reflections over 18 months, so I have plenty of evidence." Give two reasons why the number alone tells you almost nothing, and state one question that would tell you more.

    Show the answer
    1. Level matters more than count. Forty level 1 entries evidence less than six entries that reach level 3 or 4, because outcomes 1, 6 and 7 cannot be evidenced by description alone.
    2. Distribution matters more than count. Forty entries all written in the final month are a retrospective reconstruction; ten spread across eighteen months, written within a day of each session, are evidence. The guide's warning about quotas exists for exactly this reason — reflection is supposed to be about quality rather than quantity.
    3. The question that tells you more: "For each of the seven outcomes, which entry and which artefact?" A student who can answer that in thirty seconds has a portfolio; one who has to search does not, whatever the count.
    Why this is worth knowing: this is the difference between the audit and a word count. The audit is a lookup test, not a volume test.

  6. Ethics in the portfolio. Your service placement involves children, and your best photograph shows a child's face clearly. You did not get consent. Explain the issue and state what you should do with the photograph and with the reflection.

    Show the answer
    1. The issue. An identifiable image of a minor, published in a portfolio read by people outside the placement, without consent from the organisation or a guardian. Safeguarding policies at most placements forbid it outright, and "it is only for CAS" is not a consent process.
    2. The photograph. Do not use it. Either remove it, or crop to hands and materials, or obtain written consent retrospectively through the placement's safeguarding lead — and if consent is not given, remove it regardless of how good the picture is.
    3. The reflection. Keep the content but anonymise it: an initial rather than a name, no family or medical detail. The experience is still evidenced; the identification risk is removed.
    4. The useful part. The fact that the best photograph is the one you cannot use is itself genuine outcome 7 material — it raises the question of who the photographs are for. Write that down rather than deleting the incident.
    Why this is worth knowing: consent is not a formality that slows the portfolio down. Photographs of people you do not know are the most common ethical failure in CAS portfolios, and the easiest to avoid.

  7. Prepare the final interview. State the four-sentence structure you would use for each of the seven outcomes, and explain why naming your weakest outcome is a strength rather than a risk.

    Show the answer

    Structure: one sentence stating the outcome → the specific moment that evidences it → where the evidence sits in the portfolio (entry date plus artefact) → what you would still change. Four sentences, seven outcomes.

    Why naming the weakest outcome is a strength. The final interview is a verification exercise, not a performance. A student who says "outcome 6 was thin, here is what I read and what I changed my mind about" demonstrates outcome 1 (an honest picture of themselves) in the room, using the weakest part of their portfolio. A student who claims all seven are strong invites the coordinator to look harder, and the coordinator will find the thin one anyway — at which point the claim, not the gap, is the problem.

    And the gap itself is survivable if it was addressed. What is not survivable is a gap discovered by the coordinator in the final interview with three weeks left, because by then nothing real can be done about it.

    Why this is worth knowing: coordinators are checking whether the portfolio supports a claim. Making every claim checkable, including the uncomfortable ones, is what makes the interview short.

  8. Plan versus experience. A student writes a detailed CAS project plan in September but the project does not start until the following June, and by then the plan is obsolete. Does the plan still count as outcome 3 evidence? Explain.

    Show the answer

    Yes — and the obsoleteness helps rather than hurts. Outcome 3 requires that you initiated and planned a CAS experience; a dated plan written before the activity is precisely that, and the guide's minimum one-month requirement runs from planning to completion, so a long planning gap is contemplated by the requirement rather than excluded by it.

    The reflection is what makes it strong. An unexecuted plan left unexplained looks like procrastination. The same plan accompanied by "the reason this took nine months is that the first two organisations did not reply and I did not chase them for six weeks" is outcome 1 evidence, and the gap between the September plan and the June reality produces the adaptation material that makes the project's reflections worth reading.

    What you must not do is silently replace the September plan with a June one and present it as the original. Keep both, dated, and write about the difference. Two plans are better evidence than one, and a portfolio that shows a plan being revised looks like a project; one that shows a single suspiciously tidy plan looks like a reconstruction.

    Why this is worth knowing: the dated-artefact test is about honesty and order, not about the plan being right. Plans that change are the norm, and the change is the evidence.
Vocabulary
reflection — written thinking about an experience; in CAS it is the primary evidence for the seven outcomes.
portfolio — the collection of your profile, experiences and evidence; the only record of your CAS.
profile — the opening part of the portfolio: your starting point, strengths and areas for growth.
CAS experience — a single, usually sustained activity in one of the three strands.
CAS project — a collaborative, well-considered series of sequential experiences, at least one month from planning to completion.
learning outcome — one of the seven things your CAS must evidence.
evidence — anything that corroborates a reflection: a plan, a log, an email, feedback, an artefact.
artefact — something you made that did not exist before.
contemporaneous — written at the time, while the detail was still available.
retrospective — written afterwards, from memory; weaker and less credible.
self-review — your own assessment of your progress, usually against the seven outcomes.
audit — a written check that each of the seven outcomes has evidence somewhere in the portfolio.
initial interview — the first of at least three interviews with your CAS coordinator, near the start.
progress interview — the interview around the end of DP1, in which the project is discussed.
final / summative interview — the last interview, in DP2, where you outline how you achieved the seven outcomes.
supervisor — someone connected with the activity who guides you, is responsible for safety, and can comment on your engagement.
consent — permission required before photographing or recording an identifiable person.
anonymise — remove names and identifying details from what you write.
perseverance — continuing when continuing is difficult; evidenced by the week you nearly stopped, not by attendance.
reciprocity — a relationship in which benefit flows both ways; the standard real service is held to.
dignity — the worth of a person that service must not diminish, including by being photographed.
autonomy — the right of the people you work with to make their own decisions, including about your help.
global significance — an issue beyond the school gate: environment, inequality, public health, migration, technology and society.
quota — a required number of reflections; the guide warns against them.
adaptation — changing the plan in response to what happened; the richest reflection material in CAS.
