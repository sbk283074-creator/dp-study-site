Reflection, portfolio and the interviews

CAS has no marks, so the only thing that exists at the end is your portfolio — and inside it, the only thing with real weight is your reflection. This page is about writing reflections that evidence a learning outcome instead of narrating an afternoon, and about the three interviews that bookend the whole thing.

Minimum 3 interviews Reflection is the primary evidence

What the portfolio is

The guide does not prescribe a format — schools choose, and most use some version of three parts:

PartWhat goes thereWhat it is for
Profile Who you are at the start: your starting point, what you think your strengths and weak spots are, what you intend to get out of CAS. This is what makes the growth visible later. A portfolio with no baseline cannot show change.
Experiences Each activity and the project, with dates, your role, and your reflections. The substantive part. Dated, contemporaneous entries are worth far more than a retrospective summary.
Evidence Plans, logs, emails, feedback, certificates, photographs with consent, the artefact. Corroboration. Evidence without reflection proves nothing; reflection without evidence is assertion.
Quality, not quantity

The guide is unusually direct about this: reflection should be purposeful, "about quality rather than quantity", and setting a quota of reflections per activity turns it "into an obligation, which is contrary to the purpose". You are not required to reflect on every single experience. You are required to have evidence, somewhere, for all seven outcomes.

The practical consequence: reflect when something happens that is worth reflecting on — a plan that failed, a person who surprised you, a decision you got wrong — and do not write filler entries to hit a number. Three genuine reflections beat thirty dutiful ones, and coordinators can tell the difference instantly.

What a strong reflection does

The guide describes reflection as moving through four things. In order of increasing value:

The single most common failure

A portfolio full of level-1 entries — "today we did X, it went well, I enjoyed it" — written retrospectively in the last month. This evidences almost nothing. Retrospective reflection is worse than sparse reflection, because you have forgotten the specific moments that would have made it real, and because a dated entry written months after the event is visible as such.

The fix is mechanical: five minutes after each session, while the detail is still in your head. Not an essay — a few lines capturing what actually happened and one thing you did not expect.

Three rewrites

Here is the same material written badly, adequately, and well. The difference is not eloquence; it is what the writer is willing to say.

Weak

Today we went to the community centre and helped with the children's reading session. It went well and the children were very enthusiastic. I enjoyed the experience and felt that it was worthwhile. I think this is important for my CAS service strand and I look forward to continuing next week.

Why it fails: pure level 1 with a stock feeling attached. Nothing specific, no difficulty, no change, no question. This entry would look identical if the writer had done something completely different.

Adequate

Third session. The boy I have been paired with can decode words accurately but does not understand what he has read, so I have been asking him to retell the story in his own words instead of reading more pages. Today he managed two sentences of retelling where last week he managed none. I was surprised how hard it is to resist just reading the page to him — it is faster, and I had to stop myself three times.

Why it passes: specific, contains an actual observation about the learner, names a real difficulty the writer had, and shows the approach changing in response to evidence. It evidences outcomes 2 and 4. It stops just short of level 4.

Strong

Third session. He can decode but not comprehend, so I switched from more pages to retelling. He got two sentences today where last week he got none — but I am no longer sure the improvement is mine. He has also been attending a school intervention I did not know about, and I cannot separate the two effects. I keep catching myself preferring the version where I am the cause.

What I actually find difficult is the silence. Waiting thirty seconds for him to find a word feels unbearable and I fill it, and I think filling it is the thing that stops him learning to do it. I have started counting to ten in my head instead. I do not know whether being useful here requires a skill I do not have, or whether it requires me to stop doing something I am good at. I am not sure which is worse.

Why it is strong: it contains an honest threat to the writer's own narrative ("I keep catching myself preferring the version where I am the cause"), a specific behavioural change, and an open question. It evidences outcome 1 (a named weakness), outcome 2 (developing a new skill by suppressing an old one), and it is unmistakably written by someone who was there.

How to prompt yourself

When you are staring at a blank entry, these questions reliably produce level 3 and 4 material:

  • What did I expect to happen, and what actually happened?
  • What did I find difficult, and was the difficulty the task or me?
  • If I had to do it again starting tomorrow, what would I change first?
  • What did someone else do that I could not have done?
  • What question does this raise that I cannot answer yet?
  • What am I tempted to write here because it sounds good, rather than because it is true?

That last one is the most productive question on the list.

Supervisors

A supervisor is someone connected with the activity who guides you, is responsible for safety, encourages reflection, and can comment on your engagement if asked. You will usually have one per activity or per placement.

Getting value out of a supervisor

Most students treat the supervisor as a signature. Instead, ask them two questions early: what does doing this well look like from your side? and what do people usually get wrong here? Both answers are reflection material, and both make you noticeably better at the activity. Then, near the end, ask for specific feedback on one thing you tried to improve — a generic "he was very helpful" comment is worth little, but "she redesigned the sessions after the second week and attendance went up" is real evidence.

The three interviews

The guide requires a minimum of three interviews with your CAS coordinator or adviser: at least two in the first year and one in the second. They are documented, and feedback is recorded.

InterviewWhenWhat it coversHow to prepare
1 · Initial Near the start of the programme Your starting point, what you already do, what you want from CAS, and where the gaps are. Write your profile first — honestly, including what you are bad at. A coordinator who knows your weak spots can direct you; one who only hears your strengths cannot.
2 · Progress Usually around the end of DP1 Progress so far, and the CAS project — its plan, its collaborators, its timing. Bring the plan. Bring the seven-outcome audit. Bring whatever is currently going wrong, because this is the last interview before the summer and the last point at which problems are cheap.
3 · Final Before the programme ends Summative: you outline how you achieved the seven learning outcomes. Prepare the seven outcomes as seven short answers, each pointing to a specific moment and a specific piece of evidence. Walk in able to say, for each one, "here is where".
What the final interview is not

It is not a viva and it is not a test you can fail on the day. It is a check that the portfolio supports the claim that you met the seven outcomes. The students who struggle are not the ones with weak CAS — they are the ones whose CAS was fine but was never written down. If you can point to evidence, the interview is short.

The seven-outcome audit

Do this twice: once before the end-of-DP1 interview, and once a month before the final one. Make a seven-row table and fill it in.

OutcomeWhere is the evidence?Is the reflection there?Gap?
1 · Strengths and areas for growth
2 · Challenges, new skills
3 · Initiate and plan
4 · Commitment and perseverance
5 · Collaboration
6 · Global significance
7 · Ethics

Outcomes 3, 6 and 7 are the three that most often come up empty, because ordinary school activities do not produce them by accident. If one is blank in DP1, you have time to do something real about it. If it is blank in DP2, you do not.

Practice

  1. Diagnose the reflection. Here are three entries. For each, say which level it reaches, which outcome it could evidence, and what one sentence would be needed to lift it a level.

    • (a) "We trained for two hours. It was tiring but good preparation for the match on Saturday."
    • (b) "I have now attended every session for eight weeks. I have not missed one even when I had homework."
    • (c) "The woman running the centre thanked us for coming, which felt good, and I think it is valuable to give back to the community."
    Show the answer

    (a) Level 1–2. Description plus a stock feeling. Could evidence outcome 4 only weakly, and only as part of a longer pattern. Lifting sentence: "I have been avoiding the drills that expose my weaker foot, so I asked to be put on the left this week and it went badly — that is the specific thing I need to keep doing." Now it evidences outcome 1 and 2.

    (b) Level 1 with a claim. Perfect attendance is evidence of commitment, so outcome 4 is genuinely supported — but there is no reflection, only an assertion. Lifting sentence: name the week you almost did not go and what decided it. Perseverance that was never tested is not perseverance.

    (c) Level 2, and the weakest of the three ethically. "Give back to the community" and the thank-you being the emotional payoff are exactly the posture outcome 7 asks you to examine. The service is framed as something the writer bestows. Lifting sentence: "I am not certain we were useful — the centre reorganised the room after we left, and I cannot tell whether that means we helped or got in the way." That one sentence turns a self-congratulatory entry into a genuine outcome 7 reflection.

    The pattern: every lift comes from naming something specific that went against the writer — a weakness, a temptation, a doubt. Reflections improve when the writer stops protecting their own narrative.

  2. The gap you cannot backfill. It is March of DP2. You have a full record of activity and service, plenty of evidence, and your audit shows outcome 3 (initiate and plan) is empty — everything you did was something you joined. What is the honest position, and what can still be done?

    Show the answer

    The honest position: you cannot manufacture outcome 3 retrospectively. Writing "I planned this" about an activity you joined, four months after the fact, is a false statement in a portfolio whose entire purpose is honest self-reporting. Do not do it, and do not dress up a minor logistical task as initiation.

    What can still be done: start something small, now, and let it be small. A three-session initiative you genuinely designed is real evidence; a rewritten history is not. Examples that fit in six weeks: organise a single revision clinic for younger students on a topic you know well; set up and run a short lunchtime club with two other people; design and run one fundraising or awareness event for a cause you are already involved with.

    Then be straight about it at the final interview. "Outcome 3 was the gap; here is what I noticed and what I did about it in March" is a far stronger position than a portfolio that claims everything was fine. Coordinators are looking for evidence of the outcomes, not for a flawless record.

    And note the lesson for the audit: this gap was visible in DP1, when a summer of lead time was available. The cost of the audit being done twice is twenty minutes; the cost of it being done once, late, is this.

    Why this matters: the audit exists precisely so that this problem surfaces at a point where it is cheap to fix.

  3. Prepare the final interview. Write the seven answers you would give — one short paragraph each, each naming a specific moment and a specific piece of evidence.

    Show what "good" looks like

    The test for each answer is whether it contains a specific, checkable moment rather than a general claim. Compare:

    Weak: "I demonstrated commitment by attending regularly throughout the two years."

    Strong: "Commitment is in the session log — 34 of 38 sessions across eighteen months, and the four I missed are explained in the entry for 12 November. The week I nearly stopped is the week of the mock exams, and the entry there says what changed my mind."

    The second answer points at artefacts, admits the gap, and identifies the moment when the outcome was actually at risk. That is what makes an interview short: the coordinator can verify every claim in about two minutes.

    Structure to use for each of the seven: one sentence stating the outcome → the specific moment → where the evidence sits in the portfolio → what you would still change. Four sentences each, seven outcomes, and you are done.

    Why this matters: the final interview is a verification exercise. Make everything verifiable and it becomes a formality.

  4. Evidence or not? Rank these five pieces of evidence from strongest to weakest, and say why: a certificate of participation; a dated plan you wrote before the project started; a supervisor's generic letter; a session log with dates and one line each; a photograph of you at the activity.

    Show the answer
    1. The dated plan. It is contemporaneous, it is yours, and it is the only item on this list that evidences outcome 3. Nothing else can be backdated.
    2. The session log. Contemporaneous and dated, which is what makes it credible — even one line per session beats a polished retrospective essay.
    3. The certificate. It proves attendance, which is real but thin: it evidences outcome 4 and little else.
    4. The generic supervisor letter. Confirms you turned up and were pleasant. Almost no outcome content unless it names something specific you changed.
    5. The photograph. Weakest, and the only one with an ethical condition attached. It proves presence, not engagement, and without consent it should not be in the portfolio at all.

    The general principle: evidence is strong in proportion to how hard it is to produce retrospectively. Plans, logs and dated reflections are hard to fake after the fact; certificates and photographs are easy to collect without ever having engaged.

    Why this matters: if your portfolio is mostly certificates and photos, it looks like participation. The dated plan and the log are what make it look like a project.
