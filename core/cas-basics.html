DP core · Creativity, activity, service

CAS: the requirement, in full

CAS is the one part of the diploma with no grade attached — and the one part that can stop you being awarded a diploma at all, no matter how many points you score. It asks you to do something in the world for eighteen months and to be able to show what it did to you. This page sets out the requirement precisely: the three strands, the seven learning outcomes, the eighteen-month commitment, the myths, how to choose and sustain experiences, and how a school actually signs you off. Most of what circulates about CAS is either out of date or invented, so every claim here is stated in the form the guide states it.

Not formally assessed Completion is a diploma condition 3 strands · 7 outcomes · ≥18 months · 1 project · 3 interviews Effort: 2–4 hours a week, every week
Which guide applies to you

CAS runs on the Creativity, activity, service guide (for students graduating in 2017 and after). It was not changed in the same review round that rewrote the extended essay for first assessment 2027, and no new CAS guide has been announced for 2028 or 2029. So the requirement below is the one you will be held to. Confirm the exact portfolio platform, the interview schedule and the deadlines with your CAS coordinator — schools run the same requirement with very different paperwork, and where a school rule is stricter than the guide's, the school rule is the one that applies to you.

1. What CAS actually is

CAS is the deliberate counterweight to the rest of the diploma. Five of your six subjects are examined by sitting still and writing; CAS is the part that asks you to do something in the world and then think about what it did to you. It is not assessed, which means there is no grade to optimise — but it is required, which means there is a failure condition to avoid.

Strip away the paperwork and the requirement is three sentences long. You undertake experiences in three strands — creativity, activity, service. You think about them, on the record, as they happen. And by the end you must be able to evidence seven learning outcomes. Everything else you have heard about — hour counts, minimum numbers of activities, the portfolio platform your school uses — is either school policy or myth.

Why the IB built it

Two reasons, and they pull in different directions. The first is developmental: the IB's stated aim is that students leave the programme able to plan their own involvement, to persist when an activity stops being novel, and to notice the ethical dimension of what they do. None of that can be examined in a hall, so it is required instead. The second is defensive: a diploma made entirely of written exams produces students who are excellent at being assessed. CAS is the component that cannot be assessed at all, and that is exactly why the IB guards it.

The consequence for you is a strange asymmetry: CAS has no upside to optimise and a catastrophic downside to avoid. Spend the minimum effort needed to make it structurally safe, spend it early, and put the rest of your energy into the 42 points that are actually graded.

1.1 The five stages every experience passes through

The guide describes CAS experiences in five stages: investigation, preparation, action, reflection and demonstration. You do not need to write about all five for every experience, but you do need to have passed through them — and the two that students skip are the two that produce the best evidence.

StageWhat you actually doThe artefact it leaves behind
Investigation Find out what is needed from the people who need it — not what you assumed, not what suits your timetable. Notes from a conversation, dated before you committed. The most useful document in a thin portfolio.
Preparation Turn the answer into a plan with roles, a sequence and a named risk. A dated plan. This is what makes outcome 3 provable; almost nothing else does.
Action Do it. Weekly, usually, for months. A log with dates, with the weeks that went badly visible in it.
Reflection Say what happened, what it means, and what you will do differently. Dated reflections at intervals, not one at the end — see CAS — reflection & portfolio.
Demonstration Show what changed, in you or in the setting, to someone else. An artefact: the thing that did not exist before, plus evidence someone else saw it.
Trap — the two stages everyone skips

Investigation and preparation happen before anything visible, so students skip them and start at action. The cost is not administrative — it is that outcomes 3 (initiate and plan) and 6 (global significance) are nearly impossible to evidence without them. Outcome 3 asks for a plan; a plan written after the activity is not a plan. Outcome 6 asks you to have engaged with an issue; "I turned up and it was sad" is not engagement. Four weeks of investigation before you start is the cheapest insurance in the whole core.

2. The three strands

Every CAS experience sits in at least one of three strands. The strands are not categories of hobby — they are categories of what the experience does to you. That is why the same activity can legitimately count in two strands at once (designing and coaching a training programme is creativity and activity) and why an ostensibly similar activity can count in none.

StrandThe guide's definitionWhat this rules out
Creativity Arts, and other experiences that involve creative thinking. Appreciating other people's creativity does not count. Attending a concert, visiting an exhibition, watching a film — these are consumption, not creativity. You must be the one making something, or making a decision about how something is made.
Activity Physical exertion contributing to a healthy lifestyle, complementing academic work elsewhere in the DP. A single-event fun run or one weekend hike. The guide explicitly warns that single incidents of engagement "can lack depth and meaning". Activity must be sustained.
Service An unpaid and voluntary exchange that has a learning benefit for the student. The rights, dignity and autonomy of all those involved are respected. Paid work. Anything where the main beneficiary is you and the "service" is incidental. And — the one the guide is most pointed about — service that stays at "doing for" people. Real service moves to "engaging with" people.

2.1 Creativity: making, not consuming

The word in the definition that does the work is thinking. Creativity is not a talent requirement; it is a requirement that you are making decisions rather than receiving a finished thing. That is why the guide allows "other experiences that involve creative thinking" alongside the arts — and why a student who has never drawn anything can satisfy this strand properly.

Counts comfortably

Writing and rehearsing a play then performing it. Composing, arranging or producing music. Building something functional — a robot, a set, an app, a costume, a lighting plan. Devising a training programme. Editing a film. Designing and making the materials for an event. Cooking a menu you designed for a real service.

Does not count, or counts weakly

Being in the audience. A school trip to a gallery. "I really enjoy music" as a profile statement. An examined art class — that is a course. Copying a tutorial exactly. Anything where someone else made the creative decisions and you supplied labour.

The test: can you point to a decision you made that a reasonable person could have made differently? If yes, you are doing creative thinking. If every decision was made for you, you are consuming, however much you enjoyed it.

2.2 Activity: sustained physical effort

Activity is the strand students treat as a formality and then fail on a technicality. It requires physical exertion contributing to a healthy lifestyle, and it must be sustained — which rules out the single-event pattern. Training with a team twice a week for a year counts. A structured running, climbing or swimming programme with progression counts. One sponsored run, one weekend hike, a single sports day, or being a spectator does not. The grey area is a school season that ends after eight weeks: thin on its own, sustained if it sits alongside off-season training. The fix is always continuity, never intensity.

Note the complement clause — activity should complement "academic work elsewhere in the DP". Two years of the diploma is sedentary, and the strand exists partly to make you move during the weeks when every other part of your life is a chair. Choose something that survives the exam timetable at reduced intensity.

2.3 Service: an exchange, not a sacrifice

Service is defined as an unpaid and voluntary exchange with a learning benefit for the student. That is a strange definition if you were expecting altruism, and it is deliberate: the IB is not asking you to be selfless, it is asking you to be in a real relationship with people whose circumstances differ from yours, in a setting where you cannot be the expert.

Two clauses carry real weight. Unpaid: paid work is not service, and a paid internship does not become service because the employer is a charity. And the rights, dignity and autonomy of all involved are respected — the clause that turns voluntourism, photogenic poverty and "helping" a community that did not ask for your help into CAS problems rather than CAS achievements. Section 10 covers it properly.

The guide's own progression is from doing for to engaging with. Shelving books for a year is doing for. Asking the librarian what the children actually need, then redesigning the reading programme twice, is engaging with. Both count; only the second evidences much.

Trap — the double-counting rule

Anything already used to satisfy another Diploma requirement cannot be CAS. Your physics investigation is not CAS. Your maths exploration is not CAS. Your computer science collaborative project is not CAS, and your business research project is not CAS. Neither is anything you submit, present or are assessed on in a subject. This catches a lot of science and CS students, who assume their project work will double as creativity. It will not, and discovering it in the final interview is a bad afternoon.

The clean version: if the same artefact appears in a subject portfolio and in your CAS portfolio, one of them has to go. Keep them separate from the start, because you cannot separate them retrospectively if the work is the work.

3. The seven learning outcomes

These are the actual requirement. Everything you do in CAS is in service of evidencing these seven things, and the completion decision your coordinator makes is a decision about these seven — not about how impressive your activities were.

#Learning outcomeIn plain languageWhat proves itCommon misreading
1 Identify own strengths and develop areas for growth You have an honest picture of yourself, including the weak parts, and you picked at least one activity that stretches a weak part. A named weakness identified before the activity, plus evidence of you working on it. The profile section is where the baseline lives; without a baseline there is no growth to point at. "I am good with people and need to work on time management" — a generic strength and a generic weakness, neither connected to anything you did.
2 Demonstrate that challenges have been undertaken, developing new skills in the process You chose difficulty, and you can now do something in March you could not do in September. A difficulty you chose and a new, describable skill. Best evidenced when the skill is dated: "the part I could not do at the start was…; by week N I could…". Continuing for two years something you were already excellent at. Mastery maintained is not a challenge undertaken.
3 Demonstrate how to initiate and plan a CAS experience You started something rather than being placed in something, and there is a plan predating it. A dated plan written before the first session, showing decisions you made — goal, roles, sequence, risk, contingency. Almost nothing else evidences this. Retro-fitting "I planned" onto an activity you joined. Coordinators spot it instantly: there is no artefact from before the activity.
4 Show commitment and perseverance in CAS experiences You kept going over months, including when it stopped being fun or convenient. A dated log across months with the difficult weeks visible. Perseverance that was never tested is not perseverance, so the week you nearly stopped is the most valuable entry you have. "I attended every session." Attendance is not commitment if nothing ever competed with it.
5 Demonstrate the skills and recognise the benefits of working collaboratively You worked with other people in a way that changed the outcome, and you can say what the group produced that you could not have. A specific outcome solo work would not have produced, plus an honest account of a disagreement or a dependency. If one member left and the project would not have changed, it was parallel work. "We divided the tasks and worked well together." That is coordination, and it says nothing about the benefit.
6 Demonstrate engagement with issues of global significance At least some of your CAS touches something bigger than the school gate, and you engaged with the issue rather than naming it. A real connection between your local activity and a larger issue — environment, inequality, public health, migration, technology and society — where you have read, listened or looked at data beyond the activity itself. "This relates to poverty, which is a global issue." One clause, no mechanism, no engagement. That is worse than leaving it out.
7 Recognise and consider the ethics of choices and actions You noticed the ethical dimension — including your own position in it — and thought it through rather than resolving it with a slogan. An ethical question you actually faced, where the honest answer was uncomfortable, and where you can say what you decided and what it cost. "It is important to respect people's dignity." Outcome 7 needs a dilemma, not a value.
The one-sentence version

You do not need seven activities for seven outcomes. One well-chosen project can evidence five or six of them at once. What you need is a portfolio in which every one of the seven can be pointed to — somewhere, in something, with evidence and reflection behind it. The most common failure is not a missing outcome; it is an outcome that is genuinely visible in your activities but never written down.

Why this works Why the outcome is a claim about evidence, not about activity
  1. None of the seven outcomes can be judged by watching you. Your coordinator was not at most of your sessions and will not be at any of the rest. So the outcome is not "did you collaborate" — it is "can the portfolio show that you did".
  2. That makes CAS a documentary exercise wearing the clothes of an experiential one: an unrecorded CAS programme is indistinguishable from no CAS programme.
  3. Evidence is credible in proportion to how hard it is to produce after the fact. A dated plan written before session one is hard to fake; a glowing summary written in May of DP2 is trivially easy whether or not anything happened.
  4. Hence the rule that governs everything on this page: produce the artefact at the moment, not at the end. Two minutes after a session is worth twenty minutes six months later.
  5. When this is wrong: if you have started and have no plan, do not write a fake one. Write a dated "plan as of now", say plainly that it is late, and reflect on why — that is honest outcome 1 evidence.

4. The 18-month rule, and what "sustained" actually means

The guide requires at least 18 months of sustained engagement, ideally weekly, with a reasonable balance between the three strands. Eighteen months is longer than the DP1–DP2 academic year split, and that is the point: it forces your CAS through a summer and through an exam period, which is exactly where commitment gets tested.

"Sustained" is the word students ignore. It does not mean "long". It means four things at once.

TestSustained looks likeBolted-on looks like
Frequency Weekly or fortnightly, across two academic years and a summer, with the gaps explainable. Two intense blocks: three weeks in October of DP1 and a fortnight in March of DP2.
Continuity of relationship The same people, the same organisation, who notice when you are not there. A different organisation each term, each one met once.
Adaptation You changed what you do in response to what happened — the plan diverged and you can say why. Session 12 is the same as session 1. Nothing you learned changed anything.
Ownership Someone depends on you; if you stopped, something would not happen. You are interchangeable. Any other student could have filled the slot.
Worked example 1 Two activity strands, one sustained and one bolted-on

Student A. September of DP1 to March of DP2: Tuesday and Thursday squad training with a local club, plus a match most weekends. Log shows 78 of 96 sessions, with the 18 absences clustering in the exam periods and each explained. In November she tore a hamstring, could not train for five weeks, and took over designing the under-16s' warm-up, which she ran for the rest of the season.

Student B. The same eighteen months: a gym membership from October, lapsed in February; a half-marathon in June, trained for over five weeks; a six-week climbing course in the summer; a ski trip logged as "activity, 6 days". Nothing after February of DP2.

Both logged meaningful hours. Only one has a strand.

  • Frequency. A: weekly for eighteen months. B: four episodes with gaps of three to five months.
  • Relationship. A: one club, one coach, teammates who noticed her absence. B: nobody at any of the four settings would know if she never returned.
  • Adaptation. A: the injury forced a genuine redesign, and the new role is the source of her best reflection. B: every episode ended because it was finished.
  • Ownership. A: the warm-up is hers. B: replaceable at every point.

What B should do in March of DP1, while it is cheap. Keep the club, drop the rest, and commit through the summer into DP2 at reduced frequency during exams. Nine months of one thing beats four bursts of four, and takes less time.

The transferable test: if you deleted your busiest month, would your CAS still make sense? If yes, the busy month was decoration. If the whole strand collapses, it was sustained.
Trap — intensity is not the same as duration

"I did 200 hours in the summer" is the common defence of a bolted-on programme, and it fails twice: there is no hour count in the guide at all, and a summer block cannot evidence perseverance, because nothing competes with it. The week you kept going with a chemistry test the next morning is worth more than any fortnight in August. Continuity through the hard weeks is the product.

What to do if you are already late
  1. Count the months you actually have. At the start of DP2 you have twelve to fifteen months — enough for the requirement, not enough for another false start.
  2. Keep the longest-running thing you have, at any frequency. Continuity is the asset.
  3. Add one new experience now, in the emptiest strand, with the plan written before the first session so it earns outcome 3.
  4. Do the seven-outcome audit this week and fix the two weakest outcomes with something real rather than better writing — see CAS — reflection & portfolio.
  5. Tell your coordinator. One who learns about a gap in March of DP2 can do something; one who learns at the final interview cannot.

5. The myths

Six beliefs about CAS are repeated so often that students treat them as requirements. None of them is in the guide. Each one costs you something real — usually by pushing you towards activity-shaped work instead of relationship-shaped work.

MythWhere it came fromWhat the requirement actually saysWhat believing it costs you
"You need 150 hours." The 150 figure is SL teaching time from a different table in a different document, copied between handbooks and websites until it looked official. There is no hour count anywhere in the CAS guide. What is required is sustained engagement over at least 18 months with a reasonable balance. Students who log everything end up somewhere between 100 and 250 hours — an observation, not a target. You optimise for time served instead of continuity, and you treat 150 as a finish line and stop.
"CAS is a numbers game." University application culture. There is no required number of experiences. Three strands, seven outcomes, one project, eighteen months, reasonable balance. Three sustained experiences can satisfy all of it; fourteen shallow ones satisfy none of it. Collecting destroys the two outcomes that need time — 4 and 1 — and leaves a portfolio of first sessions with no seconds.
"Service has to be abroad, or at a big charity." Trip brochures; overseas service photographs well. Nothing in the definition of service mentions distance, a plane, a fee or a registered charity. Some of the strongest service in the diploma happens fifteen minutes from the school gate. You spend the summer and a large sum on a week abroad that evidences almost nothing, while the local nine-month placement never happens. See section 10.
"Creativity means I have to be artistic." The name of the strand. Creativity is "arts, and other experiences that involve creative thinking". Designing a database, devising a training programme, solving an event's logistics, building something functional — all of it counts. Students who do not draw or sing declare themselves bad at creativity and resent a token art project instead of using what they are actually good at.
"I have to lead everything." A misreading of outcome 3 (initiate and plan). Outcome 3 requires that you can show how to initiate and plan a CAS experience — one, evidenced by a dated plan. Joining an organisation and redesigning one part of what it does evidences it better than founding something nobody asked for. You avoid the placements that would teach you something because they are not "yours".
"CAS is graded." Confusion with TOK and the EE, which are graded and do convert into points. CAS is not formally assessed and has no grade. Your coordinator decides whether you have completed it and records that decision. No A to E, nothing to convert into points. You write for an imaginary examiner instead of the one person who will read it, and produce polished generalities that evidence less than an honest note.
And two smaller ones
  • "The CAS project must combine two or more strands." The guide says a project can address any single strand or combine two or three. Many school handbooks state the stricter version. Follow your school's — it is stricter, not wrong, and it is not worth arguing about.
  • "CAS finishes when the project finishes." The eighteen-month clock is independent of the project. Students who run the project in the summer of DP1 and then stop have a completed project and an incomplete programme.

6. How to choose experiences

Choice is where most CAS programmes are won or lost, and it happens in the first six weeks. The mistake is to choose activities. Choose settings — a place where you will be known, needed and slightly out of your depth for a long time — and the activities follow.

A decision procedure that works
  1. Write the honest profile first. One page: what you are good at, what you are bad at, what you have already done for years, and what you have quietly avoided. The avoided column is your CAS programme.
  2. Fill the emptiest strand first. Most students arrive with creativity and activity already covered by existing hobbies and with nothing at all in service. Service placements take four to eight weeks to arrange, so start the asking in week one.
  3. Apply the three questions to each candidate: does it stretch something weak? will it still be running in nine months? does it put you in a relationship with someone who is not a friend of yours? Two yeses is acceptable; three is ideal.
  4. Prefer settings where someone can supervise you. A supervisor — someone at the organisation or in school who sees you work — is worth more to your evidence than any photograph.
  5. Check the disqualifiers. Is it paid? Is it assessed in a subject? Is it a single event? Is it something you would put on an application primarily because it photographs well? Any yes is a problem.
  6. Commit to three, maximum four. One per strand plus the project. Then write the plan for the project before you start any of them, so that outcome 3 is satisfied while it is still easy.
Worked example 2 One student choosing a coherent set

The student. Amara, DP1. HL mathematics, HL physics, SL economics; strong at football; plays guitar badly and alone; has never done anything for anyone outside her family; quietly terrified of talking to strangers. Four hours a week in term, more in the summer.

Step 1 — the honest profile. Strengths: reliable, physically fit, good at explaining mathematics to friends. Weaknesses: avoids unstructured social contact, has never finished a creative project, gives up when she is not immediately good at something. Avoided column: performing, talking to strangers, patience with beginners.

Step 2 — the emptiest strand. Service, completely. Activity is covered by football; creativity only nominally by guitar.

Step 3 — what she chooses.

  • Service: weekly homework-support sessions at a nearby secondary school, with a named teacher as supervisor — chosen because it forces contact with strangers, uses her one real strength, and runs for two years.
  • Creativity: not guitar — lighting and sound for the school drama production. A real deadline, a real audience, decisions she has never made, twice a year.
  • Activity: football continues, plus the off-season running programme so the strand survives March.
  • Project: with two others, a six-week science-and-sport programme for Year 5 pupils at the partner school — planned in May, delivered in July and August, evaluated in September. It spans all three strands and is the natural child of the placement.

Why it is coherent. Every piece reinforces the others, and she added only one genuinely new commitment. The set attacks her avoided column from three directions at once, which is what makes outcome 1 real rather than written.

What she rejected. A fortnight building houses abroad (expensive, one-off); founding a new club (would compete with the placement for the same four hours); a hospital role that turned out to be filing (nobody would have noticed if she stopped).

The pattern: she did not choose three activities, she chose one setting — the partner school — and let creativity, service and the project grow out of it. Coherence is cheaper than breadth and evidences far more.
Trap — choosing for the application

If the sentence "this will look good on my personal statement" is doing most of the work in your decision, you have chosen wrong. Coordinators read hundreds of these and can tell immediately, because application-driven choices have a signature: they are impressive, they are short, they involve being photographed, and the reflection about them is about the student rather than the setting. The alternative test is blunt and works: would this still be worth doing if nobody outside the school ever heard about it?

7. Balancing the strands across eighteen months

"Reasonable balance" is the guide's phrase and it is deliberately vague, because what is reasonable depends on where you started. A student who has played competitive sport for six years and has never made anything does not need equal thirds — they need the gap filled. Balance is judged over the whole eighteen months, not per term.

BlockWhat is competing for your timeWhat CAS should look like
DP1 autumn Adjusting to the DP; first IAs being introduced. All three strands started, however small. Service placement being negotiated — this takes weeks, so the asking happens now. Initial interview done.
DP1 spring First real IA deadlines; mocks in some schools. Weekly rhythm established. Project planned, with a dated plan, before the second interview. Collaborators named and asked.
DP1 summer Almost nothing — the only long unbroken block in the diploma. Run the project, or the heavy part of it. Everything else continues at lower frequency.
DP2 autumn EE draft, IA crunch, predicted grades, university applications. Project finished and reflected. Seven-outcome audit done and holes fixed. Existing commitments continue — do not start anything new here unless the audit demands it.
DP2 spring Final IAs, revision. Maintenance only. Frequency drops, continuity does not break. Final interview.
Exams Everything. Either genuinely paused and recorded as paused, or kept at one session a fortnight. Silence is fine; an unexplained six-week hole is not.
Worked example 3 Two plans: a heavily loaded student and a lighter one

Plan A — heavily loaded. Four HL subjects, a science, an EE in physics, competitive engineering applications. Available for CAS: two hours a week in term, plus the summer.

  • Creativity — the CS club's magazine: one issue edited a term, four issues across two years. Low weekly cost, high decision content, and it exists anyway.
  • Activity — the school badminton club, one lunchtime a week all year, fortnightly through the exam period. Forty minutes a week for eighteen months is a strong strand.
  • Service — mentoring a primary-school mathematics club, one hour a fortnight, five minutes away. No travel cost, and it uses what he is already good at.
  • Project — with two others, a six-week summer robotics programme at the same primary school: planned in May, run in July, evaluated in September.

Why it works: nothing requires more than an hour at a time, everything is on site or nearby, and the heaviest item sits in the summer. The plan survives January of DP2 because it never asked for more than two hours a week.

Plan B — lighter load. Three HL, three SL, an English EE, more slack. Available: four to six hours a week in term plus the summer.

  • Creativity — ceramics, one evening a week for two years, ending in a public exhibition.
  • Activity — a rowing club with a real programme: three sessions a week in DP1, two in DP2, one regatta a term.
  • Service — an animal rescue, three hours every other Saturday for two years, with a named supervisor.
  • Project — a ten-week creative-arts programme she designs and runs with two others at a day centre, from the summer of DP1 into the autumn of DP2.

Why it works: she has the slack to buy depth — two years of one craft rather than a term of six — and her risk is the opposite of Plan A's: CAS can start eating the revision timetable.

The comparison worth noticing: both plans contain exactly four commitments and roughly the same total hours. Plan A buys safety by keeping everything small and local; Plan B buys richness by concentrating time. Neither is better CAS — each is right for the student who has to live inside it.
Rebalancing

Balance is judged over the whole period, so a lopsided start is fixable — but only if you notice it. Do this check at the end of DP1: for each strand, name the setting, the frequency and the person who would notice if you stopped. If you cannot name all three for a strand, that strand is not running, whatever your portfolio says. Fixing it in June costs an email; fixing it in February of DP2 costs a crisis.

8. Evidence, verification and completion

Nobody from the IB reads your portfolio. Your CAS coordinator — the teacher at your school who runs the programme — decides whether you have completed CAS, on the evidence, and records that decision. Understanding that sentence explains most of how CAS behaves in practice: there is no external examiner to persuade, but there is one person who has to be able to justify the decision if anyone ever asks.

8.1 What "complete" requires

RequirementWhat satisfies itWhat the coordinator looks for
Eighteen months, sustained Continuous involvement across both years, with a reasonable balance of strands. Dates. Entries spread across six blocks, not clustered in two.
One CAS project Collaborative, sequential, well-considered, at least a month from planning to completion. A plan dated before the first session, and more than one session. See CAS — the project.
Three interviews At least two in DP1, at least one in DP2, feedback recorded. That they happened at the right moments, and that advice given was acted on.
Seven learning outcomes Evidence for each, mainly through reflection. A specific moment and a specific artefact for each — not a general claim.
Portfolio, supervisor, self-review No mandated format; most schools use Profile / Experiences / Evidence. Someone who saw you work can comment on your engagement, and you have reviewed your own progress. That it is contemporaneous, that a stranger could find any of the seven in two minutes, and that the self-review names a weakness.

8.2 The supervisor

A supervisor is someone at the organisation or in school who sees you work and can speak to your engagement. They are not a second coordinator and they do not assess you. Their value is that they are the only person in your CAS who is not you.

Ask them for one specific comment on a specific thing: whether you were reliable, whether you changed anything, whether the setting was different because you came. "She was here every week" gives you attendance; "the Tuesday group only worked because she redesigned it after week three" gives you outcomes 3, 4 and 5 at once. The difference is in the briefing.

Worked example 4 A supervisor conversation that produces evidence

Weak version. "Hi, could you sign my CAS form and say I did the volunteering?" Result: a signature and a sentence. Useless for every outcome except, weakly, outcome 4.

Strong version. "I'm trying to show that I planned something and then changed it when it did not work. You saw weeks one to twelve. Three questions, if you can: did the sessions change after the first few weeks, and was that my doing? Was there a point where I should have done something differently and did not? And did anything stop happening when I had to miss those two weeks in November?"

What that produces. An answer with dates and mechanisms, which the student can then quote in the portfolio, disagree with, or be embarrassed by — all three are better than a signature. If the supervisor says "actually no, you were pretty rigid until I told you to change it", that is not a problem: it is outcome 1 evidence, because the gap between how you saw it and how someone else saw it is exactly what self-knowledge looks like on paper.

The move: never ask a supervisor to confirm you. Ask them a question you do not know the answer to. Confirmation evidences nothing; a discrepancy evidences outcome 1.

8.3 What happens at the end, and what happens if you do not finish

At the final interview you outline how you achieved the seven outcomes, and the coordinator makes the completion decision. It is recorded, and it is binary.

The consequence of non-completion is severe and widely misunderstood. A student who scores comfortably above 24 points, passes every subject, writes a good TOK essay, but does not complete CAS does not receive the diploma — they receive DP course results instead. A candidate with 40 points and no CAS receives DP course results. This is the same class of failure as an E in TOK or the EE, and unlike those two it is entirely within your control, because there is no grade to aim at. There is no partial credit, and no rescuing it with strong exam performance.

The practical read

Treat CAS as a deadline problem, not a quality problem. Nobody fails CAS because their reflections were not profound enough. People fail CAS because they had not started the project by the end of DP1, or because their portfolio has a gap where outcome 3 or outcome 7 should be, and they discover it in the final interview with three weeks to go. Everything on this page is about not being that person. The mechanics of the portfolio and the interviews themselves are in CAS — reflection & portfolio.

9. CAS and your other commitments

CAS is the only part of the diploma with no deadline attached, which is exactly why it loses every argument with the parts that have one. The fix is structural: give it a fixed slot at a fixed time and let everything contested happen around it.

Six subjects About 1,170 taught hours plus several hundred of your own. This is where 42 of the 45 points live. CAS is 2–4 hours a week, roughly one HL subject's homework. It should never be the first thing you sacrifice.
The extended essay About 40 hours of student work plus 3–5 hours of supervision. Do not let the project run in the same six weeks as the EE draft — sequence them. See EE — pathway & question.
Internal assessments and TOK Two to four IAs per subject, clustered in DP1 spring and DP2 autumn; 100 TOK taught hours plus two tasks. Those two terms are when CAS frequency drops — plan the dip and record it. A service placement is a legitimate TOK real-life situation, but never submit the same artefact for both: see TOK — the course.
Three rules that keep CAS alive
  1. Never miss the same session twice. One absence is a fact; two is a pattern, and patterns are what coordinators remember. If you must drop to fortnightly, say so in advance and write it down.
  2. Put the project in the summer. It is the only long unbroken block in the diploma and the only place a collaborative project can realistically live. Plan it in May, run it in July and August, evaluate it in September.
  3. Do not start anything new after the October of DP2. Anything begun then will be shallow by March, and shallow is worse than absent because it displaces the thing it replaced.
Worth doing once

Plan the whole two years on one page before DP1 gets difficult — the core deadlines, the IA clusters, the exam periods and your CAS blocks together. Study plan is built for exactly this, and the version that matters is the one that shows you where CAS and the EE collide.

10. Ethics and safeguarding

Two clauses in the definitions carry most of the ethical weight: service must respect "the rights, dignity and autonomy of all those involved", and outcome 7 asks you to recognise and consider the ethics of your own choices and actions. Together they make CAS the only part of the diploma where being thoughtless is itself a failure.

10.1 The line between service and voluntourism

Voluntourism is not defined by geography — a two-day placement in your own city can be voluntourism, and a year-long commitment abroad need not be. It is defined by who the arrangement is actually for. Ask four questions honestly:

  1. Would the organisation pay a local person to do this if you did not come? If yes, you may be displacing work rather than adding it. If the honest answer is uncomfortable, that is outcome 7 material — but only if you write it down.
  2. Does the money spent on getting there exceed the value delivered? A ¥20,000 trip that produces a week of unskilled labour is a transaction in which the traveller is the customer.
  3. Did the community ask for this? Service that arrives with a plan and no conversation is doing for, not engaging with.
  4. Does the activity still make sense if nobody ever sees a photograph of it? If the answer is no, what you are producing is an image, not a service.

None of this makes short trips forbidden. It makes unexamined short trips weak evidence. A student who goes, asks these questions, writes honestly about the answers — including the answers that reflect badly on the trip — has better outcome 7 evidence than a student who never went anywhere.

10.2 Consent, risk and appropriate service

IssueWhat goes wrongWhat good practice looks like
Working with children or vulnerable adults A student turns up weekly at a setting that never asked for a background check; the school finds out in DP2. Any required check is arranged through the school before the first session, in writing. Never work unsupervised on your own initiative.
Photographs and data A portfolio full of identifiable images of children, or of a setting's private records. No identifying photographs without written consent. Blur faces by default. Never upload a document belonging to the organisation.
Physical and environmental risk Construction, altitude, unfamiliar transport, animals, unsupervised travel — arranged by students. A named adult accepts the risk in writing before you start. Your school's risk policy governs.
Work you are not qualified to do Students teaching, caring or building with no training, on the reasoning that help beats no help. Do what an untrained but reliable person genuinely can: accompany, organise, run sessions alongside a qualified person, prepare materials. Ask what is needed.
Confidentiality A reflection naming a person's circumstances, or repeating something said in confidence. Write about your own response and decisions. Anonymise everyone. If a reflection would embarrass someone who read it, it does not go in.
Trap — the photograph reflex

The single most common ethical failure in a CAS portfolio is not a bad activity; it is a portfolio of images of people in harder circumstances than the student's, used as evidence. Ask what the photograph is for. If it evidences a thing you made, fine. If it evidences that you were there, it is doing work that outcome 7 exists to make you examine — and a coordinator who sees twenty such images learns nothing about your learning and a great deal about your priorities.

The connection worth making

Outcomes 6 and 7 are the same intellectual move the core rewards elsewhere: taking a position about something that matters and justifying it while taking the best counter-argument seriously. That is what the EE's discussion and evaluation want, and what TOK's knowledge questions want. Practise it once in CAS and it pays three times — see EE — writing & criteria and TOK — the essay.

11. Failure modes

Almost nobody fails CAS for lack of a good idea. Here is what actually goes wrong, in order of frequency, with the point at which each stops being cheap to fix.

FailureWhat it looks likeDetectableThe fix, and the last moment it works
One strand quietly empty Eighteen months of creativity and activity; service is two Saturdays in DP2. At the initial interview Start something weekly in the empty strand. Cheap until the October of DP2; after that every option is shallow.
Bolted-on rather than sustained Four intense bursts separated by months of nothing. By looking at the dates in your own log Keep the longest-running setting, drop the rest. Works at any point, best early.
Outcomes 3 and 7 with nothing behind them Everything you did was something you joined; nothing raised an ethical question you noticed. At the seven-outcome audit, end of DP1 Add one initiated experience and one real ethical question. After February of DP2 you can write about it but not do it.
The project never started DP2 arrives with no project and a vague intention to do something in the spring. At the end-of-DP1 interview Plan in June, run in the summer. After the October of DP2 no unbroken block remains — see CAS — the project.
The evidence gap It all genuinely happened, but everything was written in the final month. Mid-programme, by opening your own folder Start the log now. You can backfill a fortnight honestly; you cannot backfill a year.
Double-counting with a subject, or abandonment A CS IA or physics investigation appears in the portfolio; or six enthusiastic months then nothing from February of DP2. At any point — "am I submitting this?" / week by week from your own attendance Keep CAS and coursework separate from the start; if you are fading, shrink to the minimum viable version and finish it.
The one that actually bites

Almost nobody fails CAS because their reflections were not profound enough. They fail because the project was not planned by the end-of-DP1 interview and the summer — the only long unbroken block in the diploma — passed without it. Then DP2 arrives with IAs, the EE, predicted grades and university applications, and CAS is the only thing with no deadline attached, so it goes last. Plan the project before the second interview, even if you plan to run it later. A plan dated in June is outcome 3 evidence even if the sessions happen in August.

12. Practice

Each of these is a decision you will actually have to make. Answer on paper before opening the answers.

  1. Does it count? For each of the following, say which strand it falls under (if any) and whether it would be accepted as CAS evidence.

    • (a) Playing in the school orchestra, which you have done since age nine and are now first violin.
    • (b) Attending a professional orchestra concert and writing a review for the school paper.
    • (c) Teaching beginners' violin to Year 7 students every Tuesday for a term.
    • (d) Doing your physics investigation on the acoustics of the school hall.
    • (e) A one-day charity fun run.
    Show the answer

    (a) Weak. Creativity, and sustained — but it evidences almost nothing: no new challenge, no area for growth, no initiation. Outcomes 1 and 2 are not met by continuing something you have already mastered.

    (b) No. Creativity "is not met by the appreciation of the creative efforts of others". Watching is not creating. The review is closer, but a single one-off "can lack depth and meaning".

    (c) Yes — the strongest of the five. Creativity (planning and adapting teaching), service (unpaid exchange with a learning benefit), sustained (outcome 4), collaborative (outcome 5), and outcome 3 if you set it up rather than being assigned it. One activity, four or five outcomes.

    (d) No. It is part of your physics course; anything used for another Diploma requirement cannot also be CAS.

    (e) Barely. A single incident of engagement. It can sit inside a sustained activity strand, but alone it evidences almost nothing.

    Why this is worth knowing: the activities that earn CAS are the ones where you are responsible for something over time and have to adapt. The ones that do not are the ones where you turn up. That single distinction resolves most "does it count?" questions in ten seconds.

  2. Mapping outcomes. You are planning to start a coding club for younger students at a local school, running for one term, with two friends. Map it against the seven learning outcomes: which does it evidence naturally, and which would you still need to do something else for?

    Show the answer

    Evidenced naturally:

    • 3 (initiate and plan) — strongly; this is the outcome most portfolios lack. Write the plan before session one or you lose it.
    • 5 (collaboration) — you are running it with two friends; reflect on what the division of labour made possible, and on the disagreement you will definitely have.
    • 2 (challenge, new skills) — teaching is a different skill from knowing; planning a session sequence is new.
    • 1 (strengths and growth) — only if you name the growth honestly: "I can code but I am bad at explaining, and this forced me to fix that".
    • 4 (commitment) — one term of weekly sessions is enough, provided you reflect across the term rather than once at the end.

    Probably NOT evidenced:

    • 6 (global significance) — a local coding club is not obviously global. You can connect it to who gets access to technical education, but the connection must be genuine and thought through, not bolted on.
    • 7 (ethics) — nothing here forces an ethical question. Outcome 7 needs its own moment, usually from the service strand.

    The useful conclusion: a good project that still leaves two holes — far better to know that in DP1, when you can add something, than at the final interview.

    Why this is worth knowing: outcomes 6 and 7 are the two most commonly missing at the end, because they are the two that ordinary school activities do not produce by accident. Audit for them specifically.

  3. Sustained or bolted-on? Two students both claim a full activity strand. Decide which meets the eighteen-month requirement and give the two reasons that decide it.

    • Student X: 40 hours in a summer sports camp in DP1; 30 hours of a January ski trip; 25 hours of a March climbing course in DP2. Total 95 hours, three settings, three certificates.
    • Student Y: 90 minutes of squad training every Tuesday from September of DP1 to March of DP2, at one club, with a log showing 61 of 78 sessions and the 17 absences explained.
    Show the answer

    Student Y meets it. Student X does not, and the hour totals are irrelevant to that conclusion.

    Reason one — continuity of relationship. The requirement is sustained engagement over eighteen months. X has three episodes separated by gaps of five to eleven months, in settings where nobody would notice if he never returned. Y has one club, one coach, and teammates affected by whether she turns up.

    Reason two — perseverance must be tested to be evidenced. Y's log has a difficult stretch in it (17 absences, mostly exam periods). X's three episodes were short, enjoyable and competed with nothing.

    What X should do. Keep the climbing course and convert it into a weekly commitment through to the end. Ninety minutes a week for thirty weeks gives him a strand; 95 hours in three bursts does not.

    Why this is worth knowing: the guide asks for sustained engagement, not accumulated hours. If you ever find yourself adding up your hours to check whether you have done enough CAS, you are answering a question that was never asked.

  4. The failure arithmetic. A candidate finishes with 38 points, an A for TOK and a B for the EE — but has not completed CAS. What do they receive, and what is the single most useful thing they could have done differently in DP1?

    Show the answer

    They receive DP course results, not the diploma. 38 points plus A/B on the core is a strong set of results on paper, and it is irrelevant: non-completion of CAS is one of the conditions under which the diploma is not awarded. The points do not rescue it, because it is not a points problem.

    The single most useful thing: having planned the CAS project before the end-of-DP1 interview, and having done a written seven-outcome audit at that point. Almost every real CAS failure is a scheduling failure — a portfolio with a hole in it, discovered too late to do anything real about.

    Note the asymmetry with TOK and the EE: those two can cost you points and, at grade E, the diploma. CAS only ever costs you the diploma — but it costs it absolutely, and it is the one of the three with no grade attached, which is exactly why it gets postponed.

    Why this is worth knowing: CAS has no upside to optimise and a catastrophic downside to avoid. Spend the minimum effort needed to make it structurally safe, and spend it early.

  5. Service or not? Three proposals. Which is genuine service, and why do the others fail the definition?

    • (i) A week-long trip abroad to build a school building, with the evenings spent sightseeing, at a cost of ¥20,000.
    • (ii) Volunteering at a local library shelving books once a week for a year.
    • (iii) Running free one-to-one maths tutorials for students two years below you, where you plan the sessions around what each student actually got wrong.
    Show the answer

    (iii) is the strongest, (ii) is thin but real, (i) is the one to be careful about.

    (iii) meets every part of the definition: unpaid, voluntary, a genuine exchange, with a learning benefit for you — you have to diagnose someone else's misunderstanding, which is harder than understanding it yourself. It also evidences outcomes 1, 2, 4 and 5.

    (ii) is unpaid and sustained, so it counts — but it evidences little beyond outcome 4: no challenge, no initiation, no relationship. It is "doing for" rather than "engaging with". The fix is one conversation: ask the librarian what does not get done, and take that on.

    (i) raises the ethical questions outcome 7 exists to surface. Is the community better served by untrained students building for a week than by local paid labour? Does the cost structure serve the travellers or the recipients? Are the photographs about the project or the applicant? A service experience that never asks them is weak outcome 7 evidence even if the activity happened.

    Why this is worth knowing: does the activity still make sense if nobody ever sees a photograph of it, and would the people you worked with say they wanted it? If yes to both, it is probably real service.

  6. Myth spotting. Identify the myth behind each of these five statements and state what the requirement actually says.

    • (a) "I have 112 hours logged, so I need another 38 before I'm done."
    • (b) "I'm not artistic, so I'll just do the school play as backstage crew and hope that's creativity."
    • (c) "The real service is the trip to Kenya in the summer — everything else is just helping out."
    • (d) "I've joined nine clubs so my CAS will look broad."
    • (e) "I'm aiming for a strong CAS grade to boost my points."
    Show the answer

    (a) The 150-hour myth. There is no hour count anywhere in the CAS guide; 150 is SL teaching hours from a different table. What is required is sustained engagement over eighteen months. Hours are an observation, not a target.

    (b) The "creativity means artistic" myth. Creativity is arts and other experiences involving creative thinking — backstage work can be excellent if he makes decisions (designing the set, planning cues) rather than only following instructions.

    (c) The "service must be abroad" myth. Nothing in the definition mentions distance, a fee or a plane. A week abroad evidences less than a year of weekly local work, and if it was not asked for it raises the outcome 7 questions rather than answering them.

    (d) The numbers-game myth. No number of experiences is required. Nine clubs means nine first sessions and no perseverance, relationship or depth.

    (e) The "CAS is graded" myth. CAS is not formally assessed and has no grade. TOK and the EE convert into up to three points; CAS converts into nothing. The coordinator makes a binary completion decision.

    Why this is worth knowing: every myth pushes you towards more, bigger or more photogenic. The requirement pushes you towards longer, fewer and more local. Knowing which voice you are listening to is most of the skill.

  7. Ethics. Your project team is running activities at a care home. On the first visit one member takes dozens of photographs of residents, including several who are distressed, and posts the best nine to social media with a caption about "giving back". You are asked to sign the team's evidence form. What is wrong, what do you do, and how does this become outcome 7 evidence rather than a disaster?

    Show the answer

    What is wrong. Three failures. Consent: no permission from the organisation, the residents or their families. Dignity: photographing people when they are distressed and publishing it is the opposite of what the service definition requires. Purpose: the images exist to make the photographers look good, not to evidence any learning — voluntourism in its purest form.

    What you do, in order. Ask for the posts to come down today. Tell the care home manager yourself, before the school finds out. Do not sign anything that presents the photographs as evidence. Tell your CAS coordinator. Then agree a written rule for the team: no identifying photographs, nothing posted from a session, and evidence takes the form of plans, logs and reflections.

    How it becomes outcome 7. Outcome 7 asks you to recognise and consider the ethics of choices and actions — including your own. The honest reflection writes down the part that reflects badly: that you saw it, did not speak at the time, and why that was hard (group loyalty, the photographs were good, nobody else objected). Then what you decided and what it cost. A portfolio containing this episode, handled honestly, has stronger outcome 7 evidence than one that never had a problem — and one that quietly omits it has none at all.

    Why this is worth knowing: outcome 7 is not a test of whether you behaved well. It is a test of whether you noticed the ethical dimension and thought it through. The students who have the strongest outcome 7 are usually the ones who got something wrong and wrote about it properly.

Vocabulary
CAS — creativity, activity, service: the non-assessed but required element of the DP core.
strand — one of the three kinds of CAS experience: creativity, activity, service.
CAS experience — a specific event or commitment in which you engage with one or more strands.
CAS project — a collaborative, well-considered series of sequential CAS experiences lasting at least one month from planning to completion.
learning outcome — one of the seven things your portfolio must be able to evidence.
sustained — continuous over time; the test that separates a real commitment from a burst of activity.
reasonable balance — the guide's phrase for how the three strands should be spread across the eighteen months.
CAS portfolio — the record of your experiences, reflections and evidence; no format is mandated.
CAS coordinator — the teacher who runs CAS at your school and decides whether you have completed it.
CAS adviser — the person who conducts your interviews; often the same person as the coordinator.
supervisor — someone at the setting or in school who sees you work and can comment on your engagement.
self-review — your own honest assessment of progress against the seven outcomes.
formative interview — the initial and mid-programme interviews, while there is still time to change things.
summative interview — the final interview, at which completion is decided.
unpaid and voluntary exchange — the definition of service: no pay, no compulsion, and a learning benefit for you.
global significance — an issue that extends beyond the local setting: environment, inequality, health, migration, technology and society.
ethics — the dimension of a decision concerning what is right, and who bears the cost.
voluntourism — service arranged primarily for the experience and image of the traveller rather than for the recipients.
initiative — starting something rather than being placed in it; the substance of outcome 3.
perseverance — continuing when it has stopped being easy; the substance of outcome 4.
collaboration — working so that the outcome depends on more than one person.
safeguarding — the policies that protect children and vulnerable people you work with.
DP course results (DPCR) — what you receive instead of the diploma if a core condition is not met.
