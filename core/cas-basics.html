CAS: what you actually have to do

CAS is the one part of the diploma with no grade attached — and the one part that can stop you being awarded a diploma at all, no matter how many points you score. This page sets out the requirement precisely, because most of what circulates about CAS is either out of date or invented.

Not formally assessed Completion is a diploma condition
Which guide applies to you

CAS runs on the Creativity, activity, service guide (for students graduating in 2017 and after). It was not changed in the same review round that rewrote the extended essay for first assessment 2027, and no new CAS guide has been announced for 2028 or 2029. So the requirement below is the one you will be held to. Confirm the exact portfolio platform and deadlines with your CAS coordinator — schools run the same requirement with very different paperwork.

What CAS is for

CAS is the deliberate counterweight to the rest of the diploma. Five of your six subjects are examined by sitting still and writing; CAS is the part that asks you to do something in the world and then think about what it did to you. It is not assessed, which means there is no grade to optimise — but it is required, which means there is a failure condition to avoid.

The design is simple: you undertake experiences, you reflect on them, and by the end you must be able to evidence seven learning outcomes. Everything else — the hours you hear about, the number of activities, the portfolio platform — is either school policy or myth.

The three myths, killed
  1. "You need 150 hours." No. There is no hour count anywhere in the CAS guide. The 150 figure is SL teaching hours from a different table and has been repeated by so many websites that students treat it as real. What the guide actually requires is sustained engagement over at least 18 months.
  2. "You must do one activity per strand per term." Not a requirement. What is required is a reasonable balance between creativity, activity and service across the whole 18 months, judged by your coordinator.
  3. "The CAS project must combine two or more strands." The guide says a project can address any single strand or combine two or three. Many school handbooks state the stricter "two or more" version. Follow your school's version — it is stricter, not wrong, and it is not worth arguing about.

The three strands

StrandThe guide's definitionWhat this rules out
Creativity
 Arts, and other experiences that involve creative thinking. Appreciating other people's creativity does not count. Attending a concert, visiting an exhibition, watching a film — these are consumption, not creativity. You must be the one making something, or making a decision about how something is made.
Activity
 Physical exertion contributing to a healthy lifestyle, complementing academic work elsewhere in the DP. A single-event fun run or one weekend hike. The guide explicitly warns that single incidents of engagement "can lack depth and meaning". Activity must be sustained.
Service
 An unpaid and voluntary exchange that has a learning benefit for the student. The rights, dignity and autonomy of all those involved are respected. Paid work. Anything where the main beneficiary is you and the "service" is incidental. And — the one the guide is most pointed about — service that stays at "doing for" people. Real service moves to "engaging with" people.
Why the "unpaid" word matters

Service is defined as an exchange with a learning benefit for you. That is a strange definition if you were expecting altruism, and it is deliberate: the IB is not asking you to be selfless, it is asking you to be in a real relationship with people whose circumstances differ from yours, in a setting where you cannot be the expert. Tutoring, coaching, running a club, working with younger students, supporting a community organisation — these work because you have to adapt to someone else's needs. Something that only looks good on a university application usually fails that test, and coordinators can tell.

The seven learning outcomes

These are the actual requirement. Everything you do in CAS is in service of evidencing these seven things. The order below is the guide's order.

#Learning outcomeWhat it really asks you to show
1 Identify own strengths and develop areas for growth That you have an honest picture of yourself — including the parts that are weak — and picked activities that stretch the weak ones. Playing the piano you are already good at evidences nothing here. Teaching piano to beginners does.
2 Demonstrate that challenges have been undertaken, developing new skills in the process That you chose difficulty. The guide's own gloss is that students come to "see themselves as individuals with various abilities and skills, of which some are more developed than others".
3 Demonstrate how to initiate and plan a CAS experience That you started something rather than being placed in something. This is the outcome most students evidence badly, because they only ever join existing clubs.
4 Show commitment and perseverance in CAS experiences Regular involvement and active engagement over time. This is what kills the "one big thing in the last month" plan: you cannot evidence perseverance retrospectively.
5 Demonstrate the skills and recognise the benefits of working collaboratively Real work with other people, and a reflection that says what collaboration actually produced that solo work would not have.
6 Demonstrate engagement with issues of global significance That at least some of your CAS touches something bigger than the school gate — environment, inequality, public health, migration, technology and society.
7 Recognise and consider the ethics of choices and actions That you noticed the ethical dimension and thought about it, including your own position. Voluntourism, photogenic poverty, and "helping" a community that did not ask for your help are the classic places this gets tested.
The one-sentence version

You do not need seven activities for seven outcomes. One well-chosen project can evidence five or six of them at once. What you need is a portfolio in which every one of the seven can be pointed to — somewhere, in something, with evidence and reflection behind it. The most common failure is not a missing outcome; it is an outcome that is genuinely visible in your activities but never written down.

The hard requirements

What is actually mandatory
  • At least 18 months of sustained engagement, ideally weekly, with a reasonable balance between creativity, activity and service.
  • At least one CAS project — a collaborative, well-considered series of sequential experiences, with a minimum of one month from planning to completion.
  • A minimum of three interviews with your CAS coordinator or adviser: at least two in the first year and one in the second year — an initial interview near the start, a progress interview (usually around the end of DP1, where the project is discussed), and a final summative interview before the programme ends.
  • Evidence of all seven learning outcomes, mainly through reflection.
  • A completed portfolio. The guide does not mandate a format; most schools use Profile / Experiences / Evidence.
What silently does not count

Anything already used to satisfy another Diploma requirement. Your physics investigation is not CAS. Your maths exploration is not CAS. Your computer science collaborative project is not CAS. The guide is explicit that CAS experiences must be distinct from what you submit for a course. This catches a lot of science students, who assume their lab work will double as creativity.

What "not formally assessed" really means

The portfolio is not graded — there is no A to E. But successful completion of CAS is a requirement for the award of the IB Diploma. Your coordinator makes the completion decision and records it.

The consequence is severe and widely misunderstood. A student who scores comfortably above 24 points, passes every subject, but does not complete CAS does not receive the diploma — they receive DP course results instead. This is the same class of failure as an E in TOK or the EE, and unlike those two it is entirely within your control, because there is no grade to aim at. There is no partial credit, and no rescuing it with strong exam performance.

Practical read

Treat CAS as a deadline problem, not a quality problem. Nobody fails CAS because their reflections were not profound enough. People fail CAS because they had not started the project by the end of DP1, or because their portfolio has a gap where outcome 3 or outcome 7 should be, and they discover it in the final interview with three weeks to go. Everything on the next two pages is about not being that person.

Where CAS sits in the two years

Before DP1
Initial CAS interview. Set up the portfolio. Start at least one creativity and one activity experience — these are the easy ones to start and the ones that take longest to show anything.
DP1 autumn
Three strands running. Begin looking for a service placement; real organisations take weeks to reply, so start the asking now.
DP1 spring
Second interview. The CAS project must be planned by now — the guide expects it to be discussed at this interview. Recruit collaborators.
DP1 summer
Run the CAS project, or the heavy part of it. This is the only long block of time you get with no exams in it.
DP2 autumn
Project finished and reflected. Audit the seven outcomes one by one and fix holes while there is still time to do something real about them.
DP2 winter / spring
Final summative interview: you outline how you achieved the seven outcomes. Portfolio complete. Nothing new started after this point.

Practice

Each of these is a decision you will actually have to make. Answer on paper before opening the answers.

  1. Does it count? For each of the following, say which strand it falls under (if any) and whether it would be accepted as CAS evidence.

    • (a) Playing in the school orchestra, which you have done since age nine and are now first violin.
    • (b) Attending a professional orchestra concert and writing a review for the school paper.
    • (c) Teaching beginners' violin to Year 7 students every Tuesday for a term.
    • (d) Doing your physics investigation on the acoustics of the school hall.
    • (e) A one-day charity fun run.
    Show the answer

    (a) Weak. It is creativity and it is sustained, but it evidences almost nothing: there is no new challenge, no identifiable area for growth, and you did not initiate it. It can sit in your portfolio as context, but outcome 1 and outcome 2 are not met by continuing something you have already mastered.

    (b) No. The guide is explicit that creativity "is not met by the appreciation of the creative efforts of others". Watching is not creating. Writing the review is closer — but a single review is a one-off, and one-offs "can lack depth and meaning".

    (c) Yes, and it is the strongest of the five. It is creativity (the planning and adapting of teaching), it is service (an unpaid exchange with a learning benefit), it is sustained (outcome 4), it is collaborative (outcome 5), and if you set it up rather than being assigned it, it evidences outcome 3. One activity, four or five outcomes — this is what good CAS looks like.

    (d) No. It is part of your physics course. Anything used to satisfy another Diploma requirement cannot also be CAS.

    (e) Barely. A single incident of engagement. It can be part of a sustained activity strand, but on its own it evidences almost nothing, and it is exactly the "box-ticking" pattern coordinators push back on.

    The pattern: the activities that earn CAS are the ones where you are responsible for something over time and have to adapt. The ones that do not are the ones where you turn up.

  2. Mapping outcomes. You are planning to start a coding club for younger students at a local school, running for one term, with two friends. Map it against the seven learning outcomes: which does it evidence naturally, and which would you still need to do something else for?

    Show the answer

    Evidenced naturally:

    • 3 (initiate and plan) — strongly, and this is the outcome most portfolios lack. You are starting it.
    • 5 (collaboration) — you are running it with two friends; reflect on what the division of labour made possible.
    • 2 (challenge, new skills) — teaching is a different skill from knowing. Planning a session sequence is a new skill.
    • 1 (strengths and growth) — only if you name the growth honestly. "I can code but I am bad at explaining, and this forced me to fix that" is the reflection that makes this outcome real.
    • 4 (commitment and perseverance) — one term of weekly sessions is enough, provided you reflect across the term rather than once at the end.

    Probably NOT evidenced:

    • 6 (global significance) — a local coding club is not obviously global. You can connect it (digital inequality, who gets access to technical education) but the connection must be genuine and thought through, not bolted on.
    • 7 (ethics) — nothing here forces an ethical question. Outcome 7 needs its own moment.

    The useful conclusion: this is a good project that still leaves two holes. That is normal — and it is far better to know that in DP1, when you can add something, than at the final interview.

    Where this matters: outcome 6 and outcome 7 are the two most commonly missing at the end, because they are the two that ordinary school activities do not produce by accident.

  3. The failure arithmetic. A candidate finishes with 38 points, an A for TOK and a B for the EE — but has not completed CAS. What do they receive, and what is the single most useful thing they could have done differently in DP1?

    Show the answer

    They receive DP course results, not the diploma. 38 points plus A/B on the core is a strong set of results on paper, and it is irrelevant: non-completion of CAS is one of the conditions under which the diploma is not awarded. The points do not rescue it, because it is not a points problem.

    The single most useful thing: having planned and launched the CAS project before the end-of-DP1 interview, and having done a written seven-outcome audit at that point. Almost every real CAS failure is a scheduling failure — a portfolio with a hole in it, discovered too late to do anything real about. Nobody has ever failed CAS because a reflection was not insightful enough.

    Note the asymmetry with TOK and the EE: those two can cost you points and, at grade E, the diploma. CAS only ever costs you the diploma — but it costs it absolutely, and it is the one of the three with no grade attached, which is exactly why it gets postponed.

    The lesson: CAS has no upside to optimise and a catastrophic downside to avoid. Spend the minimum effort needed to make it structurally safe, and spend it early.

  4. Service or not? Three proposals. Which is genuine service, and why do the others fail the definition?

    • (i) A week-long trip abroad to build a school building, with the evenings spent sightseeing, at a cost of ¥20,000.
    • (ii) Volunteering at a local library shelving books once a week for a year.
    • (iii) Running free one-to-one maths tutorials for students two years below you, where you plan the sessions around what each student actually got wrong.
    Show the answer

    (iii) is the strongest, (ii) is thin but real, (i) is the one to be careful about.

    (iii) meets every part of the definition: unpaid, voluntary, a genuine exchange, and with a learning benefit for you (you have to diagnose someone else's misunderstanding, which is a different and harder skill than understanding it yourself). It also naturally evidences outcomes 1, 2, 4 and 5.

    (ii) is unpaid and sustained, so it counts — but it evidences little beyond outcome 4. There is no clear challenge, no initiation, and no relationship. It is the definition of "doing for" rather than "engaging with", which is precisely the distinction the guide draws.

    (i) raises the ethical questions that outcome 7 is designed to surface. Is the community better served by untrained students building for a week than by local paid labour? Does the cost structure serve the travellers or the recipients? Are the photographs about the project or about the applicant? These are not rhetorical — a service experience that fails to consider them is weak evidence for outcome 7 even if the activity happened. If you do something like this, the reflection has to engage the ethics honestly, including the parts that reflect badly on the trip.

    The test: does the activity still make sense if nobody ever sees a photo of it, and would the people you worked with say they wanted it? If yes to both, it is probably real service.
