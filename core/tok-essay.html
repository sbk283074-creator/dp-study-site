Theory of knowledge — the essay

One title chosen from six, 1,600 words, marked by a stranger. That is the whole task, and it carries roughly two-thirds of your TOK grade. The essays that earn top marks are not the ones with the most impressive examples; they are the ones where every paragraph is doing something to a single argument. This page teaches you to build one.

1,600 words Externally assessed ≈67% of the TOK grade · graded A–E
Confirm before you rely on it

Check the word limit, the number of prescribed titles, the submission route and the exact criterion wording with your TOK teacher for your session. The guide your school is using is the authority; this page teaches the method, which does not change. The practice titles below are written in the style of IB prescribed titles — they are for training, not a prediction of the 2028 list.

1. The task, stated exactly

You must respond to one of six prescribed titles released for your examination session. You choose one, you quote it verbatim at the top of your essay, and you explore it in at most 1,600 words. You may not adapt, paraphrase or improve the title. A response on a topic of your own choosing scores nothing, because it is not a response to the task.

FeatureWhat it isWhat it means for you
Titles Six prescribed titles per session, released in advance by the IB. You get to choose. Choice is not a formality — see section 2. Most students pick in ten minutes and regret it for six weeks.
Length 1,600 words maximum. Short. That is the point. There is no room for background, for literature review, or for three examples per claim. Budget it — see section 5.
Focus Titles are about the areas of knowledge. Most require you to discuss two. The exhibition is where themes go. The essay is where AOKs go. Do not write an essay about technology; write about the natural sciences using a technological example.
Assessment Externally assessed, holistically, on how clearly, coherently and critically you explore the title. Graded A–E; worth roughly two-thirds of TOK. There is no separate mark for structure, for examples, for grammar. One judgement, formed by reading the whole thing. Which means every paragraph is either helping that impression or hurting it.
The mistake that makes everything else irrelevant

Writing about a topic you chose instead of the title you were given. It happens like this: the title is "to what extent is certainty attainable", the student has a wonderful prepared section on machine learning, and the machine-learning section arrives whether or not it answers the question. If you cannot say, in one sentence, how this paragraph bears on the title, the paragraph is decoration. Cut it.

2. Choosing between titles: the fifteen-minute test

Give yourself a full fifteen minutes per shortlisted title before you commit. For each of the six, run this test and write your answers down. The title that survives all five checks is your title.

How to choose — the 15-minute test
  1. Can you state two real claims for it? Not positions someone might hold — positions you can argue for, with reasons you actually believe. If you can only find one, the essay will be one-sided, and a one-sided essay is capped.
  2. Can you state two real counter-claims? Counter-claims you find genuinely uncomfortable, not straw men you can knock over in a sentence. If your counter-claim is weak, your evaluation is theatre.
  3. Do you have two examples you understand from the inside? Drawn from your own work — a proof, an experiment, a program, a text. Not "Galileo", not "the atomic bomb", unless you know them properly.
  4. Do those examples come from two different areas of knowledge? And do the AOKs contrast in their methods? Friction is what produces analysis.
  5. Can you answer "so what?" in one sentence? What would someone believe differently after accepting your conclusion? If nothing, the title has no stakes and the essay will be flat.

The decision rule: choose the title with the strongest second-best answer. A great title is not one where you are certain; it is one where the other side is genuinely strong and you can still argue past it. Certainty produces assertion, not analysis.

Choose this

"To what extent does the use of models limit the knowledge we can claim?" — you have a proof, an experiment and a simulation, and you can argue both that models limit us and that they are the only route to depth.

Avoid this

"Is there knowledge that we should not pursue?" — the answer is obviously yes, everyone agrees, and you will spend 1,600 words restating a consensus with illustrations.

Choose this

A title where your two AOKs disagree about what counts as evidence.

Avoid this

A title you picked because you already have an essay on it. Recycled material is the single most common cause of a mid-band essay, because it was never written to this title.

3. Breaking one title into two or three knowledge questions

A prescribed title is usually too big to answer in one move. Your job in the first fifteen minutes of planning is to split it into two or three knowledge questions that you can actually answer, and that together add up to the title.

Worked example 1 Splitting a title into knowledge questions

Title: "To what extent is certainty attainable in the production of knowledge? Discuss with reference to mathematics and the natural sciences."

  1. Find the key term. "Certainty" — and note immediately that the title does not say which kind. That ambiguity is your material, not a problem.
  2. KQ 1 (mathematics): Does a proof deliver certainty about the world, or only certainty about the consequences of axioms?
  3. KQ 2 (natural sciences): Can empirical methods deliver certainty at all, or only degrees of confidence that are conventionally treated as enough?
  4. KQ 3 (the synthesis): If mathematics gives certainty without content and the sciences give content without certainty, is "certainty" the wrong thing to want from knowledge?
  5. Check: each KQ is answerable in about 400 words; each is contested; and answering all three answers the title. That is the test.
Answer: three knowledge questions, one per body section, with the third doing the evaluative work rather than introducing new material.
Worked example 2 Splitting a title that names no areas of knowledge

Title: "Does it matter if knowledge is produced by a process we do not fully understand?"

  1. Choose AOKs for friction, not familiarity. Computer science is not an AOK; the nearest are mathematics (the model as a formal object) and the natural sciences or human sciences (the model as an empirical claim). Use mathematics and the human sciences.
  2. KQ 1: If a proof is verified by software no human reads, does mathematics still rest on understanding?
  3. KQ 2: If a model predicts recidivism or diagnosis better than any human but cannot explain itself, does the human sciences' standard of explanation make that knowledge defective?
  4. KQ 3: Is "understanding" a requirement for knowledge, or a preference of certain communities of knowers — and what follows either way?
  5. Check: the third question is where you take a position rather than balance two.
Answer: the title is answered by deciding whether understanding is intrinsic to knowledge or instrumental to it — a genuine fork, not a compromise.

4. The architecture of a strong essay

4.1 The thesis must be a knowledge claim, not a topic announcement

Compare these three openings to the same title. Only the third is a thesis.

TypeExampleDiagnosis
Topic announcement "In this essay I will discuss certainty in mathematics and the natural sciences, and consider different perspectives." Announces a plan, takes no position, wastes 25 words. The reader learns nothing except that you have not decided yet.
Definition dodge "Certainty is difficult to define, and in this essay I will use the dictionary definition." Defines instead of argues. If the essay's conclusion depends entirely on your definition, the essay is about a word.
Knowledge claim "Certainty is not a single property that some areas of knowledge have more of; it is a trade-off between necessity and content, and mathematics and the natural sciences sit at opposite ends of it. Recognising this makes 'certainty' a poor standard by which to rank knowledge." Takes a position, is contestable, tells the reader what the essay will establish, and already implies the structure. This is a thesis.
How to build a thesis in four moves
  1. Name the tension. "X appears to be a single thing, but it is really two things pulling apart…"
  2. Take a side. "…and the more defensible reading is that…"
  3. Give the reason in advance. "…because the method that produces one cannot produce the other."
  4. State the consequence. "If this is right, then we should stop asking whether an area of knowledge is certain and start asking what it has given up to be so."

Sentence frames: "The apparent conflict between X and Y dissolves once we notice that…" · "It is tempting to say P; however, P fails to account for…" · "This essay argues that Q, and that the consequence of Q is R."

4.2 The paragraph engine

Every body section runs the same six moves. Not every move needs its own paragraph, but every move must happen, and in this order:

  1. Claim — one sentence, defensible, clearly about the knowledge question.
  2. Example — one case, specific enough that a reader could check it.
  3. Analysis — the longest move. Explain the example in terms of the knowledge question: what it shows about the method, the evidence standard, or the limits of the area of knowledge.
  4. Counter-claim — the strongest opposing reading of the same example, or a different case that pulls the other way.
  5. Evaluation — why your reading survives the counter, or what exactly it has to give up.
  6. Implication — one sentence on what is now different. This is the move students skip and the one examiners notice.
What this earns

The impression an examiner forms is built almost entirely from moves 3, 5 and 6. Analysis is what makes an example count; evaluation is what makes the essay critical rather than balanced; implication is what makes it worth reading. A student who does all three consistently, with two AOKs and two real examples, is in the top band. A student who does moves 1–2 well and 3–6 weakly is mid-band no matter how elegant the prose.

4.3 The conclusion

Two jobs, in this order: (1) answer the title in one sentence, in your own words, without re-importing the title's phrasing; (2) state what follows from that answer. Job 2 is the one that separates a strong essay from a competent one, and it is where you should spend your last 60 words.

Weak conclusionStrong conclusion
"In conclusion, certainty is more attainable in mathematics than in the natural sciences, although both have their strengths. It is important to consider different perspectives on knowledge." "If certainty is bought by giving up content, then the natural sciences are not a failed attempt at mathematics; they are a different bargain. The practical consequence is that we should stop treating scientific revision as a defect and start asking, of any knowledge claim, what it had to give up to be as secure as it is."
Three ways to ruin a conclusion
  1. Restating the introduction in different words. The reader has already read the introduction; telling them again signals that nothing happened in between.
  2. Introducing a new example. If it mattered, it belonged in the body. In the conclusion it reads as an afterthought and cannot be analysed.
  3. Copping out. "It depends on the situation and everyone is entitled to their view." You have just spent 1,600 words arguing that some views are better justified than others. Say which.

6. Examples: the single biggest differentiator

If you improve one thing about your essay, improve this. The gap between a mid-band and a top-band essay is almost always the handling of examples, and it is not close.

The rule

One specific example explored in depth beats three named in passing. And every example must be explained in terms of the knowledge question, not merely described. An example that is described is decoration; an example that is analysed is evidence. The test: after every example, write a sentence beginning "This matters because…" If you cannot, the example has done no work.

Weak handlingStrong handlingWhat changed
"Science is provisional. For example, Newtonian physics was replaced by Einstein's theory of relativity." "Newtonian mechanics was not refuted so much as relocated: at speeds small compared with c it remains the theory we use to land spacecraft. What changed was the domain over which we claim the theory holds, not the truth of everything it says. That is what scientific revision actually consists of — and it is why calling the older theory 'false' misdescribes both the practice and its success." The example is no longer a name; it is a case with a structure. The student then says what follows from that structure.
"Mathematics is certain because 2 + 2 = 4 and this can never change." "The necessity of '2 + 2 = 4' is conditional: it follows from the Peano axioms. The discovery that the parallel postulate is independent of Euclid's other four shows what happens when a 'self-evident' axiom is revealed as a choice — the certainty survives, but it attaches to the implication, not to the antecedent." Used an example from your own course, and used it to make a distinction rather than to illustrate a platitude.
"Indigenous knowledge is also important. For example, Aboriginal Australians have knowledge of the land." Don't use this as a passing reference. Either use a specific, named practice — with its transmission method and its validation standard — or leave it out. Naming a community and gesturing at its knowledge is worse than silence; it borrows authority you have not examined. Specificity is a courtesy owed to the example. Generic references to any culture read as filler.
How to upgrade an example in four questions
  1. Can I make it more specific? Not "an experiment" but "the 2019 Event Horizon Telescope images, assembled from eight radio observatories by an algorithm that had to choose among infinitely many images consistent with the data".
  2. What method produced it? Name the method explicitly — proof, controlled experiment, statistical inference, source criticism, close reading.
  3. What does the method buy, and what does it cost? Every method buys something and pays for it. Naming the price is analysis.
  4. What would a critic say about this example? If you cannot answer, you have not chosen a real example; you have chosen an illustration.
The four example mistakes
  1. Example-dropping. The example appears, is described for 80 words, and the paragraph moves on. No analysis, no link back.
  2. The famous-example reflex. Galileo, the atomic bomb, the flat earth, Monty Hall. Fine if you know them deeply; disastrous if you know them from a TOK textbook, because you will reproduce the textbook's analysis rather than your own.
  3. Three examples where one would do. Three shallow examples signal that you do not trust any of them.
  4. The example that is not about knowledge. A story about a scientist's courage is not an example of anything epistemic. If the example would work equally well in a CAS reflection, it is the wrong example.

7. The criteria, as behaviour

The essay is marked holistically against one judgement — how clearly, coherently and critically you explore the title. You cannot game a rubric you cannot see, but you can recognise the behaviours that produce each impression. Read the table as a description of the reader's experience.

What the reader noticesTop-band essayMid-band essay
Focus on the title Every paragraph can be shown to bear on the title in one sentence. The title's key term is interrogated, not just used. The essay drifts into a general discussion of the topic. The title is quoted at the top and not seen again until the conclusion.
Argument A position is taken in the first 200 words and is developed, qualified and defended. The tension is held throughout. Perspectives are listed — "some argue X, others argue Y" — and the essay stops there. Balance is mistaken for evaluation.
Counter-claim The strongest opposing case is stated at length and then answered, or conceded partially and the consequences traced. The counter-claim is a sentence, or a straw man, or appears only in the conclusion as a qualification.
Examples Two or three, specific, understood from inside, each analysed for what it shows about the method of its area of knowledge. Named, described, and left. Often chosen because they are famous rather than because they are precise.
Areas of knowledge Used purposefully: the contrast between their methods drives the argument. Name-dropped as labels. "In the natural sciences…" followed by a claim that would be unchanged in any other area.
Conclusion Answers the title and states what follows — a consequence, a reframe, a cost. Restates the introduction, or sums up, or refuses to decide.
Voice Precise, hedged where hedging is honest, committed where commitment is warranted. Either absolute ("this proves that…") or permanently hedged ("it could be argued that maybe…"), with no principle deciding which.
Why this works Why "balance" is the enemy of a top-band essay
  1. The task asks for a critical exploration, not a survey. Critical means you take a position and test it.
  2. A balanced essay shows that you understood two sides. It does not show that you can adjudicate between them, and adjudication is the skill being assessed.
  3. Examiners reading fifty essays in a day form their impression from the moments where the writer decides. Decision is memorable; balance is forgettable.
  4. The fix is not to become dogmatic. It is to decide, then to say precisely what your decision costs. "This reading is stronger because…, and it requires us to give up…" is both committed and critical.
  5. When balance is right: when the title itself asks whether something is possible "to what extent", your answer can legitimately be "only under conditions X and Y" — but those conditions must be specified, not left as a shrug.

Hedging trap

Hedging every sentence does not make you careful; it makes you unreadable and it makes the reader suspect you have not decided. Hedge where you are genuinely uncertain — about the strength of an inference — and commit where you have an argument. "This suggests that…" is good. "It could perhaps maybe be argued that possibly…" is not English and not an argument.

9. Three full essay plans

These are complete plans for three practice titles written in the style of IB prescribed titles. Each gives the title, the knowledge questions, the thesis, a section-by-section bullet plan with the examples, and the conclusion's implication. Use them as models for your own planning, not as templates to copy.

Essay plan A Certainty — mathematics and the natural sciences

Title. "To what extent is certainty attainable in the production of knowledge? Discuss with reference to mathematics and the natural sciences."

Knowledge questions.

  1. Does a proof deliver certainty about the world, or only about the consequences of axioms?
  2. Can empirical methods deliver certainty at all, or only degrees of confidence conventionally treated as sufficient?
  3. If certainty in mathematics is bought by giving up content, is certainty the right standard by which to rank knowledge?

Thesis. Certainty is not a single property that areas of knowledge possess in different quantities; it is a trade-off between necessity and content, and mathematics and the natural sciences sit at opposite ends of it. Once this is seen, the natural sciences are not a failed attempt at mathematics, and "certainty" is a poor measure of knowledge.

Section plan.

  1. Introduction (~170 words). Quote the title. Split "certainty" into two kinds: certainty that and certainty about. Thesis as above.
  2. Mathematics (~390 words). Example: the independence of the parallel postulate — two millennia of attempts to prove it from the other four, then the construction of consistent non-Euclidean geometries. Analysis: the certainty of a proof attaches to the implication, not to the antecedent; axioms are chosen, not known. Counter: the choice is not arbitrary — it is constrained by consistency, fruitfulness and connection to other structures, and that constraint is itself knowledge. Evaluation: the counter shows mathematics is not arbitrary, but it does not restore certainty about the world. Implication: mathematical certainty is real and content-free.
  3. Natural sciences (~390 words). Example: the 5σ convention in particle physics — a threshold adopted because of the look-elsewhere effect and the cost of false positives. Analysis: the number is not a logical standard but a negotiated one; the knowledge it licenses is genuinely about the world, and genuinely revisable. Counter: revisability is a strength, not a defect — a method that could not be wrong could not learn. Evaluation: granted; but that means certainty is not available here in any form, only warranted confidence.
  4. The hardest counter (~380 words). The objection: measurement is theory-laden, so science never touches the world; and mathematics is unconstrained choice, so it never touches anything. If both are right, we know nothing. Answer: the objection assumes certainty is the only acceptable outcome. Newtonian mechanics remains usable within a domain — that is not nothing, and it is not certainty. Concession: we must give up the idea that knowledge comes in one currency.
  5. Conclusion (~180 words). Certainty is attainable only where content is surrendered. Implication: we should stop ranking areas of knowledge by how certain they are and start asking, of each claim, what it had to give up in order to be as secure as it is.
Why this plan is strong: both examples come from the student's own HL work, the two areas of knowledge contrast in method rather than in subject matter, and the third section answers an objection to the whole thesis rather than to one paragraph.
Essay plan B Models and reality — natural sciences and the arts

Title. "How can we judge whether a representation of the world is adequate? Discuss with reference to the natural sciences and the arts."

Knowledge questions.

  1. Adequate for what — prediction, understanding, or action? Does the purpose determine the standard?
  2. Can a representation be adequate when it is known to be false in every detail?
  3. Who is entitled to judge adequacy — the maker, the community of specialists, or the audience?

Thesis. Adequacy is never a property of a representation in itself; it is a relation between a representation, a purpose and a community entitled to judge. The natural sciences and the arts differ not in how accurate their representations are but in how explicitly the purpose is stated and who is licensed to arbitrate.

Section plan.

  1. Introduction (~170 words). Set up "adequate" as a three-place relation. Reject the correspondence theory as the only standard: it makes every map a failure.
  2. Natural sciences (~390 words). Example: the ideal gas model — point particles, no interactions, a law that no real gas obeys, used daily to predict behaviour to within a percent. Analysis: adequacy here means "sufficiently close for the purpose within a stated domain", and the domain is stated in advance. Counter: a model that is false in its assumptions may still be false in its consequences outside the tested domain — adequacy is always local. Evaluation: that locality is what makes the standard workable rather than embarrassing.
  3. The arts (~390 words). Example: a first-person novel that represents consciousness — no checkable correspondence, yet readers treat some accounts as more truthful than others. Analysis: the standard shifts from correspondence to recognition — a community of readers judges whether the account is adequate to an experience. Counter: recognition is parochial; a reader outside the community cannot test it. Evaluation: the same charge can be made against peer review, which also certifies for a community; the difference is the explicitness of the criteria, not the social location of the judgement.
  1. The hardest counter (~380 words). The objection: if adequacy is purpose-relative, then any representation is adequate for some purpose, and the concept collapses. Answer: purposes themselves can be criticised; adequacy claims are contestable at the level of purpose, not just of fit. Concession: this means the judgement is never purely epistemic — it always carries values.
  2. Conclusion (~180 words). Adequacy is a three-place relation. Implication: the interesting question is never "is this true to the world" but "adequate to what end, and who decided that end matters" — which makes the evaluation of representation unavoidably value-laden in both areas of knowledge.
Why this plan is strong: it refuses the easy "science is accurate, art is subjective" opposition and replaces it with a single framework that generates real differences.
Essay plan C Understanding and knowledge — mathematics and the human sciences

Title. "Does it matter if knowledge is produced by a process we do not fully understand? Discuss with reference to two areas of knowledge."

Knowledge questions.

  1. Is understanding a requirement for knowledge, or a preference of certain communities of knowers?
  2. If a process produces reliable outputs but no reasons, what exactly is missing — knowledge, or something else?
  3. Does opacity matter more in some areas of knowledge than others, and if so, why?

Thesis. Understanding is not a constituent of knowledge but a condition on our ability to be accountable for it. Where accountability matters, opacity is a defect; where it does not, opacity is merely unfamiliar. This explains why opacity is tolerated in mathematics and contested in the human sciences.

Section plan.

  1. Introduction (~170 words). Distinguish knowing that from knowing why. State that the interesting question is what the loss of "why" costs.
  2. Mathematics (~390 words). Example: computer-assisted proof — the four-colour theorem, and contemporary formal-verification projects in which a proof is checked by software no human reads in full. Analysis: the certainty of mathematics was traditionally guaranteed by a community of human checkers; the guarantee is now partly empirical, resting on claims about hardware and compilers. Counter: many long human proofs are also never checked in full by one person — the community checks them collectively and over time, and this was always a social rather than an individual guarantee. Evaluation: the counter is strong; the honest conclusion is that the change is one of degree, which is itself a significant admission.
  3. Human sciences (~390 words). Example: a model that predicts an outcome — recidivism, diagnosis, creditworthiness — better than any human and cannot say why. Analysis: here the loss is not of certainty but of accountability: a decision made about a person must be defensible to that person. Counter: human decision-makers are also opaque, and demonstrably biased; an opaque model may be fairer in aggregate. Evaluation: true — but fairness in aggregate and answerability to an individual are different goods, and only one of them is served by opacity.
  1. The hardest counter (~380 words). The objection: this argument merely restates a preference for comprehensibility. Answer: it does, and the defence is that accountability is constitutive of knowledge claims made about persons, not a preference about them. Concession: where no one is affected — classifying galaxies, say — the objection stands and opacity costs nothing.
  2. Conclusion (~180 words). Understanding matters where knowledge must be answerable. Implication: the question to ask of any opaque system is not "does it work" but "to whom must this be answerable", and the answer is different in different areas of knowledge.
Why this plan is strong: it uses the student's computer science without treating computer science as an area of knowledge, and it produces a conclusion that is a decision rule rather than a compromise.

10. Two model body paragraphs, written out in full

Both of these are complete, in the register you should be aiming for. Read them not for their opinions but for their shape: where the analysis starts, where the counter-claim is stated at full strength, and where the implication lands.

Model paragraph 1 Mathematics — certainty and the independence of the parallel postulate

The claim that mathematics delivers certainty is usually defended by pointing to the permanence of its results, but the more interesting question is what that permanence is attached to. Euclid's fifth postulate — that through a point not on a given line exactly one parallel can be drawn — was treated for over two thousand years as a truth that ought to be derivable from the other four. The attempts failed, and in the nineteenth century Gauss, Bolyai and Lobachevsky independently constructed geometries in which the other four postulates hold and the fifth does not, without contradiction. The postulate, in other words, is independent: it cannot be proved from the others, because a coherent world in which it is false can be specified. What follows is uncomfortable for the ordinary picture of mathematical certainty. The axioms were never self-evident truths that we knew; they are choices, constrained by consistency and fruitfulness but choices nonetheless. The necessity that mathematics delivers is therefore the necessity of the conditional: given these axioms, this conclusion must follow. It is absolute certainty about the implication and no certainty whatsoever about whether the antecedent describes anything real. A defender of the stronger claim has a real reply: the choice of axioms is not arbitrary, and the fact that some axiom systems generate deep, interconnected, applicable structures while others generate nothing is itself a discovery about a landscape we did not invent. That reply is forceful, and it saves mathematics from arbitrariness. But it does not restore what was lost — it relocates the achievement from knowing the axioms to be true to knowing them to be fruitful, which is a different and more contingent kind of knowledge. If this is right, then mathematical certainty is best understood as the price of content: mathematics is maximally certain precisely because it declines to say anything about the world, and any ranking of areas of knowledge by their certainty will therefore rank them by how little they claim.

What to copy: the counter-claim occupies four sentences and is stated at full strength before being answered; the paragraph ends on an implication, not a summary; and the example is analysed rather than described.
Model paragraph 2 Human sciences — opacity, accountability and machine prediction

The objection to opaque knowledge-producing systems is usually put as a complaint about understanding: a model that cannot say why it reached a conclusion does not really know anything. But this way of putting it invites an easy rebuttal, since human experts are constantly unable to articulate the basis of a judgement they make reliably — the radiologist who sees that a film is wrong before she can say what is wrong with it. If articulation is the test, we must deny knowledge to the expert, which is absurd. The argument is better put in terms of accountability rather than comprehension. When a knowledge claim is made about a person — that she is likely to reoffend, that his loan should be refused — the person affected is entitled to more than accuracy; she is entitled to a reason she could in principle contest. The requirement is not epistemic but moral, and it is not satisfied by telling her that the system is accurate on average. Seen this way, the opacity of a model is not a defect in its knowledge but a defect in its answerability, and the two come apart. The strongest counter is that human decision-makers are also unable to give adequate reasons, are demonstrably inconsistent, and carry measurable bias; a system that is opaque but statistically fairer across a population may be the better instrument for exactly the people the accountability argument claims to protect. This has real force, and it would be dishonest to dismiss it. But fairness in aggregate and answerability to an individual are different goods, and a gain in the first does not purchase the second. A person refused credit by an opaque system is not compensated by the knowledge that similar people are, on the whole, refused less often than before. The implication is that the correct question to ask of any opaque knowledge-producing process is not whether it works or whether anyone understands it, but to whom it must answer — and that answer varies by area of knowledge in a way that a single standard of "understanding" obscures.

What to copy: the paragraph explicitly rejects the weaker version of its own argument before making the stronger one; the counter-claim is granted with "this has real force"; and the final sentence converts the discussion into a decision rule.

11. The five failure modes

Failure modeWhat it looks likeThe fix
Describing the area of knowledge instead of arguing. "The natural sciences use the scientific method, which involves observation, hypothesis and experiment. Peer review is important." Two hundred words, no claim. Replace every descriptive sentence with a sentence that contains a contested claim about the method: "The reliance on peer review makes the natural sciences dependent on a social mechanism whose reliability is itself untested."
Examples that are summaries. The example is narrated for a hundred words and then abandoned. Apply the four upgrade questions in section 6. Cut the narration; keep only the features of the example that bear on the knowledge question.
A conclusion that restates the introduction. "In conclusion, this essay has shown that there are different views about…" Write the answer to the title in one sentence, then a second sentence beginning "The consequence is…". Delete everything else.
No counter-claim, or a fake one. "Some people might disagree, but they are wrong." Find the version of the objection that would make you hesitate. State it in the language of someone who believes it. Then answer it. If you cannot find one, your thesis is not yet a thesis.
The essay wanders off the title. A prepared section on machine learning, inserted whole, bearing no stated relation to the question. After each paragraph, write one sentence beginning "This bears on the title because…". If you cannot, cut the paragraph. Do this on the printed draft with a pen.
The one-hour self-edit
  1. Print it. You will see things on paper that you cannot see on a screen.
  2. Mark the thesis. If you cannot find a single sentence that takes a position, write one.
  3. Mark every counter-claim. If there are fewer than three, the essay is not critical yet.
  4. Underline every example. For each, write in the margin what it shows about the method of its area of knowledge. If the margin stays blank, the example is decorative.
  5. Read only the first and last sentences of each paragraph. They should form a coherent argument by themselves. If they do not, the paragraphs are not connected.
  6. Cut 10%. Almost always possible, almost always an improvement.

12. Practice: three titles, three theses, three outlines

For each practice title below, write your own thesis and outline first, then compare. Judge your version on whether the thesis takes a position and whether the outline forces two real counter-claims.

  1. Title D. "Are some kinds of knowledge less open to interpretation than others? Discuss with reference to mathematics and history."

    Show model thesis and outline

    Thesis. Every claim requires interpretation somewhere in the chain from evidence to conclusion; the difference between mathematics and history is not the presence of interpretation but its location. In mathematics interpretation is front-loaded into the choice of axioms and definitions and then disappears; in history it is distributed across every step and never disappears.

    Outline. (1) Split "open to interpretation" into interpretation of evidence, of method and of conclusion. (2) Mathematics: the proof of a theorem is not interpreted once the definitions are fixed — but the fixing is contested (constructivism vs classical logic; the acceptance of the axiom of choice). (3) History: the same documents support rival narratives, and the selection of which documents count is itself interpretive. (4) Hardest counter: if interpretation is present in both, is the difference merely one of degree? Answer: the difference in where it sits has a consequence — mathematical disputes can be closed, historical ones generally cannot. (5) Conclusion: the useful question is not whether a claim is interpreted but whether the interpretation can be settled, and that is a property of the method.

    Where the marks come from: the essay converts an obvious-answer title ("yes, maths is less interpretive") into a structural claim about where interpretation operates.

  2. Title E. "What role do experts play in determining what we accept as knowledge? Discuss with reference to two areas of knowledge."

    Show model thesis and outline

    Thesis. Experts do not merely inform our acceptance of knowledge claims; they determine the standards by which claims are judged, and that power is what makes deference to expertise both indispensable and dangerous. The danger is not that experts are wrong but that the standards themselves escape scrutiny.

    Outline. (1) Distinguish experts as sources of answers from experts as setters of standards. (2) Natural sciences: peer review and the 5σ convention decide what counts as a discovery before the public ever sees it; deference is unavoidable because no one can re-derive everything. (3) The arts: critics and curators decide what is worth attention, and here the standard is openly contestable in a way that physics tries to prevent. (4) Hardest counter: without experts we fall into a populism in which nothing is better justified than anything else — the replication crisis is arguably a sign that scrutiny is insufficient, not that it is excessive. (5) Conclusion: the practical upshot is that the appropriate attitude to expertise is not scepticism about conclusions but scrutiny of standards.

    Where the marks come from: it separates two functions of expertise — answering and standard-setting — and then evaluates the second, which is the less obvious one.

  3. Title F. "Is the pursuit of knowledge ever complete? Discuss with reference to the natural sciences and the arts."

    Show model thesis and outline

    Thesis. Completion is defined differently in the two areas, and the difference explains why we experience the natural sciences as permanently unfinished and the arts as occasionally finished. In the natural sciences a question is complete when it is answered; in the arts a work is complete when it will bear no more interpretation without being damaged — and both standards can fail.

    Outline. (1) Set up two senses of "complete": the exhaustion of a question and the closure of a work. (2) Natural sciences: the ideal of a final theory is itself a research programme; each answer generates finer questions, and the 5σ result is an endpoint only by convention. (3) The arts: a work's completion is not the exhaustion of interpretations but their stability — Hamlet has not been exhausted, yet we do not consider the play incomplete. (4) Hardest counter: is the difference an illusion, since scientific questions also reach practical closure — we know enough about the quantum mechanics of silicon to build every computer in this room? Answer: practical closure is real and is a different thing from completion; conflating them is what makes the sciences look endlessly unfinished. (5) Conclusion: the pursuit of knowledge is complete only where we stop needing more, which is a fact about us rather than about the world.

    Where the marks come from: it refuses to answer "no" in one word and instead relocates the question — an implication that a conclusion can actually carry.
