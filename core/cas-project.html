The CAS project

One project, at least one month, ideally planned by the end of DP1. It is the only piece of CAS with an actual design problem in it, and the only one you can fail by starting too late. Everything else in CAS is about turning up; this page is about choosing what to turn up to.

Minimum one project ≥ 1 month, planning to completion

What the project actually is

The guide defines a CAS project as a collaborative, well-considered series of sequential CAS experiences, with a minimum duration of one month from planning to completion. Three words in that definition do all the work:

WordWhat it demandsWhat fails it
Series / sequential Connected experiences that build on each other. One session is not a project; eight sessions with a shape to them is. A one-off event, however large. A single sponsored run, a single performance, a single beach clean.
Collaborative Working with other people in a way that matters to the outcome. Something you could have done alone. Having friends "help" is not collaboration; needing them is.
Well-considered There was a reason for the design. You can say why this shape and not another. Something you stumbled into and labelled a project afterwards.
One strand or several?

The guide says a CAS project can address any single strand, or combine two or three. Many school handbooks state the stricter rule that it must combine two or more. Follow your school's rule. It is stricter, not wrong, and the project is more interesting when it spans strands anyway.

Project vs experience

Students constantly misclassify these two, and it matters because only the project has the one-month, collaborative, sequential requirements. Use this test: an experience is something you join; a project is something you build.

The five stages

CAS is commonly described as running through five stages, and they are a genuinely useful planning tool rather than a formality — investigate, plan, act, reflect, demonstrate. Confirm the exact wording your school uses, but the logic holds either way: find out what is needed, design a response, do it, think about it, and then show what came of it. Most failed projects skip straight from a vague idea to the doing.

Five tests for a project idea

Run any idea through these before you commit. An idea that fails two or more will be painful.

The tests
  1. Can it start within three weeks? Anything requiring permission from an outside organisation needs that lead time, and often much more.
  2. Does it need other people? Not "would be nicer with" — genuinely needs. If you could do it alone, it is an experience, not a project.
  3. Is there a real recipient or audience? Something exists at the end that someone else experiences. A project with no external endpoint tends to quietly stop.
  4. Can you sustain it for a month without it becoming a burden? The most common project failure is not a bad idea, it is an idea sized for a student with no exams.
  5. Would it still be worth doing if nobody credited it? If the honest answer is no, the reflections will read like it — and reflection is the evidence.

Four worked designs

These are templates, not suggestions to copy. Each is annotated with what makes it work and what would sink it.

Design 1 — Creativity + Service: a student-written teaching resource

You notice that the students you tutor keep failing the same maths topic. You write, test and revise a short self-study booklet for it, with a group of three, and run two after-school sessions using it.

  • Why it works: the collaboration is real (one person cannot test a resource on a class); it is sequential (draft → test → revise → use); there is a real recipient; it evidences outcomes 1, 2, 3, 5 and arguably 6.
  • What sinks it: writing it in a weekend without testing it. Untested, it is a creative writing exercise with no service component and no evidence of perseverance.

Design 2 — Activity + Service: a primary-school sports programme

Two of you design a six-week lunchtime football or badminton programme for a local primary school that has no coach, and run it weekly.

  • Why it works: unmistakably activity, unmistakably service, six connected sessions over more than a month, and you will be forced to adapt — which is the most reflective material you will get.
  • What sinks it: not confirming the school placement before planning. This is the single most common project delay, and it is why test 1 exists.

Design 3 — All three strands: a community event with a purpose

Organise a charity concert or exhibition: the performing or making is creativity, the setup and logistics are physical, the fundraising or audience is service.

  • Why it works: the three strands appear naturally, and logistics give you real problems to reflect on.
  • What sinks it: the event becomes the whole project. A one-night event with no preparation sequence and no aftermath is a single incident. The planning is the project; the night is a milestone.

Design 4 — Service with an ethical edge

Run a regular session at a community organisation — a care home, a migrant-worker support centre, a special-needs sports club — where you are not the expert and have to learn the setting first.

  • Why it works: this is where outcome 7 (ethics) is genuinely tested rather than claimed, because you will have to think about your own position.
  • What sinks it: treating the placement as something to photograph. Reciprocity is the requirement; if the relationship is extractive, say so in the reflection and change what you are doing.
The failure mode that actually bites

Almost nobody fails CAS for lack of a good idea. They fail because the project was not started by the end-of-DP1 interview, and the summer — the only long unbroken block in the diploma — passed without it. Then DP2 arrives with IAs, the EE, predicted grades and university applications, and CAS is the only thing with no deadline attached, so it goes last. Plan the project before the second interview, even if you plan to run it later.

Running it: the practical sequence

Week −4 to −1
Investigate. Talk to the organisation or the people affected. Write down what they asked for, not what you assumed they needed.
Week 1
Recruit collaborators and agree roles in writing. Confirm a supervisor — someone at the organisation or in school who can comment on your engagement.
Week 2
Plan: sessions, dates, what "done" looks like, what could go wrong. Put the plan in the portfolio before you start — dated plans are the best evidence of outcome 3.
Weeks 3–8
Act. Keep a dated log. Record what changed from the plan and why — that gap is the reflection material.
Throughout
Reflect after each session, not at the end. See the next page for how.
After
Demonstrate: the artefact, the feedback, the numbers, the photographs with consent, and a final reflection against the seven outcomes.
Evidence worth collecting

The dated plan; attendance or session logs; emails that show you arranging things; feedback from the organisation or participants; the artefact itself; a supervisor's comment; and photographs with consent. Notice that none of this is a certificate. Certificates prove you turned up; the plan and the logs prove you built something.

Practice

  1. Project or experience? Classify each, and rewrite the ones that fail so they would pass.

    • (a) A sponsored 10 km run for charity.
    • (b) Joining the school debating squad for two years.
    • (c) Three of you design, build and maintain a vegetable garden at a community centre over a term, with the produce going to a food bank.
    • (d) A two-week summer volunteering trip.
    Show the answer

    (a) Experience, not project. One event, you did not design it, nothing sequential. Repair: become the student organiser of the run — venue, permissions, sponsorship, marshals, publicity — over two months. Now it is collaborative, sequential and yours.

    (b) Experience. Sustained and worthwhile, but you joined something that exists, and it is a single strand. It belongs in your portfolio as an experience; it is not the project.

    (c) Project, and a strong one. Sequential (design → build → maintain), collaborative, more than a month, external recipient, and it spans activity, service and arguably creativity. It also gives you outcome 6 (food insecurity, sustainability) and outcome 7 (why a food bank exists) naturally.

    (d) Depends entirely on structure. Two weeks of unconnected days is a long experience, not a project. It becomes a project if you designed it, prepared for it, and it continues afterwards — for example running a fundraiser before it and a follow-up programme after it. Note also that two weeks can be less than a month, which is the minimum.

    The pattern: "join" is an experience; "design and run" is a project.

  2. Test an idea. A student proposes: "I will make a documentary about climate change in my city." Run it through the five tests and say what you would change.

    Show the answer

    Test 1 (can it start in three weeks) — probably pass, but only if the equipment and interview subjects are already accessible. If it depends on getting interviews with officials, this is where it dies; assume a month of lead time and secure one interviewee before planning anything else.

    Test 2 (does it need other people) — fails as stated. One person with a phone can make a documentary. Repair: build a small team with defined roles — research, camera, editing, outreach — and make the outreach role load-bearing by committing to a screening for a specific audience.

    Test 3 (real recipient or audience) — currently weak. "I will put it online" is not an audience. Repair: commit to screening it at a specific school assembly, community group or council meeting. A named audience turns output into outcome and creates a deadline.

    Test 4 (sustainable for a month) — risk. Documentary editing expands to fill all available time. Repair: fix scope in advance: a 10-minute film, four interviews, one screening, done by a named date.

    Test 5 (worth doing uncredited) — pass. The subject is real and the student is interested in it.

    Verdict: a good idea that fails tests 2 and 3 as written. Both repairs are cheap if made now and impossible in three months.

    Why this matters: the tests are not bureaucracy. Each one corresponds to a specific way projects collapse, and each failure is cheap to fix at the planning stage.

  3. The timing question. You are at the end of DP1 with no project planned. Your coordinator wants it discussed at this interview. What is the honest plan?

    Show the answer

    Present a scoped plan with a named start date and a named risk — do not present a vague intention. Specifically: what the project is, who else is committed (named, and who has actually agreed), the first concrete step and when it happens, the dates of the activity, and the one thing most likely to delay it with a mitigation.

    Then use the summer. The break between DP1 and DP2 is the only long block with no exams in it, and it is when projects should actually run. A project planned in June and executed in August is on schedule. A project planned in June and executed "in the autumn" is usually a project that gets squeezed out.

    What not to do: claim the project is underway when it is not. Your coordinator is not an examiner to be managed; they are the person whose job is to stop you discovering the problem at the final interview. A plan with a hole in it, stated honestly, is far more useful than a confident fiction.

    Why this matters: the second interview is a checkpoint, not a test. Its purpose is to surface exactly this kind of gap while there is still a summer left to fix it.

  4. Ethics under pressure. Your service placement asks you to photograph the people you work with for the organisation's social media. What do you do, and how does it become reflection rather than just a decision?

    Show the answer

    Consent first, individually, and revocably — and explain what the images will be used for in language the person actually understands. Consent given to an authority figure is not consent. Where the person cannot consent (children, adults with additional needs), consent comes from a guardian, and the default should still be restraint.

    Then ask the harder question: who benefits from the image? If the answer is the organisation's fundraising or your portfolio, and not the person in it, that is the ethical problem outcome 7 is designed to make you notice. The guide's requirement is that the rights, dignity and autonomy of everyone involved are respected; an image used to elicit sympathy does not obviously satisfy that.

    Turning it into reflection: write down the decision, the competing considerations, what you chose and what it cost. "I took the photographs because the organisation asked, and I now think the request served the organisation's funding more than the participants' dignity, so I stopped using identifiable images and here is what I did instead" — that is a strong outcome 7 reflection. It contains a real tension, a decision, and a consequence.

    Why this matters: outcome 7 asks you to recognise and consider ethics, not to be ethically pure. A documented dilemma you engaged with is better evidence than an untroubled clean record.
