The CAS project

One project, at least one month from planning to completion, planned by the end of DP1. It is the only piece of CAS with an actual design problem in it, and the only one you can fail by starting too late. Everything else in CAS is about turning up; this page is about choosing what to build, sizing it so it survives contact with your calendar, and leaving behind the evidence that proves you built it.

Minimum one project ≥ 1 month, planning to completion Discussed at the end-of-DP1 interview Owns: choosing · designing · planning · running · evidencing
Why the IBO insists on it

Almost every activity in a CAS portfolio is something a student joined. That is fine, and the guide expects it — but joining things does not reliably produce the two outcomes students find hardest: outcome 3 (initiate and plan) and outcome 7 (recognise and consider the ethics of choices and actions). You cannot evidence initiation by attending something someone else designed, and you rarely meet a genuine ethical dilemma unless you are the one making the decisions. The CAS project exists to force both.

There is a colder, more practical reason to care about it. CAS is not graded, so there is no mark drifting downwards to warn you. The project is the only component with a shape you can inspect months in advance — a plan, a start date, a series of sessions — which makes it the only part of CAS you can tell is going wrong before it is too late. Treat it as a scheduling problem with a design problem attached, and it is straightforward. Treat it as an essay you will write at the end, and it is where CAS failures come from.

Where this page stops

This page owns the project itself: choosing it, testing the idea, designing the plan, running it, and capturing the evidence. CAS — the requirement owns what CAS is, the three strands, the seven learning outcomes and the 18-month rule. CAS — reflection & portfolio owns how to write a reflection and how the three interviews work. Where those topics appear here, they appear only as far as the project needs them.

1. What the project actually is

The guide defines a CAS project as a collaborative, well-considered series of sequential CAS experiences, with a minimum duration of one month from planning to completion. Three words in that definition do all the work, and almost every rejected project fails on one of them.

WordWhat it demandsWhat fails it
Series / sequential Connected experiences that build on each other. The second session depends on the first having happened. One session is not a project; eight sessions with a shape to them is. A one-off event, however large and however well organised. A single sponsored run, a single performance, a single beach clean, a single bake sale.
Collaborative Working with other people in a way that matters to the outcome. If one of you disappeared, the project would have to change. Something you could have done alone. Having friends "help" is not collaboration; needing them is. Four people each doing their own task and stapling the results together is parallel play.
Well-considered There was a reason for the design. You can say why this shape and not another, and what you asked the people affected before you decided. Something you stumbled into and labelled a project afterwards. A placement you were assigned, an existing club you attended for a term, a trip your school sells every year.
One strand or several?

The guide says a CAS project can address any single strand, or combine two or three. Many school handbooks state the stricter rule that it must combine two or more. Follow your school's rule. It is stricter, not wrong, it is not worth arguing about, and the project is more interesting when it spans strands anyway — which is why every design in section 4 below spans at least two.

1.1 Project versus experience

Students constantly misclassify these two, and it matters because only the project carries the one-month, collaborative, sequential requirements. Use this test: an experience is something you join; a project is something you build.

Trap — the retrospective relabel

The most common classification error is not a bad project, it is a good experience renamed. A student runs in the cross-country team for two years, and in DP2 writes "CAS project: cross-country" in the portfolio. It is not. They did not design it, nothing was sequential beyond repetition, and there is no plan predating the activity. The tell is always the same: there is no document dated before the activity started. If your project's earliest artefact is a photograph of the first session, you are writing history, not a project.

2. What the project is not

Four specific wrong shapes account for nearly every project a coordinator pushes back on. Learn to recognise them, and check your own idea against each one before you commit.

Not a solo essay

The project is collaborative and it happens in the world. A research write-up, a documentary you edit alone at home, a 3,000-word investigation — these may be excellent, but they are not CAS, and if they overlap with something you submit for a course they are actively inadmissible. The guide is explicit that CAS experiences must be distinct from what you submit for a course.

Not a one-off event

A single night, however spectacular, is a milestone inside a project — never the project. What makes an event legitimate is the six weeks of preparation before it and the follow-up after it: the planning is the project, the night is a checkpoint. If you deleted the event and nothing else would have happened, you do not have a project.

Not charity tourism

A short trip whose primary output is photographs of you with people in harder circumstances is the exact pattern outcome 7 exists to make you examine. Ask honestly: would the organisation have paid a local person to do this if you had not come? Does the money spent on flights exceed the value delivered? If the honest answers are uncomfortable, that is useful reflection material — but a trip that never asks the question is weak evidence, not strong evidence.

Not a group of friends doing parallel work

"We all did the same volunteering on Saturdays" is a shared experience, not collaboration. The test is structural: if one member left, would the remaining members have to redesign anything? If the answer is no, you had company, not a team. Give people roles that interlock — one owns the relationship with the organisation, one owns the session design, one owns the logistics — so that the work has to pass between you.

The four requirements, stated precisely
  1. Collaborative. At least two students (three or four is easier to sustain), with roles that depend on each other.
  2. Sequential experiences. More than one session or stage, each following from the last.
  3. Minimum one month from planning to completion. Note the wording: the clock starts at planning, not at the first session. A plan dated 1 June and a last session on 30 June satisfies the month. Two weeks of activity in July with no planning phase does not.
  4. Well-considered. You can articulate why this design, and you asked the people affected before you fixed it.

Nothing here requires the project to be large, expensive, impressive or abroad. A well-run six-week lunchtime programme at a nearby primary school satisfies every line. A fortnight in another continent does not automatically satisfy any of them.

3. The five tests for an idea

Run any idea through these before you commit. Each test corresponds to one specific way projects collapse, which is why the order matters: they are listed in the order that kills ideas. An idea that fails two or more will be painful, and every failure is cheap to fix in week one and impossible to fix in week eight.

3.1 Test 1 — can it start within three weeks?

This is a logistics test masquerading as an idea test. Anything involving an outside organisation — a school, a care home, a charity, a council, a sports club — needs lead time for a first contact, a reply, a meeting, a safeguarding check and a date. Three weeks is the optimistic case. Six to eight is normal. Some organisations will not reply at all, which is not a reason to abandon the idea but is a reason to contact three in parallel.

The repair when it fails: do not scale the idea down; scale the dependency down. Replace "we will run it at the community centre" with "we will run it at the community centre, and if that falls through we will run it in the school hall with students two years below us". Then make the first contact this week, because the answer determines the entire design.

Worked example 1 Test 1: a museum partnership

Idea. "We will run a Saturday science-activity morning for families at the city museum."

  1. Dependencies. A named contact at the museum; agreement to use a space; agreement on dates; confirmation of safeguarding requirements for working with children; possibly a risk assessment signed by the school.
  2. Honest lead time. First email to a confirmed date is realistically four to eight weeks. Museums have education departments that are busy and slow. A single email sent in week one and chased in week three is not a plan.
  3. Verdict. Fails test 1 as a project starting in three weeks. It is not a bad idea; it is an idea with a long fuse.
  4. Repair. Email three organisations in the same week — the museum, the public library, and your own school's outreach coordinator. Whichever replies first sets the venue. Meanwhile design the activity so it is venue-independent: three table-top stations that work in a library corner or a school hall equally well. The design no longer depends on the answer, so the answer cannot delay it.
The general rule: design the project so that the slowest external dependency is not on the critical path. Anything you can decide without permission, decide now.

3.2 Test 2 — does it need other people?

Not "would be nicer with" — genuinely needs. The question is whether there is any task in the project that one person physically or structurally cannot do. Coaching a football session for twenty children needs at least two adults. Filming while interviewing needs a camera operator. Running a stall while also running a fundraising campaign does not work.

The repair when it fails: make an external commitment that creates a second role. The classic move is to commit to a screening, a performance, a published resource or a session count that one person cannot staff. A documentary one person could shoot becomes a project when you commit to a screening for a named audience and to four interviews in three locations.

Worked example 2 Test 2: the documentary that fails and the repair that works

Idea. "I will make a documentary about climate change in my city."

  1. Test 2 as stated: fail. One person with a phone, a free afternoon and an editing app can make a documentary. Nothing about the design requires a second person, so any collaborators are decoration — and decoration is exactly what a coordinator will identify in the final interview.
  2. Repair A — split by artefact, not by task. Four roles that interlock: research (finding and briefing interviewees), camera, editing, and outreach. Outreach is the load-bearing one: the person who owns the screening is structurally necessary, because without an audience the film has nowhere to go.
  3. Repair B — commit externally. "We will screen it at the March assembly for Years 7 and 8, and at the April meeting of the residents' association." A named audience with a date creates a deadline, a second role, and the outcome-6 and outcome-7 material all at once.
  4. Why this is the right fix. After the repair, if the outreach person left, the project would have to be redesigned. That is the definition of collaboration, and it is the sentence you will want in your reflection: "our roles interlocked; when X was ill for two weeks we had to change the filming schedule because only she had the interviewee contacts."
Test: name the person whose absence would force a redesign. If you cannot, you have not built a team.

3.3 Test 3 — is there a real recipient or audience?

Something exists at the end that someone else experiences. "We will put it online" is not an audience. "We will hand the finished resource to the two Year 9 classes who tested it, and to the teacher who asked for it" is. A project with no external endpoint tends to quietly stop, because nothing and nobody is waiting for it.

The repair when it fails: name a specific group, a specific place and a specific date. Then ask that group what they would actually want — which also repairs the "well-considered" half of the definition.

Worked example 3 Test 3: two versions of the same idea

Version A (fails). "We will design and paint a mural about mental health awareness and post photos of it on our social media." The audience is whoever scrolls past. No one is waiting, no one asked, and the wall is presumably someone else's property that you have not mentioned.

Version B (passes). "The school counselling office has a plain corridor that Year 11 students sit in before appointments. We asked the counsellor what would help; she asked for something calm rather than a slogan. We will design three options, let the counselling office and two student focus groups choose, paint it over four weekends, and hand it over at the start of next term."

  1. The recipient is named and has agency — they chose between options, so the design was shaped by them, which is what "well-considered" means in practice.
  2. There is a deadline imposed by someone other than you.
  3. Outcomes produced: 2 (new skill, and the difficulty of designing for someone else's brief), 3 (you initiated), 5 (collaboration), 6 (mental health as a global issue), 7 (whose wall is it, who is the mural for, what if the students hate it).
  4. And the version that would fail ethically is the one where you design what you think looks good, paint it, photograph it and leave. That is decoration of someone else's space for your portfolio.
The difference: one sentence — "we asked them what they wanted, and we changed the design because of the answer."

3.4 Test 4 — can you sustain it for a month without it becoming a burden?

The most common project failure is not a bad idea; it is an idea sized for a student with no exams. Ask the brutal version of the question: in the worst week of DP2 — mocks, two IAs, university deadlines — will this still happen? If the answer is no, either shrink it or move it to the summer.

The repair when it fails: reduce frequency before you reduce duration. Six weeks of one 90-minute session beats three weeks of four sessions, because the calendar will forgive a light week and will not forgive a burst. Then fix the scope in writing: a stated number of sessions, a stated end date, and a stated minimum viable version that still counts as finished.

Worked example 4 Test 4: sizing a project against a real DP2 calendar

Idea. Four students will run a twice-weekly after-school homework club at a nearby primary school from October to May.

  1. Arithmetic. Two sessions a week for roughly 28 weeks is 56 sessions. Even at 90 minutes each plus travel, that is well over 100 hours per person. No student sustains that alongside six subjects.
  2. What actually happens. Attendance is strong in October, thins in November, collapses in the mock-exam fortnight, and the club is quietly abandoned in February. The portfolio contains eight enthusiastic entries and then nothing — which is worse than a small finished project, because it evidences an abandoned commitment rather than a completed one.
  3. Repair — shrink the frequency, fix the end date. One session a week for eight weeks, finishing before the mocks, with a named handover to a teacher who will continue it. Eight sessions, an end date that predates the danger zone, and a successor. Now it is sustainable and it has a legacy, which is better evidence than length.
  4. Add a minimum viable version. "If we only manage five sessions, the resource pack we hand over is still the deliverable." Defining the floor in advance means a partial delivery still ends with something real.
Rule of thumb: design for eight sessions, not for a season. A finished eight-session project beats an abandoned thirty-session project in every category that matters.

3.5 Test 5 — would it still be worth doing if nobody credited it?

If the honest answer is no, the reflections will read like it — and reflection is the evidence. This is not a purity test; it is a prediction about your own writing. Students who choose projects purely for their applications produce portfolios full of level-1 description, because they were never curious about the thing itself.

The repair when it fails: usually you cannot repair the motive, and you should choose something else. But note the useful nuance — a project you chose partly for strategic reasons and partly because you are genuinely interested passes. The failing case is the project where the interest is entirely absent.

Worked example 5 Test 5: the honest motive conversation

Idea. "I will organise a charity fashion show, because it looks good for applications to business courses."

  1. Test 5 as stated: fail. There is probably nothing wrong with organising a fashion show, but if the entire motive is the line on the application, the reflection will be about logistics and ticket sales — level 1 and 2 throughout — and outcome 7 will find nothing to bite on.
  2. The diagnostic question. What would you want to find out? If the answer is "nothing, I just want it to run smoothly", you are describing an event-management task, not a learning experience.
  3. Repair that works — attach a real question. "Every year the school throws away formal wear after one use. Can we run a rental-and-swap service, and does anyone actually want it?" Now there is a genuine unknown, a sustainability issue (outcome 6), an ethical dimension about who the service is really for (outcome 7), and a risk of failure that makes the reflection worth writing.
  4. Repair that does not work. Keeping the fashion show and adding a charity. Bolting a cause onto an event does not change your relationship to it, and coordinators read the difference immediately: the reflections still describe logistics.
Why this test is last and not first: it is the only test you cannot cheat by redesigning. It asks about you, not about the plan.
How to use the five tests

Do not run them in your head. Write one line per test in the portfolio, dated, before you start: lead time — first contact made on [date], reply by [date]; necessity — the role that cannot be dropped is [role], held by [name]; recipient — [named group], who asked for [thing]; sustainability — [n] sessions, ending [date], minimum viable version is [thing]; motive — the thing I want to find out is [question]. One page, ten minutes, and it is simultaneously your plan, your outcome-3 evidence and your first reflection.

4. Six worked project designs

These are annotated templates, not suggestions to copy. Each is worked through the same six lenses: the idea, why it passes the five tests, the plan, the risks, the evidence it generates, and the learning outcomes it hits. Read at least two from strands you were not planning to use — the transfer between them is where adaptations come from.

Design 1 Creativity + Service — a teaching resource you write, test and revise

The idea. You notice that the students you tutor keep failing the same topic. Four of you write a short self-study resource for it, test it on two real classes, revise it from what you learned, and hand the finished version to the department.

Why it passes the five tests.

  1. Lead time: pass. It runs inside your own school, so the only permission needed is from one head of department — a same-week conversation.
  2. Necessity: pass. One person cannot simultaneously teach a session and record what students do with it. You need an observer. That single structural fact creates a genuine second role.
  3. Recipient: pass. The classes who test it, and the department that receives the final version. Both are named.
  4. Sustainability: pass. Two writing sessions, two test sessions, one revision session, one handover — six sessions over seven weeks.
  5. Motive: pass if you actually want to know whether your explanation works. That curiosity is the whole project.

The plan. Week 1: ask two teachers what their students get wrong, and write it down — not what you assume, what they say. Week 2: draft the resource, four pages maximum. Week 3: pilot with one class while a teammate records which questions students get stuck on. Week 4: revise from the record. Week 5: second pilot with a different class. Week 6: final revision and handover, plus a one-page note for the teacher on what to change next.

The risks. Writing it in a weekend and never testing it — untested, it is a creative writing exercise with no service component and no evidence of perseverance. Also: making it too long. Four pages tested beats twenty pages untested, and you can always write a second volume.

Evidence generated. The dated draft; the observation notes from the pilots, including the questions that failed; the revised version; a short email from the teacher saying what they changed; attendance logs for the sessions.

Outcomes hit. 1 (you will discover you are worse at explaining than you thought), 2 (writing for a real reader is a new skill), 3 (you initiated it), 4 (six sessions across seven weeks), 5 (the observer role is structurally necessary). Outcome 6 is weak here unless the topic connects outward; outcome 7 is weak unless the teacher relationship raises something — it often does, around whether you are helping or showing off.

Why this design is the safest of the six: it has no external dependency, it is entirely inside the school, and it fails gracefully — even a bad resource produces excellent reflection material.
Design 2 Activity + Service — a primary-school sports programme

The idea. Three of you design and run a six-week lunchtime or after-school sports programme at a local primary school that has no coach for that sport.

Why it passes the five tests.

  1. Lead time: risk, and the reason this design appears second. The placement is the dependency. Contact three schools in week one, follow up after five days, and have a fallback of running the programme for the younger siblings of students at your own school.
  2. Necessity: pass, strongly. Twenty children and one adult is neither safe nor workable. You need at least two, and ideally three, to run stations and manage behaviour.
  3. Recipient: pass. A named class, a named teacher, a stated number of children.
  4. Sustainability: pass at one session a week for six to eight weeks.
  5. Motive: pass if you like the sport and are curious whether children can be taught it the way you were.

The plan. Weeks −3 to −1: contact schools, confirm the placement, agree dates and safeguarding requirements, and identify your supervisor (often the school's PE coordinator or a teacher). Week 0: write the six-session plan with a progression — session 1 is fundamentals, session 6 is a mini-tournament — and put it in the portfolio. Weeks 1–6: deliver, logging attendance and one thing that went differently from the plan each week.

The risks. Safeguarding, which is non-negotiable and school-specific: confirm in writing what checks you need before the first session, not in week four. Weather, if it is outdoors — book an indoor fallback room. And the biggest one: attendance collapsing in week four, which is normal and is the most valuable week in the whole project if you reflect on why.

Evidence generated. Session plans with dates; attendance registers; the progression document; photographs only with the school's written consent; a short comment from the class teacher; your weekly log.

Outcomes hit. 2 (coaching is a different skill from playing), 3 (you designed the progression), 4 (six to eight weeks), 5 (you cannot run it alone), 1 (you will find a weakness — usually patience or explanation). Outcome 6 and 7 are weak unless you engage with why the school has no coach, which is a genuinely global question about funding and physical education.

The reason this design is worth the placement hassle: you will be forced to adapt every single week, and adaptation is the raw material of reflection.
Design 3 All three strands — a community event with a purpose

The idea. Organise a concert, exhibition or performance: the making is creativity, the load-in, setup and stage management is physical activity, and the audience and fundraising is service.

Why it passes the five tests.

  1. Lead time: pass if you use a venue you control — the school hall — and fail if you need an external one with a booking system.
  2. Necessity: pass. An event needs a front of house, a technical lead, a programme and a money handler, and none of those people can also be on stage.
  3. Recipient: pass — a named audience and, if you fundraise, a named cause that has agreed to receive the money.
  4. Sustainability: pass at eight weeks of preparation for one night, which is the correct ratio.
  5. Motive: pass if you care about the performing or the cause, and fail if you only care about the photograph.

The plan. Weeks 1–2: secure the venue, the date and the cause; confirm who holds the money and how it is transferred — do this first, because cash handling is where these projects go wrong. Weeks 3–6: rehearsals or studio sessions, twice weekly, logged. Week 7: technical rehearsal, publicity, ticket or collection arrangements. Week 8: the event. Week 9: money transferred, feedback collected, debrief written, thank-you letters sent.

The risks. The event becomes the whole project. A one-night event with no preparation sequence and no aftermath is a single incident — the planning is the project, the night is a milestone. Money: never hold cash in a personal account; use the school's or the organisation's route, and get a receipt for the transfer. And scope: eight weeks of preparation for a ninety-minute event is already ambitious; do not add a second event.

Evidence generated. The dated plan; rehearsal logs; the publicity; the budget and the transfer receipt; audience numbers; feedback; the debrief. Note that the budget and receipt are among the strongest artefacts you will ever put in a CAS portfolio, because they cannot be produced retrospectively.

Outcomes hit. All seven are available here, which is unusual: 1 (organisation, performance anxiety), 2 (stage management, budgeting), 3 (you initiated it), 4 (eight weeks), 5 (heavy interlocking roles), 6 (if the cause is a global issue, and you can explain the connection properly), 7 (who the event is really for — the audience, the cause, or your portfolio; and the ethics of fundraising from a community to give to another community).

The design rule: the ratio of preparation to event should be at least five to one. If you are spending six weeks on six events, you have built a season, not a project.
Design 4 Service with an ethical edge — a sustained placement where you are not the expert

The idea. Run a regular session at a community organisation — a care home, a support centre, a club for adults or children with additional needs — where the setting is unfamiliar and you have to learn it before you can be useful.

Why it passes the five tests.

  1. Lead time: risk. Care settings and organisations working with vulnerable people have real onboarding: induction, sometimes background checks, sometimes training. Start asking in the autumn of DP1 for a placement you want in the summer.
  2. Necessity: pass if you go as a small team and divide the relationship — one person builds the ongoing relationship with the staff, another designs each session, a third keeps the log.
  3. Recipient: pass. The organisation and the specific people you work with.
  4. Sustainability: pass at weekly or fortnightly for a term.
  5. Motive: pass if you are genuinely interested in the setting; fail if you are collecting contact hours.

The plan. Confirm the placement and the supervisor in writing. Attend any induction. Spend the first two sessions observing and helping, not leading — this is the part students skip and it is where the learning is. From session three, propose something small you could take responsibility for, agreed with the staff. Review at the halfway point with the supervisor and adjust. End with a handover, not a disappearance.

The risks. Treating the placement as something to photograph. Consent here is individual, informed and revocable, and for people who cannot consent it comes from a guardian — with the default still being restraint. The second risk is the saviour narrative: assuming you know what the organisation needs because you read about it. Ask, and write down the answer even when it contradicts what you expected.

Evidence generated. Induction record; a log with dates and one observation each; the supervisor's specific feedback; any risk assessment; and — the most valuable one — the notes from the conversation where you got something wrong and changed.

Outcomes hit. 7 is the point of this design, and it is the outcome most portfolios lack. Also 1 (you will be confronted with what you are bad at), 2 (learning a setting), 4 (a term of attendance), 6 (why the organisation exists at all). Outcome 3 is weaker — a placement is something you join — unless you initiated the specific thing you took responsibility for inside it.

Why this is the hardest and most valuable of the six: it is the only design where you are not in control, and being not in control is what produces ethical reflection.
Design 5 Creativity + Activity — build something physical that outlives the project

The idea. Design and build something in the school or community that stays: a bike repair station with a student-run maintenance rota, a set of outdoor fitness equipment, a raised-bed garden whose produce goes somewhere specific, a marked running route with distance signage.

Why it passes the five tests.

  1. Lead time: risk, and larger than it looks. Anything fixed to a building or to ground needs the site owner's permission, and possibly a risk assessment and a maintenance commitment. Ask in writing, early, and ask specifically who maintains it afterwards — that question alone often decides whether the project is allowed.
  2. Necessity: pass. Design, fabrication, procurement and installation are genuinely different jobs, and lifting and installing are not solo activities.
  3. Recipient: pass — the users, named, and the site owner who receives it.
  4. Sustainability: pass at ten weeks with a build phase that has a hard end.
  5. Motive: pass if you want to make the thing; fail if you only want the opening photograph.

The plan. Weeks −2 to 0: permission, budget, and a sketch signed off by the site owner. Weeks 1–2: detailed design and materials list; cost it, and find out what it actually costs before you promise anything. Weeks 3–4: fundraising or a materials donation if needed. Weeks 5–8: build sessions, logged, with a named safety check at each one. Week 9: install and hand over, with a maintenance instruction sheet. Week 10: a follow-up visit to see whether anyone is using it.

The risks. Cost overrun — fix the scope, not the budget. Injury during build — agree what tools you may use, and who supervises. And the failure that matters most: building something nobody uses. The week-10 follow-up visit is not optional; finding out that nobody uses it is better evidence than not asking, and it is exactly the kind of honesty outcome 7 rewards.

Evidence generated. The signed-off design; the materials list and receipts; build session logs with dates and photographs of the process rather than the result; the handover document; the week-10 usage observation.

Outcomes hit. 1 (practical skills you did not have), 2 (design and fabrication), 3 (you initiated and planned it, with a paper trail), 4 (ten weeks), 5 (interlocking roles), 6 (sustainability, resource use, active lifestyles — pick one and develop it honestly), 7 (who owns it, who maintains it, what happens when it breaks, whether the community wanted it).

Why build projects produce strong portfolios: they generate physical, dated, hard-to-fake artefacts, and they end with a question — did anyone use it? — that is genuinely open.
Design 6 Service + Creativity — a digital system handed over to a community organisation

The idea. A small organisation — a food bank, a sports club, a language exchange, a youth group — needs something digital: a booking system, a simple website, an inventory sheet that works, a newsletter template. You build it with them, hand it over, train someone to use it, and follow up.

Why it passes the five tests.

  1. Lead time: pass. Small organisations reply faster than large ones, and you can approach three in parallel.
  2. Necessity: pass if you commit to training and handover — the build could be solo, but the handover, the documentation and the follow-up visit cannot be.
  3. Recipient: pass, strongly. A named organisation that will use the thing after you leave. This is the strongest form of the test.
  4. Sustainability: pass at eight to ten weeks with a defined scope.
  5. Motive: pass if you like building things that get used.

The plan. Week 1: meet them, and write down what they asked for in their words — this document is your requirements capture and it is gold. Week 2: show them a sketch or a mock-up and get it changed. Weeks 3–6: build in short cycles, showing a working version every fortnight. Week 7: write the documentation and run a training session for at least two people. Week 8: hand over, including the credentials and the source. Week 10: follow up — is it in use, and what broke?

The risks, and one is serious. Scope creep — they will ask for more once they see it works; agree a scope in writing in week 2 and treat additions as a second phase. Abandonment — a system only one person understands is a liability, which is why documentation and training are part of the project, not extras. And the serious one: the academic-honesty boundary. The guide is explicit that CAS experiences must be distinct from what you submit for a course. If you are a computer science student, this project must not become your IA, and your IA must not become this. Keep separate repositories, separate artefacts and separate write-ups, and tell your CAS coordinator and your CS teacher what you are doing before you start.

Evidence generated. The requirements document in the organisation's words; dated versions; the training session; the documentation; the follow-up email; the credentials handover.

Outcomes hit. 1 (translating someone else's need is a genuine weakness for most technical students), 2 (building for a real constraint), 3 (initiation, with a strong paper trail), 4 (eight weeks), 5 (handover needs a team), 6 (digital exclusion and who gets access to tools — if you develop it honestly rather than asserting it), 7 (who owns the data, what happens when you leave, whether you have created a dependency the organisation cannot maintain).

Why this design is included despite the boundary risk: the handover and the follow-up make it one of the few projects where you find out whether you were actually useful. Most CAS projects never get that answer.

5. Designing it: goals, roles, milestones and risk

Good projects are not more ambitious than bad ones. They are more specific. Everything in this section is about replacing a sentence you could not act on with a sentence you could.

5.1 Goal setting that survives contact with a calendar

SMART is a cliché, but it is a cliché that maps unusually well onto CAS, because the coordinator will literally ask "how do you know you finished?" Rewrite the goal as a measurable, dated statement, and you have simultaneously answered that question and produced your strongest piece of evidence.

ElementThe CAS versionVagueSpecific and dated
SpecificNamed recipients, named deliverable"Help younger students with sport.""Run six 45-minute badminton sessions for 4C at Ashgrove Primary."
MeasurableA number you can count afterwards"Raise awareness of food waste.""Divert 40 kg of surplus food per week from the school canteen to the food bank for six weeks."
AchievableSized for your worst week, not your best"Twice weekly from October to May.""Eight weekly sessions, ending before the mock exam fortnight."
RelevantConnects to at least one strand and one outcome you can name"It is good for university.""Activity (running the sessions) and service (the school has no coach) — outcomes 2, 3, 4, 5."
Time-boundA start date and an end date, both written down"Sometime next term.""Planning complete 12 June; sessions 3 July – 21 August; handover 28 August."
Trap — the goal that measures your feelings

"I want to become a more confident person" is not a project goal, because you cannot tell whether it happened and neither can your coordinator. Keep it — it belongs in your profile and it is legitimate outcome-1 material — but pair it with a goal you can count. "I want to become more confident speaking to adults I do not know, and the measure is that I will personally make the first phone call to each of the three organisations and report what happened." Now the growth has a test attached.

5.2 Roles in a group

Divide by artefact, not by task or by time. "You do weeks 1–3, I do weeks 4–6" produces two solo projects in sequence. "You own the relationship with the organisation, I own the session design, she owns the log and the evidence" produces three things that must connect — which is the definition of collaboration.

RoleOwnsWhy it is structurally necessaryFailure mode
Relationship ownerAll contact with the organisation or recipient; confirms dates, permissions and feedback.Someone has to hold the institutional memory. If contact is spread across four people, the organisation gets four contradictory emails and stops replying.Becomes the only person who knows anything, and everyone else is decorative.

With three people, merge roles — but merge them deliberately, and give each person a named deputy for their own role. A named fallback costs thirty seconds to agree and removes the single point of failure that ruins most group work.

5.3 Milestones

A milestone is a point at which something external exists: a document, a confirmation, a session that happened, a number. Four or five per project is right. Each one should have a date and an artefact, so that the project's progress can be checked without asking anyone how they feel about it.

MilestoneArtefact producedWhat breaks if it slips

5.4 The risk register and a contingency for each

Write five rows. That is the whole exercise, and it takes fifteen minutes. Every failed project had risks that everybody privately knew about and nobody wrote down.

RiskLikelihoodImpact if it happensContingency you commit to now

A member drops outMediumRoles collapse onto two peopleEvery role has a named deputy; scope reduced to the minimum viable version rather than abandoned.

Why the register is evidence, not admin

When something goes wrong — and it will — you will have a dated document predicting it and a contingency you committed to. The reflection then writes itself: "we expected the placement to be the risk; in fact it was attendance, which we had rated medium and had no real contingency for." That single sentence evidences outcomes 3 and 1 at once, because it shows you planned and it shows you can assess your own judgement. Students who did not write a register can only say "it was harder than expected", which evidences nothing.

6. Mapping the project onto the seven learning outcomes

You do not need seven activities for seven outcomes, and you should not try to force all seven into the project. What you need is a clear map: which project activity evidences which outcome, and where the evidence sits. Build this table before you start, then fill in the last column as you go — it becomes your seven-outcome audit for free.

OutcomeProject activity that evidences itThe artefact that proves itWhen captured
1 · Identify own strengths and develop areas for growth The role you chose because it exposed a weakness — the phone calls if you hate phones, the session design if you are disorganised. The profile written before the project, naming the weakness; a mid-project entry where the weakness actually caused a problem. Before session 1, and at the mid-point
2 · Demonstrate that challenges have been undertaken, developing new skills The skill the project forced: negotiation with an outside organisation, session planning, budgeting, safeguarding, public speaking, fabrication. The first attempt at the skill compared with a later one — a first email and a later email, a first plan and a revised plan. Continuously; capture the first attempt
3 · Demonstrate how to initiate and plan a CAS experience The whole planning phase: the recipient conversation, the five tests, the goal statement, the risk register. The dated plan, in the portfolio, before session one. Nothing else evidences this outcome. Before session 1 — this is the one you cannot backfill
4 · Show commitment and perseverance in CAS experiences Turning up across the full run, including the weeks that were inconvenient. The session log with dates and attendance; the entry for the week you nearly did not go. Every session, one line
5 · Demonstrate the skills and recognise the benefits of working collaboratively A moment where the group did something none of you could have done alone, and a moment where the division of labour caused a problem. The role list with named deputies; a decision-log entry recording a disagreement and how it was resolved. When it happens, not at the end
6 · Demonstrate engagement with issues of global significance The explicit connection between the local project and the larger issue — and the work you did to understand it, not just the claim. Trap — bolting outcome 6 on at the end

The most common weakness in project portfolios is a final paragraph claiming global significance for something entirely local: "this relates to global inequality because…". Coordinators discount this completely, and it can drag down otherwise solid reflections. If the genuine connection is thin, say so and develop the thin connection honestly — "a local coding club is not obviously a global issue, and here is the actual argument for why digital exclusion makes it one, and where that argument is weak" — rather than asserting a link you have not thought about. Then make sure outcome 6 has real evidence somewhere else in your CAS. See CAS — the requirement for the outcomes in full.

7. Evidence and documentation

Evidence is strong in proportion to how hard it is to produce retrospectively. That single principle explains the whole ranking below, and it is the reason a dated one-page plan is worth more than a folder of certificates.

ArtefactStrengthWhat it evidencesCan it be faked later?

Emails and meeting notes with the organisationVery strongOutcome 3, 5, 6No — email headers carry dates.

Trap — "I'll write it up later"

This is the failure mode of CAS, stated in five words. It fails for three separate reasons, and it is worth understanding all three because the fix follows from them.

  1. Memory. The specific moment that would have made a reflection real — the child who said something unexpected, the exact reason you changed the plan — is gone within a fortnight. What survives is a generalisation, and generalisations evidence nothing.
  2. Dating. An entry written in March about something that happened in August is visibly retrospective. Coordinators are not looking for perfection; they are looking for whether you were there. Retrospective entries read like they were written by someone reconstructing, because they were.
  3. Arithmetic. In March of DP2 you will have IAs, the EE, predicted grades and applications. The write-up you "will do later" is competing with six deadlines that have dates, and it has none. It loses, every time.

The mechanical fix: five minutes after each session, three lines — what happened, what was different from the plan, one thing you did not expect. Not an essay. Three lines, dated, and the whole problem disappears. See CAS — reflection & portfolio for what to do with those three lines afterwards.

How to use it — the evidence habit
  1. One folder, named, from day one. Everything goes in it: drafts, emails, logs, photographs, budgets.
  2. Date every file in its name. 2026-07-14_session3_log beats log_final_v3.
  3. Send yourself the plan by email the day you finish it. This costs nothing and produces an independently timestamped copy of the single most valuable artefact you own.
  4. Keep the first draft of everything. The improvement between draft one and draft three is outcome 2; if you overwrote draft one, you have thrown the evidence away.
  5. Consent in writing, before the photograph. Not afterwards, not verbally, not implied by the organisation's permission.

8. Reflection during the project

This page owns the project; CAS — reflection & portfolio owns reflection in depth. The only point that belongs here is about timing, because the project determines when the material exists.

Reflection has to happen while the project is running, because the project is what generates the material. Four moments are guaranteed to produce something worth writing, and you can put them in the plan in advance:

After the recipient conversation

The moment you find out what they actually wanted is the moment your design changes. Write down what you assumed and what they said. That gap is outcome 3 and outcome 7 material simultaneously.

After the first session

The first session never goes to plan, and the specific way it fails tells you what you did not understand about the recipients. This is usually the single best reflection in the portfolio.

At the mid-point

Compare the plan with reality in the same entry: what you said would happen, what did, and what you are changing. A revised plan dated at the mid-point is strong outcome 3 evidence, not an admission of failure.

When something goes wrong

A dropout, a cancellation, a complaint, an empty room. These are not interruptions to the project; they are the project, as far as your portfolio is concerned. Write them the same day.

9. Group dynamics

Most projects that fail do so for organisational reasons, not intellectual ones. Everything below is preventable with two habits agreed in the first meeting: a decision log and named roles with deputies.

9.1 Free-riders

The free-rider problem is almost always a design problem before it is a people problem. If roles are vague ("we'll all help with everything"), there is no such thing as not doing your share, because nobody knows what a share is. Fix the design first: named owner per artefact, with a deliverable and a date attached.

If someone still does not deliver, the sequence matters:

  1. Ask once, privately and specifically. Not "are you doing your bit?" but "the session plan for week 3 was due Friday — what happened?" Specificity makes it a fact, not an accusation.
  2. Offer the smallest real change. Often the person is overwhelmed by something else, and a smaller or different role solves it in one conversation.
  3. Raise it with the group once. Not to shame them — to agree a reallocation that everybody knows about.
  4. Tell your CAS coordinator before it becomes a crisis. This is the step everybody skips. Your coordinator cannot fix it in June if you tell them in June. Told in week two, it is a scheduling conversation.
  5. Reduce the scope rather than carry them. Carrying a free-rider through eight weeks turns your project into a resentment, and resentment produces terrible reflections.
Trap — the silent rescue

Two people quietly do everything, say nothing, and then write a final reflection about how they learned leadership. Coordinators see this pattern constantly and it evidences far less than the honest version: "we did not solve it, we absorbed it, and here is what I would do differently — I would have raised it in week two." The honest version evidences outcome 1 (you can see your own failure) and outcome 5 (you can describe what collaboration actually requires). The heroic version evidences nothing except that you are willing to work unpaid.

9.2 The decision log

One shared document. Every meeting adds five things: date, who was present, what was decided, who does what by when, and — the one everybody omits — what was rejected and why. When someone asks in week six why you are doing it this way, the answer is on the record instead of being reconstructed from three different memories.

The decision log is not admin. It is the artefact that makes outcome 5 evidence rather than assertion, and it is where the best reflection material in group projects lives: disagreements recorded in real time, before anybody knows how they turned out.

9.3 Conflict

Disagreement about the project is normal and useful; disagreement about who is doing the work is corrosive. Distinguish them. For the first kind, the fix is a decision rule agreed in advance — majority vote, or the person who owns that artefact decides, or escalate to the coordinator. Agreeing the rule before you need it is most of the work.

For the second kind, go back to artefacts: what was due, from whom, by when, and is it late? Turning a personality conflict into a factual question about a deliverable defuses most of it.

9.4 When someone drops out

It happens — illness, family moves, a change of school. Handle it in this order:

  1. Do not silently reassign. Put the change in the decision log with the date, so the record shows what happened and when.
  2. Activate the deputy you named for that role in week one. This is why the deputy exists.
  3. Re-test the scope against test 4. Three people cannot necessarily deliver a design sized for four. Cut sessions, not quality — eight sessions at full quality beats twelve at half.
  4. Tell the recipient if the change affects them, and tell them what you are doing about it. Reliability is part of the relationship, and how you handle a reduction is itself evidence.
  5. Reflect on it. A dropout is one of the richest moments in a group project: it is the point at which the plan met reality and you had to decide something. Write it the week it happens.

10. How people actually fail CAS

Almost nobody fails CAS for lack of a good idea. Here is what actually happens, in order of frequency. Each failure has a cheap fix and a point after which it has none.

The project was never started DP2 arrives with no project, no plan, and a vague intention to do something in the spring. At the end-of-DP1 interview Plan in June, run in the summer. After the October of DP2, there is no unbroken block of time left and every remaining option is worse.

Then DP2 arrives with IAs, the EE, predicted grades and university applications, and CAS is the only thing with no deadline attached, so it goes last. Plan the project before the second interview, even if you plan to run it later. A plan dated in June is outcome 3 evidence even if the sessions happen in August.

11. A realistic timeline

Twelve to sixteen weeks from idea to completion, with the planning phase deliberately front-loaded. Note how much of the timeline is before the first session: that is not padding, it is the part that satisfies the one-month rule and produces outcome 3.

Weeks −4 to −2
Investigate. Talk to the organisation or the people affected. Write down what they asked for, not what you assumed they needed. Contact three organisations in parallel, not one in sequence.
Weeks −2 to 0
Recruit and confirm. Named collaborators who have actually agreed. Roles with deputies. A supervisor — someone at the organisation or in school who can comment on your engagement. Confirm safeguarding and any checks in writing.
Week 0
Plan, date it, and put it in the portfolio before you start. Goal statement, roles, milestones, risk register. Email it to yourself. This single artefact is the strongest evidence in the entire portfolio and the only one you cannot produce later.
Week 1
Pilot. First session. Expect it to go wrong. Write the log entry the same day, naming one specific thing that surprised you.
Weeks 2–8
Deliver. One line per session, dated. Record what changed from the plan and why — that gap is the reflection material. Photographs only with consent.
Week 5
Mid-point review. Compare plan with reality in one entry. Revise the plan and date the revision. Ask the supervisor the two useful questions: what does doing this well look like from your side, and what do people usually get wrong here?
Weeks 9–12
Finish, hand over, evidence. The artefact, the feedback, the numbers, the transfer receipt, the thank-you letters, the final reflection against the seven outcomes. Update the outcome map.
Week 14
Follow-up. Is anyone still using it? This one visit produces more honest reflection than the previous twelve weeks, and it is the step almost everybody skips.

12. Practice

Answer on paper before opening the answers. Each of these is a decision you will actually have to make.

  1. Project or experience? Classify each, and rewrite the ones that fail so they would pass.

    • (a) A sponsored 10 km run for charity.
    • (b) Joining the school debating squad for two years.
    • (c) Three of you design, build and maintain a vegetable garden at a community centre over a term, with the produce going to a food bank.
    • (d) A two-week summer volunteering trip abroad.
    • (e) Attending a weekly art class for a term and producing a portfolio of work.
    Show the answer

    (a) Experience, not project. One event, you did not design it, nothing sequential. Repair: become the student organiser of the run — route permissions, sponsorship, marshals, publicity, race-day logistics — over two months. Now it is collaborative, sequential and yours.

    (b) Experience. Sustained and worthwhile, but you joined something that exists and it is a single strand. It belongs in the portfolio as an experience; it is not the project.

    (c) Project, and a strong one. Sequential (design → build → maintain), collaborative (three people, and maintenance requires a rota), more than a month, an external recipient, and it spans activity, service and arguably creativity. It also gives you outcome 6 (food insecurity, land use) and outcome 7 (why a food bank exists; whether a school garden is a real contribution or a gesture) naturally.

    (d) Depends entirely on structure. Two weeks of unconnected days is a long experience, not a project — and two weeks can be less than the one-month minimum. It becomes a project if you designed it, prepared for it, and it continues afterwards: a fundraiser before it, a follow-up programme after it, and a relationship with the organisation that predates the trip.

    (e) Experience. Genuine creativity and genuine commitment, but you are a student in a class. Repair: run the class — find a group of younger students who want to learn the technique, design a six-session course with two friends, and exhibit the results. The teaching is the service and the design is the initiation.

    Why this is worth knowing: "join" is an experience; "design and run" is a project. Every rewrite above adds the same two things: a design decision that was yours, and a sequence that depends on it.

  2. Convert an experience. A student proposes: "I will read to residents at a care home once a week for a year." Classify it against the four requirements, and convert it into a project with the smallest possible change.

    Show the answer

    As stated, it is an experience, not a project. It is sustained and genuinely worthwhile — excellent outcome 4 evidence — but it fails three of the four requirements: it is not collaborative (one person), not sequential beyond repetition, and not well-considered in the sense the guide means, because the shape was not designed by anyone.

    The smallest conversion: ask the activity coordinator one question — "what do the residents never get, that you wish they did?" — and then design something in response, with two other people, over six to eight weeks. If the answer is "music, we have no one who can play", you organise a six-week music session rota with a named programme per week. If it is "one-to-one conversation, we are too short-staffed", you design a paired visiting scheme with a training session for the volunteers.

    What the conversion adds: a design decision that is yours (outcome 3); roles that interlock, because six weeks of sessions cannot be staffed by one person (outcome 5); a sequence, because each week builds on the last (sequential); and a start point you can date. The reading continues alongside it as an experience.

    The trap in this conversion: do not stop asking after the first answer. The first thing an over-stretched organisation suggests is often the thing that is easiest for them, not the thing they most want. Ask twice, and write down both answers.

    Why this is worth knowing: most students already have an activity that is one question away from being a project. The conversion is usually smaller than they think.

  3. The mid-project pivot. Week three of six. You planned for twenty children; four turn up. Your group wants to continue as planned and "push harder on publicity". What should you do, and how does this become the best evidence in your portfolio?

    Show the answer
    1. Stop treating the number as the failure. The design was wrong, not the children. Publicity assumes the offer is right and only the marketing is weak — that is the least likely explanation and the least useful one to act on.
    2. Ask the four who came, and the staff, why. The usual answers are the timing, the competition with something else, or that the activity was not what was wanted. Any of those is fixable; none of them is a publicity problem.
    3. Change one variable and re-measure. Move the session to a different day, or change the activity to what the four actually enjoyed, or run it in the classroom during a lesson slot rather than at lunchtime. One change, so you can attribute the result.
    4. Log the decision with the date and record what you expected to happen.

    Why this is the best evidence in the portfolio. Almost every CAS portfolio contains only successes, which is why coordinators discount them. An entry that says "we designed for twenty and got four; here is what we asked, here is the single change we made, here is what happened, and here is what I still do not understand about why people did not come" contains a real failure, a real decision and an open question. It evidences outcomes 1, 2, 3 and 7 at once, and it is unmistakably written by someone who was there.

    The version that wastes it: continuing to run sessions for four children for three more weeks, saying nothing about the gap, and writing "it went well" each week.

    Why this is worth knowing: things going wrong is not an interruption to the project. As far as your portfolio is concerned, it is the project.

  4. Mapping. Take Design 2 (the primary-school sports programme) and map it against all seven learning outcomes: which does it evidence naturally, which weakly, and what would you add for the weak ones?

    Show the answer

    Natural:

    • 2 — coaching is a different skill from playing; session planning is new.
    • 3 — you designed the progression and wrote the plan.
    • 4 — six to eight weeks of attendance, logged.
    • 5 — you cannot run twenty children alone; the roles interlock.
    • 1 — but only if you name the weakness honestly. "I can play but I cannot explain, and I got frustrated in week three" is the sentence that makes this real.

    Weak:

    • 6 — a local sports programme is not obviously global. The genuine connection is physical-education funding and childhood activity levels; develop it with actual reading and say where the argument is weak.
    • 7 — nothing in the design forces an ethical question, unless you engage with why the school has no coach, or with consent for photographs, or with what happens to the children who cannot keep up.

    What to add: for outcome 6, one conversation with the class teacher about why the school has no coach, written up. For outcome 7, decision about photographs of children — a real dilemma with two defensible answers and a cost either way.

    Why this is worth knowing: a good project still leaves holes, and knowing where they are in DP1 is the entire point of the outcome map.

  5. Evidence ranking. Rank these from strongest to weakest and justify the ordering: a dated plan written before the project started; a certificate of participation; a supervisor's generic letter; a session log with dates and one line each; a photograph of you at the activity; the email thread with the organisation.

    Show the answer
    1. The dated plan. Contemporaneous, yours, and the only item here that evidences outcome 3. It cannot be backdated.
    2. The email thread. Independently timestamped by the mail system, and it shows initiation, negotiation and collaboration all at once.
    3. The session log. Contemporaneous and dated; even one line per session beats a polished retrospective essay.
    4. The certificate. Proves attendance, which is real but thin — outcome 4 and little else.
    5. The generic supervisor letter. Confirms you turned up and were pleasant. Almost no outcome content unless it names something specific you changed.
    6. The photograph. Weakest, and the only one with an ethical condition attached. It proves presence, not engagement, and without consent it should not be in the portfolio at all.
    Why this is worth knowing: evidence is strong in proportion to how hard it is to produce after the fact. If your portfolio is mostly certificates and photographs, it looks like participation rather than a project.

  6. Group dynamics. It is week four of a six-week project. One of four members has missed three of four sessions and has not delivered the session plan they own. You are the relationship owner. What do you do, in order, and how does it become evidence rather than just a bad experience?

    Show the answer
    1. Ask once, privately and specifically. "The week-3 plan was due Friday and it did not arrive — what happened?" Specificity makes it a fact rather than an accusation, and it often reveals an external cause.
    2. Offer the smallest real change. A different or smaller role frequently solves it in one conversation. Do this before escalating, because it is cheapest.
    3. Raise it with the group once, to reallocate visibly. Not to shame — so that everyone knows who owns what from now on.
    4. Activate the deputy you named in week one, and log the change with the date.
    5. Re-test the scope. Three people may not deliver a design sized for four. Cut sessions, not quality.
    6. Tell your coordinator now, in week four. Told early it is a scheduling conversation; told in March it is a crisis.

    Making it evidence. The decision-log entry dated in week four is the artefact. The reflection should name what you did, what you avoided doing, and what you would do differently — "I said nothing for two weeks because I did not want the conflict, and that delay cost us the week-3 session" evidences outcome 1 and outcome 5 far better than a story about how you carried the team.

    Why this is worth knowing: the free-rider problem is a design problem before it is a people problem. Vague roles make "your share" undefinable.

  7. The one-month rule. A student plans a project on 1 June, runs four sessions between 10 and 24 June, and finishes on 30 June. Another student does two weeks of intensive volunteering in July with no planning phase. Which satisfies the minimum duration, and why does the distinction matter?

    Show the answer

    The first satisfies it; the second does not. The rule is a minimum of one month from planning to completion. The clock starts at the planning, not at the first session. A plan dated 1 June and a completion on 30 June is exactly a month, with four sessions inside it.

    The second fails twice. Two weeks is shorter than a month, and there is no planning phase, so there is no start point from which to measure. Even four weeks of the same volunteering, with no plan and no sequence, would fail the "well-considered" part of the definition.

    Why it matters: the wording is doing deliberate work. The IBO wants planning to be part of the project, not a chore before it. That is also why the plan is the strongest evidence you can produce — it is the artefact the rule was written to require.

    Why this is worth knowing: students routinely measure the project from the first session to the last, which both under-counts their time and throws away the planning phase as evidence.

  8. The overlap trap. You are a computer science HL student. You want to build a booking system for a local youth club as your CAS project, and you also need an IA product. Explain the risk and the exact boundary you would enforce.

    Show the answer

    The risk. The guide is explicit that CAS experiences must be distinct from what you submit for a course. If the same artefact is both your IA and your CAS project, the CAS work is inadmissible — and the IA may be questioned too, because the client, the documentation and the evaluation would be shared. This is the trap that catches exactly the students who are trying to be efficient.

    The boundary, enforced from the start:

    • Different artefacts. Not two write-ups of one system. Either two different systems for two different users, or — better — a CAS project with no assessed component at all.
    • Separate repositories and separate documents, created on day one, so the separation is structural rather than retrospective.
    • Declared in advance to both your CAS coordinator and your CS teacher. Being transparent about the boundary is what makes it credible; discovering the overlap in DP2 is what makes it look like a shortcut.
    • Keep the CAS version genuinely collaborative. The IA must be your own work, and that constraint is a useful test: if a task could not appear in an individual IA, it is CAS.

    Also note the upside of separating them: the CAS project becomes the rehearsal. You interview a real client, capture requirements, and hand over a system — all before you start the IA, where those are the most heavily weighted marks.

    Why this is worth knowing: the overlap is usually accidental and always discovered late. Deciding the boundary in week one costs one conversation; discovering it in DP2 can cost you the CAS evidence entirely.

Vocabulary
CAS project — a collaborative, well-considered series of sequential CAS experiences, lasting at least one month from planning to completion.
CAS experience — a single activity or sustained involvement in one strand; something you join rather than build.
sequential — arranged so that each stage follows from and depends on the previous one.
collaborative — requiring other people in order to succeed, not merely allowing them to help.
well-considered — designed for a reason you can state, usually after asking the people affected.
strand — one of the three CAS categories: creativity, activity, service.
initiative — starting something yourself rather than being placed in it; the core of outcome 3.
recipient — the named person, group or organisation the project is for.
beneficiary — the party who gains from the project; should not only ever be you.
lead time — the delay between making a request and being able to act on it.
dependency — something outside your control that the project needs in order to proceed.
critical path — the sequence of tasks that determines the earliest possible finish.
placement — an agreed position volunteering with an external organisation.
supervisor — someone connected with the activity who guides you, is responsible for safety, and can comment on your engagement.
consent — informed, individual and revocable permission before photographs or data are used.
safeguarding — the checks and rules that protect children and vulnerable people working with volunteers.
reciprocity — a relationship in which both sides gain, rather than one side being helped at.
artefact — anything the project produced that can be pointed at: a plan, a resource, a build, a log.
contemporaneous — recorded at the time, not reconstructed later.
retrospective — written after the event, from memory; weak evidence in CAS.
milestone — a dated point at which something external exists and progress can be checked.
contingency — a pre-agreed alternative if a risk materialises.
risk register — a written list of what could go wrong, with a response for each.
decision log — a shared record of what was decided, by whom, and what was rejected and why.
scope — the agreed boundary of what the project will and will not do.
scope creep — the quiet expansion of a project beyond what was agreed.
minimum viable version — the smallest version that still counts as finished.
free-rider — a group member who benefits from the work without contributing to it.
handover — formally passing the project or artefact to the people who will use it after you.
stakeholder — anyone with an interest in how the project turns out.
