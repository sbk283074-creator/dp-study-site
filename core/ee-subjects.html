The extended essay in your subjects

A strong maths essay and a strong literature essay look almost nothing alike: different evidence, different structure, different idea of what an argument is. This page gives you the specific conventions, traps and evidence rules for each of your six subjects, then a comparison table so you can choose in five minutes. One rule first: you normally write the essay in the language of the subject — so a Chinese A essay is written in Chinese, and the reflective statement on the RPF is written in the same language as the essay. Confirm that with your coordinator, but plan on it.

What is confirmed and what is not

The 4,000-word essay limit, the 30-mark criteria split and the two pathways come from the IB's own published curriculum update for the extended essay with first assessment in May 2027. The per-subject conventions below are established practice and are stable across guides, but each subject group now has its own detailed subject-specific guidance — ask your coordinator for the current guidance for your subject group and read it in week 1. Where a rule is session-specific I have said so rather than inventing it.

How much first-hand material each subject demands

Before the detail, the decision that actually shapes your year. "First-hand material" means material you produce or work on directly — a derivation you write, data you collect, code you run, a text you close-read — as opposed to published literature you report. More first-hand material means more control, but also more work that can go wrong.

Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL

What examiners are looking for

Command of the mathematics, and a question that is genuinely mathematical rather than a historical or applied gloss on mathematics. The examiner wants to see you do mathematics and then judge what you have done: a derivation, a proof, a counterexample, a comparison of two methods, an investigation of where a theorem's conditions are necessary. Topic beyond the syllabus is welcome, but depth on a syllabus topic beats shallowness on an exotic one.

The characteristic shape of a strong maths EE

  1. Introduction — the question, why it is not settled by a standard result, and the argument you will run.
  2. Definitions and known results — stated precisely, with the conditions attached. This is criterion B and it is not optional: in mathematics, knowing the hypotheses is the knowledge.
  3. The main construction — numbered lemmas, propositions or steps, each proved, each used by the next.
  4. A test of the result — a worked case, a counterexample, a computation that shows the bound is sharp, or a case where the method fails.
  5. Evaluation — how strong is the result? Is the condition necessary or merely sufficient? What does it not cover?
  6. Conclusion — the answer, the precise conditions under which it holds, and the natural next question.
The traps that cap maths essays
  1. Textbook exposition with no argument. "The Riemann integral" written beautifully is still a C. If no sentence in your essay is false-or-true-but-contestable, there is no argument.
  2. An application essay that is really another subject. "The mathematics of music" that spends 3,000 words on acoustics is a physics essay. Keep the mathematics load-bearing.
  3. Hand-waving at the hard step. "It can be shown that…" at the one place where the actual mathematics happens. Examiners look for exactly that sentence.
  4. No conditions on theorems. Stating a result without its hypotheses is the fastest way to lose criterion B marks in this subject.
  5. Recycling a syllabus topic without extension. You must go beyond what the course already examines, in depth if not in topic.
What counts as evidence, and how to cite it

In mathematics the evidence is the reasoning. A proof you write yourself is primary evidence; so is a computation you carry out. Published proofs, theorems and papers are your secondary sources and are cited like any other — but you must make clear which steps are yours and which are quoted. Convention: state quoted results as "Theorem (Name, year)" with a citation, then prove everything else yourself. If you adapt someone's proof, say "following the argument of …" and cite. Cite textbooks for standard results, journal articles for recent or specific ones, and give the exact statement you are using, not just a reference to the whole book.

Physics HL

What examiners are looking for

Physics essays are judged on whether you understand the model, not just the phenomenon. The examiner wants to see a physical model stated with its assumptions, a consequence derived or measured, and then a judgement about where the model holds and where it breaks. Personal engagement with the physics — your own measurement, your own re-analysis of published data, your own numerical solution — is what lifts the essay, but only when it serves the argument.

The characteristic shape of a strong physics EE

  1. The model and its assumptions — stated explicitly. This is the single biggest difference from an IA: the assumptions are the object of study.
  2. What the model predicts — derived, or quoted with a citation and then checked.
  3. The test — your own data, published data you re-analyse, or a numerical solution; with an uncertainty budget that says what the test can and cannot resolve.
  4. Comparison — residual against uncertainty, not "it roughly agrees".
  5. Evaluation of the model — which assumption failed, by how much, and what that implies about the domain of validity.
The traps that cap physics essays
  1. An IA-style practical that never becomes an argument. "Investigating the effect of length on the period of a pendulum" is an IA. It asks what happens. The EE must ask which explanation accounts for what happens — see the exemplar on the writing page.
  2. "Human error". Never write it. Name the effect, estimate its size, and say whether it matters.
  3. Data without an uncertainty budget. A number with no uncertainty cannot be compared with a prediction, so there is no evaluation and criterion D suffers.
  4. Quoting a formula with no derivation or justification. Criterion B: show where the model comes from.
  5. Over-claiming from a school-scale experiment. "This proves the theory wrong" when you have measured to 2%. Say what your precision can actually support.
Evidence and citation

Legitimate evidence: your own measurements with a full uncertainty analysis; re-analysis of a published dataset (cite the dataset and its authors); a numerical solution you code and validate against an analytic case; and published experimental or theoretical results used as comparison. Cite papers for specific results and coefficients — "Nelson & Olsson (1986) give the coefficient of the \(\theta^{4}\) term as \(11/3072\)" — not as vague appeals to authority. Cite datasets with their repository and version. Cite any code you did not write. Your own raw data belongs in an appendix, with a sample in the main text.

Computer science HL

What examiners are looking for

A technical investigation grounded in computational principles, not a software development project. The deliverable is an essay with an argument; code, simulations and datasets are the evidence. Strong essays apply relevant theory — complexity, data structures, security, machine-learning evaluation — to a focused question, define measurable evaluation criteria in advance, and then explain why the results came out as they did. A rigorous literature-based essay is equally valid; a descriptive overview is not.

The characteristic shape of a strong CS EE

  1. The computational claim — usually about performance, correctness, or the behaviour of a class of algorithms.
  2. Theoretical expectation — what complexity analysis or the literature predicts.
  3. Experimental or analytical design — with the evaluation criteria fixed before you run anything: accuracy, precision, recall, throughput, memory, key size. Fixing them afterwards is the most common methodological error in this subject.
  4. Results and interpretation — not "X was faster", but "X was faster because …, and the gap closes when …".
  5. Evaluation — threats to validity: hardware, runtime, dataset bias, choice of hyperparameters, and what your result does not generalise to.
The traps that cap CS essays
  1. A software project with no evaluative essay. "I built an app" is the IA, not the EE. If your argument survives deletion of the program, the program is decoration.
  2. Reusing IA data or output. Not permitted. The EE must not duplicate another component.
  3. Benchmarking without controlling for anything. Two algorithms on different data, different hardware, different input sizes — the comparison means nothing.
  4. Performance claims from a single run. One measurement is not a result. Repeat, and report variance.
  5. Describing the algorithm instead of investigating it. Explanation is not research.
  6. Forgetting that datasets are sources. Cite them, and discuss their bias — dataset bias is excellent criterion D material.
Evidence and citation

Legitimate evidence: your own implementations and benchmarks (with controlled conditions and repeated trials); analysis of a public dataset; simulation results validated against an analytic case; a structured literature review with a defined selection method; and formal analysis of an algorithm. Cite: papers for algorithms and complexity results, repositories for code you used, dataset creators and versions for data, and library documentation for any non-trivial dependency. Code you wrote yourself should be referenced in the text and included as an appendix if it is short, or linked with a repository citation if it is long. Acknowledge any AI assistance explicitly.

Business management SL

What examiners are looking for

A business management essay is about a real organisation and a real decision. It applies tools and theories to a question that somebody in that organisation could plausibly care about, and then evaluates whether the tools actually delivered an answer. Description is the enemy: a company profile, however polished, scores almost nothing. What scores is a decision that could have gone the other way, analysed with evidence, and evaluated honestly.

This is a group 3 (individuals and societies) essay. You are well placed for it: you take business management at SL, so you already own the tools, and the research habits from the business research project transfer almost directly — just at 4,000 words instead of 1,800, with a heavier demand on evaluation and on the key concepts.

The characteristic shape of a strong business management EE

  1. The organisational problem. A named organisation facing a decision with at least two defensible options. If there is only one sensible answer, there is no essay.
  2. The conceptual frame. Which two or three tools you will use, and why not the others. Justifying the tools you rejected is one of the cheapest ways to earn criterion B.
  3. The evidence base. The organisation's own documents (annual report, accounts, strategy statements), plus at least one piece of primary research you generate, plus the market context.
  4. Analysis. Each tool is applied to produce a finding. A tool that produces a diagram but no conclusion has not been used.
  5. Evaluation. Where the tools disagree with each other, whose interests each answer serves, and what the tools cannot see.
  6. A conditional conclusion. "Do X, provided Y holds" — not "X is the best strategy".
The traps that cap business management essays
  1. The company report. "The history and structure of …" is a brochure. Every paragraph must be answering the research question, not describing the firm.
  2. Tool-stacking. SWOT, then PEST, then BCG, then Ansoff, none of which changes any conclusion. Three tools used properly beat eight described.
  3. No access, no essay. Before you commit, check you can actually get two years of financials, or one interview, or the internal document. A question you cannot evidence is not ambitious, it is broken.
  4. Confusing it with the IA. The business research project is 1,800 words, marked A–G, and requires three to five supporting documents. The EE is 4,000 words marked on five different criteria. Do not reuse it.
  5. Trusting the firm's own publicity. An annual report is a marketing document with legally required numbers in it. Use the numbers; interrogate the narrative.
  6. Undated or uncurrencied figures. Financial years differ between firms and countries. State the year, the currency, and the accounting standard every single time.
Evidence and citation

Primary evidence is the organisation's own material — annual reports, financial statements, strategy documents, press releases, product data — plus anything you generate yourself: an interview, a survey, a set of your own calculations from published accounts. Reproduce key documents in an appendix and quote from them selectively. Secondary evidence is industry data, market research, reputable journalism and academic literature, used to test the firm's claims rather than to repeat them.

For interviews: get written consent before you record, transcribe as soon as possible, and be explicit about who the person is and what their role lets them see — a warehouse supervisor and a regional director have different vantage points, and saying which one you spoke to is part of the evidence. If you anonymise the organisation, state why; if you name it, you must be prepared for your analysis to be unflattering, and that is fine — criticism is not bias, provided it is evidenced.

English A: language and literature SL

What examiners are looking for

Close analysis of how meaning is made, grounded in specific textual detail, and placed in relation to other readings. In language and literature you may work on literary or non-literary bodies of work — advertisements, speeches, journalism, digital media — but the method is the same: identify a formal or stylistic feature, show what it does, and argue for an interpretation that a reader could reject. The old essay categories have been removed, which gives you more freedom: you are no longer forced into a category, but you must therefore justify your choice of texts and approach yourself.

The characteristic shape of a strong English A EE

  1. The textual problem — something in the text(s) that does not settle easily: a contradiction, a shift, an ambiguity, a gap between what is said and what is shown.
  2. Positioning — what other readers have said, and where you differ. Two or three critics engaged with, not ten listed.
  3. Close reading in sections — each section takes a passage or a feature and shows what it does; each ends with a conclusion the next section uses.
  4. The consequence — how your reading changes the understanding of the whole work or body of work.
  5. Evaluation — the limits of your approach: what your chosen lens cannot see.
The traps that cap language A essays
  1. Plot summary. Retelling the story earns nothing. Every paragraph must be analysis, and you should assume the examiner has read the book.
  2. A survey of opinions. "Critic A says X, critic B says Y, critic C says Z" is a literature review. You must take a position and test it against them.
  3. Biography and context as evidence. What the author intended is not what the text does. Context supports an argument; it never substitutes for one.
  4. Quotation without analysis. A quote followed by a paraphrase of the same quote is the most common dead paragraph in the subject.
  5. Theme-hunting. "Alienation in X" produces a list of examples. Pick a device, a moment, or a structural feature instead.
Evidence and citation

Primary evidence is the text itself: passages, line or page references, and — for non-literary bodies of work — the artefacts themselves, reproduced or described precisely. Always cite the edition you used; page numbers differ between editions and examiners check. Secondary evidence is criticism, which you cite to engage with, not to borrow authority from. Use MLA or your school's preferred style consistently. If you quote a critic, quote exactly and give the page; if you paraphrase their position, still cite it. For non-literary material, include the source, date and medium, and consider reproducing the artefact in an appendix.

Use your subject combination

You are in an unusual position: maths AA HL, physics HL and CS HL together cover the same intellectual territory from three angles. That makes two things possible that most students cannot do.

  • A genuinely justified interdisciplinary essay. Physics + CS on numerical error versus physical chaos, or maths + CS on the gap between asymptotic and observed complexity — in both, deleting either subject destroys the question.
  • Deep single-subject work with cross-checking. A physics essay whose theory you verify numerically, or a CS essay whose complexity bounds you derive properly. The other subjects become method, not decoration.
  • Business management as the applied option. A BM essay lets you use the quantitative habits you already have — ratios, break-even, expected value — on a real organisation, and it is the only one of the six where a conversation with a person counts as data. It also pairs unusually well with CS: a question about algorithmic management or platform economics is genuinely two-subject.

Conversely, a Chinese A or English A essay is a genuine change of pace: completely different evidence, different structure, and a chance to show range. Choose it if you would enjoy it — not because it looks easier. It is not.

Practice

These four questions are the ones that actually decide your subject choice. Answer them on paper before you commit — the subject is very hard to change once a supervisor is assigned.

  1. Test two candidate questions against criterion B. Here are two draft questions in physics. For each, say whether the method is identifiable from the question alone, and rewrite the one that fails.

    • (a) "How does the concentration of a copper(II) sulfate solution affect the voltage produced by a galvanic cell?"
    • (b) "To what extent can the Nernst equation predict the cell potential of a Cu/Zn galvanic cell across concentrations of 0.01–1.0 mol dm⁻³ at 298 K?"
    Show the answer

    (a) fails; (b) passes. In (a) the dependent and independent variables are clear, but there is no standard against which the result will be judged. The essay can only report "voltage went up", which is a description. Criterion B asks whether the knowledge and understanding are effective — which in practice means: does the question force the use of a subject-specific method?

    (b) forces the method. Naming the Nernst equation means the essay must derive or state it, identify the reaction quotient, decide what to do about activity versus concentration (the interesting complication at 1.0 mol dm⁻³), and then test a prediction against data. The phrase "to what extent" obliges a judgement about where the model breaks down, which feeds straight into criterion D.

    The repair for (a): "How closely does the measured EMF of a Cu/Zn cell follow the Nernst prediction as Cu²⁺ concentration varies from 0.01 to 1.0 mol dm⁻³, and can the deviation at high concentration be accounted for by activity coefficients?" — now the method, the range and the point of failure are all in the question.

    Where the marks come from: a question that names its own method gives you criterion B almost for free and makes the evaluation paragraph write itself.

  2. Choose between a maths EE and a physics EE. You are equally interested in both, and your strongest subject is mathematics. What should decide it?

    Show the answer

    Neither "interest" nor "which is my best subject" is the deciding factor — the deciding factor is where you can be critical.

    A maths AA HL essay earns criterion C by proving, generalising and then interrogating a result: you state conditions, prove a claim, test the boundary cases where it fails, and explain why. The "evidence" is your own reasoning, which is excellent — but it means the essay stands or falls entirely on whether you can find a genuinely non-routine question. A question that any textbook answers is not an essay.

    A physics essay earns criterion C by comparing a model against measured reality, which means the evaluation paragraph has natural content (uncertainty, systematic error, the domain where the model fails). That is easier to write well — but it requires lab access and a data set you can actually defend.

    The decision rule. Choose maths if you have a specific result you want to extend and can already see how the proof would go. Choose physics if you have access to equipment and want the easier route to a strong criterion D. If you have neither a candidate result nor equipment, do not choose either — an English A or Chinese A essay with a genuinely arguable literary question beats a science essay with no data.

    Where the marks come from: subject choice is not assessed, but it determines how hard criteria C and D are to satisfy. Pick the subject where criticism is easiest, not the one where you feel safest.

  3. The interdisciplinary trap. A student proposes: "To what extent can game theory (mathematics) explain the escalation of the arms race (history)?" Is this a good interdisciplinary EE? What would make it one?

    Show the answer

    Not yet — this is the single most common failure mode of interdisciplinary essays. As written, it is a maths essay with a historical example bolted on: game theory does the explaining and history supplies the illustration. The two disciplines never constrain each other, so there is no interdisciplinary understanding to assess, and the essay scores as a weak single-subject essay in both.

    What makes it genuinely interdisciplinary: the conclusion must be one that neither discipline could reach alone, and each discipline must limit the other. For example: "Game-theoretic models of arms races predict escalation under a specific set of payoff assumptions; the archival record shows decision-makers acting on perceptions that violate those assumptions. Which modification of the model — changing the payoff structure, or abandoning the assumption of rational actors — better accounts for the observed sequence, and what does that imply about the explanatory limits of rational-choice models in history?"

    Now history constrains mathematics (the model must be modified to fit the record) and mathematics constrains history (the modification must be formal enough to generate a testable prediction). Neither discipline is decorative.

    The test to apply: can you state a conclusion that a mathematician would not accept from the model alone and a historian would not accept from the archive alone, but which both would accept together? If not, write a subject-focused essay instead.

    Where the marks come from: interdisciplinary essays are assessed on the integration. Two competent halves written side by side score lower than one strong subject-focused essay.

  4. Resource reality check. Rank these four essay plans by risk, given that you have no laboratory access over the summer and your school library has one database subscription.

    • (i) A chemistry-practice essay on reaction kinetics.
    • (ii) A Chinese A essay comparing two of your set works.
    • (iii) A CS essay benchmarking sorting algorithms on large data sets.
    • (iv) An English A essay on a contemporary novel not on your set list.
    Show the answer

    Lowest risk: (ii). The primary texts are on your shelf, the secondary literature is available, and your teacher knows the works. Everything you need is in the house.

    Low risk: (iii). A laptop and a compiler are enough; the data is generated, not collected, so there is no access problem. The risk is different — CS essays fail on originality, not on resources, so you need a question whose answer is not simply "quicksort is faster".

    Higher risk: (iv). A novel outside the set list is allowed and often good, but you must secure the text, enough secondary criticism to have something to argue with, and a supervisor willing to read it. With one database subscription, check that criticism exists before choosing the novel.

    Highest risk: (i). No laboratory access means no first-hand data, which means the kinetics essay can only be a literature review. Literature reviews are permissible in some subjects but they make criterion D very hard, because you have no method of your own to evaluate. Do not start an essay whose evidence you cannot obtain by February.
