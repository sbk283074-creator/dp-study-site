DP core · 00 · The three things every candidate does

The DP core

Theory of knowledge, the extended essay and CAS are the three things every DP student does regardless of their subjects. Two of them are graded and can win you three extra diploma points — or, if you fail them, cost you the diploma outright. The third has no grade at all and can still cost you the diploma outright. This page is the map: what each element actually is, how the three points are really calculated, where the failure conditions hide, and how all of it fits into the eighteen months you have.

Class of 2028 · May 2028 session TOK: 2022 guide EE: new 2027 guide CAS: unchanged, completion only 3 of your 45 points
Why the core is worth your time

Three diploma points is roughly the difference between 38 and 41 — which is the difference between a good university offer and a great one, and sometimes between an offer and a rejection. The work is not enormous: one 1,600-word essay, one 950-word exhibition, one 4,000-word research essay, and a sustained commitment to CAS spread over eighteen months. Students lose the core points through neglect, not difficulty. Nobody ever failed the extended essay because it was too hard; people fail it because they started it in February of DP2.

There is a second reason, and it is the one students miss. The core is the only part of the diploma where you can hand in a draft, get told what is wrong with it, and hand in a better version. Your exam scripts are written once, in May, under a clock, with no feedback loop. The EE and the TOK essay are built over months with a supervisor who is contractually obliged to read your work and tell you where it is weak. That makes them the most improvable marks you own.

Do the core early and in DP1, and your final year becomes a six-subject exam instead of a nine-component ordeal. Leave it late and every IA deadline collides with every core deadline in the same six weeks — which is precisely how the failure conditions below actually happen.

1. The three elements at a glance

Three elements, three completely different kinds of requirement. The most common student error is to treat them as three essays of different lengths. They are not: one is a taught course with two assessed tasks, one is a research project with a supervisor, and one is a sustained commitment with no grade at all.

"Not graded" is not the same as "not required"

CAS has no A to E and no marks. It nevertheless has the sharpest consequence of the three: incomplete CAS means the diploma is not awarded, full stop. A candidate with 40 points and no CAS receives DP course results instead of a diploma. The asymmetry is worth internalising — TOK and the EE can cost you points or the diploma; CAS can only ever cost you the diploma, but it costs it absolutely, and because there is no grade to optimise it is the element students postpone.

2. How TOK and the EE convert into points

Your TOK grade and your EE grade are not added together. They are looked up in a matrix, and the matrix returns a number of diploma points between 0 and 3. Read the table as: EE grade across the top, TOK grade down the side.

2.1 How to read it

Three things to notice before you use the table for anything.

  1. The points come from the pair, not the individual grades. An A in TOK is not worth anything on its own. It is worth 3, 3, 2 or 2 depending on what you got for the EE. There is no such thing as "an A is worth 3 points".
  2. The E row and the E column are not numbers. They are a different kind of entry entirely. Grade E in either component is a failing condition: the diploma is withheld. It is not a zero — it is a switch. An A in the other component does not flip it back.
  3. The table is nearly symmetric but not quite. A + D = 2 while D + A = 2, so those match; but B + D = 1 while D + B = 1 too. In fact this matrix is symmetric: swap the grades and the points are unchanged. That matters, because it means there is no advantage to being stronger in TOK than in the EE, or the other way round. Invest in the weaker one.

2.2 The step-function insight

The matrix is a step function, not a sum. That single fact drives every strategic decision you will make about the core.

If the matrix were a sum, improving a grade would always gain you something. It is not, so it often gains you nothing at all. Concretely, from a starting point of TOK B and EE C (2 points):

Move you makeNew pairNew pointsGain
Improve TOK by one grade (B → A)A + C20
Improve EE by one grade (C → B)B + B20
Improve EE by two grades (C → A)B + A3+1
Improve TOK by one and EE by oneA + B3+1

Read that again: one grade of improvement in either direction is worth nothing. You have to cross a boundary in the table. The boundary between 2 and 3 runs diagonally, so the cheapest route across it is usually to improve both grades by one step, or the lower one by two.

What the matrix is really telling you

An A or B in one component cannot rescue an E in the other — the E is a failing condition outright, and no amount of excellence elsewhere touches it. And the difference between a C/C (1 point) and an A/A (3 points) is two diploma points, which is more than the gap between a 6 and a 7 in any single subject, and infinitely cheaper to win: two pieces of writing you can redraft, rather than three exam papers you cannot.

The practical rule that follows: work out which cell you are heading for before you revise anything. If you are sitting comfortably inside a cell — a solid B in TOK with a C in the EE, say — then a marginal improvement to either grade earns nothing, and the correct move is either to push hard enough to cross the diagonal, or to stop and spend that time on a subject grade instead. Effort spent inside a cell is effort thrown away. Confirm the matrix against your coordinator's copy of the current rules, but the shape has been stable for years.

This is the opposite of how most students behave — they polish the component they are already good at, which is exactly the move the table refuses to reward.

2.4 Three worked candidate scenarios

Worked example 1 The candidate who polishes the wrong component

Scenario. Amara has a strong TOK teacher and enjoys the course. By DP2 she has an A for TOK and a C for the EE. She has three weeks of study leave and is considering spending them pushing TOK from A to a "safer A".

  1. Locate the cell. TOK A, EE C → 2 points. Already the maximum TOK can contribute in that column.
  2. Test the proposed move. TOK cannot go higher than A. There is no cell above A + C. The time spent produces exactly zero additional points.
  3. Find the move that does pay. A + B = 3. The EE is the only component with room in it. One grade in the EE — from C to B — is worth one diploma point.
  4. Where that grade actually comes from. The EE criterion with the most marks is discussion and evaluation (8 of 30). Essays that score 2 or 3 there treat evaluation as a final paragraph of generic limitations. Adding three specific, sized evaluation sentences — what the method could not distinguish, how big the uncertainty is, what a better design would change — is a few hours of work and is the single densest mark-earning edit available in the entire core.
Answer: 2 points now, 3 available, and the entire gain sits in the component she was planning to ignore. The lesson generalises: once one component is at A, all remaining value is in the other one.
Worked example 2 The candidate in the sticky middle

Scenario. Ben is predicted B for TOK and C for the EE. His school's internal deadline for the final EE draft is in three weeks. He is deciding whether to spend those weeks on the EE or on physics.

  1. Locate the cell. B + C = 2 points.
  2. Test single steps. B → A gives A + C = 2. C → B gives B + B = 2. Neither gains anything. The three weeks, spent on the EE alone, are worth zero points.
  3. Find the real price. To reach 3 he needs either A + B (one step in each) or B + A (two steps in the EE). Since he cannot improve TOK and the EE in the same three weeks, the honest answer is that this window cannot reach the next cell.
  4. Decide like an economist. Two options: (i) spend the three weeks and accept 2 points, having used the time; (ii) spend the three weeks on physics, where a 6 to a 7 is exactly one point, and accept 2 core points anyway. Option (ii) buys the same core outcome and a possible subject point. Option (i) buys the same core outcome and nothing else.
  5. Unless. There is one exception. If Ben's EE is currently at risk of a D — if the draft is unfinished, or the evaluation is missing entirely — then the move that matters is defensive, not offensive: B + D = 1, and a further slip puts him at the E column, which is not a points problem at all. Guaranteeing the C is worth more than any point he could win elsewhere.
Answer: 2 points either way, so the EE work in this window has no upside — but it may have a large downside if the draft is genuinely unfinished. Check for downside first, then spend the remaining time where a step crosses a boundary.
Worked example 3 The candidate for whom the matrix is irrelevant

Scenario. Chen scores 40 points across six subjects — comfortably above the 24-point threshold — with an A for TOK. His EE was submitted two days before the deadline after a fortnight of continuous work, and comes back an E.

  1. Do not open the matrix. The E column is not a number. It does not matter that TOK was an A, or that the subject total was 40.
  2. What he receives. DP course results for the six subjects, not the IB Diploma. The three core points he would have earned are irrelevant; so is the 40.
  3. Why this happens, mechanically. Grade E is awarded when the work does not meet the lowest descriptor across the criteria — typically when the essay is unfinished, or is purely descriptive with no line of argument, or the reflection sessions did not happen. Two days before the deadline is not the cause; it is the symptom. The cause was a research question never fixed and a draft never shown to the supervisor.
  4. The cheapest insurance that existed. Three mandatory reflection sessions, spaced across the process, at which the supervisor is required to record that they took place. A candidate who attends all three cannot submit an unfinished essay by accident, because there is a meeting in the way. That is the entire point of the structure.
  5. The asymmetry to take away. The core has a floor that matters more than its ceiling. Moving from C to A is worth at most one point. Falling to E is worth the whole diploma. Budget your effort to guarantee the floor first.
Answer: no diploma. The matrix is irrelevant once an E appears, which is why the highest-value work in the core is the work that guarantees you are nowhere near the E column.

3. The three ways to lose the diploma through the core

There are exactly three, and they are not equally common. Learn what each one looks like in practice, because they are all recognisable months in advance.

3.1 Grade E in TOK or the EE

This is not a low score. It is a condition under which the diploma is not awarded, and no subject performance offsets it.

What it officially isWhat it looks like in practiceThe early warning sign
E in the EE The work does not reach the lowest descriptor on the criteria. An essay that is descriptive rather than argumentative — a literature review with an introduction; an unfinished essay missing its evaluation or conclusion; an essay with no recognisable research question; an essay where the reflection sessions never happened and there is nothing on the RPF. You cannot state your research question in one sentence, or your "answer in one sentence" has not changed in two months.
E in TOK The essay or the combined TOK grade does not reach the lowest descriptor. An essay that never engages the prescribed title, or that describes two areas of knowledge without ever comparing them; an exhibition whose commentary describes three objects instead of connecting them to the prompt; work that is largely copied or substantially over the word limit. Your draft reads like an encyclopedia entry. If you can delete the title from your essay and nothing changes, you are in danger.

3.2 Not completing CAS

Also a failing condition, and the most common way students discover this too late, because there is no grade drifting downwards to warn them. Completion is a decision your coordinator makes at the end, on three questions: was there at least 18 months of sustained engagement, is there a CAS project, and can all seven learning outcomes be pointed to somewhere in the portfolio?

The failure is almost never "the student did nothing". It is a portfolio with a hole in it: outcome 3 (initiate and plan) has nothing behind it because every activity was one the student was placed in, or outcome 7 (ethics) has nothing behind it because nothing they did raised an ethical question. Those holes are fixable in DP1 and unfixable in the last three weeks of DP2. Do a written seven-outcome audit at the end of DP1 — see CAS — the requirement.

3.3 Leaving all of it to the last eight weeks

Technically not a rule. Operationally the cause of the first two.

Here is the collision, spelled out. In a typical school, the DP2 spring term contains: the business management research project, the maths exploration final draft if it slipped, the physics IA write-up, the CS IA final build, the TOK essay, and the final CAS interview. Six deadlines, six teachers, one fortnight each. The work does not get worse because students are lazy; it gets worse because there are not enough hours, and the component with the most distant deadline loses. The core loses, because the core's deadlines are set by your school and can be missed without anyone chasing you in week one.

The three ways to lose the diploma through the core
  1. Grade E in either TOK or the EE. Not a low score — an automatic failing condition, regardless of your subject total.
  2. Not completing CAS. Also a failing condition, and the most common way students discover this too late.
  3. Leaving both to the last eight weeks. Technically not a rule, but it is how the first two actually happen.

The general defence against all three is the same and it takes ten minutes: write down the date of every core milestone, put it next to every IA deadline, and look for the weeks where three things land at once. Then move something. Everything in section 9 below exists to make that possible.

4.1 How to check a "first assessment" date yourself

Do not take a website's word for it — including this one. The check takes two minutes and it is worth learning, because you will need it every time you open a textbook or a YouTube channel.

How to use it — the five-step check
  1. Find the guide, not a summary of it. On ibo.org, go to Programmes → Diploma Programme → Curriculum, and open the subject's page. The published guide PDF is the only primary source. Note the date on the cover.

  3. Apply the rule. A guide applies to you if its first assessment year is 2028 or earlier, and no later guide has superseded it. Your session is May 2028, so 2027 and 2028 guides apply; 2029 guides do not.
  4. Check for a transition note. When a guide changes, the IB publishes which internal-assessment forms, cover sheets and submission routes belong to which session. This is where schools' paperwork differs, so ask your coordinator rather than assuming.
  5. Treat everything else as hearsay. Revision guides, tutoring sites and video channels lag by two to five years. If a resource mentions options (computer science), or a world studies EE category, or a Paper 3 (physics), it is written for a course you are not taking.

5. What theory of knowledge actually is

TOK is not a subject you learn facts in; it is a subject you learn to interrogate facts with. Your other subjects ask first-order questions — questions answered inside a discipline using that discipline's methods. "What is the derivative of x sin x?" is first-order. TOK asks second-order questions — questions about the discipline itself. "Why does a proof give a kind of certainty an experiment cannot, and is that certainty worth the price?" That cannot be answered by doing more mathematics.

5.1 The skeleton: one core theme, two optional themes, five areas of knowledge

The core theme (compulsory)

Knowledge and the knower. The force is in the word and: what counts as knowledge depends on who is doing the knowing, where they stand, and what they are accountable to. You cannot study one without the other.

Two optional themes (your teacher picks two of five)

Knowledge and technology, knowledge and language, knowledge and politics, knowledge and religion, knowledge and indigenous societies. A theme is a lens, not a topic.

Five areas of knowledge (all compulsory)

Mathematics, the natural sciences, the human sciences, history, the arts. You study all five. Each is defined by what it claims to know and by the method it uses to get there.

Why the split matters

The essay leans on the areas of knowledge; the exhibition leans on the themes. If you are writing about mathematics or the natural sciences, you are probably doing essay work. If you are writing about technology or politics, you are probably doing exhibition work.

5.2 The knowledge-question move

This is the single skill the whole course is built on, and it is worth learning properly in DP1, because both assessed tasks depend on it. A topic is a thing you could write an essay about. A knowledge question is a question about how anyone could know something in that area. The move has four steps.

How to use it — turning a topic into a knowledge question
  1. Name the topic plainly. "Machine learning classifiers." "The half-life of carbon-14." No jargon yet.
  2. Name the method that produces the knowledge. "Trained on a labelled dataset and validated on a held-out test set." "Measured by counting decays and fitting an exponential." If you cannot state the method, you cannot build the question — this is why TOK rewards students who actually know their subjects.
  3. Ask what that method buys and what it costs. Every method buys something — certainty, generality, precision, reach — and pays for it. A proof buys necessity and pays by saying nothing about the world. An experiment buys contact with the world and pays by never being final.
  4. Attach a criterion word. "Reliable", "justified", "objective", "certain", "useful". A knowledge question without a criterion is unfalsifiable — "is science good?" has no answer to argue about; "does the requirement that results be reproducible make the natural sciences more reliable or merely more conservative?" does.
Worked example 4 From a physics HL topic to a knowledge question

Starting topic. Measurement uncertainty — every value you report in the physics IA carries a ±, and a difference is only real if it exceeds the combined uncertainty.

  1. Topic: deciding whether a measured difference is real.
  2. Method: quantify the uncertainty, require the effect to exceed it, and require independent methods to agree within theirs. In particle physics this becomes a formal threshold — the five-sigma convention for a discovery.
  3. What it buys: a public rule for when a community is entitled to claim something, which protects it from enthusiasm and from fraud. What it costs: a real but small effect is indistinguishable from noise until enough data accumulate, so the method decides not just what is true but when it may be said.
  4. Criterion: reliability.
  5. Knowledge question: "Does the requirement that results be reproducible make the natural sciences more reliable, or merely more conservative — more likely to reject a true anomaly than to accept it?"
  6. Why this is examinable: it has two sides that can both be argued with the same example. The five-sigma rule prevented false discoveries; it also delayed the acceptance of effects that later turned out to be real. You can take a position and be challenged on it, which is exactly what an examiner needs in order to award anything.
Answer: the question is specific, attaches to a criterion, and its example cuts both ways — which is what makes it worth 1,600 words rather than one paragraph.
Worked example 5 From a computer science HL topic to a knowledge question

Starting topic. A neural network classifies images with 96 % accuracy on a held-out test set, and nobody can say why it produced any particular label.

  1. Topic: prediction without explanation.
  2. Method: optimise millions of parameters against a labelled dataset; validate on data the model has not seen; evaluate with a confusion matrix.
  3. What it buys: accuracy at a scale no human can match, on tasks where the rule cannot be stated. What it costs: no reason, no justification, and no way to tell a robust classification from a lucky one that exploited an artefact of the dataset (a model that learned to recognise the hospital, not the disease).
  4. Criterion: knowledge versus reliable prediction.
  5. Knowledge question: "If a system can reliably predict an outcome but cannot give reasons for its prediction, are we entitled to say that it knows anything — and if not, what does that tell us about how much of our own knowledge is really justified?"
  6. The second half is what makes it a TOK question rather than a CS question. It turns the lens back on the knower, which is the core theme doing its work.
Answer: the question uses a piece of your own subject as evidence and then asks about the nature of knowledge — not about AI policy. That is the difference between a TOK essay and an editorial.
Trap — three ways the knowledge-question move fails
  1. The question is too big. "What is truth?" cannot be answered in 1,600 words; you will produce a paragraph of definitions and then drift. Shrink it until it has a method attached.
  2. The question has an obvious answer. "Is peer review important?" — yes, everyone agrees, and you will spend 1,600 words restating a consensus with illustrations. No tension, no marks. Choose pairs of AOKs whose methods genuinely clash: mathematics and history, natural sciences and the arts. Not mathematics and physics — those are cousins, and there is no friction to interrogate.
  3. The question is really a policy question. "Should AI be regulated?" is answered with opinions. "On what grounds can we say a system that cannot give reasons knows anything?" is answered with argument. Same material, one is examinable and one is not.

6. What the extended essay actually is

An independent, self-directed piece of research, finished with a formal essay of no more than 4,000 words, marked by a stranger against five criteria worth 30 marks. It is decided in the first three weeks — not by how well you write, but by whether you picked a question that can carry four thousand words of reasoning. A brilliant writer with a descriptive question caps at a C. An average writer with a genuinely arguable question, a defensible method and a supervisor they actually used can reach an A.

6.1 The two pathways

Subject-focused

You investigate one question through the concepts, theories and methods of one DP subject. One disciplinary lens, one set of conventions, one supervisor. The traditional route, and the right one unless you can pass the test below.

Interdisciplinary

Your question genuinely requires two DP subjects. The test: write the sentence "Subject X is necessary because …", then show it to the teacher of the other subject and ask them to attack it. If they can delete their subject from the sentence and the question still stands, you are subject-focused — register it as such.

6.3 The supervisor relationship

Your supervisor is a teacher in your school who guides the research process. Expect roughly three to five hours of their time in total across the whole essay, including the three mandatory reflection sessions. That is not much. Spend it well.

Trap — three ways students waste a supervisor
  1. Going silent for six weeks. Supervisors cannot help work they have not seen, and they are required to record the reflection sessions. Silence kills the process.
  2. Sending chapter-by-chapter for editing. They are permitted to review one full draft. Send section 1, then section 2, then section 3, and you have spent your entitlement on fragments — nobody has seen whether the argument hangs together.
  3. Arguing with feedback instead of testing it. You do not have to accept every suggestion. You do have to try it. Write the version they asked for, then compare. Often they are right; when they are not, you now have a much better reason than "I prefer mine".

6.4 The three reflection sessions and the viva voce

Session 1 — initial
Near the start. Bring three candidate research questions, each already tested for focus and for whether a source base exists. Purpose: agree the question and the method. Leave with one question, not three.
Session 2 — interim
While you are working, usually around the point where a draft exists in pieces. Bring the research log and a one-page argument sketch: claim, main reason, strongest objection, answer. This is the most useful meeting of the whole process, because the argument can still be changed.
Session 3 — viva voce
After the essay is finished. Short. It is not a defence or an oral exam with a grade — it is a check that the work is yours, that you understand what you submitted, and that the process happened. Prepare four questions: what was your biggest change of mind, what is the weakest part of your argument, what would you do differently, and what did you learn that you can use elsewhere.
Within 48 h
Write the reflective statement on the Reflection and Progress Form — no more than 500 words, separate from the 4,000. It is assessed under criterion E and it is about growth and transferable skills, not a diary of what you did in which week.

6.5 What 4,000 words actually feels like to plan

Four thousand words sounds enormous until you budget it, and then it sounds tight. Here is a budget that works for a subject-focused essay with three body sections.

0–300
Introduction: the question verbatim, why it matters, what the essay will argue. State the answer in one sentence here — it is allowed, and it makes criterion C easier because every paragraph can be checked against it.
300–800
Framework: define the terms you will use, set the scope, and state the method with the reason you chose it. Justify one method you rejected. This is criterion A and part of criterion B.
800–1,700
Body 1: the first line of evidence, fully worked. Every paragraph: claim, evidence, interpretation, and one sentence on what the evidence does not show.
1,700–2,600
Body 2: the second line of evidence, chosen so it could in principle contradict the first. If it cannot, you have two illustrations rather than an argument.
2,600–3,300
The strongest counter-argument, taken seriously and answered. Not a paragraph of limitations at the end — a real objection placed where it hurts.
3,300–3,750
Discussion and evaluation: three specific questions — what can this method not distinguish, how large is the uncertainty, and what would a better design change. Eight marks live here.
3,750–4,000
Conclusion: the answer to the research question, with its scope stated. Not a summary of the sections — a sentence someone could disagree with.
+500 (separate)
The reflective statement on the RPF, written after the viva voce. Confirm with your coordinator whether an abstract is required in your session and whether it counts toward the 4,000.

Full detail on the criteria, the research log and the ten most common ways essays lose marks is on EE — research, writing & criteria. Ranked research questions for maths, physics, computer science, English A, Chinese A and business management are on EE — in your subjects.

7. What CAS actually is

CAS is the deliberate counterweight to the rest of the diploma. Five of your six subjects are examined by sitting still and writing; CAS asks you to do something in the world and then think about what it did to you. The design is simple: you undertake experiences, you reflect on them, and by the end you must be able to evidence seven learning outcomes.

7.1 The three strands

StrandThe definitionWhat this rules out
CreativityArts, and other experiences that involve creative thinking.Appreciating other people's creativity does not count. Attending a concert, visiting an exhibition, watching a film — those are consumption. You must be making something, or making a decision about how something is made.
ActivityPhysical exertion contributing to a healthy lifestyle, complementing academic work elsewhere in the DP.A single-event fun run or one weekend hike. Single incidents of engagement lack depth and meaning; activity must be sustained.
ServiceAn unpaid and voluntary exchange that has a learning benefit for the student, where the rights, dignity and autonomy of all involved are respected.Paid work. Anything where the main beneficiary is you. Service that stays at "doing for" people: the guide wants you to end up "engaging with" them.

7.2 The seven learning outcomes

#Learning outcomeWhat it really asks you to show
1Identify own strengths and develop areas for growthAn honest picture of yourself, including the weak parts, and activities that stretch them. Playing the piano you are already good at evidences nothing. Teaching piano to beginners does.
2Demonstrate that challenges have been undertaken, developing new skills That you chose difficulty and learned something new in the choosing.
3Demonstrate how to initiate and plan a CAS experienceThat you started something rather than being placed in something. The outcome most portfolios lack, because students only ever join existing clubs.
4Show commitment and perseverance in CAS experiencesRegular involvement over time. This is what kills the "one big thing in the last month" plan: perseverance cannot be evidenced retrospectively.

6Demonstrate engagement with issues of global significanceThat some of your CAS touches something bigger than the school gate — environment, inequality, public health, migration, technology and society.

7.3 The hard requirements and the project

What is actually mandatory
  • At least 18 months of sustained engagement, ideally weekly, with a reasonable balance between creativity, activity and service.
  • At least one CAS project — a collaborative, well-considered series of sequential experiences, with a minimum of one month from planning to completion. It may address one strand or combine two or three; follow your school's version, which is often the stricter "two or more".
  • A minimum of three interviews with your CAS coordinator: at least two in the first year and one in the second — an initial interview, a progress interview around the end of DP1 where the project is discussed, and a final summative interview.
  • Evidence of all seven learning outcomes, mainly through reflection.
  • A completed portfolio. The guide does not mandate a format; most schools use Profile / Experiences / Evidence.
The three myths, killed
  1. "You need 150 hours." No. There is no hour count anywhere in the CAS guide. The 150 figure is SL teaching hours from a different table, repeated by so many websites that students treat it as real. What the guide requires is sustained engagement over at least 18 months. If you logged everything, most students end up somewhere between 100 and 250 hours across the two years — but that is an observation, not a requirement.
  2. "You must do one activity per strand per term." Not a requirement. What is required is a reasonable balance across the whole 18 months, judged by your coordinator.
  3. "Anything I do for a subject can count." The opposite. The guide is explicit that CAS experiences must be distinct from what you submit for a course. Your physics investigation is not CAS. Your maths exploration is not CAS. Your computer science collaborative project is not CAS. This catches a lot of science students.
Practical read

Treat CAS as a deadline problem, not a quality problem. Nobody fails CAS because their reflections were not profound enough. People fail CAS because they had not started the project by the end of DP1, or because their portfolio has a gap where outcome 3 or outcome 7 should be, and they discover it at the final interview with three weeks to go. Do a written seven-outcome audit at the end of DP1 and fix the holes while there is still time to do something real about them. See CAS — the project and CAS — reflection & portfolio.

8. Effort budgeting: where the core sits against six subjects

Before you can plan, you need honest numbers. These are planning figures, not official ones — the only official hours in the core are TOK's 100 teaching hours and the extended essay's roughly 40 hours of student work plus 3–5 hours of supervision. The rest is what the work actually takes.

The honest arithmetic, and why the core still wins

Raw hours per point make the core look expensive: 1,170 teaching hours buy 42 points (about 28 hours per point), while roughly 140 hours of core work beyond the TOK lessons buy 3 (about 47 hours per point). So do not believe anyone who tells you the core is free.

But that comparison is misleading in three ways, and each of them is a reason to do the core properly.

  1. You can redraft the core and you cannot redraft an exam. The 3 core points are the only points in the diploma for which feedback exists before the mark is fixed.
  2. The marginal cost is tiny. Going from a C to a B in the EE — one diploma point — is a handful of hours of targeted editing (evaluation, justification of method, tightening the line of argument). Going from a 6 to a 7 in HL mathematics is a whole additional grade of exam performance.
  3. The downside is unbounded. Subject grades cost you points when they fall. A core failure costs you the diploma. Effort spent guaranteeing the floor is worth more than effort spent reaching for the ceiling.

The practical rule: budget the core the way you would budget rent — automatically, first, every week, before anything discretionary. Two hours a week from DP1 week three is all it takes, and it is the single highest-return habit in the two years.

9. How the core interacts with your subjects

9.1 The EE is not a long IA

This is the most expensive misconception in the whole core, because it produces an essay that looks competent and caps at a C. The two assessments reward different things.

An internal assessment / explorationThe extended essay
RewardsMethod: a clean procedure, good data, correct treatment of uncertainty, personal engagement.A sustained line of argument built on sources and evidence, with evaluation at every stage.
ShapeAim → method → data → analysis → conclusion → evaluation. Question → framework → argument in three or four moves → counter-argument → weighed conclusion.
Length1,800 words (business research project), 12–20 pages (maths exploration), a build plus documentation (CS).4,000 words, plus a 500-word reflective statement.
What a good one containsA measurement you personally made.A claim someone could disagree with, defended against the best objection.
Why recycling failsAn IA has no need for a counter-argument, and its evaluation section is a paragraph.The EE's largest single criterion — 8 of 30 marks — is discussion and evaluation. An IA scaled up has almost nothing here.

What you can reuse is the topic area and the technical understanding, not the artefact. A physics IA on pendulum damping and a physics EE on the same apparatus are fine; the EE has to ask a question whose answer is contested, and to argue it. See Physics — skills & IA, Maths — the exploration and CS — the IA for what the IA side wants.

9.2 Mining your HL subjects for TOK material

The strongest TOK essays and exhibitions are written by students who raid their own subjects for examples. The reason is simple: you can discuss the method of something you have actually done, whereas a borrowed example only lets you describe it. Generic material — "the internet", "science has changed" — gives an examiner nothing to award.

Your subjectThe materialThe knowledge question it supports
Mathematics AA HLProof: a deductive chain from axioms, certain relative to those axioms and silent about whether they describe anything.If mathematical knowledge is certain but empty of content about the world, in what sense is it knowledge of the world?

Computer science HLA classifier that is accurate but cannot give reasons; a dataset that determines what a model can learn.Can a system that predicts reliably but cannot justify anything be said to know? What does that reveal about our own claims?

The connection you should exploit

TOK and the EE share one skill: taking a position and justifying it while taking the best counter-argument seriously. Practise it once and it pays in both. It also pays in the individual oral, in Paper 2, and in any university interview you ever sit — which is why the "second-order" habit is worth more than the three points it is nominally worth.

Before DP1
Set up, do not start. Initial CAS interview; portfolio created; one creativity and one activity experience started — these are the easiest to begin and the slowest to show anything. (Sep–Dec)

The deadline collision you are planning around

Note that the DP2 T1 block above contains the EE submission, the TOK exhibition, three subject IAs and the start of the TOK essay. It is the single densest term of the diploma for almost every candidate. Two consequences follow. First, anything you can move into the DP1 summer, move — it is the only empty space in the two years. Second, do not accept a school deadline you can privately beat: finishing the EE draft in the summer rather than in October is the difference between a considered essay and a rushed one, and it costs you nothing but a few weeks of a holiday.

12. Practice: does your core plan actually work?

Eight questions. Answer them honestly on paper before opening the answers — if you cannot answer the first two, you do not have a plan yet, and that is worth knowing now rather than in January.

  1. Points arithmetic. A candidate gets a B for TOK and a C for the EE. (a) How many diploma points do they receive? (b) Which single-component improvement would gain them a third point, and how many grade steps does it take? (c) The same candidate gets an E for the EE instead — what happens, and why does it matter more than the lost point?

    Show the answer

    (a) Two points. The matrix is read at the intersection of the two grades: row B, column C = 2. Not three, not one — the points come from the combination, not from the individual grades.

    (b) Neither single-step move gains anything, which is the point. From B + C = 2: improving TOK to A gives A + C = 2, and improving the EE to B gives B + B = 2. Both are worth zero. The routes to 3 are EE by two steps (B + A) or both components by one step each (A + B). Since the candidate cannot improve both in the time available, the honest answer is that one component needs two grades.

    (c) The diploma is at risk, not just a point. Grade E for either TOK or the EE is a failing condition: not a low score that costs you points, but a condition under which the diploma is not awarded at all. That is why the effort floor matters more than the ceiling for the core — the highest-value work you can do is the work that guarantees you are nowhere near an E.

    Why this is worth knowing: three points is the difference between two adjacent total scores, which for a borderline university offer is the whole game. And one E is the difference between a diploma and a set of course results.

  2. Marginal value. Two candidates both sit at 2 core points. Candidate X has A + C; candidate Y has C + A. Each has ten hours to spend. Who has a point available, and on which component?

    Show the answer

    X can gain one point; Y can too — but on the opposite component in each case, and for opposite reasons.

    X (TOK A, EE C). TOK is already at A, so there is no cell above. The only move is EE: A + B = 3. One grade in the EE is worth one diploma point. Ten hours spent on TOK would be worth exactly nothing.

    Y (TOK C, EE A). The mirror image. The EE is maxed, so the TOK is the only component with room: B + A = 3. One grade in TOK is worth one point.

    The general rule this illustrates: because the matrix is symmetric, there is never an advantage to being stronger in one component. The available point is always in the component that is further from A. Students systematically do the opposite, because they prefer working on the thing they are already good at.

    Why this is worth knowing: "work on your weakness" is usually motivational advice. Here it is literally what the mark scheme rewards.

  3. The sequencing question. You have 18 months. The TOK essay, the TOK exhibition and the EE all land in the same academic year, and your six subjects have their own internal assessments. Propose an order of work that protects the diploma, and justify it in terms of the failure modes in section 3.

    Show the answer

    Do the exhibition early, the EE over the long haul, and the TOK essay last-but-fast.

    1. Exhibition early (DP1 T2 into DP2 T1). It is the smallest of the three tasks and the one most dependent on a single good idea — three objects that actually connect to a prompt. Doing it early removes a whole component from the pile and rehearses the "knowledge question plus real-world object" move that the essay also needs.
    2. EE continuously (DP1 T1 into DP2 T1). It is the longest task and the one with external dependencies: supervisor feedback, sources, data, and three reflection sessions with mandatory spacing. You cannot compress it, so it must start first. Question fixed before the summer, full draft over the summer, submitted in the autumn of DP2.
    3. TOK essay last (DP2 T1–T2). It is 1,600 words and, crucially, you can only write it well once you have the subject knowledge to draw real examples from. A TOK essay written in DP1 by a student who has not yet studied the material is thin. Compressed but well-informed beats spread-out and shallow.

    What this protects against. Against the E: nothing is left to the final fortnight, so no component can be submitted unfinished, and the three mandated reflection sessions stand in the way of silence. Against the lost points: the two components that respond most to genuine thought get the time when you actually know something.

    Why this is worth knowing: most core failures are scheduling failures. The work is not harder than your subjects; it is just easier to postpone.

  4. Which guide applies? For each of the following, say whether it applies to a May 2028 candidate, and why: (i) the 2014 computer science guide with options; (ii) the mathematics guide with first assessment 2021; (iii) the new extended essay guide with first assessment 2027; (iv) the new mathematics guide with first assessment 2029; (v) the physics guide with first assessment 2025.

    Show the answer

    (i) No. Computer science moves to the new guide with first assessment 2027, so your 2028 session uses it. The old options and the old Paper 2/3 structure do not apply.

    (ii) Yes. The mathematics guide with first assessment 2021 is still in force for you, and no later mathematics guide has taken effect before your session.

    (iii) Yes. First assessment 2027 means your session is the second examined under it — the two pathways, the five criteria and the three reflection sessions are what you are assessed against.

    (iv) No. First assessment 2029 is after your exams. Resources written for it describe a different course; ignore them entirely.

    (v) Yes. First assessment 2025, so it is already in force when you sit. The practical consequence is no options and no Paper 3.

    The general rule: "first assessment" is the year of the first examination session under a guide. Your session is May 2028, so a guide applies if its first assessment year is 2028 or earlier and no later guide has superseded it.

    Why this is worth knowing: the internet is full of material for the wrong versions of these courses, especially computer science. Checking the date is five seconds; revising the wrong syllabus costs months.

  5. Is it a knowledge question? For each, say whether it is a knowledge question or something else, and rewrite the failures. (a) "Should artificial intelligence be regulated?" (b) "To what extent does the choice of a training dataset determine what a machine-learning model can be said to know?" (c) "What is truth?"

    Show the answer

    (a) Not a knowledge question — it is a policy question. It is answered with opinions and produces an editorial. Rewrite: "On what grounds can we say a system that cannot give reasons for its outputs knows anything?" That has two arguable sides and a criterion embedded in it.

    (b) Yes. It names a method (training data selection), it attaches a criterion ("what can be said to know"), and it turns the lens back on the knower. It is also answerable with material from your own CS course, which means you can discuss the method rather than describe the topic.

    (c) Not usable — it is too big. "What is truth?" cannot be answered in 1,600 words; you will produce definitions and then drift. Rewrite: "In the natural sciences, is a superseded theory better described as false or as limited — and what does the answer tell us about what scientific knowledge claims?"

    The test to apply every time: can you state the method that produces the knowledge, and is there a criterion word (reliable, justified, certain, objective) that the question turns on? If either is missing, you do not have a knowledge question yet.

    Why this is worth knowing: this one move determines the ceiling on both assessed TOK tasks. Getting it right in DP1 is worth more than any amount of essay polishing later.

  6. EE versus IA. A student plans to extend their physics internal assessment on pendulum damping into their extended essay by collecting more data and writing it up at greater length. Explain two structural reasons this is likely to cap at a C.

    Show the answer
    1. There is nothing to argue about. An IA rewards method: a clean procedure, good data, treated uncertainty. Its question is answered by running the experiment. The EE's largest criterion — discussion and evaluation, 8 of 30 marks — requires weighing competing explanations and sizing what the conclusion does not show. More data of the same kind produces more of the same result, not more to weigh.
    2. There is no counter-argument. Criterion C rewards a line of argument, which requires a claim someone could reject. If the experiment simply confirms the model, the strongest objection is missing, and a literature review replaces the analysis.

    The fix is not more data, it is a different question. "To what extent can the departure of a pendulum's period at large amplitude be explained by the neglected series terms rather than by systematic error?" has two competing explanations for the same number, each of which could win. That is criterion D material, and the same apparatus answers it.

    Why this is worth knowing: the EE and the IA look similar and are marked on almost opposite things. Recognising that before you choose a question is worth an entire grade.

  7. CAS audit. A candidate has, at the end of DP1: two terms of school orchestra (continued from before the DP), weekly football training, and a term of tutoring younger students in mathematics, which they set up themselves. (a) Which of the seven learning outcomes are already evidenced? (b) Which are missing, and what would you do in DP2 to fix them?

    Show the answer

    (a) Evidenced, or evidenced with a written reflection:

    • Outcome 4 (commitment and perseverance) — two terms of two sustained activities is enough, provided the reflection is written across the term rather than once at the end.
    • Outcome 3 (initiate and plan) — strongly, from the tutoring, because they set it up. This is the outcome most portfolios lack.
    • Outcome 2 (challenge, new skills) — teaching someone else's misunderstanding is a genuinely different skill from understanding it yourself.
    • Outcome 1 (strengths and growth) — only if the reflection names the growth honestly. "I can do the maths but I am bad at explaining, and this forced me to fix that" is the sentence that makes this outcome real.

    (b) Missing or thin:

    • Outcome 5 (collaboration) — orchestra is collaborative in a weak sense, but nothing here required the candidate to work interdependently on a shared output. The CAS project is the natural fix.
    • Outcome 6 (global significance) — nothing yet touches anything beyond the school gate. Fix: connect the tutoring to a real issue (educational inequality, who gets access to technical or mathematical support) or add a service placement with an external organisation.
    • Outcome 7 (ethics) — nothing here forces an ethical question. It needs its own moment, and a reflection that engages it honestly.

    Why the orchestra is nearly worthless here: it was started before the DP and the candidate is already accomplished, so it evidences no new challenge and no initiation. It can sit in the portfolio as context; it earns almost nothing.

    Why this is worth knowing: outcomes 5, 6 and 7 are the three most commonly missing at the final interview, because ordinary school activities do not produce them by accident. Discovering that in DP1 is a scheduling problem; discovering it in DP2 is a diploma problem.

  8. The effort decision. It is October of DP2. Your TOK essay is drafted at a solid B, your EE is submitted, your CAS portfolio is complete with all seven outcomes evidenced, and you have approximately twenty hours of free study time before the mocks. Where should the twenty hours go, and what does the points matrix say about it?

    Show the answer

    Nowhere in the core, unless your TOK B is genuinely at risk.

    1. Establish the cell. You do not know your EE grade yet, but the risk that matters is the E column, and an EE that was submitted complete, with three reflection sessions held and an RPF signed, is not an E. The floor is secure, which was the first priority and is now done.
    2. Test the upside. The TOK essay is roughly two-thirds of the TOK grade. Twenty hours might lift a B towards an A. But if the EE comes back a C, then B + C = 2 and A + C = 2 — the improvement is worth zero points. If the EE comes back a B, then B + B = 2 and A + B = 3 — one point. So the expected value is somewhere between zero and one point, and you cannot know which.
    3. Compare with the alternative. Twenty hours in a subject where you are on a grade boundary buys a point with much higher probability and much lower variance, because you know exactly what the boundary is and what the paper asks.
    4. The correct decision: spend a small amount — two or three hours — pressure-testing the essay's counter-argument, which is where TOK marks are actually won, and put the rest into subjects. Do not spend twenty hours polishing a component whose payoff depends on a grade you have no control over.

    The principle: once the floor is safe, spend effort where it crosses a boundary you can see. The core's boundaries are partly hidden from you until May, which is a reason to have finished it long before then.

    Why this is worth knowing: "work hardest on the core" is wrong advice once the diploma is safe. The matrix tells you when to stop — and knowing when to stop is as valuable as knowing where to start.
The connection you should exploit
  • Your subjects are your TOK and EE material. A maths HL student can write about proof, certainty and the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics; a physics student about models, measurement and paradigm shift; a CS student about whether a machine can "know" anything. Use examples you actually understand — examiners reward depth over decoration.
  • The EE and the IAs want different things. The IAs reward method and data; the EE rewards a sustained line of argument built on sources. Do not recycle an IA as an EE — it is the most common way to cap at a C.
  • TOK and the EE share one skill: taking a position and justifying it while taking the best counter-argument seriously. Practise it once and it pays in both.
  • CAS is the only place the ethics habit is trained. Learning outcomes 6 and 7 — global significance and ethics — are the same thinking the EE's reflection criterion and TOK's core theme want. Students who engage with them properly in CAS write better reflections everywhere else.
  • → Maths exploration and Physics IA: what the IA side of the line wants, so you keep the two assessments separate.
  • → Computer science IA: the client interview, success criteria and evaluation — the same habits that make a strong criterion D in the EE.
  • → Business research project: the 1,800-word project is the closest thing in your subjects to an EE; do not confuse it with one.

marginal value — what one additional unit of effort actually gains you.
failing condition — a condition under which the diploma is not awarded, regardless of points: grade E in TOK or the EE, or incomplete CAS.
first assessment — the year of the first examination session examined under a given guide.
theory of knowledge (TOK) — the 100-hour course about how knowledge claims are justified.
first-order question — a question answered inside a discipline using its methods.
second-order question — a question about a discipline: its methods, limits and standards of evidence.
knowledge question — a second-order question about how anyone could know something in a given area.
core theme — the compulsory TOK theme, knowledge and the knower.
optional theme — one of five TOK themes; your teacher selects two.
area of knowledge (AOK) — mathematics, natural sciences, human sciences, history or the arts.
TOK essay — 1,600 words on one of six prescribed titles, externally assessed.
TOK exhibition — three objects and one prompt from thirty-five, with at most 950 words of commentary.
extended essay (EE) — an independent research essay of no more than 4,000 words.
pathway — the EE route you choose: subject-focused or interdisciplinary.
criterion — one of the five dimensions the EE is marked against, worth 6/6/6/8/4 marks.
supervisor — the teacher who guides the EE process, runs the reflection sessions and signs the RPF.
reflection session — one of three mandatory meetings with your supervisor; the last is the viva voce.
viva voce — the concluding interview after the essay is finished.
RPF (Reflection and Progress Form) — the form recording the sessions and carrying your 500-word reflective statement.
line of argument — a claim, developed through evidence, that answers the research question.
CAS — creativity, activity, service: the ungraded third element of the core.
strand — one of the three CAS areas: creativity, activity or service.
learning outcome — one of the seven things your CAS portfolio must evidence.
CAS experience — a single sustained engagement with one strand.
CAS project — a collaborative series of experiences with at least one month from planning to completion.
CAS portfolio — the evidence of your experiences, reflections and interviews.
sustained engagement — regular involvement over at least 18 months, not concentrated into a few weeks.
seven-outcome audit — checking each learning outcome one by one and identifying the gaps while there is time to fix them.
DP course results (DPCR) — what a candidate receives instead of the diploma when a core requirement is not met.
