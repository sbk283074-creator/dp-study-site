The DP core

Theory of knowledge, the extended essay and CAS are the three things every DP student does regardless of subjects. Two of them are graded and can win you three extra diploma points — or, if you fail them, cost you the diploma entirely. This section of the site is what you need for both.

Class of 2028 TOK: 2022 guide EE: new 2027 guide
Why the core is worth your time

Three diploma points is roughly the difference between 38 and 41 — which is the difference between a good university offer and a great one. The work is not enormous: one 1,600-word essay, one 950-word exhibition, one research essay, and a sustained commitment to CAS. Students lose the points through neglect, not difficulty. Do them early, in DP1, and your final year becomes a six-subject exam instead of a nine-component ordeal.

The three elements at a glance

Theory of knowledge A 100-hour course about how we know what we claim to know. Two assessed tasks: the essay and the exhibition. Essay externally assessed; exhibition internally assessed and externally moderated. A–E
Extended essay An independent research essay of roughly 4,000 words on a topic of your choosing — either subject-focused or interdisciplinary. Externally assessed by an IB examiner. A–E
CAS Creativity, activity, service: sustained experiences plus a project, over 18 months, evidencing seven learning outcomes. Not graded — but it is a condition of the diploma. Complete / not complete
The three ways to lose the diploma through the core
  1. Grade E in either TOK or the EE. Not a low score — an automatic failing condition, regardless of your subject total.
  2. Not completing CAS. Also a failing condition, and the most common way students discover this too late.
  3. Leaving both to the last eight weeks. Technically not a rule, but it is how the first two actually happen.

What the matrix is really telling you

An A or B in one component cannot rescue an E in the other — the E is a failing condition outright. And the difference between a C/C (1 point) and an A/A (3 points) is two points, which is more than the gap between a 6 and a 7 in any single subject. Per hour of effort, the core is the highest-yield part of the diploma. Confirm the matrix against your coordinator's copy of the current rules, but the shape has been stable for years.

Practice: does your core plan actually work?

Four questions. Answer them honestly on paper — if you cannot answer the first two, you do not have a plan yet, and that is worth knowing now rather than in January.

  1. Points arithmetic. A candidate gets a B for TOK and a C for the EE. (a) How many diploma points do they receive? (b) What is the smallest change in grades that would gain them a third point? (c) The same candidate gets an E for the EE instead — what happens, and why does it matter more than the lost point?

    Show the answer

    (a) Two points. The matrix is read at the intersection of the two grades: row B, column C = 2. Not three, not one — the points come from the combination, not from the individual grades.

    (b) Only one of the two possible improvements helps — and it is probably not the one you would guess. From B + C = 2, look at the neighbours: A + C = 2 and B + B = 2, but B + A = 3. So taking TOK from B to A changes nothing; taking the EE from C to B also changes nothing. The only single-grade move that gains a point is EE from C to A. Alternatively, TOK → A and EE → B together give A + B = 3.

    The general lesson: the matrix is a step function, not a sum. Effort spent on a grade that does not cross a boundary earns nothing at all. Before you revise, work out which single change actually moves you to the next cell — and note how often the answer is "improve the lower grade", not the one you enjoy more.

    (c) The diploma is at risk, not just a point. Grade E for either TOK or the EE is a failing condition: it is not a low score that costs you points, it is a condition under which the diploma is not awarded at all. That is why the effort floor matters more than the ceiling for the core — the highest-value work you can do is the work that guarantees you are nowhere near an E.

    Why this is worth knowing: three points is the difference between two adjacent total scores, which for a borderline university offer is the whole game. And one E is the difference between a diploma and a set of course results.

  2. The sequencing question. You have 18 months. The TOK essay, the TOK exhibition and the EE all land in the same academic year, and your six subjects have their own internal assessments. Propose an order of work that protects the diploma, and justify it in terms of the two failure modes described above.

    Show the answer

    Do the exhibition early, the EE over the long haul, and the TOK essay last-but-fast.

    1. Exhibition first (early in year 1). It is the smallest of the three tasks and the one most dependent on a single good idea. Doing it early removes a whole component from the pile and gives you practice at the "knowledge question + real-world object" move that the essay also needs.
    2. EE continuously (year 1 into year 2). It is the longest task and the one with external dependencies — supervisor feedback, sources, data, the three reflection sessions with mandatory spacing. You cannot compress it, so it must start early; the research question should be fixed before the summer and the draft finished before the autumn of year 2.
    3. TOK essay last (autumn of year 2). It is 1,600 words and, crucially, you can only write it well once you have the subject knowledge to draw real examples from. A TOK essay written in year 1 by a student who has not yet studied the material is thin. Compressed but well-informed beats spread-out and shallow.

    What this protects against. Against the E: nothing is left to the final fortnight, so no component can be submitted unfinished. Against the three points: the two components that respond most to genuine thought (EE and TOK essay) get the time when you actually know something.

    Why this is worth knowing: most core failures are scheduling failures. The work is not harder than your subjects; it is just easier to postpone.

  3. Which guide applies? For each of the following, say whether it applies to a May 2028 candidate, and why: (i) the 2014 computer science guide with options; (ii) the mathematics guide with first assessment 2021; (iii) the new extended essay guide with first assessment 2027; (iv) the new mathematics guide with first assessment 2029.

    Show the answer

    (i) No. Computer science moves to the new guide with first assessment 2027, so your 2028 session uses it. The old options and the old Paper 2/3 structure do not apply.

    (ii) Yes. The mathematics guide with first assessment 2021 is still in force for you.

    (iii) Yes. First assessment 2027 means your session is the second examined under it — the two pathways and the five criteria are what you are assessed against.

    (iv) No. First assessment 2029 is after your exams. Resources written for it describe a different course.

    The general rule: "first assessment" is the year of the first examination session under a guide. Your session is May 2028, so a guide applies if its first assessment year is 2028 or earlier and no later guide has superseded it.

    Why this is worth knowing: the internet is full of material for the wrong versions of these courses, especially computer science. Checking the date is five seconds; revising the wrong syllabus costs months.

  4. Turn a subject into core material. Take one thing you know well from mathematics AA HL and one from physics HL, and for each, write a knowledge question it could support in a TOK essay.

    Show the answer

    Mathematics — proof. A proof is a deductive chain from axioms, so a proved theorem is certain relative to its axioms and says nothing at all about whether those axioms describe anything. Knowledge question: "If mathematical knowledge is certain but empty of content about the world, in what sense is it knowledge of the world?" Example: non-Euclidean geometry was logically impeccable long before it turned out to describe spacetime — the certainty was always there, the applicability was not.

    Physics — measurement. Every measurement is reported with an uncertainty, and a result is accepted when independent methods agree within theirs. Knowledge question: "Does the requirement that results be reproducible make the natural sciences more reliable, or merely more conservative — more likely to reject a true anomaly than to accept it?" Example: the 5σ convention means a real but small effect is indistinguishable from noise until enough data accumulate, so the method itself decides what can be known and when.

    Why subject examples win marks. Both of these are specific enough to be examined, and both come from work you have already done, which means you can discuss the method rather than describing the topic. Generic examples ("the internet", "science has changed") give an examiner nothing to award.

    Why this is worth knowing: the strongest TOK essays are written by students who raid their HL subjects for examples. You already have the material; the core pages show you how to use it.
The connection you should exploit
  • Your subjects are your TOK and EE material. A maths HL student can write about proof, certainty and the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics; a physics student about models, measurement and paradigm shift; a CS student about whether a machine can "know" anything. Use examples you actually understand — examiners reward depth over decoration.
  • The EE and the IAs want different things. The IAs reward method and data; the EE rewards a sustained line of argument built on sources. Do not recycle an IA as an EE — it is the most common way to cap at a C.
  • TOK and the EE share one skill: taking a position and justifying it while taking the best counter-argument seriously. Practise it once and it pays in both.

A working timeline for the core

DP1 autumn
EE: choose pathway and supervisor, draft three research questions, kill two of them. CAS: start one creativity and one activity commitment. TOK: learn to write knowledge questions.
DP1 spring
EE: full draft completed before the summer, with the reflection started. CAS: launch the CAS project. TOK: exhibition planned and objects chosen.
DP2 autumn
EE: final draft, supervisor's final reflection session (viva voce), submit. TOK: exhibition commentary finished and assessed; essay title chosen.
DP2 winter
TOK: essay drafted, counter-argument pressure-tested, submitted before the mocks. Everything in the core is now off your plate before exam season.
