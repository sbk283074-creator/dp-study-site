Theory of knowledge — the exhibition

The exhibition asks you to show, using three real objects, that the questions TOK asks are not classroom inventions — they are already happening in the world around you. It is internally assessed and externally moderated, it is worth roughly a third of your TOK grade, and it is the component students most often treat as easy and then lose marks on. It is not easy. It is short, which is a different thing.

One prompt from thirty-five Three objects · ≤950 words ≈33% of the TOK grade · graded A–E
Confirm before you rely on it

Check with your TOK teacher: the exact word limit and what it includes (whether object labels and descriptions count), the file format and submission route your school uses, how images must be presented, and whether your school has any additional rule about objects being personally connected to you. The method on this page does not change; the administrative details can.

1. The task, stated exactly

You choose one prompt from the IB's list of thirty-five prescribed prompts. You choose three objects that connect to it. You write a commentary of at most 950 words in total — that is, around 300 words per object — explaining what each object shows about knowledge and how it bears on the prompt. Your teacher assesses it; the IB moderates the assessment.

RuleWhat it isWhy it exists
One of thirty-five prompts. Prescribed by the IB and fixed for the whole syllabus. You use it verbatim. The prompt is the spine. Every word you write must be traceable back to it.
Three objects. Real things from the world, each with a specific real-world context. They may be physical or digital — a photograph, a screenshot, a tweet, a document, a tool. Specificity is what makes the exhibition yours. A generic object produces a generic commentary.
Not created for the exhibition. An object may be something you made, but not something you made for this task. The objects must be evidence of TOK happening in the world, not illustrations you manufactured to prove a point you had already decided.
At most 950 words. The total written commentary, across all three objects. Brevity forces judgement. You cannot describe; you can only justify.
An image for each object. Each object is presented with an image and identified clearly. The moderator must be able to see what you are talking about. An object the reader cannot picture is an object that is doing no work.
The one-sentence test for every commentary

For each object, you must be able to complete this sentence honestly: "This object shows that ___ about knowledge, and that bears on the prompt because ___." If the first blank is empty, you have described the object instead of using it. If the second blank is empty, you have written a mini-essay that happens to sit next to a photograph.

2. Choosing a prompt

You are choosing thirty-five times over, so use a filter rather than reading the list hopefully. A good prompt for you is one you can attach to three specific objects you actually have access to.

How to choose — four filters, in order
  1. Do I have three objects already in mind, in under two minutes? Not three ideas — three specific, nameable things you can photograph or screenshot this week. If not, move on.
  2. Can each object carry a different question? If all three would make the same point, the exhibition is one idea repeated three times, which is the most common structural failure.
  3. Can I connect it to a theme I have actually studied? The exhibition is where the core theme and your two optional themes pay off. A prompt that lets you use "knowledge and technology" or "knowledge and language" is worth more than one that does not.
  4. Is there a genuine tension? The best exhibitions have at least one object that complicates what the other two suggest.

Strong prompt for you

Prompt 23 — "How important are material tools in the production or acquisition of knowledge?" You own a physics rig, a notebook of simulation code and your grandfather's slide rule. Three objects, three different answers.

Strong prompt for you

Prompt 6 — "How does the way that we organise or classify knowledge affect what we know?" A dictionary organised by radical, a library call number, your own folder system. Classification made concrete.

Weak choice

Prompt 18 — "Are some things unknowable?" Objects tend to become abstract symbols (a black hole image, a question mark) rather than things with a real-world context, and the commentary becomes metaphysics.

Weak choice

Any prompt where your three objects are all famous historical artefacts you know only from the internet. You will describe them; you will not know them.

3. Choosing objects

3.1 What makes an object good

TestPassFail
Specificity "The 4 June 2025 printed edition of a particular newspaper's front page, which I bought at the station." "A newspaper." Anyone could produce this; it has no date, no outlet, no context, and it will produce a commentary about "the media".
Real-world context A specific screenshot of a specific model's output, from a program you ran, on a date. "Artificial intelligence." A field is not an object. There is nothing to photograph and nothing to justify.
Personal connection Your grandfather's slide rule; the lab book you wrote; the dictionary you learned characters from. A stock image from a search engine. It has no connection to you and no context the reader can check.
Distinct question Object 1 asks whether a tool limits what can be measured; Object 2 asks whether a tool can produce rather than merely record; Object 3 asks whether a tool changes which questions are askable. All three ask "does technology change how we get knowledge", with different props.
Not made for this task A graph produced for your physics internal assessment last term. A poster you drew last weekend to illustrate your exhibition.
On the "personal object" question — verify this one

You will often hear that at least one object must be personally connected to you. The published guidance does not, as far as can be verified, state a bare "at least one" rule; what it does say is that objects should have a specific real-world context and that a personal connection keeps the exhibition original and evidences your engagement. Treat it this way: aim for all three objects to be personally connected, and treat one as the absolute minimum. Then confirm the requirement with your TOK teacher — schools apply this differently, and your teacher's word is the one that counts.

The three most overused objects — and exactly why they fail
  1. A generic newspaper or "a news article". It fails on specificity first and on thinking second. Because everyone has seen a newspaper, everyone produces the same commentary: "the media can be biased, so we should check sources." That is a platitude wearing an object. If you must use a newspaper, use a named front page from a named day, and ask about the specific editorial decision the page made.
  2. The atomic bomb, or a photograph of Oppenheimer. The object arrives already carrying enormous moral weight, so students describe the history instead of analysing knowledge, and the link to the prompt gets asserted in one sentence at the end. Borrowed gravity is not analysis. It also tends to collapse every prompt into the same essay about ethics.
  3. A smartphone. It is a category, not an object. Which phone? Whose? Doing what? Without answers, the only available commentary is "technology has changed how we access information" — which is true, is not a knowledge question, and could be written by anyone with a phone.

The rule behind all three: if you could replace your object with a different one and your commentary would not change, you chose the wrong object.

4. How the three objects must relate

This is where most exhibitions are won or lost. The failure is not choosing bad objects; it is choosing three good objects that then behave as three unconnected mini-essays stapled together.

How to make three objects into one exhibition
  1. Assign each object a distinct knowledge question before you write a word. Write the three questions down and check that they are genuinely different — not three phrasings of one question.
  2. Make at least one object complicate the others. If Objects 1 and 2 suggest that tools determine what we can know, Object 3 should be a case where a tool merely makes an old method faster without changing what is known. Tension is what makes the exhibition analytical.
  3. Use one vocabulary consistently across all three. The same two or three TOK concepts — evidence, certainty, interpretation — should be doing work in each commentary. That is what makes the three read as one piece.
  4. Cross-reference explicitly. "Where the slide rule limited precision, this simulation removes the limitation entirely and creates a different problem…" One sentence of cross-reference per commentary is enough, and it is the clearest signal to the reader that this is one exhibition.
  5. Make sure each object can stand alone. The objects do not have to interlink; each must be independently justifiable against the prompt. Do not build a chain where object 3 makes no sense without object 2.

5. How to write each commentary

Each commentary is roughly 300 words and follows the same five moves. Notice that description is not one of them beyond a single clause.

1 · ~25 words
Identify the object and its context. One or two clauses. Name it precisely and say where it comes from and why it is in front of you. Nothing else — no background, no history.
2 · ~90 words
Say what it shows about knowledge. This is the substance. Make a claim about how knowledge is produced, validated, limited or communicated — not about what the object is.
3 · ~70 words
State the knowledge question the object raises. One sentence, clearly a knowledge question, distinct from the other two objects' questions.
4 · ~80 words
Link it to the prompt. Explicitly. Use the prompt's own words. Do not leave the reader to infer the connection.
5 · ~35 words
Implication or tension. One sentence on what follows, or on how this object complicates what the others suggested.
The sentence that earns the mark

Every strong commentary contains a version of this sentence: "What this object shows about knowledge is that ___." Write it deliberately, put it early, and make sure the rest of the commentary is an expansion of it. Weak exhibitions never contain it, because they are busy saying what the object is.

Weak sentenceStrong sentence
"This is a photograph of a slide rule, which was used before calculators were invented." "What this slide rule shows about knowledge is that the precision of a calculation was once a property of an instrument rather than of the problem — which means the questions an engineer could sensibly ask were set by the tool in his hand."
"This screenshot shows a Python program I wrote." "What this notebook shows about knowledge is that a simulation produces consequences rather than observations: it tells us what follows from equations we already had, and its authority therefore depends entirely on a separate act of validation."

6. Images and conventions

  • One image per object, clearly labelled "Object 1", "Object 2", "Object 3", each with a short title and a one-line description of what it is and where it comes from.
  • Use your own photograph or an original screenshot. A stock image has no real-world context, and "I found this online" is not a context.
  • If the object contains text in Chinese or another language, give a brief translation in the description. The moderator must be able to follow your argument.
  • Redact personal data. If a screenshot contains names, faces or identifying information, obscure it. This is basic hygiene and no one will penalise you for it.
  • Keep the descriptions short. They are labels, not commentary. Your words are budgeted at 950 and should be spent on justification.
  • One file, in the format your school specifies. Confirm whether labels and descriptions count towards the 950 words; schools and teachers handle this differently.

7. The criteria, as behaviour

Like the essay, the exhibition is assessed as a single judgement rather than a sum of separate marks: the question the reader is asking throughout is does this exhibition successfully show how TOK manifests in the world around us? You cannot see the rubric, so recognise the behaviour instead.

What the reader looks forTop-band behaviourWeak behaviour
Connection of objects to prompt Each object's connection to the prompt is explicit and argued, and the prompt's own words recur because the argument needs them. The connection is asserted in a single sentence, or the commentary discusses a topic the object suggests rather than the prompt.
Justification, not description The bulk of each commentary explains what the object shows about knowledge. The bulk describes the object, its history and its maker, ending with one line about knowledge.
Critical thinking Each object raises a real question; at least one object complicates the picture the others give. All three objects make the same point; there is no tension anywhere in the exhibition.
Use of TOK concepts Two or three concepts — evidence, perspective, certainty — used precisely and doing work. Either no TOK vocabulary at all, or a list of terms dropped in without being applied to the objects.
Choice of objects Specific, contextualised, personally connected, and each clearly chosen for a reason that is stated. Generic: a phone, a newspaper, a famous photograph. Interchangeable with any other student's exhibition.
Coherence across the three Cross-references between objects; a consistent vocabulary; three questions that differ. Three disconnected pieces that share only a heading.
Observance of the conventions Within the word limit, three labelled images, clean academic register. Over the word limit, unlabelled images, or descriptions so long they crowd out the justification.
Why this works Why "specific and personal" is really an epistemological requirement, not a rule
  1. The task is to show how TOK manifests in the world around you. A generic object manifests TOK in the world around everyone, which is another way of saying it manifests nothing in particular.
  2. A specific object has a history, a maker, a place and a use. Those are the details from which a knowledge question can be built — they are the evidence.
  3. A personal object is one whose context you can actually vouch for. You know why it was kept, what it replaced, and what it was used for. That is knowledge the reader does not have and cannot manufacture.
  4. Therefore specificity is not decoration: it is what makes justification possible. A generic object cannot be justified, only described, and description is precisely what the assessment does not reward.
  5. When this is the wrong tool: do not manufacture a fake personal connection to an object you know nothing about. If your only link is "my cousin owns one", pick a different object. Invented context is detectable and worse than a modest object honestly described.

8. Two complete exemplar exhibitions

Both are written in full, at roughly the right length per commentary. They are models of method. Do not reuse them — the objects are personal to a hypothetical student precisely because the whole point is that they cannot be reused.

8.1 Exemplar exhibition A — material tools (physics and computer science)

Chosen prompt (Prompt 23): "How important are material tools in the production or acquisition of knowledge?"

Object 1

A photograph of the photogate and pendulum rig in my school physics laboratory, with the page from my own lab book recording the period measurements and their uncertainty.

Object 2

A screenshot of a Jupyter notebook I wrote for my Computer Science HL work, integrating a damped-oscillator equation numerically and plotting the result.

Object 3

My grandfather's slide rule (), kept in its original case, which he used for surveying calculations in the 1970s.

Commentary — Object 1

What this apparatus shows about knowledge is that a measurement is not a reading taken from nature but a product manufactured by an instrument. The pendulum's period is not something the photogate observes in any simple sense: the gate defines a beam, the beam defines an interruption, the interruption defines an event, and a clock converts the event into a number. Every one of those steps is a design decision made before any data exists, and each carries its own uncertainty — the width of the bob, the alignment of the beam, the resolution of the timer. My lab book page records a period of 1.94 s with an uncertainty of ±0.02 s, and the uncertainty is not an admission of failure but a description of what the apparatus could deliver. The knowledge question this object raises is: to what extent is a measurement a discovery about the world rather than a consequence of the instrument chosen to make it? That bears directly on the prompt, because it suggests material tools are not neutral accessories to knowledge but partly constitutive of it — the tool is already present in the answer. The implication is uncomfortable: two apparatus built on different principles can produce genuinely different numbers for the same quantity, and "what is the period?" turns out to be incomplete without "measured how".

Commentary — Object 2

What this notebook shows about knowledge is that a material tool can produce knowledge without measuring anything at all. The code integrates a differential equation that has no closed-form solution; what it produces is not an observation of a pendulum but the consequences of equations I already accepted. In that respect it is closer to a proof than to an experiment, and its authority has a different source: it comes from the validity of the model and the correctness of the method, not from contact with the world. The knowledge question this object raises is therefore different from the first: can a tool generate new knowledge, or can it only unpack what was already implicit in the equations we gave it? This connects to the prompt by showing that the importance of a material tool is not limited to extending our senses — a tool can also extend our reasoning, and the two extensions carry different kinds of authority. The tension with Object 1 is deliberate: the photogate gave me a number whose uncertainty I could estimate from the apparatus, whereas the simulation gives me a curve whose uncertainty depends on a separate act of validation I have not yet performed. A tool that computes is not automatically a tool that tells the truth.

Commentary — Object 3

What my grandfather's slide rule shows about knowledge is that a tool can determine which questions are worth asking, not merely how quickly they can be answered. The instrument performs multiplication and trigonometry to roughly three significant figures, and that limit is not an inconvenience imposed on a fixed set of problems — it shaped the set. Surveying methods of that period were designed so that the calculations they required stayed within what the slide rule could deliver in the field; a method needing four-figure precision was, in practice, not a method anyone could use. The knowledge question here is distinct from the other two: not whether a tool shapes the answer, and not whether it can produce knowledge, but whether it constrains the range of questions a community of knowers can formulate. That is the deepest sense in which the prompt's "how important" should be taken. The tension this introduces is the useful one: the slide rule constrained by limitation, whereas my simulation constrains by abundance — it will happily produce a curve for any parameter I supply, whether or not the parameter is meaningful. Taken together, the three objects suggest that material tools matter most where they are invisible: not when they help us, but when they have already decided what we would think to ask.

8.2 Exemplar exhibition B — classification (Chinese A and English A)

Chosen prompt (Prompt 6): "How does the way that we organise or classify knowledge affect what we know?"

Object 1

The radical index () at the front of the I used throughout primary school, photographed at the page for .

Object 2

A screenshot of the library catalogue entry for a book I needed for my extended essay, showing its subject headings and shelfmark.

Object 3

A screenshot of the folders and tags in my own note-taking system, showing the subject folders I created and the untagged notes that have accumulated outside them.

Commentary — Object 1

What this index shows about knowledge is that a filing system can smuggle a theory in without arguing for it. To look up an unfamiliar character by radical, I had to decide which component of the character was its classifying part — and that decision is already an analysis of the writing system. The radical system organises characters by a blend of semantics and shape: , and sit under , and the arrangement teaches, silently and repeatedly, that these characters belong together. It is a genuine piece of knowledge about the language, and I acquired it years before anyone could have explained it to me, purely by using the index. The knowledge question this object raises is whether a classificatory scheme can teach us something true, or whether it only teaches us to navigate itself. That bears on the prompt directly: the organisation here is not a neutral container for knowledge I obtained elsewhere; it produced knowledge as a by-product of being used. The implication is that the same knowledge would be far harder to reach through a pinyin-ordered index, which organises by sound alone — so the choice of scheme changes not just the effort of retrieval but what the user ends up knowing about the structure of the language.

Commentary — Object 2

What this catalogue entry shows about knowledge is that classification decides which neighbours a piece of knowledge has, and neighbours determine which connections are visible. My essay sits at the intersection of two subjects, and the catalogue can only shelve it once. The subject headings assigned to it place it in one discipline's section and therefore next to books it partly belongs with and away from books it partly belongs with too. A researcher browsing the shelves will encounter one half of that neighbourhood and never the other; the classification has not destroyed the connection, but it has made it unlikely to be found. The knowledge question here is distinct from the first: not whether a scheme teaches, but whether it hides — specifically, whether what a system makes difficult to find is, in practice, effectively unknown. This connects to the prompt because it shows organisation working as a filter on attention rather than as a description of content. The tension with Object 1 is the point: the radical index created knowledge by juxtaposition, while the library shelfmark destroys it by separation. The same mechanism — deciding what sits next to what — is constructive in one case and restrictive in the other, which suggests the effect of classification cannot be judged in the abstract, only against what a particular scheme is used for.

Commentary — Object 3

What this folder structure shows about knowledge is that the categories I chose when I was organised are now the limits on what I can retrieve. There are forty-odd untagged notes at the bottom of the list, and I know there is something in one of them about a point my teacher made about a poem; I cannot find it. I have the information in a sense — it is stored, it is mine, no one has taken it — and yet it is unavailable to me in exactly the way that information I never had is unavailable. The knowledge question this object raises is a third one again: at what point does knowledge that cannot be retrieved stop being knowledge at all? That bears on the prompt because it relocates the problem from institutions to individuals: the organising schemes that matter most to a student are the ones they built themselves, precisely because no one audits them. And the tension with the other two objects is what makes the exhibition cohere rather than repeat: the dictionary index and the library shelfmark are public systems that someone else designed and that can in principle be criticised and changed, whereas my tagging system is private, unexamined, and quietly determining what I can think with. Organisation affects what we know most powerfully where nobody has thought to question it.

What both exemplars do that weak ones do not
  1. Each commentary opens by stating what the object shows about knowledge — not what the object is.
  2. Each raises a genuinely different knowledge question: shaping the answer / producing knowledge / limiting the question; teaching / hiding / retrieval.
  3. Each names the prompt's concern explicitly and in the prompt's terms.
  4. Each ends with a tension or implication that connects it to the other two rather than closing it off.
  5. Objects are specific and personal, and the student's own subjects supply the substance.

9. The mistakes that cost the most marks

MistakeWhat it looks likeWhat it costs and how to fix it
Describing the object. Two hundred words on the history of the object, twenty on knowledge. The single biggest loss. Fix by writing the "what this shows about knowledge is…" sentence first and deleting anything that does not expand it.
Three objects, one idea. All three commentaries conclude that perspective matters / that tools matter / that context matters. Assign distinct knowledge questions on paper before drafting. If two questions are paraphrases, replace an object.
The prompt appears once. Quoted at the top, then never mentioned again. Use the prompt's own words at least once in each commentary. It is not repetition; it is relevance.
Generic objects. A phone, a newspaper, a world map with no specific edition. Replace with a specific instance you can vouch for. Ask: could another student swap in a different object without changing a word?
No tension anywhere. The exhibition agrees with itself three times. Make one object genuinely complicate the others, and say so in the commentary.
Commentaries that never cross-reference. Three separate pieces sharing a title. Add one sentence per commentary connecting it to another object — "where Object 1 …, Object 2 …".
Over the word limit. 1,150 words of commentary. Costs you directly and wastes words you did not need. Trim description first; justification is the last thing to cut.
An object made for the task. A diagram you drew specifically to illustrate your exhibition. Replace it. The objects must be evidence from the world, not aids you manufactured.

10. Planning worksheet

Step 1
Shortlist three prompts. For each, write down three objects you could photograph or screenshot this week. Keep the prompt with the most specific, most personal objects.
Step 2
For each object, write one sentence: "This object shows that ___ about knowledge." If you cannot, discard the object now — it is cheaper than discarding it after drafting.
Step 3
Write the three knowledge questions underneath. Check they are different questions, not three phrasings. Rewrite until they are.
Step 4
Decide which object will complicate the other two. Write its tension in one sentence.
Step 5
Choose two or three TOK concepts (from evidence, certainty, truth, interpretation, power, justification, explanation, objectivity, perspective, culture, values, responsibility) that will run across all three commentaries.
Step 6
Draft each commentary to the five-move structure in section 5. Aim for 300 words. Do not exceed 950 in total.
Step 7
Edit with a pen, on paper. Underline the "what this shows about knowledge" sentence in each commentary. If it is not there, write it. Delete every sentence of pure description.
Step 8
Add cross-references between commentaries, check images are labelled, confirm the word count and the file format with your teacher.

11. Practice: prompts, three-object proposals, and justifications

For each prompt below, a three-object proposal is given with a one-paragraph justification. Cover the justification, then write your own proposal for the same prompt and compare on two criteria: are the objects specific, and are the three questions distinct?

  1. Prompt 12: "Is bias inevitable in the production of knowledge?"

    Show proposal and justification

    Objects. (1) The marking rubric my school gave us for a physics internal assessment, with the criterion descriptors highlighted. (2) A screenshot of the autocomplete suggestions my search engine offers when I type the first three characters of a surname common in China. (3) A page from my Chinese A textbook's introduction, listing which authors were selected for the anthology.

    Justification. Object 1 raises whether bias can be designed out: the rubric exists precisely to remove the marker's discretion, so the question is whether explicit criteria eliminate bias or merely relocate it into the choice of criteria. Object 2 raises a different question — whether bias can be structural rather than personal, since no individual decided those suggestions; they are the residue of millions of other people's searches, which makes bias something the tool has rather than something anyone intends. Object 3 raises the third question, about selection: the anthology's bias is in which writers were ever eligible, which is a decision made before assessment and therefore invisible to it. The three are distinct — designed-out bias, structural bias, and selection bias — and Object 2 complicates Object 1 by showing a case where transparency of method does not remove bias, because the method's inputs were already skewed.

  2. Prompt 31: "How can we judge when evidence is adequate?"

    Show proposal and justification

    Objects. (1) A screenshot of a physics data set of mine with error bars, from an experiment where twelve readings were enough to draw a conclusion. (2) A screenshot of a confusion matrix from a classifier I trained, showing 94% accuracy on a test set. (3) A single line from a poem I am studying in English A, quoted in two different critical editions with different punctuation.

    Justification. The three objects make "adequate" mean three different things, which is the whole point. Object 1: adequacy as statistical sufficiency — enough readings that the error bars exclude the competing hypothesis, and the standard is quantitative. Object 2: adequacy as predictive performance — and here the complication is that 94% on a test set can be inadequate if the test set was drawn from the same distribution as the training data, so adequacy depends on a question about how the evidence was gathered rather than how much of there is. Object 3: adequacy as interpretive fit — no quantity of evidence settles which punctuation is right, and the judgement is made against a scholarly tradition rather than a threshold. Together they force the conclusion that "adequate" is answerable only relative to a standard, and that the interesting question is who set the standard and whether it can be criticised.

  3. Prompt 22: "What role do experts play in influencing our consumption or acquisition of knowledge?"

    Show proposal and justification

    Objects. (1) The IB's own command-term glossary from my physics data booklet — the definitions of "determine", "evaluate" and "discuss". (2) A screenshot of the citation count and journal name on a paper I used for my extended essay. (3) A screenshot of a video recommendation from a mathematics channel explaining a proof I had already been taught in class.

    Justification. Object 1 raises the question of experts as licensers of vocabulary: before I can answer a question, an authority has decided what counts as answering it, and the command terms quietly determine what kind of knowledge I am even allowed to display. Object 2 raises a different question — experts as filters on attention, since I chose that paper partly because of the journal, which means the community's prestige system decided what I read before I evaluated a word of it. Object 3 raises the third: experts as substitutes for understanding, where the explanation was so clear that I stopped being able to tell whether I understood the proof or had merely followed it. The complication is in Object 3 and it is the strongest point in the exhibition: expert mediation can produce the feeling of knowledge without the thing, which is a cost that Object 1 and Object 2 do not capture.

  4. Prompt 35: "In what ways do values affect the production of knowledge?"

    Show proposal and justification

    Objects. (1) A screenshot of the loss function and training objective in a classifier I wrote, with the line that weights false positives against false negatives. (2) The page of my school's research-ethics form that asks whether a project involves human participants. (3) A photograph of the acknowledgements page of my Chinese A set text, listing the scholars whose annotated edition was used.

    Justification. The three locate values at three different depths. Object 1 puts values inside the method: the weighting of one error type against another is a moral decision expressed as a number, and it is made before any data is seen — which means the knowledge produced is value-laden in a way that no amount of rigour later can remove. Object 2 puts values around the method: the ethics form constrains what may be asked at all, so values determine the boundary of the knowable rather than the content of the known. Object 3 puts values in the transmission: which annotated edition a student reads determines which reading of a classical text they inherit, and the values are those of a scholarly tradition centuries old. Object 1 complicates the other two because it shows values entering at the most technical and least visible point — a single line of code — which is exactly where the claim that a method is value-free is most often made.
