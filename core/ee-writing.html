Research, writing and the criteria

You have a research question. Now you have to produce four thousand words that would convince a sceptical reader who already knows the subject. That is a different skill from anything else in the diploma — the exams reward correct answers, the IAs reward method, and the EE rewards a sustained argument. This page is the method: how to research without accidentally plagiarising, what a line of argument actually is, and exactly where each of the thirty marks is won or thrown away.

Essay: max 4,000 words RPF: one reflective statement, max 500 words Criteria: 6 · 6 · 6 · 8 · 4 = 30
Confirm these numbers with your coordinator

The 4,000-word essay limit, the 500-word reflective statement on the Reflection and Progress Form (RPF), and the 30-mark criteria split (framework 6, knowledge and understanding 6, analysis and line of argument 6, discussion and evaluation 8, reflection 4) are confirmed against the IB's own published curriculum update and multiple school sources quoting the guide for first assessment 2027. What I could not verify — because the official subject brief is behind bot protection — is whether your session requires an abstract and whether it counts toward the 4,000 words, and the exact word-count rules for appendices and tables. Ask. Do not guess.

The research workflow

Research is not reading until you feel informed. It is a cycle with four stages, and you will run it three or four times before you write a word of the final draft.

The research cycle
  1. Ask a sharper sub-question. Your RQ is too big to research directly. Break it into three to five sub-questions, each of which you can answer with a specific kind of evidence. Example: for the pendulum question — (a) what does the series expansion predict at 60°? (b) what uncertainty does my apparatus impose? (c) are the two separable?
  2. Go where the evidence for that sub-question lives. Not "search the topic" — search the sub-question. Different sub-questions have different literatures.
  3. Record it immediately in your research log, with a one-line note on what it settled and a one-line note on what it failed to settle.
  4. Re-plan. Which sub-question is now weakest? That is where you search next. Repeat until every sub-question has at least two sources that speak to it, or until you have decided to narrow the RQ so that it does.

Judging a source in ninety seconds

The five checks
  1. Who wrote it, and what are their credentials in this specific field? A distinguished physicist writing about education is not an authority on education.
  2. Where was it published, and was it peer-reviewed? Peer review is not a guarantee of truth, but it is a guarantee that someone competent checked the method.
  3. How old is it? In CS and physics, five years is old. In literary criticism, a 1970 article can still be central — and being superseded is itself something you can discuss.
  4. What is it arguing, and what would count against it? If you cannot identify the source's own thesis, you have not read it, you have only recognised the words.
  5. Who cites it, and how? Follow the citation trail in both directions. This is the single fastest way to find the three sources you actually needed.
The note-taking trap that produces plagiarism

Almost nobody plagiarises on purpose. They do it like this: you read a source, you type a "summary" into your notes by rephrasing sentence by sentence, and three weeks later you paste those notes into the essay. The paraphrase is now close enough to the original to be a breach, and it is in your own file — which makes it look deliberate.

  1. Never copy a sentence into your notes, even "temporarily". If a passage is worth having exactly, quote it, put it in quotation marks, and record the page number in the same action.
  2. Write notes as answers to your sub-questions, not as summaries of sources. The heading of each note should be a sub-question, not a citation. This forces your brain to process rather than transcribe.
  3. Close the source before you write the note. If you cannot write it with the source closed, you have not understood it — go back and read again.
  4. Keep a "dissent" column: one line on what this source cannot explain. That column becomes your evaluation section.

Why examiners can tell

An essay with a real research log behind it cites specific things: a page, a figure number, a coefficient, a dataset row. An essay written from vague memory cites whole works — "Smith (2019) argues that…" with nothing you could check. Specificity is the visible trace of real research, and it is what separates the top of criterion B from the middle.

How the essay is structured

There is no IB-mandated section template, and the conventions differ by subject — a maths essay has numbered sections and lemmas, a literature essay has continuous prose. What is fixed is the job each part does.

Background / contextGive the reader what they need to follow the argument — nothing more.Definitions, established results, the state of the debate. Every sentence must be needed later.~10%
MethodShow that your approach can actually answer the question.What you did, why that method and not another, what its limits are, and — if people or data are involved — consent and anonymisation.~8%

Four structural mistakes that cost marks immediately
  1. Saving the line of argument for the conclusion. If your argument first appears at the end, the body was a literature review and criterion C is capped.
  2. A "findings" body. Sections titled "Source A", "Source B", "Experiment 2" produce description, not reasoning. Title sections by the move they make: "Why the drag model cannot account for the residual", not "The drag experiment".
  3. Background that never gets used. Every paragraph of context you do not later lean on is a paragraph an examiner reads as padding. Cut it.
  4. A conclusion that restates instead of answers. "In conclusion, this essay has examined…" is worth nothing. Begin the conclusion with the answer.

What a line of argument actually is

This is the difference between a B and an A, so slow down here.

A line of argument is a claim plus the chain of reasons that makes a reader accept it, with the strongest objection dealt with inside the chain rather than at the end. It is not a list of everything you found. It is not a summary of your sections. It is a single position that the whole essay exists to establish, and it must be possible to state it in three sentences.

How to build one: the five-move template

Build your line of argument before you draft
  1. Move 1 — State the claim. One sentence, the answer you currently believe. Write it in the introduction and again in the conclusion, better worded.
  2. Move 2 — Give the main reason. "This is true because …" The because must point at evidence, not at your conviction.
  3. Move 3 — Give the strongest objection. Not a straw man. If you cannot think of one, ask your supervisor or use the best source that disagrees with you. An essay with no real objection has no argument, only an assertion.
  4. Move 4 — Answer the objection with evidence. Not by dismissing it. Either show it is smaller than it looks, or concede part of it and shrink your claim accordingly.
  5. Move 5 — Say what would change your answer. This is the single most reliable generator of criterion D marks, and almost no students do it.

Sentence frames you can use directly:

  • "The obvious explanation for X is Y. It fails because …"
  • "A stronger reading is that X is really Z; this would predict …, which is what we observe."
  • "Against this, [source] argues … The objection has force if …, but the data shows …"
  • "The limit of this argument is …; it matters because …"
  • "This conclusion would have to be revised if … were shown to be …"
Worked example A line of argument built from scratch (physics)

Research question: "To what extent can the departure of a simple pendulum's period from \(T_{0}=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\) at amplitudes up to 60° be accounted for by the neglected terms of the small-angle expansion rather than by systematic experimental error?"

  1. Move 1 — the claim. "Most of the departure is theoretical; apparatus error accounts for a small, quantifiable remainder."
  2. Move 2 — the reason. The exact period is \(T = 4\sqrt{\ell/g}\,K(\sin(\theta_{0}/2))\), and expanding the complete elliptic integral gives \(T/T_{0} = 1 + \tfrac{1}{16}\theta_{0}^{2} + \tfrac{11}{3072}\theta_{0}^{4} + \cdots\). At \(\theta_{0}=60^{\circ}=\pi/3\) this predicts a fractional increase of about 1.7%.
  3. Move 3 — the objection. Air drag and finite string mass also lengthen the period, and a student apparatus introduces timing error of order 0.02 s. Perhaps the observed increase is apparatus, not theory.
  4. Move 4 — the answer to the objection. Drag and string mass are, to first order, amplitude-independent, whereas the series term grows as \(\theta_{0}^{2}\). So measure the period across a range of amplitudes and fit the residual to \(a + b\theta_{0}^{2}\). If \(b\) matches \(1/16\) within uncertainty and \(a\) is small, theory wins. This turns the objection into a measurement — which is the whole point.
  5. Move 5 — what would change it. If \(a\) came out comparable to \(b\theta_{0}^{2}\) at small amplitudes, the apparatus term would dominate and the attribution would fail. And if the fitted \(b\) exceeded \(1/16\), the model would be missing a physical effect, not just a term.
Why this is a line of argument and not a lab report: the experiment exists to separate two competing explanations for the same number. Remove the objection and there is nothing to investigate; remove the theory and there is nothing to compare against. That mutual dependence is criterion C, and move 5 is criterion D.
Connection to TOK

Moves 3 and 5 are exactly what TOK rewards: taking the best counter-position seriously, and identifying what would falsify your own claim. If you have built a real line of argument here, half the work on your TOK essay — choosing and testing a counterclaim — is already done. Do not submit the same content twice, but do reuse the habit.

The five criteria: what earns marks and what caps you

Examiners mark positively with a best-fit model: they read the whole essay and choose the descriptor that most accurately describes it. Total is 30. The table below gives you the specific error that loses the marks in each criterion — that is the useful part.

Criterion A — Framework for the essay (6 marks)

Do the research question, research methods and structural conventions followed provide an effective framework for the essay?

Top-band workWhat caps you at a C
The RQ is explicit, focused and clearly arguable. The method stated is obviously capable of answering it. Structure follows the subject's conventions (numbered results in maths; continuous argument in literature) and is signposted. Word count is used where it matters. The RQ is a topic with a question mark, or appears for the first time halfway down page 2. The method is described but not justified. Sections are in an arbitrary order.
The specific mistake

Describing your method instead of justifying it. "I measured the period ten times" is description. "Ten trials reduce the standard error of the mean to below the 0.1% level required to distinguish the \(\theta_{0}^{4}\) term from noise" is justification. One sentence of why this method and not another is the cheapest two marks on the paper.

Criterion B — Knowledge and understanding (6 marks)

Does the student demonstrate knowledge and understanding of the subject matter being used in their research?

Top-band workWhat caps you at a C
Terminology is used precisely and fluently; concepts are explained where they matter and assumed where they do not. Sources are engaged with at the level of the argument they make, not their conclusion. Knowledge is deployed to support the line of argument. Concepts are named but not explained; a term is used in several inconsistent senses across the essay; knowledge sits next to the argument rather than inside it.
The specific mistake

Displaying knowledge that does no work. Two pages on the history of your topic that the argument never uses does not demonstrate understanding — it demonstrates that you can look things up. Every piece of knowledge must be load-bearing. Ask of each paragraph: "what does the reader now believe that they did not before?" If the answer is "nothing, but now they know a fact", cut it.

Criterion C — Analysis and line of argument (6 marks)

Does the student analyse the information presented in the essay and produce a line of argument?

Top-band workWhat caps you at a C
Every section advances the argument and depends on the previous one. Evidence is interpreted, not reported: you explain why a result is what it is. The claim is developed through the essay and is visibly stronger at the end than at the start. Findings are summarised rather than analysed; sections could be reordered without damage; the essay's thesis is stated in the introduction and simply repeated in the conclusion.
The specific mistake

The paragraph that reports instead of interprets. "Figure 3 shows that the period increases with amplitude." That is the reader's job, and the examiner already saw it. Write instead: "Figure 3 shows an increase of 1.6% at 60°, which is 0.1% below the series prediction — a discrepancy consistent with the amplitude-independent drag term estimated in §3, and therefore not evidence against the model." Same fact; one is a caption, the other is analysis. Do this in every single body paragraph and criterion C takes care of itself.

Criterion D — Discussion and evaluation (8 marks)

Does the student discuss the findings and evaluate the essay? This is the highest-weighted criterion — more than a quarter of the whole essay. It is where the A is decided.

Top-band workWhat caps you at a C
Evaluation is distributed through the essay, not confined to the end. Limitations are specific and weighed by how much they matter. Alternative interpretations are considered and answered. The conclusion is proportionate to the evidence and states what would change it. A "limitations" paragraph at the end listing generic problems ("time constraints", "limited sources"). Discussion restates findings. The conclusion over-claims beyond what the method supports.
The specific mistake

Generic limitations. "A limitation was that I only had access to a limited number of sources" is worth nothing — every candidate could write it. A real limitation names the size of the effect: "Because the timing resolution is 0.01 s, the residual \(a\) can be determined only to ±0.3%, which means a drag contribution below that threshold cannot be distinguished from zero — so my conclusion holds only for drag effects above roughly 0.3%." That is evaluation. It takes two sentences.

The three evaluation questions to answer somewhere in the essay
  1. What is the strongest alternative explanation of your evidence, and why is it weaker?
  2. How large is your largest source of uncertainty, and does it change the answer?
  3. What single finding would force you to withdraw your claim?

Answer all three explicitly and you have done most of the work for 8 marks.

Criterion E — Reflection (4 marks)

Does the student evaluate the effect of the extended essay learning experience on them as a learner? This is assessed through the single reflective statement on the RPF, not through the essay.

Top-band workWhat caps you at a C
Focuses on growth as a learner and transferable skills. Names a specific intellectual difficulty and how the approach changed because of it. Shows what will be carried into other work. Concise, honest, and specific to this research. A chronological diary ("first I chose a topic, then I read…"). Only 4 marks, but students routinely lose three of them this way.

Writing the reflection properly

The new guide sharpened this: reflection is about your growth as a learner and the transferable skills you developed, not about your week-by-week progress. Four marks, one statement, written after the viva voce. It is the only ATL skill assessed by a dedicated criterion, so treat it as an assessed component, not an afterthought.

The four paragraphs that work
  1. What changed in your thinking. Name the moment your understanding shifted — a source that contradicted you, a method that failed, a definition you had been using loosely.
  2. How you adapted. Not "I worked harder". What specifically did you change: the question, the method, the way you searched, the way you took notes?
  3. The transferable skill. Name it and say where it will go next: evaluating a source's method before its conclusion; separating a claim from its evidence; managing a project with no external deadline.
  4. What you would do differently, and why that is not regret. "I would have fixed the method before collecting data, because the re-collection cost three weeks and the analysis would have been stronger" — this shows you understood the process, which is the whole point.

Keep a Researcher's Reflection Space — an informal notes file — from week one. When you sit down to write the 500 words, you should be selecting from notes, not inventing. Reflection written from memory in one evening reads exactly like it.

Weak (do not write this)Strong (write this)
"I learned a lot about physics and improved my research skills.""I had assumed the theoretical correction would be a small refinement; discovering it was the dominant effect forced me to treat the apparatus error as the object of study rather than an inconvenience."
"The hardest part was finding sources.""My first three sources disagreed because they used different definitions of the same term; resolving that taught me to check a definition against the method that produced the result before accepting the result."
"I would manage my time better next time.""I drafted the background before fixing the method, which meant rewriting it twice; in future I will establish what I need to know before writing what I already know."
"This experience will help me at university.""Reading a paper for its method rather than its abstract is now automatic, and I used it last month when evaluating a claim in my CS internal assessment."
Two ways to lose reflection marks outright
  1. Writing it as a summary of the essay. The examiner has read the essay. Do not tell them what is in it.
  2. Writing it in a different language from the essay. Your reflective statement and your essay must be in the same language. Confirm the rule with your coordinator, but plan on it: if you write the essay in Chinese, write the RPF statement in Chinese.

The reflection sessions and the viva voce

Three mandatory sessions with your supervisor: an initial session when you are forming the question, an interim session while you are working, and a final concluding interview — the viva voce — after the essay is finished. The viva voce is short. It is not a defence or an oral exam with a grade; it is the conversation that lets your supervisor confirm the work is yours and that gives you the material for the RPF statement.

Preparing for the viva voce
  1. Re-read your own essay the day before. You will be asked about it and you will have forgotten which source said what. Make an index card: RQ, claim, three key sources, biggest limitation.
  2. Prepare answers to four questions you will certainly be asked: Why this question? What was the hardest decision you made? Where did you change your mind? What would you do differently?
  3. Prepare one thing you are proud of and one thing you are unsure about. Naming your own weak point is the strongest signal of genuine engagement, and it feeds directly into the reflection.
  4. Bring your research log. If asked where a source came from, you should be able to show the trail.
  5. Write the RPF statement within 48 hours, while the conversation is fresh. Do not let it drift into the following month.

Academic honesty, AI tools, and citation

Everything in your essay, your code and your reflective statement must be your own work. The same work cannot be submitted for two components — your EE must not duplicate your IA, and it must not duplicate another DP component.

Use of AI / external helpPermitted?How to handle it
Finding academic sources you then read in fullYesCite the sources you actually read. No need to credit the search tool.
Generating synthetic test data for a simulationYes, with careYou must defend why the data is representative of the case your argument concerns. Say so in the method.
Help with testing infrastructure, boilerplate, debuggingYesAcknowledge it in the method or an appendix note.
Summarising a source you have not readNoThe source must be read by you, or it must not be in your bibliography.
Generating or structuring your written contentNoThis is the line. Any AI-generated prose in the essay is a breach.
Proofreading and language correction by another personLimitedChecking is fine; rewriting your sentences is not. Ask your school's policy — it varies.
Using a published dataset, graph, image or codeYesCite it as you would any source, including the licence or repository where relevant.
Citation: the rules that matter
  1. Pick one style and use it everywhere. The IB does not mandate a style; consistency is what is checked. MLA, APA and Chicago are all fine.
  2. Cite at the point of use, not at the end of the paragraph. The reader must be able to tell which sentence came from which source.
  3. Quote exactly, mark it as a quote, give the page. If you change anything, use square brackets or an ellipsis.
  4. A paraphrase still needs a citation. Changing the words does not change the ownership of the idea.
  5. Bibliography and in-text citations must match. Every in-text citation appears in the bibliography; every bibliography entry is used.
  6. Cite code, data and images too — including anything you generated with help.
  7. Do not use footnotes to dodge the word count. Explanatory material in footnotes is generally counted as part of the essay; confirm the exact rule with your coordinator.

A realistic sixteen-week timeline

Sixteen weeks is comfortable if you start it in DP1. The milestones below assume roughly forty hours of work, which is what the IB expects — about two and a half hours a week, plus three heavier weeks.

Week 1
Initial reflection session. Three candidate RQs written out. Each passes the five tests and the 20-minute source test. Supervisor meeting 1.
Week 2
RQ locked and registered. Research log opened. Ethics check done: does this method need consent? Sub-questions written.
Weeks 3–5
Research phase. Target 15–25 sources logged, with the "dissent" column filled for every one. Kill weak sub-questions and narrow the RQ if the evidence is not there.
Week 6
Interim reflection session. Bring the log and a one-page argument sketch: claim, main reason, strongest objection, answer. This is the single most useful meeting of the whole process.
Week 7
Section plan with word budgets. Line of argument written out in full prose — 300 words, standalone. If you cannot write it, you do not have one yet.
Weeks 8–11
Draft. Body first, in whatever order is easiest; introduction and conclusion last. Do not edit while drafting. Aim to finish at 4,600 words.
Week 12
Structural edit alone: does each section depend on the previous one? Is the argument visible in the section titles? Cut to 4,200.
Week 13
Full draft to supervisor — the one draft you are entitled to have reviewed. While waiting: check every in-text citation against the bibliography.
Week 14
Apply the feedback. Then the evaluation pass: add the three evaluation questions, make every limitation specific and sized. Cut to 4,000.
Week 15
Final reflection session — the viva voce. Prepare the four questions. Within 48 hours, write the 500-word reflective statement on the RPF.
Week 16
Proofread, format, check the bibliography, confirm the file is clean with no comments left in it. Submit before the school deadline, not on it.
The ten most common reasons extended essays lose marks
  1. The question is descriptive. No argument is possible, so criteria C and D collapse together. Fix it in week 1 or not at all.
  2. The body is a list of findings. Sections that could be reordered are sections doing no work.
  3. Evaluation is confined to a final paragraph of generic limitations. Eight marks.
  4. The conclusion over-claims. "This proves…" when the method supports "this suggests, within the following limits…".
  5. Sources are reported, not engaged with. Summarising conclusions instead of examining methods.
  6. The method is described but never justified. Cheap marks, routinely dropped.
  7. Background material that is never used. Padding is visible and it displaces evaluation.
  8. Citation inconsistency. A missing bibliography entry or a paraphrase with no citation is an academic-honesty risk, not a formatting slip.
  9. An IA submitted as an EE. Method and data without an argument. Extremely common in physics and CS.
  10. The reflection is a diary. Four marks, and three of them go.

Practice

Cover the answers. Each of these is a real decision you will have to make in your own essay, and each one is worth marks in a specific criterion.

  1. Criterion C — turning reporting into analysis. Below are two paragraphs on the same piece of evidence. Identify which one earns criterion C, and rewrite the weak one so it would.

    Version AVersion B
    The study found that the yield of the reaction increased from 41 % to 63 % when the temperature was raised from 40 °C to 60 °C. The researchers concluded that higher temperature improves yield. This shows that temperature is an important factor in this reaction. The 22-point rise in yield between 40 °C and 60 °C is large relative to the ±3 % uncertainty on each measurement, so the trend is real — but the study holds every other variable fixed, which means it supports a claim about this reaction under these conditions and not about the mechanism. In particular, an Arrhenius-style exponential would predict a steady rise, whereas the data flatten above 55 °C; that flattening is the interesting result and the paper does not discuss it.
    Show the answer

    Version B earns the marks. Version A restates the source's own conclusion and then adds a generic summary sentence ("temperature is an important factor") that no one would dispute — that is reporting, and it is the single most common way criterion C is capped at 2 or 3.

    What B does that A does not:

    1. It sizes the effect against the measurement uncertainty, so it judges whether the finding is strong enough to carry an argument.
    2. It states the scope of what the evidence can support (this reaction, these conditions) — which is exactly what lets you use it without over-claiming later.
    3. It notices what the source did not say — the flattening above 55 °C — and turns that gap into the thing your essay will investigate. That is the move that produces an original line of argument.

    The repair pattern for your own draft. Take each paragraph that summarises a source and add three sentences: (i) how strong is this evidence, and against what standard; (ii) what exactly does it license me to claim, and what does it not; (iii) what does it leave unexplained that my essay needs. If you cannot write sentence (iii), you have found a place where your essay has no work to do — either find the gap or drop the source.

    Where the marks come from: criterion C rewards interpretation and the construction of a line of argument. Every sentence of type (iii) is evidence that the argument is yours rather than the sources'.

  2. Criterion D — writing the evaluation. Your essay argues that "the introduction of proportional representation in [country] reduced the effective number of parties contesting elections". Write the three evaluation sentences that criterion D is looking for, given that you have data for only two election cycles after the reform and no data on voter preferences.

    Show a model answer

    1. A limitation of the evidence, sized. "Two post-reform cycles is a thin basis for a trend claim: with n = 2 the effective-number-of-parties series cannot distinguish a structural shift from the ordinary cycle-to-cycle noise of a party system, so the finding is best read as consistent with the hypothesis rather than as confirming it."

    2. A limitation of the method. "Effective number of parties is computed from vote shares, so it registers changes in how votes are distributed but not in how voters' underlying preferences moved; a party system can look more fragmented while preferences are unchanged, and I have no preference data with which to separate the two."

    3. An alternative interpretation and why it is less persuasive. "The fall could instead be attributed to the concurrent change in district magnitude, which the reform package altered at the same time; because the two changes were legislated together, my design cannot separate them, and the honest conclusion is that the reform bundle — not proportional representation alone — is what the data speak to."

    Why this earns criterion D. Each sentence names a specific weakness rather than a generic one ("more research is needed", "the sample was small"), says what the weakness does to the conclusion, and stops short of abandoning the argument. The third one is the highest-value: engaging an alternative explanation and explaining why it cannot be ruled out is exactly the discussion-and-evaluation behaviour the criterion describes.

    Where the marks come from: discussion and evaluation is worth 8 marks — the largest single criterion. Essays that treat it as an apologetic final paragraph score 2–3; essays that integrate it score 6–8.

  3. Criterion E — reflection that is not a diary. Here are three reflection extracts. Rank them and explain the ranking in terms of what the criterion actually assesses.

    • (i) "At first I found it hard to find sources, but then I learned to use the library database and it got easier. I am proud of how much I learned."
    • (ii) "My supervisor suggested I look at election data, which was helpful. I then wrote the essay and checked it carefully."
    • (iii) "My first question assumed that party fragmentation could be measured by counting parties, which I now think is wrong: counting treats a party with 40 % and one with 2 % identically. Switching to the effective-number measure changed my conclusion in one place — the 1993 result stopped looking like a break — and I would now check the measure before the data in any future project."
    Show the answer

    Ranking: (iii) ≫ (i) > (ii).

    (iii) is a strong reflection because it does three things at once. It identifies a decision the writer made and the assumption behind it; it shows the decision being revised in a way that changed the essay's content (the 1993 reading); and it extracts a transferable habit — interrogate the measure before the data — that would apply to a different project. That is precisely the "growth and transferable skills" language of the current criterion.

    (i) is weak but not empty. It describes an obstacle and a response, which is reflection of a sort, but it stays at the level of logistics and ends in a feeling ("I am proud"). Nothing changed in the essay as a result, and nothing generalises.

    (ii) is the weakest because it is a narrative of what happened, largely crediting the supervisor, with no decision, no revision and no generalisation. Narrative is not reflection.

    The test to apply to your own reflections: for each entry ask — did a decision of mine get reversed, and did that reversal change something visible in the essay, and could the lesson be stated as a rule I would apply next time? Three yeses is a strong entry; one yes is a diary.

    Where the marks come from: criterion E is only 4 marks, but it is assessed through the three reflection sessions and the viva as well as the essay, and it is the criterion most often lost entirely by students who keep a log of what they did.

  4. Putting it together — the line of argument. A student writes: "This essay will discuss the causes of the 2008 financial crisis, looking at deregulation, subprime lending and securitisation." Explain why this is not a line of argument, and write two versions that are — one weak-but-acceptable, one strong.

    Show the answer

    Why it fails. It is a topic plus a list. It contains no claim that anyone could disagree with, so there is nothing to argue, nothing to counter, and no way for the essay to be right or wrong. Every essay with this shape ends up as three themed summaries stapled together, which is the classic criterion C cap.

    Weak-but-acceptable (a position, but binary): "Securitisation, not deregulation, was the necessary condition for the crisis: it is the mechanism without which the losses in subprime lending could not have been distributed widely enough to threaten the system. Deregulation made the losses larger but not systemic."

    Strong (a structural claim with identifiable stakes): "The standard account treats deregulation and securitisation as independent contributing causes. I argue they are linked causally in one direction only: securitisation created the demand for the risky loans that deregulation then permitted. If this is right, then reforms that re-regulate lending without addressing securitisation would not have prevented the crisis — and the post-crisis reforms that focused on bank capital rather than on securitisation were aimed at the wrong link."

    Why the second is stronger. It is falsifiable (you could look for evidence of securitisation-driven demand), it has a counter-claim built in (the two factors are independent), and it has a consequence — a claim about which reforms were misdirected. That consequence is what makes the conclusion more than a restatement, and it is what criterion C rewards at the top of the band.

    Where the marks come from: a line of argument must be a claim someone could reject. If your thesis sentence cannot be disagreed with, it is a topic, not an argument.

What follows is an outline written for this site, not a finished essay. It is a planning document of the kind you should produce in week 7. Research question

To what extent can the departure of a simple pendulum's period from \(T_{0}=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\) at amplitudes up to 60° be accounted for by the neglected terms of the small-angle expansion rather than by systematic experimental error?Exemplar outline — Physics, subject-focused pathway

Why this question

The small-angle approximation is taught as a fact and abandoned as soon as it is used. Almost nobody asks how much it costs, or whether the cost is visible at school scale. The question is focused (one system, one amplitude range), arguable (two competing explanations: theory and apparatus), answerable (the series coefficients are published; the measurement is a school laboratory task), and not answerable with one fact (the answer is an attribution, which requires weighing).

Line of argument

  1. Claim: the departure is predominantly theoretical; apparatus error contributes a smaller, separable term.
  2. Main reason: the elliptic-integral expansion predicts a fractional increase of \(1.7\%\) at 60°, and the prediction is amplitude-dependent as \(\theta_{0}^{2}\).
  3. Strongest objection: air drag, string mass and finite timing resolution also lengthen the measured period, and do so at all amplitudes.
  4. Answer to the objection: because the competing effects differ in their amplitude dependence, fitting the residual to \(a+b\theta_{0}^{2}\) separates them; the apparatus contributes to \(a\), the theory to \(b\).
  5. What would change the answer: a fitted \(b\) significantly different from \(1/16\), or an \(a\) large enough to swamp the quadratic term at small amplitudes.

Reflective statement — exemplar paragraph

For most of this project I treated the small-angle approximation as a fact I already understood, and my first plan was simply to measure how wrong it was. That framing assumed the interesting quantity was the size of the error. It was only when two sources disagreed about whether the discrepancy was physical or instrumental that I realised the real object of study was the attribution of the error, and that my method could not distinguish the two causes at all. Rebuilding the experiment around a fit to \(a+b\theta_{0}^{2}\) rather than a single amplitude measurement changed what I was doing entirely: the data I had already collected became insufficient, and I had to re-run it. The lesson I will carry forward is that a method which cannot separate two competing explanations produces only a number, not an answer — a habit I have since used when reading a paper's method before its abstract.Exemplar — approximately 150 words of a 500-word statement
What makes that paragraph work

It names a specific intellectual error (wrong object of study), a specific consequence (the method had to change and data had to be re-collected), and a transferable habit stated as a general principle that is then applied elsewhere. There is no chronology, no "I learned a lot", no summary of the essay. That is what the four marks are for.
