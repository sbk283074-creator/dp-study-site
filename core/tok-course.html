Theory of knowledge — the course

TOK is not a subject you learn facts in; it is a subject you learn to interrogate facts with. You already know a great deal of mathematics, physics, computer science and literature. TOK asks a different kind of question about all of it: what makes something knowledge rather than a belief, and what are you entitled to claim once you have it? Everything on this page exists to make you able to answer that well.

Guide: first assessment 2022 100 teaching hours Two assessed tasks: essay 67% · exhibition 33%
Confirm before you rely on it

Show this page to your TOK teacher and confirm the details for your session — especially which two optional themes your school has chosen, how your school handles "ways of knowing", and the exact wording and word limits of the two tasks. The method on this page will not change; the administrative numbers can.

1. What TOK actually is

Here is the fastest way to understand TOK: every other subject in your diploma asks first-order questions — questions that are answered inside a discipline, using that discipline's methods. "What is the derivative of x sin x?" is a first-order question. "What is the half-life of this isotope?" is a first-order question. "Who narrates Great Expectations?" is a first-order question.

TOK asks second-order questions — questions about the discipline itself. "Why does a proof give us a kind of certainty that an experiment cannot, and is that certainty worth the price?" That question cannot be answered by doing more mathematics. It is answered by thinking about what mathematics is and what mathematicians do.

Not thisThis instead
Not a philosophy course. You are not being asked to master Kant or to settle whether knowledge is justified true belief. You may use philosophical ideas, but the mark is for your argument about a knowledge question, not for your ability to reproduce someone else's. A course in second-order reasoning about real knowledge. You take knowledge you actually have — from maths, physics, CS, your languages — and ask what entitles anyone to call it knowledge.
Not a current-affairs course. "Should AI be regulated?" is a policy question. Discussing it produces opinion, produces nothing markable, and produces essays that read like editorials. A course in the structure of the debate. "On what grounds can we say a system that cannot give reasons knows anything?" is a knowledge question. It uses AI as its material but it is not about AI policy.
Not an opinion essay. "In my opinion, science is more reliable than art" is not an argument; it is a preference with a semicolon. A course in justified evaluation. Every claim you make must be tested against the strongest version of the opposing claim, and then you must say what follows.
Not a memory test. Nothing in TOK is worth memorising except the vocabulary you need to think precisely. A transferable skill. The ability to take a position, steelman the opposition, and state the implications is exactly what the extended essay, the IAs and a university admissions interview all reward.
What actually gets marked

Two tasks. The essay (1,600 words, one of six prescribed titles, externally assessed, roughly two-thirds of the TOK grade) is assessed on how clearly, coherently and critically you explore the title. The exhibition (one prompt from thirty-five, three objects, at most 950 words of commentary, internally assessed and externally moderated, roughly one-third) is assessed on how well your objects show TOK operating in the world around you. Both are graded A–E. What neither task rewards is description: knowing about an area of knowledge earns nothing; using it to test a claim earns everything.

Connection

The essay leans on the areas of knowledge; the exhibition leans on the themes. That single sentence explains most of the course design and most of the confusion students have in DP1. If you are writing about mathematics or the natural sciences, you are probably doing essay work. If you are writing about technology, language, politics, religion or indigenous societies, you are probably doing exhibition work. Both are explained in TOK — the essay and TOK — the exhibition.

2. The skeleton of the course

The 2022 guide organises TOK into three layers. Learn these three lines and you will never be lost in a TOK lesson again:

  1. One compulsory core theme — knowledge and the knower. Everyone does this.
  2. Two optional themes, chosen by your teacher from five. Which two your school picked matters to you, because the exhibition is where themes pay off.
  3. Five areas of knowledge, all compulsory. You do not choose; you study all five. This is where the essay lives.
The most common structural error

Students treat the five areas of knowledge as a buffet and write an essay about their two favourites. That is not wrong in itself — but the reason to use two areas is contrast. Choosing mathematics and physics gives you almost no contrast, because their methods and standards of evidence are close cousins. Choosing mathematics and history, or the natural sciences and the arts, gives you a genuine clash of methods to interrogate. Pick for friction, not for comfort.

2.1 The core theme: knowledge and the knower

The core theme is the only theme everyone does, and it is the one that makes TOK personal. Its force is in the word and. It is not "knowledge" and separately "knowers"; it is the claim that you cannot study one without the other, because what counts as knowledge depends on who is doing the knowing, where they stand, and what they are accountable to.

Concretely, "knowledge and the knower" obliges you to do four things, and you should be able to do all four on demand:

ObligationWhat it forces you to doExample from your subjects
Reflect on yourself as a knower. Name your own position, assumptions and biases before you evaluate anyone else's. You find a proof convincing partly because you have been trained to; that training is a position, not a neutral vantage point.
Recognise communities of knowers. Almost all knowledge is produced by groups with shared standards, not by isolated individuals. Peer review in physics; the mathematical community accepting a computer-assisted proof; the readership that decides a poem is worth preserving.
Ask who is excluded. If knowledge is made by communities, ask whose knowledge was never collected. Indigenous ecological knowledge excluded from "science"; women's contributions to early computing recorded as "computing" rather than programming.
Accept accountability. Knowing creates obligations — to justify, to act, to refrain. A climate model carries obligations for policy; a data set carries obligations about consent.
How to use the core theme when you are stuck

Any time an argument stalls, run this three-question drill: (1) Who is the knower — an individual, a community, an institution, a machine? (2) What does that knower have to do to be taken seriously — show a proof, show data, show a source, show a credential? (3) What would a different knower have to do instead? If you can answer those three, you have the raw material of a genuine knowledge question.

2.2 The five optional themes

There are exactly five optional themes in the 2022 guide. Your teacher selects two for your course. The five are:

Knowledge and technology

How tools change what can be known, who can know it, and what gets forgotten when a tool becomes invisible. Directly usable with your computer science.

Knowledge and language

How the medium of a claim shapes the claim: translation, terminology, framing, the vocabularies a discipline makes available or withholds.

Knowledge and politics

How power selects which knowledge is funded, published, taught, classified or suppressed — and how knowledge in turn legitimises power.

Knowledge and religion

What counts as evidence and authority where revelation, tradition and scripture are the sources, and how such knowledge claims coexist with scientific ones.

Knowledge and indigenous societies

Knowledge held, transmitted and validated outside the institutions of academic science: oral transmission, place-based knowledge, and the question of who owns it.

A theme is not a topic. "Technology" is not a theme you write about; it is a lens you put over a knowledge question. The test of whether you are using a theme properly is this: if you can remove the theme from your essay and the essay is unchanged, you were not using the theme.

How to use one theme properly — a four-step procedure
  1. State the lens in one sentence. "Knowledge and technology: tools do not merely record observations; they decide which observations are possible."
  2. State the counter-lens. "But a telescope does not decide what the moons of Jupiter do; it only extends the reach of an unchanged method."
  3. Find a case where the two answers diverge. A radio telescope does not merely extend the eye — it converts radio flux into a false-colour image, and the conversion is a theoretical act. That is a case where the lens bites.
  4. Say what follows. If instruments embody theory, then "I saw it with my own eyes" is never theory-free, and the authority of observation has to be earned rather than assumed.

Notice that step 4 is the whole point. Steps 1–3 are table stakes; step 4 is what separates a strong response from a competent one.

2.3 The five areas of knowledge

All five are compulsory in the 2022 guide; you do not select. Each one is characterised below by what kind of knowledge it produces, what its methods are, and the questions it cannot escape.

Below is the same map in prose. Learn it in whichever form you prefer, but learn it — you cannot build a knowledge question about an area of knowledge whose method you cannot state in one sentence.

Mathematics

Mathematics produces knowledge of necessary relations among abstract objects: if the axioms hold and the reasoning is valid, the conclusion must hold, and no future discovery can overturn it. Its method is deductive proof from definitions and axioms; observation and experiment play no justifying role at all (they may suggest what to prove, which is a different matter). Its characteristic knowledge questions are about the source and scope of that necessity: is mathematics discovered or invented? What is the status of an axiom — is it known, assumed, or chosen? Does a proof show that a statement is true or only that it follows? And why, given that mathematics is not about the physical world at all, is it so unreasonably effective in physics?

Natural sciences

The natural sciences produce provisional, empirically answerable claims about the physical world. Their methods are model-building, controlled experiment, measurement with quantified uncertainty, statistical inference, and the social machinery of peer review and replication. Nothing in the natural sciences is ever finally proved; the most that is claimed is that a theory has survived serious attempts to break it. Its characteristic knowledge questions follow from that provisionality: when is a model reliable enough to act on? Is a superseded theory false or merely limited (is Newtonian mechanics refuted, or just restricted to low speeds and weak fields)? What is the difference between a measurement and an inference? And can we ever claim to know about entities — electrons, fields, quarks — that we only ever detect through instruments built from theories about them?

Human sciences

The human sciences produce knowledge of human behaviour, minds and societies, and they do it under a handicap the natural sciences do not have: their objects of study know they are being studied and respond accordingly. Methods include controlled experiment, survey, interview, case study, ethnography, and statistical modelling of large data sets, and generality is always threatened by context — a result that holds for one population at one time may not travel. Characteristic knowledge questions: can there be an objective science of creatures who interpret the researcher back? Are the categories of the human sciences discovered or imposed on the people they describe (is "intelligence" a thing measured or a thing constructed)? How much does the researcher's presence alter the phenomenon? And when a finding is statistical, what does it license us to say about any individual?

History

History produces knowledge of the past, and it must do so without any of the resources that make the sciences reliable: the past cannot be observed, cannot be re-run, and cannot be experimented on. Its methods are source criticism, corroboration between independent sources, contextualisation, inference to the best explanation, and the construction of narrative. Its evidence is not the past itself but whatever traces happened to survive — and survival is not random; it correlates with power, literacy, climate and storage conditions. Characteristic knowledge questions: is history discovered or constructed? Can we know the past when our evidence has been filtered by accident and by the victors? Does the historian's necessary act of selection make history a form of interpretation rather than report? And what is the epistemic status of a counterfactual — "if the archduke's driver had not taken the wrong turn"?

The arts

The arts produce knowledge that is embodied in artefacts, performances and forms rather than stated in propositions — knowledge of what something is like, of how a way of seeing feels from inside, of patterns and possibilities. Their methods are making, performing, interpretation, formal analysis and criticism, and the knowledge they produce is often not fully convertible into sentences without loss. Characteristic knowledge questions: can a work of art be a form of knowledge if it asserts nothing that could be true or false? Who fixes a work's meaning — its maker, its text, its audience, or its moment? Can the arts give us knowledge of what it is like to be someone else, and if so, is that knowledge or is it empathy? And why do we treat an artefact as a witness to its time when it was made by an unrepresentative individual?

3. Ways of knowing in the 2022 guide — get this right

This is the single most common thing returning teachers and older websites get wrong, so read it carefully.

Under the old guide there was a canonical list of eight ways of knowing: sense perception, reason, emotion, language, intuition, imagination, faith and memory. Students were expected to work through them as a checklist. That list is not part of the 2022 guide in the same way. There is no prescribed list of ways of knowing that you must work through, and language — which used to be a way of knowing — is now an optional theme ("knowledge and language"). If you write an essay or exhibition commentary that treats "the eight ways of knowing" as a required framework, you are reproducing a course you are not sitting.

What replaces that list is not a list at all. It is two other things:

The twelve TOK concepts

Evidence, certainty, truth, interpretation, power, justification, explanation, objectivity, perspective, culture, values, responsibility. These are the lenses the guide suggests you use to explore knowledge. They are not a checklist either — you use the two or three that bite on your question.

The knowledge framework

Four elements that recur across the whole course: scope, perspectives, methods and tools, ethics. Every area of knowledge and every theme can be interrogated through all four.

How to talk about "ways of knowing" without losing marks

Ways of knowing are still useful vocabulary, and very many TOK teachers still teach them, because "this discipline leans on sense perception where that one leans on reason" is a genuinely sharp observation. Use them that way: as a repertoire you draw on when it helps, never as a list you must complete. Two rules keep you safe: (1) never write "the eight ways of knowing are…" as if it were a current requirement; (2) never let a way of knowing carry an argument on its own — always attach it to a specific area of knowledge and a specific example. Confirm with your teacher how your school handles this, because departmental practice varies.

3.1 The twelve TOK concepts, and how each one is a knife

A concept is only useful if it cuts. Here is what each of the twelve is for — the question it lets you ask that you could not ask as precisely without it.

3.2 The knowledge framework: four elements

The framework is the guide's own scaffolding for generating questions. If you are ever asked to produce a knowledge question about an area of knowledge and your mind goes blank, walk the four elements in order and something will come out.

  1. Scope — what does this area cover, what problems does it address, what makes it worth pursuing, and what are its open questions? (What practical problems can be solved with this knowledge? What motivates the pursuit of it?)
  2. Methods and tools — how is knowledge actually produced here, what counts as a fact, what counts as good evidence, and how have the tools changed what can be known? (Does "good evidence" vary between disciplines? How important are material tools?)
  3. Perspectives — whose perspective is embedded, whose is missing, and how has knowledge here changed over time? (Are some kinds of knowledge less open to interpretation than others? Is understanding another knower's perspective essential?)
  4. Ethics — what constraints should apply, and what responsibilities fall on the knower? (Should the pursuit of knowledge here be subject to ethical constraints? What responsibilities rest on the knower?)
Why this works Why the framework reliably produces genuinely good questions
  1. A knowledge question must be about knowledge rather than inside a discipline. That is what makes it second-order.
  2. The four elements are precisely the four places where a discipline can be looked at from outside: what it covers, how it works, who is doing it, and what it obliges.
  3. Those four are exhaustive enough that at least one of them will generate a contested question for any area of knowledge — including ones you know little about.
  4. They are also independent enough that they generate different questions, which is what you need when you must find two or three angles on one essay title.
  5. What it is the wrong tool for: the framework generates questions, not answers. A question generated by "ethics" still has to be answered with an argument, an example and a counter-claim. Students who stop at step 1 produce a list of questions and call it an essay.

4. The one skill that decides your TOK grade: writing a knowledge question

Almost every weak TOK essay is weak for the same reason: the student answered a topic, or answered a yes/no question, or answered a factual question, when the task required a knowledge question. Get this right and the essay and the exhibition both get dramatically easier.

One honest caveat on knowledge questions

The 2022 guide's assessment language centres on the prescribed title for the essay and the prompt for the exhibition, and some teachers note that the old requirement to formulate your own knowledge question has been dropped from the essay. That does not change what follows. Knowledge questions are the instrument you use to break a title into arguable parts and to work out what an object shows — you will use them constantly even if you never print one in your essay. Ask your teacher how they want knowledge questions used in your drafts.

4.1 The four things people confuse

TypeExampleWhy it fails
A topic (a noun phrase) "Machine learning in medicine." There is nothing to argue with. A topic can be described, not answered. Essays built on topics become surveys, and surveys score in the middle bands.
A yes/no question "Do machines know things?" It invites a one-word answer and then dies. Structurally it pushes you towards stating a definition and defending it, which is not an argument about knowledge — it is a argument about a word.
A first-order (factual) question "How accurate is this diagnostic model?" The answer lives inside computer science and is settled by running the model. It is a real question but it is not a knowledge question; nothing about knowledge itself is at stake.
A genuine knowledge question "On what grounds can we attribute knowledge to a system that produces correct outputs but cannot give reasons for them?" It is second-order, it is contested, two well-informed people can answer it differently, and the answer requires an argument rather than a lookup.
How to convert a topic into a knowledge question — the six-step method
  1. Start with the topic as a noun phrase. e.g. "machine learning in medicine".
  2. Ask: whose knowledge, in which area of knowledge? This anchors the question. Here: the natural sciences (diagnosis) and mathematics/computer science (the model).
  3. Find the method. What does this area do to produce knowledge? Here: statistical pattern extraction from labelled data, validated on held-out test sets.
  4. Find the tension. Locate two defensible answers that pull apart. Here: (a) the model tracks the world reliably, so it knows; (b) it has no reasons and no understanding, so at most it contains information, which is not the same as knowing.
  5. Open the question with an operator. Replace "is/are/do" with to what extent, in what sense, on what grounds, how can we judge, what would have to be shown, under what conditions. These operators forbid a one-word answer.
  6. Run the two-answer test. Can an intelligent, well-informed person answer it one way and another answer it the opposite way, both with reasons? If yes, you have a knowledge question. If the answer is obviously yes or obviously no, go back to step 4.

Sentence frames you can reuse: "To what extent does X determine what we can know about Y?" · "On what grounds should we accept Z as knowledge, given that…?" · "In what sense, if any, can A be said to know B?" · "What would have to be true for P to count as evidence for Q?" · "Does the use of method M limit the certainty available about R?"

The three ways conversions go wrong
  1. The question is answered by definition. "Is mathematics a science?" reduces to what you mean by "science". If a question can be settled by stipulating a definition, it is not a knowledge question.
  2. The question is too big. "What is truth?" cannot be answered in 1,600 words; you will produce generalities. Attach it to an area of knowledge and a case: "What does it mean to call a physical theory true?"
  3. The question has an obvious answer. "Is bias present in the production of knowledge?" — yes. "Is bias inevitable, or can method remove it?" — now you have something to argue about.

4.3 Twelve practice conversions

Convert each topic into a genuine knowledge question. Then open the answer and compare — not to check whether you matched my wording, but to check whether yours is second-order, contested, and operator-opened.

  1. Topic: cryptocurrency and digital money.

    Show answer

    Model: "On what grounds does a community of knowers come to treat a record as trustworthy, when trust is produced by a protocol rather than by an institution?"

    Why it works: it is about the source of trust, not about whether Bitcoin is a good investment. It contrasts two mechanisms for producing the same epistemic good, which is exactly the kind of contrast an essay can exploit.

  2. Topic: eyewitness testimony in criminal trials.

    Show answer

    Model: "To what extent should memory be treated as a source of knowledge when it is demonstrably reconstructive rather than reproductive?"

    Why it works: "reconstructive rather than reproductive" is the contested claim; the question asks what follows from it for a practice that depends on memory.

  3. Topic: GDP as a measure of a country.

    Show answer

    Model: "What are the consequences for knowledge when a discipline's central concept is defined by what is measurable rather than by what matters?"

    Why it works: it targets the values and methods elements at once, and it generalises beyond economics.

  4. Topic: museum artefacts and repatriation.

    Show answer

    Model: "Does the location of an artefact affect what can be known from it, or only who gets to know it?"

    Why it works: it splits a political question into an epistemic one and an access one, which lets you argue that removing an object from its context destroys knowledge while returning it may create different knowledge.

  5. Topic: machine translation (e.g. translating or poetry).

    Show answer

    Model: "Can a system that has no experience of a world produce knowledge of meaning, or only of the statistical distribution of words?"

    Why it works: it links your two language subjects to computer science and draws on the classic argument that meaning requires more than pattern.

  6. Topic: computer simulations in physics.

    Show answer

    Model: "Is simulation a distinct way of producing knowledge, or is it deduction and observation wearing new clothes?"

    Why it works: it forces you to take a position on whether a genuinely new method has appeared, which is a better question than "are simulations reliable".

  7. Topic: IQ tests.

    Show answer

    Model: "To what extent does the act of measuring a human capacity create the thing it claims only to measure?"

    Why it works: sharp, general, and it makes the human sciences' reflexivity problem concrete.

  8. Topic: infographics and data visualisation.

    Show answer

    Model: "How does presenting quantitative knowledge visually change what its audience is able to challenge?"

    Why it works: it is not about whether charts mislead; it is about what visual presentation does to the possibility of criticism.

  9. Topic: endangered languages.

    Show answer

    Model: "When a language disappears, is knowledge destroyed, or only a particular way of expressing knowledge that could in principle be restated?"

    Why it works: a real, unresolved dispute about whether language is a vessel for knowledge or a constituent of it.

  10. Topic: peer review.

    Show answer

    Model: "Is peer review a method for producing knowledge or a social mechanism for licensing it — and does the difference matter?"

    Why it works: the distinction is real and the "does the difference matter" clause forces evaluation rather than description.

  11. Topic: chess engines.

    Show answer

    Model: "Can a system that cannot lose a game be said to understand the game, and what would count as evidence either way?"

    Why it works: "what would count as evidence either way" is the clause that makes the question answerable in an essay rather than endlessly debatable.

  12. Topic: maps and national borders.

    Show answer

    Model: "To what extent does representing territory in a map create the political reality it claims only to record?"

    Why it works: it inverts the usual assumption that representation follows reality, and it connects the optional theme of knowledge and politics to a concrete object you could put in an exhibition.

5. TOK through your own subjects

You are sitting Mathematics AA HL, Physics HL, Computer Science HL, English A Lang&Lit SL and Chinese A SL. That combination is unusually good for TOK, because it spans the two hardest edges in the course: the near-certain end (mathematics) and the interpretive end (literature), with the model-dependent middle (physics) and the newly strange middle (machine learning) in between. Use it.

Why examiners reward your own subjects

The single strongest signal of a top-band essay is an example the writer clearly understands from the inside. Examiners can tell within a paragraph whether you have actually done the mathematics or are repeating something you read about it. An example from your own HL work, explored in depth, is worth more than three famous historical cases named in passing.

5.1 Mathematics — proof, certainty, axioms, discovery

The central puzzle. Mathematical knowledge is the most certain knowledge we have, and it is about nothing. A theorem, once proved, cannot be overturned by any future discovery: the infinitude of the primes will still be true when the sun goes out. And yet mathematics appears to describe nothing in the physical world — there are no perfect circles in nature.

Why this works The parallel postulate, and what it shows about axioms
  1. Euclid stated five postulates and derived the whole of geometry from them. Four are terse. The fifth — that through a point not on a line, exactly one parallel line can be drawn — is long and awkward.
  2. For over two thousand years, mathematicians tried to derive the fifth from the other four. The assumption was that the postulates are self-evident truths, and a truth should be provable from the others.
  3. In the nineteenth century, Gauss, Bolyai and Lobachevsky independently constructed geometries in which the other four hold and the fifth fails — and these geometries were consistent.
  4. Therefore the fifth postulate is independent of the others: it cannot be proved from them, because a world in which it is false is not contradictory.
  5. What this means: the axioms were never self-evident truths that we knew. They are choices. Mathematical certainty is the certainty of the conditional: if these axioms, then this conclusion, necessarily. It is absolute certainty about the implication, and no certainty at all about whether the antecedent describes anything.
  6. When this is the wrong tool: do not conclude "mathematics is therefore arbitrary". The choice of axioms is not arbitrary — it is constrained by fruitfulness, by consistency, and by whether the resulting structure connects to others. That constraint is itself an interesting epistemic fact, and pretending it away is the lazy version of this argument.

Knowledge questions you can actually use.

  • Does a proof establish truth, or only entailment? If the latter, is mathematical certainty a kind of certainty we should envy or a kind we should be suspicious of?
  • If axioms are chosen rather than known, on what grounds are they chosen — fruitfulness, consistency, intuition, usefulness to physics? Does that make mathematics closer to an art or to a natural science?
  • Is mathematics discovered or invented? A sharper version: if two isolated civilisations would necessarily arrive at the same theorems given the same definitions, in what sense is there anything left to discover?
  • If a proof is too long for any human to check, and is verified instead by a proof assistant, has the basis of mathematical certainty changed — from a community of human checkers to trust in software? (The four-colour theorem and, more recently, formal verification projects make this live.)
  • Why is mathematics so effective in physics? Either the world has mathematical structure (a striking metaphysical claim) or we keep only the mathematics that works and discard the rest (a selection effect). Which is more defensible?

5.2 Physics — models, measurement, paradigm shift, truth

The central puzzle. Physics is our most successful empirical knowledge, and every theory it has ever produced has eventually been superseded. What, then, are we learning about the world — and what does it mean to call a current theory true?

Models vs reality

A point mass, a frictionless plane, an ideal gas: none exists. Modelling works by throwing away almost everything. The knowledge question is not "are models accurate" but what licenses us to trust a claim produced by a system we know to be false.

Measurement

A reading is not nature speaking. It is the endpoint of a chain of theory-laden instruments, calibrations and corrections. Ask: what would have to be true of the apparatus for this number to be evidence? That is what an uncertainty budget really is.

Paradigm shift

Kuhn's claim is that successive theories are not progressively truer but incommensurable — "mass" in Newton and in relativity are not the same concept. Is physics cumulative or discontinuous? Answer with a case, not a slogan.

Realism vs instrumentalism

Do electrons exist, or is "electron" a posit that organises our observations predictively? Both positions are live among physicists, which is exactly why it is a good TOK question.

Knowledge questions you can actually use.

  • Is a superseded theory false or merely restricted? Newtonian mechanics is used to land spacecraft. If it is false, we are acting on a falsehood and succeeding — which needs explaining.
  • In what sense can a theory be true when every theory so far has been replaced? Is it rational to expect our current theories to be replaced, and does that expectation undermine or strengthen our confidence in them?
  • The 5σ threshold in particle physics is a convention, adopted because of the cost of false positives and the look-elsewhere effect. Is that an epistemic standard or a social one — and does the distinction survive?
  • Blind analysis — hiding the result from the analysts until the method is fixed — manufactures objectivity by preventing a human from seeing the answer. What does that tell us about whether objectivity is a property of a method or of a person?
  • Is a simulation an experiment? It has features of both: you run it and observe, but what you observe is the consequences of equations you wrote.

5.3 Computer science — machine knowledge, simulation, algorithmic authority

The central puzzle. A trained model performs tasks that we would count as evidence of knowledge in a human — diagnosis, translation, play — and it does so by a procedure that seems to involve no understanding at all.

Why this works The regress of justification, and why it bites machine learning
  1. A traditional mark of knowledge is that the knower can give reasons: asked "how do you know?", they can produce a justification rather than a shrug.
  2. A deep neural network cannot do this in any ordinary sense. It produces an output, and the best available "explanation" is a heat map over input features — a post-hoc reconstruction, not the actual path of the computation.
  3. So: if giving reasons is required for knowledge, the model does not know. It stores and transmits information, which is not the same thing.
  4. But the counter-argument is strong too: human experts constantly act on knowledge they cannot fully articulate — a radiologist's sense that a film is wrong, a native speaker's grammaticality judgement. If we deny knowledge to the model on the grounds of inarticulacy, consistency may require us to deny it to the expert too.
  5. What this means: the question is not settled by appealing to "understanding"; it is settled by deciding which criterion of knowledge we are willing to give up. That decision is the argument.
  6. When this is the wrong tool: do not use "it is just statistics" as a conversation stopper. It is true and it settles nothing — everything from a regression to a human's pattern recognition is "just" something. The interesting work is in saying what follows.

Knowledge questions you can actually use.

  • In what sense, if any, does a model that cannot give reasons know anything? And if it does not know, what is the correct attitude towards a correct output it produces?
  • Is a simulation a third way of knowing, alongside deduction from axioms and observation of the world? Or does the validation step quietly reduce it to one of the other two?
  • Algorithmic authority: a recommendation engine or a risk score carries the prestige of mathematics while being opaque in practice. Is this a new and harder form of argument from authority — one that is more difficult to contest than a human expert because the reasons are not available?
  • Training data encodes past practice. When a model is trained on historical hiring decisions and then described as objective, what has happened to the value judgement — has it been removed, or hidden inside the choice of training set and loss function?
  • If a proof is verified by software, is mathematics still the most certain knowledge — or has it borrowed certainty from an empirical claim about hardware and compilers?

5.4 The two language subjects — translation, determinacy, literary knowledge

The central puzzle. You read the same world through two very different languages, and you know from experience that some things survive the crossing and some do not. That experience is a TOK resource most students never cash in.

Translation and loss

The cliché is that something is "lost in translation". Press on it: what would have to be true of meaning for it to be lost? That metaphor assumes meaning is a fixed content carried by words. If meaning is closer to use, translation is not loss but re-creation — and sometimes a gain.

Does a text have a determinate meaning?

Three answers compete: the author's intention fixes it; the text's public conventions fix it; the reader's community fixes it. Each has a consequence for what literary study is for.

Literary knowledge vs factual knowledge

We say "we know that Hamlet delays". We do not know it in the way we know that water boils at 100 °C at 1 atm. What kind of knowledge is it — knowledge of a possibility, of a pattern, of what an experience is like? And is calling it knowledge a category error?

Commentary traditions (Chinese A)

A classical poem reaches you through two millennia of commentary. Who owns its meaning — the poet, the tradition, or you? This is the "whose knowledge is it" question in its most concrete form.

Knowledge questions you can actually use.

  • Can knowledge that is essentially bound to one language be translated without remainder — and how would we tell, given that any test of adequacy is itself conducted in a language?
  • If a literary work can support interpretations its author could not have intended, is the author's intention relevant to its meaning or merely to its history?
  • Is the knowledge a novel gives the same kind of thing as the knowledge a history gives? Both can be true to experience; only one can be checked against records. Does that make one of them less than knowledge?
  • When a text is used as evidence of the attitudes of its time, what assumption are we making about the representativeness of a single, usually elite, author?
  • In Lang&Lit you study non-literary texts — advertisements, speeches, policy documents. What does it mean to read such a text for knowledge about its moment rather than for its truth? Is that a different epistemic activity from reading it as a claim?
Connection to the extended essay

Every one of the questions in section 5 could become an EE research question with a subject-specific method attached — a mathematics EE on the independence of the parallel postulate, a physics EE on model validation, a CS EE on the interpretability of a classifier, an English A EE on translation and loss. TOK is where you find the question; the EE is where you answer it with evidence and sources. See EE — pathway & research question.

5.5 Business management — the human sciences: generalisation, prediction and values

The central puzzle. Business management is grouped with the human sciences, and it shares their defining awkwardness: its subject matter is made of people who know they are being studied. That one fact breaks three assumptions you have inherited from physics, and noticing where it breaks is worth marks in every TOK assessment you sit.

Generalisation without laws

Maslow's hierarchy, the Ansoff matrix, the BCG matrix — none of these is a law in the sense that \(F=ma\) is a law. They are descriptions of patterns that admit exceptions as a matter of course. So what kind of claim is a business theory? If a theory fails to predict in a specific case, has it been falsified or merely misapplied?

Prediction changes what it predicts

If a firm publishes a forecast and its competitors act on it, the forecast alters the outcome. A physics prediction about a pendulum does not change the pendulum. This reflexivity is the deepest methodological difference between the natural and human sciences, and business supplies the cleanest examples of it.

Values inside the method

"Stakeholder", "ethical sourcing", "sustainability" are not neutral descriptors — they embed a view about who matters. In physics you can usually separate the measurement from the value judgement. Here the object of study is made of intentions, so the separation is far harder to defend.

The quantitative surface

Ratio analysis, break-even and expected value look and feel like physics. Do they carry mathematics' certainty across, or only its appearance? A break-even chart is only as good as its cost estimates — and those are human judgements wearing numbers.

Knowledge questions you can actually use.

  • Can a generalisation that admits exceptions in individual cases still count as knowledge — and what would have to be true for us to say it had been falsified?
  • When a published business forecast changes the behaviour it forecasts, is that a failure of the method or simply a difference in kind from prediction in the natural sciences?
  • Does using quantitative tools in a human science import the certainty of mathematics, or only its authority? What test would distinguish the two?
  • When two stakeholders evaluate the same decision differently, are they disagreeing about facts or about values — and how could we tell, given that each will describe the other as mistaken?
  • To what extent does aggregate data (market share, average spend) conceal the individuals it describes? Is that concealment a methodological necessity or an ethical choice?
