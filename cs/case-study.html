Computer Science HL · Assessment · Examined in Paper 1

The case study

Once per examination cycle the IBO publishes a document describing an emerging technology in use in a real setting — a hospital, a port, a city, a farm. You study it in class for 30 hours at HL (15 at SL) and then meet questions about it in Paper 1. The questions are unseen; the context is not. That combination is the whole difficulty: you cannot memorise your way to these marks, but you can prepare for them in a way that is almost mechanical. This page gives you the method.

HL 30 teaching hours · SL 15 Assessed in Paper 1 at both SL and HL No Paper 3 — the old 2014 arrangement is gone
Why the IBO added it

Computer science has a problem that physics does not: the content dates. A student who learned "the latest storage medium is the Blu-ray disc" was wrong before they graduated. Rather than chase the market, the IBO chose a fixed core (themes A and B) plus one renewable component that changes with every cycle. The case study exists so that the course always has something current in it without the syllabus having to be rewritten.

The second reason is assessment design. Themes A and B can be examined with questions that have right answers. The case study is where the IBO asks questions that do not: should this system be built? who loses? who decides? is this trade-off acceptable? Those are the questions that separate a 5 from a 7 in Paper 1, and they cannot be asked about a sorting algorithm.

What the case study actually is

The IBO publishes a case study document — typically 8–15 pages — that describes one organisation or sector adopting an emerging technology. It usually contains: background on the organisation and the problem it faces, a description of the technology and how it was introduced, diagrams of the technical architecture, data or performance figures, quotes from the people involved, and some mention of cost, regulation or public reaction.

What it isWhat it is not
A pre-seen document: released in advance, studied in class, taken into the exam as knowledge (not as a paper — you do not get it in the exam hall). Not a comprehension test. No question asks "according to page 4, what year was the system installed?"
Context for questions that test theme A content. A question about sensors or encryption or databases will be asked about the case study's system. Not a separate syllabus. There is no "case study content" to learn beyond the document itself.
A source of stakeholders, technologies and trade-offs that you will be asked to reason about. Not a memory test. Quoting figures from the document earns nothing unless you use them to support a judgement.
Examined inside Paper 1, at both SL and HL, alongside ordinary theme A questions. Not Paper 3. If you are reading a resource that says Paper 3, it is describing the old course.
30 recommended teaching hours at HL. Not a separate grade. There is no "case study mark" — the marks sit inside your Paper 1 score.
Exam technique — how the questions are actually worded

Case-study questions in Paper 1 are unseen, but they are built from a small number of archetypes. In practice you will meet three:

  1. Describe / explain the technology in context — usually 4–6 marks. "Explain how the system uses sensor data to …", "Describe the role of the digital twin in …".
  2. Analyse the impact on a named stakeholder — usually 6–8 marks. "Analyse the impact of the system on patients / drivers / warehouse staff." The stakeholder is always named or clearly implied, and marks are lost by writing about "people" in general.
  3. Evaluate a proposed change or a competing solution — usually 8–12 marks. "Evaluate the proposal to …", "Discuss whether X would be a better approach than Y." This is the highest-tariff item on the paper and the one students run out of time on.

Each archetype has a repeatable answer shape. They are given below with a worked answer for each.

Trap — three ways students throw away case study marks
  1. Writing about technology in general. "AI can be biased because training data may be unrepresentative" is a correct sentence that scores zero if the question was about this system. Every technical point must be attached to something in the case study: this sensor, this dataset, this group of users.
  2. Listing stakeholders instead of analysing them. Naming five groups and saying "they may be affected positively or negatively" is description. Impact requires a mechanism: what the system does → what changes for this group → why that matters to them.
  3. Evaluating without criteria. "It is good because it is efficient" is an assertion. An evaluation compares options against criteria that come from the case study (cost, accuracy, privacy, equity of access, reliability, staff workload) and then weighs them. No criteria, no judgement, no top band.

The four-pass reading method

Reading the case study once, carefully, is the single most common wasted effort in this course. You will forget almost all of it, and you will not have built the two artefacts (the stakeholder table and the data-flow list) that exam questions actually consume. Read it four times, each time for one purpose, and produce one artefact per pass. Budget about three hours in total.

Pass 1
~30 min
Narrative — what happened here? Read it like a news article, fast, without notes. Then close it and write, from memory, one paragraph of no more than 80 words answering: who is the organisation, what problem did they have, what did they build, when, and what happened next.
Artefact: that paragraph. If you cannot write it, you did not understand the narrative — reread.
Pass 2
~60 min
Stakeholders — who is in this story? Now read with a highlighter and list every person, group, role and organisation mentioned or clearly implied. Include the quiet ones: the people who clean the building, the residents near the depot, the customer who does not own a smartphone.
Artefact: a stakeholder table (see below) with at least eight rows, filled in completely.
Pass 3
~60 min
Technologies and data flows — what does the machine actually do? For every technical noun in the document, write down: what data it produces or consumes, where that data is stored, where it is processed (device, edge, cloud), and what decision or action it triggers.
Artefact: a data-flow chain (sensors → transmission → storage → processing → decision → actuation → feedback) and a one-line definition of every technology named.
Pass 4
~45 min
Implications — what is at stake? Work through the four lenses deliberately: Social, Economic, Ethical, Legal. For each, write two findings. Then answer the question the document usually does not answer: who is missing?
Artefact: an implications grid with at least eight cells, plus a list of three excluded or under-represented groups.
How to use it — turn the artefacts into a one-page crib sheet

When all four passes are done, compress everything onto a single side of A4: the 80-word narrative, the stakeholder table (names only, plus one word each for benefit and harm), the data-flow chain as six boxes with arrows, and eight implication bullets. You cannot take it into the exam, which is exactly the point — the act of compressing forces you to know it. Rewrite the sheet from memory once a week from the day it is made until the exam. A student who can redraw their crib sheet from memory on the morning of Paper 1 will not be surprised by any case-study question, whatever it asks.

The stakeholder analysis framework

A stakeholder is anyone affected by the system, anyone who can affect it, and anyone who is not in the room when it is decided. Most students list the first category and stop. Use four columns, and do not allow a row until all four are filled.

ColumnThe question you answerWhat a weak entry looks likeWhat a strong entry looks like
Who benefits?Whose life, income, safety or convenience improves, and by what mechanism?"The company — it saves money.""Shift planners: the model produces next week's roster in 40 seconds instead of 3 hours, so they can re-run it when someone calls in sick."
Who is harmed?Who bears a cost, loses autonomy, loses work, or carries a new risk?"Some people might lose their jobs.""Agency staff: their hours are allocated by a model whose objective function minimises cost, so they are systematically offered the least profitable shifts first."
Who decides?Who chose the system, who sets its parameters, who can switch it off, who is accountable when it is wrong?"The government / the management.""The city transport authority sets the objective weights, but the vendor owns the model weights, so neither can fully explain or reverse an individual decision."

How to use it — sentence frames that carry marks

Under exam pressure you do not rise to the occasion, you fall to your habits. Install these frames and use them verbatim until they are automatic:

  • Benefit: "For [stakeholder], the system [does X], which means [concrete change]."
  • Harm: "For [stakeholder], the system [does X], which exposes them to [specific risk] because [mechanism]."
  • Decision power: "[Stakeholder] controls [parameter/choice], but does not control [other parameter], so accountability for [outcome] is unclear."
  • Exclusion: "Because the system requires [precondition], [group] cannot participate, so their needs are absent from [dataset/decision]."
  • Weighing (the sentence that unlocks the top band): "This matters more than [other consideration] here because [fact from the case study]."
Why this works Why "who is excluded?" is the highest-value column
  1. Exam questions on stakeholders are usually analyse or evaluate. Both require you to go beyond what the document states. The benefits and the decision-makers are almost always stated explicitly in the case study — so writing them down earns you the marks that everyone gets.
  2. The harms are sometimes stated, usually in a quote from a critic. Half the cohort will find them.
  3. The excluded are almost never stated. Identifying them requires you to infer from the technical design: the system needs a smartphone, so anyone without one is outside it; the model was trained on data from one district, so the other districts are misrepresented; the interface is in one language, so non-speakers cannot consent meaningfully.
  4. Therefore the exclusion column is where the discriminating marks live. It is also the column that is generated by technical knowledge (theme A), which is why the case study is examined in Paper 1 and not on its own: the IBO wants technical understanding converted into social judgement.
  5. When this is the wrong tool: if the question says describe or state, do not reach for stakeholders — answer the mechanism question narrowly and move on. Over-answering a 2-mark describe with a stakeholder essay wastes four minutes and scores nothing extra.

The three question archetypes, with worked answers

Archetype 1 — describe / explain the technology in context (4–6 marks)

Shape: one sentence of mechanism → one sentence of context (attach it to the case study) → one sentence of consequence. That is three marks. For six marks, do it twice with a linking word (furthermore, however, as a result). Stop there — more than three linked points on a 6-mark question is repetition, and repetition is not marks.

Worked answer 1 Archetype 1 · "Explain how the system uses sensor data to control traffic signals." (6 marks)

Question context: the invented Northbridge case study further down this page.

Model answer.

  1. Mechanism. Inductive loops in the road surface and anonymised overhead cameras generate a vehicle count per approach every five seconds; each junction cabinet runs a small model locally that estimates queue length from that count.
  2. Context. The junction sends its queue estimate over the council's fibre network to the corridor controller, which compares the queue on each approach and extends the green phase for whichever approach is longest, subject to a minimum and maximum phase length.
  3. Consequence. Because the decision is made at the cabinet rather than in the cloud, a signal can react within one cycle even if the network link to the control centre is down — but it also means the junction cannot see the corridor-wide picture, so it cannot anticipate a queue arriving from the next junction upstream.
Where the marks are: 2 for the sensing and estimation (a named sensor + what is derived from it), 2 for the control decision (how the phase is chosen, with the constraint), 2 for the consequence (what the local processing enables and what it costs). Notice that every sentence contains something specific to this system — "inductive loops", "cabinet", "minimum and maximum phase length". A generic answer about "smart traffic lights using AI" scores 1 or 2 at best.

Archetype 2 — analyse the impact on a named stakeholder (6–8 marks)

Shape: name the stakeholder in your first sentence and use their name again in your last. Give at least two distinct impacts, each with a mechanism. One impact must be a cost or risk — an analysis that finds only benefits is not an analysis. Finish with a weighing sentence: which impact dominates for this group and why.

Worked answer 2 Archetype 2 · "Analyse the impact of the adaptive corridor system on bus drivers." (8 marks)

Model answer.

  1. Benefit with mechanism. Bus drivers benefit directly: their vehicles transmit position and occupancy to the corridor controller, which grants them signal priority at junctions when they are behind schedule, so their average delay per junction falls and their schedule becomes achievable. This reduces the pressure to make up time by driving aggressively or skipping breaks.
  2. Cost with mechanism. However, the same telemetry makes drivers continuously measurable. Dwell time at each stop, acceleration and braking events and schedule adherence are all logged, and the operator's dashboard ranks drivers against each other. Drivers therefore experience the system as performance surveillance: behaviour changes to optimise the measured variable rather than the actual service, for example by waiting at a stop to avoid arriving early.
  3. Second-order effect. Because priority is granted to buses that are behind schedule, a driver who is on time never receives it, so the system rewards lateness in a way that is invisible in the aggregate statistics the council publishes.
  4. Weighing. For bus drivers the two effects are of similar size: the reduction in delay is real and daily, but the monitoring changes the nature of the job. Which dominates depends on how the operator uses the data — if the rankings are used only for route planning, the benefit clearly wins; if they are used in individual performance review, the surveillance effect dominates. The case study does not say which, and that ambiguity is itself the most important finding.
Where the marks are: 2 for a benefit with a mechanism, 2 for a cost with a mechanism, 2 for a second-order or unintended effect, 2 for the weighing sentence that reaches a conditional conclusion. Note the phrase "the case study does not say which" — admitting a genuine gap in the evidence, and saying what would resolve it, is a top-band move, not a weakness.

Archetype 3 — evaluate a proposed change or a competing solution (8–12 marks)

Shape: state the criteria first (two to four, drawn from the case study), then apply each option to each criterion, then judge. A table in your planning is fine; the answer itself must be prose. The two things that cap this question at half marks are: no criteria, and no judgement. Use this frame:

"The proposal should be judged on [criterion 1], [criterion 2] and [criterion 3]. Against [criterion 1], X is better because …, whereas Y …. Against [criterion 2] the position reverses because …. Overall, [option] is the stronger choice for this context, provided that [condition]; if [condition] does not hold, [other option] would be preferable because …."

Worked answer 3 Archetype 3 · "Evaluate the proposal to replace the corridor's local control with a single central cloud model." (10 marks)

Model answer.

  1. Criteria. The proposal should be judged on three criteria that matter in this context: responsiveness (a corridor reacts on the timescale of seconds), resilience (the system must keep working when the network does not), and equity (signal priority is currently distributed between junctions by a rule the council publishes).
  2. Responsiveness. A central model can see every junction at once, so it can anticipate a queue propagating upstream rather than reacting to it after it forms. That is a genuine improvement in corridor throughput, and it is the main argument for the change.
  3. Resilience. Against resilience the position reverses. Local control degrades gracefully: each cabinet falls back to a fixed-time plan when the link drops. Central control has a single failure domain — if the cloud region or the fibre backhaul fails, every junction loses adaptive control simultaneously. The council's own winter outage data in the case study suggests this is not hypothetical.
  4. Equity. Centralisation also changes who can be held to account. The current per-junction rule can be read by a resident; a model that sets 200 signal timings from weights nobody outside the vendor can inspect cannot. This is the criterion on which the proposal is weakest, because the affected residents have no route to contest a decision they cannot see.
  5. Judgement. Overall, the proposal is technically stronger and institutionally weaker. It should be adopted conditionally: keep local fallback plans and publish the objective function, so that the corridor gains the anticipatory benefit without acquiring a single point of failure and an opaque decision-maker. If the vendor will not publish the objective function, the resilience and equity costs outweigh the throughput gain, and the local architecture should be retained.
Where the marks are: 2 for stating contextualised criteria, 2–3 for a developed advantage, 2–3 for a developed disadvantage on a different axis, 2 for a judgement that is conditional rather than absolute. The word "provided that" is doing enormous work here — absolute verdicts on 10-mark evaluate questions almost always look naive, because real design decisions are trade-offs.
Exam technique — where the case study sits in your two hours

HL Paper 1 is 2 hours. Case-study questions typically account for a substantial minority of the marks and they are the slowest marks on the paper. Do not start with them. Work this order: (1) the whole paper, 90 seconds, ticking what you can do cold; (2) all short-answer theme A questions, fast; (3) the case-study describe/explain items; (4) the big case-study analyse/evaluate last, with a ring-fenced 25 minutes. If you write the evaluation first you will spend 35 minutes on 10 marks and leave 6 marks of SQL unwritten.

Vocabulary of emerging technology

Learn these thirteen terms. For each one, the second column matters more than the first: knowing the kind of question the term is a doorway into is what turns a definition into marks.

TermOne-line definitionThe kind of exam question it appears in
Sensor fusionCombining readings from two or more different sensors to produce an estimate that is more accurate or more complete than any single sensor's. Explain how the system knows X; explain why one sensor is not enough. Also a justification question: "explain why the system uses both a camera and a loop detector".
Edge computingProcessing data on or near the device that produced it, rather than sending it to a central server.Evaluate — latency, bandwidth cost, and resilience when the network fails. Nearly always paired with privacy: "data never leaves the site".
Digital twinA live computational model of a physical system, continuously updated with real sensor data so it can be used to predict and to test changes safely. Describe the role of the model; explain why the operator tests changes on the twin before the real system. Often attracts a "what are the risks of relying on it?" follow-up.
Federated learningTraining a shared model across many devices without the raw data ever leaving those devices — only model updates are sent and aggregated. Evaluate in a privacy context; to what extent does it solve the data protection problem? (Answer: partly — updates can still leak information.)
BlockchainA distributed, append-only ledger replicated across many nodes, where agreement on new entries is reached by consensus rather than by a central authority. Evaluate the use of it for this application — usually the correct answer is that it solves tamper-evidence, not truth, and costs energy and throughput. Never just describe it.
Internet of Things (IoT)Everyday physical objects embedded with sensors, processors and network connectivity so they can send and receive data. The substrate of most case studies. Appears in describe the architecture and in security questions (attack surface, default credentials, patching).
Biometric dataMeasurable physical or behavioural characteristics used to identify a person — fingerprint, face, iris, gait, voice. Almost always discuss or evaluate: consent, irreversibility (you cannot reissue a face), false-match rates that differ between demographic groups.
Algorithmic biasSystematic, unfair differences in a system's outcomes between groups, arising from the data it was trained on, the features chosen, or the objective it optimises. Explain how bias could arise here, and evaluate a proposed mitigation. Requires a mechanism, not the word "biased".
Data minimisationThe principle that a system should collect, keep and process only the data it actually needs for its stated purpose, for only as long as it needs it. Evaluate the system's data practices; evaluate a proposal to retain data for longer. Pairs with storage questions (A3) and encryption (A2).
ConsentA freely given, specific, informed and revocable agreement by a person to the processing of their data. To what extent can users meaningfully consent here? Strong answers attack "freely given" (no alternative service) and "informed" (nobody reads 40 pages).
Right to explanationThe claim that a person subject to an automated decision is entitled to an account of how and why it was made. Discuss — usually against a black-box model. Good answers note the tension with model complexity and with commercial confidentiality.
SurveillanceSystematic monitoring of behaviour or movement, typically by an authority, and typically without the monitored person's knowledge or meaningful ability to opt out. Discuss whether the system constitutes surveillance; evaluate the trade-off between safety and privacy. Rewards the "function creep" argument: data collected for X is later used for Y.
Digital divideThe gap between those who have access to, and the skills for, digital technology and those who do not — by income, age, geography or disability. Analyse the impact on a group; evaluate a rollout. This is your "who is excluded?" column with a name on it.
Connection — the vocabulary is theme A in disguise

None of these thirteen terms is new content. Sensor fusion and IoT are A1 fundamentals (sensors, ADCs, embedded systems). Edge computing, blockchain and consent are A2 networks and security. Data minimisation is A3 databases — data dictionary design, retention, and why you do not store what you do not need. Federated learning, digital twin and algorithmic bias are A4 machine learning. The case study is not a fifth thing to learn; it is the place where theme A is examined attached to people. If a term feels unfamiliar, it is because the underlying A-topic is weak, not because the case study is hard.

A full practice case study: the Northbridge Adaptive Corridor

What follows is invented, in the register the IBO uses, with the level of detail a real case study has. Treat it as your case study for this page: do the four passes on it, build the artefacts, then attempt the eight questions below with the document closed.

Practice case study — Northbridge

Background. Northbridge is a city of 340,000 people with a single north–south arterial road, the Corridor, carrying 42,000 vehicles per day and 11 bus routes. Congestion on the Corridor costs the city an estimated £18 million per year in lost time. In 2026 the city council and a transport technology company, Meridian Systems, deployed an Adaptive Corridor System (ACS) along 6.2 km and 31 signalised junctions.

How it works. Each junction has inductive loops cut into the road surface, an overhead camera with on-device inference, and a roadside cabinet containing an industrial computer. The cabinet fuses the loop count and the camera's vehicle classification into a queue-length estimate every five seconds, and chooses the next signal phase using a model that was trained on traffic data from a comparable city. Buses and emergency vehicles transmit their position and schedule status over a dedicated short-range link; the cabinet grants them a green-light extension when they are more than 90 seconds behind schedule. Each cabinet sends its decisions and its sensor summaries to a central platform over the council's fibre network, where they are stored in a cloud data warehouse alongside fare-card taps, weather feeds and the city's roadworks calendar.

The digital twin. Meridian maintains a simulation of the Corridor, calibrated each night against the previous day's sensor data. The council's traffic engineers use it to test proposed timing plans and road layouts before implementing them. Meridian's engineers also use it to retrain the junction model.

What happened. After twelve months the council reports a 14 % reduction in average journey time on the Corridor, a 22 % reduction in bus journey-time variability, and a 9 % increase in cycling. Residents' associations in the two districts adjacent to the Corridor report that traffic has increased on their residential streets. The bus operators' union has raised a formal objection to the driver dashboard, which ranks drivers on schedule adherence, dwell time and harsh-braking events. Two junctions in the low-income district of Southgate were excluded from the deployment because their cabinets could not be upgraded within budget; they still run fixed-time plans. Cycling groups have asked for the camera data to be released so they can audit junction safety; the council has refused, citing the possibility of re-identifying individuals from video.

Costs and governance. The system cost £6.1 million to install and £480,000 per year to operate. The contract runs for eight years. The model and its weights are Meridian's intellectual property; the council sets three parameters — the weight given to bus delay, to pedestrian waiting time, and to general traffic throughput. The council publishes an annual summary report. It does not publish the objective function or the per-junction decision logs.

Eight unseen-style questions on Northbridge

These are written in the register of Paper 1. Answer them in full sentences, timed, with the case study closed, before opening the answers.

Question 1 State two types of data collected by the Northbridge system, and one decision each is used for. (4 marks)
  1. Inductive loop vehicle counts — used to estimate queue length at each approach, which determines how long the green phase is extended.
  2. Bus position and schedule status — used to decide whether to grant a green-light extension (granted only when the bus is more than 90 seconds behind schedule).
  3. Also acceptable: camera classification (vehicle type, used to weight the queue estimate); fare-card taps (demand analysis in the warehouse); weather feeds (model input); roadworks calendar (planning).
Answer: any two pairs, one mark each for the data type and one for its use. Note that the mark is for the decision, not for a vague "to improve traffic". Name the decision.
Question 2 Explain why the junction model runs in the roadside cabinet rather than in the cloud. (4 marks)
  1. Latency. Signal decisions must be made on the timescale of seconds; a round trip to a cloud region adds delay and variability that is unacceptable when a phase is about to change.
  2. Resilience. If the fibre link to the central platform fails, a cabinet that computes locally continues to control its junction, whereas a cloud-dependent junction would fail — potentially at all 31 junctions at once.
  3. Bandwidth. Raw camera and loop data at five-second intervals from 31 junctions is far more data than it is economic to transmit; the cabinet reduces it to a queue estimate and sends only that.
Answer: two developed reasons, 2 marks each. This is edge computing (A1/A2) attached to a context — the generic version of this answer is worth knowing, but only the "31 junctions", "five-second" and "fibre link" details make it worth 4.
Question 3 Describe the role of the digital twin in the Northbridge system, and explain one limitation of relying on it. (5 marks)
  1. Role. The digital twin is a continuously updated simulation of the Corridor, calibrated nightly against the previous day's sensor data. The council's engineers use it to test proposed timing plans and road layouts before changing the real road, and Meridian uses it to retrain the junction model — so changes can be trialled without any risk to real traffic.
  2. Limitation. A twin is only as good as its calibration, and it is calibrated on observed behaviour. It cannot predict how drivers will respond to a change they have never experienced — for example, the rat-running onto residential streets, which is a response to the new timings and not a feature of the old data. Any behaviour change induced by the system is outside the model.
  3. A second acceptable limitation: the twin is Meridian's, so the council cannot independently verify it; and errors in calibration are silent.
Answer: 3 marks for the role (calibration + two uses) and 2 for one developed limitation. "It is a simulation" alone scores 1 — the marks are for what it is used for and where it breaks.
Question 4 Analyse the impact of the Adaptive Corridor System on residents of Southgate. (8 marks)
  1. Identify the specific harm. Two of Southgate's junctions were excluded from the deployment on budget grounds and still run fixed-time plans, so Southgate residents receive none of the 14 % journey-time improvement reported for the Corridor.
  2. Mechanism of a second harm. Because the Corridor is now faster, it attracts traffic from parallel routes and from Southgate's own streets; Southgate therefore experiences the induced traffic and congestion caused by a system it does not benefit from. The benefit and the cost are separated by district.
  3. Data exclusion. Southgate is partially instrumented, so its travel patterns appear incompletely in the warehouse. Decisions about future phases of the deployment will be made using data that under-represents the district that is already worst served — a feedback loop that makes the exclusion self-reinforcing.
  4. Any benefit. An indirect benefit exists: some Southgate residents use the Corridor as drivers or bus passengers, and cleaner air on the Corridor may marginally improve local air quality.
  5. Weighing. The indirect benefit is small and shared with the whole city; the two harms are concentrated on one already lower-income district. The distributional effect is therefore clearly negative for Southgate as a district, even though the system is a net benefit to the city — and that gap between the aggregate headline figure and the local experience is the most important thing the council's annual report does not capture.
Answer: 2 for the named exclusion, 2 for the induced-traffic mechanism, 2 for the data-representation feedback loop, 2 for the weighing that distinguishes the district-level from the city-level conclusion. The move that earns the last two marks is noticing that "14 % faster" and "Southgate is worse off" can both be true.
Question 5 Evaluate the council's decision not to release camera data to cycling groups. (8 marks)
  1. Criteria. The decision should be judged on the public benefit of audit (junction safety), the privacy risk to individuals captured on video, and the credibility of the council's stated process.
  2. Against release. Overhead video of a public road, retained and combined with five-second timestamps, is not anonymous in the way a vehicle count is: routine movements can be reconstructed and individuals re-identified, particularly for people with predictable patterns. The council has a genuine data-protection obligation, and releasing raw video would be irreversible — once published it cannot be recalled.
  3. For release, or for a middle option. However, the refusal is absolute rather than proportionate. The cycling groups want safety findings — near-miss counts, conflict rates, signal timing compliance — not video of identifiable people. Aggregated, thresholded statistics, or analysis performed inside the council's environment with only the results released, would meet the audit need without the re-identification risk.
  4. Governance point. The refusal also sits awkwardly beside the council's willingness to let Meridian use the same data commercially. Data that is too sensitive for a residents' safety audit but acceptable for a vendor's model training is a defensible position only if the distinction is published and justified; the case study gives no evidence that it is.
  5. Judgement. The decision is right in its aim and wrong in its form. Refusing raw video is justified; refusing all derived safety data is not, and it costs the council the independent scrutiny that would detect exactly the kind of error a digital twin cannot. The proportionate response is controlled release of aggregate safety metrics.
Answer: 2 for criteria, 2–3 for the privacy argument, 2–3 for the proportionality counter-argument with a concrete alternative, 2 for the judgement. Notice that the strongest answers find the third option — the exam is rarely asking you to choose between yes and no.
Question 6 The council proposes to extend the system by adding facial recognition to the overhead cameras to identify drivers who run red lights. Discuss two ethical issues this raises. (6 marks)
  1. Consent and function creep. The cameras were deployed and justified to the public as traffic-flow sensors, with on-device inference explicitly presented as a privacy protection — no images leave the cabinet. Adding biometric identification uses data collected for one purpose for a quite different one, without a fresh mandate. Consent obtained for congestion management is not consent for law enforcement, and the public justification for the original deployment is retrospectively invalidated.
  2. Irreversibility and differential error. A face is not a password: it cannot be reissued if the database is breached, and it is captured from people who have committed no offence. Facial recognition error rates also differ between demographic groups, so false matches — and therefore wrongly issued fines — will not be distributed evenly across the population, which is an equity problem as well as an accuracy one.
  3. (A third acceptable issue: proportionality — automated fines for a traffic offence, with no human review before penalty, and the chilling effect of pervasive identification on lawful movement and assembly.)
Answer: 3 marks per developed issue. One mark is for naming the issue; two more for the mechanism and the consequence. "It is a privacy issue" is one mark — the why is worth two.
Question 7 Meridian reports that its model correctly identifies congested junctions in 92 % of cases. In a sample of 200 junction-minutes, 40 were actually congested; the model flagged 50, of which 34 were genuinely congested. Calculate the precision and the recall of the model on this sample, and explain why the 92 % figure is misleading. (6 marks)
  1. True positives (flagged and actually congested) = 34. False positives (flagged but not congested) = 50 − 34 = 16. False negatives (congested but not flagged) = 40 − 34 = 6.
  2. Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 34 / 50 = 0.68.
  3. Recall = TP / (TP + FN) = 34 / 40 = 0.85.
  4. \[\text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP} = \frac{34}{50} = 0.68 \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} = \frac{34}{40} = 0.85\]
  5. Why 92 % is misleading. Accuracy is (TP + TN) / total = (34 + 154) / 200 = 0.94, and the quoted 92 % is of this kind. But congestion is rare: only 20 % of junction-minutes are congested. A model that flagged nothing at all would score 80 % accuracy while being useless. Accuracy therefore flatters a model on an imbalanced dataset, and the two figures that actually matter to the operator are different: precision of 0.68 means a third of the interventions are unnecessary (wasted signal changes, and a loss of trust among engineers), while recall of 0.85 means 15 % of genuine congestion is missed.
  6. Which matters here? For a signal controller, missed congestion (low recall) is the more expensive error, because an unmanaged queue propagates; unnecessary phase extension is comparatively cheap. So the model should be retuned to favour recall — and the 92 % figure gives no help whatsoever in deciding that.
Marks: 1 for TP/FP/FN identified, 1 + 1 for the two calculations, 2 for the imbalance explanation, 1 for saying which error is worse in this context and why. This is A4.2/A4.3 content appearing inside a case study question — exactly the kind of crossover Paper 1 likes.
Question 8 To what extent does the governance of the Adaptive Corridor System allow affected residents to hold decision-makers to account? (10 marks)
  1. Establish what accountability requires. Affected people must know a decision was made, must be able to understand why, and must have a route to contest it. Assess the system against all three.
  2. Knowing. Partly satisfied: the annual summary is published, but the per-junction decision logs are not, so a resident whose street has become a rat-run cannot find out what changed or when.
  3. Understanding. Largely not satisfied. The council sets three weights, but the model and its weights are Meridian's intellectual property and the objective function is not published — the right-to-explanation problem in concrete form.
  4. Contesting. Formally possible, practically weak: accountability is split between a body that is accountable but not in control (the council) and a body in control but not accountable to residents (Meridian), and an eight-year contract makes exit expensive.
  5. Counter-argument. Accountability is not absent — the annual report, named officers and the union's successful formal objection are real mechanisms, if slow ones.
  6. Judgement. To a limited extent. More accountable than a wholly private system, less accountable than the fixed-time timing tables it replaced, which any resident could read. The deficit is structural rather than intentional, and could be largely fixed without touching the technology: publish the decision logs and the objective function, and require the twin to be independently auditable.
Marks: 2 for a framework, 2 + 2 + 2 for the three tests applied with evidence, 2 for the judgement that is explicitly partial. "To what extent" questions are never answered with "yes" or "no"; the mark is in locating the extent precisely and saying what would change it.
Trap — the question you did not get asked

In Question 4, most students write about "residents" in general and lose four marks. In Question 8, most students answer "is the system bad?" instead of "to what extent can residents hold decision-makers to account?" and lose six. Before writing, underline the command term and circle the noun phrase after it. If the question names a stakeholder, your answer must name them twice. If it says to what extent, your last sentence must contain a word like partly, limited, or only when.

Exam technique — invent your own questions

The best preparation for unseen questions is writing them. Once a month, take one stakeholder from your table and write a question beginning "Analyse the impact of the system on …", then answer it in 25 minutes. After three rounds you will have covered six stakeholders, and you will have discovered which rows of your table are thin — those are exactly the rows the examiner is most likely to ask about, because they are the interesting ones.

Check yourself

Practice

  1. A classmate says: "The case study questions are unseen, so there is no point revising the case study — you just have to be good at writing." Give two reasons why this is wrong.

    Show answer
    1. Unseen questions test reasoning about known context. Every mark is awarded for a point attached to the case study's stakeholders, technologies or data. Without the context prepared you can only write generic material, which scores in the lowest band regardless of how well it is written.
    2. Prepared knowledge is what frees the time. A student who has the stakeholder table memorised spends zero minutes deciding who to write about and 25 minutes writing; one who has not spends 8 minutes deciding and runs out of time. In a 2-hour paper that difference is worth more than any writing skill.

  2. A question asks you to evaluate a proposal. Your answer describes the proposal in accurate technical detail and concludes "therefore it is a good idea". Explain two specific reasons this cannot reach the top band.

    Show answer
    1. No criteria were stated, so nothing has been weighed. An evaluation is a comparison against stated criteria from the context; without them the answer is an assertion with technical decoration.
    2. The judgement is absolute. Real design decisions are trade-offs, and a conclusion that does not say under what condition it holds — and what would reverse it — has not engaged with the disadvantages at all. Also, only advantages were considered: an evaluation that finds no cost is incomplete by definition.

  3. Why is accuracy a poor metric for a system that detects a rare event, and which two metrics should be reported instead?

    Show answer

    When the positive class is rare, a model that never predicts it still achieves high accuracy, so accuracy cannot distinguish a useful model from a useless one. Report precision \(= TP/(TP+FP)\) — of the cases flagged, how many were real — and recall \(= TP/(TP+FN)\) — of the real cases, how many were caught. Which of the two matters more depends on which error is more expensive in the application, and a good answer says so.
