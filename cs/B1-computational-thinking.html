B1 · Computational thinking

Computational thinking is not "thinking about computers" — it is the habit of taking a vague, messy real-world problem and reshaping it until a machine could carry it out. The five strands the syllabus names (think procedurally, logically, ahead, concurrently, abstractly) are not a list to memorise; they are five questions you ask yourself when you get stuck. Do this topic first, because every other B topic assumes it, because the IB pseudocode you learn here is the language most Paper 2 questions are written in, and because the four marks that separate a 5 from a 7 in Paper 1 are almost always marks for reasoning rather than for facts.

Why this exists

The 2027 guide moved the course away from "know a lot of definitions" and towards "solve an unfamiliar problem and justify the solution". That is computational thinking by another name. A Paper 1 question about a warehouse robot is not testing whether you have met warehouse robots before; it is testing whether you can identify the inputs and outputs, spot the precondition that has been broken, decompose the task into testable modules, and explain what you have decided to ignore. Every one of those moves lives on this page. If you can only memorise five things from the whole CS course, memorise the five questions in the table below.

The five questions

Before any machinery, here is the whole topic in one table. Read the middle column as a checklist you run whenever a question hands you a scenario you have never seen. The right-hand column is the artefact the examiner can actually mark — which is why "think about the problem" earns nothing but "construct a decision table" earns four.

Trap — naming the strand is not answering the question

"This problem requires computational thinking, specifically thinking procedurally and thinking ahead." is worth zero marks, even though every word of it is true. The marks are always for the artefact: the named sub-problems, the completed decision table, the stated precondition. A reliable habit: never write the name of a strand without writing the thing it produced on the next line.

1 · Thinking procedurally

SL / HL

Intuition

You cannot eat a whole cow. You can eat a steak. Procedural thinking is the realisation that every large problem is a bundle of small ones, and that the small ones are often problems you have already solved. The cook does not have a "make dinner" skill; she has boil, chop, season, fry, and she combines them differently each night. Programs work the same way.

Formal idea

Decomposition is breaking a problem into sub-problems until each one is small enough to be solved, tested and understood on its own. Modularity is the property that results: the solution is built from separate, named units (sub-procedures, functions, modules, classes) that communicate through a narrow, well-defined interface. Top-down design (also called stepwise refinement) is the method: start with one sentence describing the whole task, split it into three to seven smaller sentences, then split each of those, and stop when a sentence can be written as five or six lines of pseudocode.

Two criteria tell you when to stop decomposing:

Testability

You can describe the input and expected output of the piece without referring to anything outside it. If you need the whole system running to check one piece, the piece is too big or too entangled.

Single responsibility

You can name the piece with one verb and one noun — "validate member", "calculate fine", "format report". If the honest name contains "and", it is two pieces.

The procedure

How to use it — top-down design in five moves
  1. Write the one-sentence brief. "Work out which pupil has the highest average mark." No code, no variables.
  2. Name the data. What exists before the program runs (inputs) and what must exist afterwards (outputs)? This is thinking ahead; you cannot skip it.
  3. Split into three to seven steps at the top level, in order. Draw them as a structure chart (below).
  4. Mark the repeated shapes. Any step that appears again with a different value is one sub-procedure with a parameter, not two pieces of code.
  5. Refine until atomic. Stop when a box is a loop, a selection, or a call to something you already have. Then write pseudocode bottom-up: the leaves first, so each is testable before it is used.

Parameters, return values and the interface

A sub-procedure is a contract, and the contract is written in three lines: what goes in (parameters), what comes out (return value, or nothing), and what must be true when it is called (its precondition). Everything else is private to the sub-procedure and no other part of the program is allowed to know about it.

Note — pass by value and pass by reference

With pass by value the sub-procedure receives a copy, so the caller's variable cannot change. With pass by reference it receives the address of the caller's variable, so assignments inside do change it. The IB pseudocode model defaults to pass by value unless the question says otherwise; Python passes immutable values (numbers, strings) by value-like semantics and mutable objects (lists, dictionaries) by reference; Java passes primitives by value and object references by value. The exam mark often sits on the sentence "I am assuming pass by value", so write it. See B2 Programming for the implementation detail.

Structure charts (hierarchy charts)

A structure chart shows the modules and the calls between them: a box for each module, a line from caller to called. It deliberately does not show sequence, selection or iteration — those belong in flowcharts and pseudocode. A structure chart answers one question: "who uses whom?" The tree shape is the point: a good design is a tree, because a cycle in the call graph means two modules that cannot be tested or changed independently.

Exam technique — three ways to lose procedural marks

(1) Drawing a structure chart with arrows showing sequence (1 → 2 → 3). Lines in a structure chart mean "calls", nothing else; sequence goes in the pseudocode. (2) A decomposition with two or three giant pieces ("read the data", "process the data", "output the data") — that is a description of every program ever written, not a decomposition. Aim for five to eight named leaves. (3) Forgetting the shared module: if two branches of your chart do the same lookup, say so, because "one parameterised sub-procedure used twice" is usually its own mark.

Worked example 1 IB-style · 8 marks · decompose a library loans system

Question. A school library lends books to pupils. A pupil may borrow up to four books at a time and each loan lasts 14 days. A pupil with an overdue book may not borrow. The librarian wants a program that records a loan, records a return, calculates any fine (10 cents per day late) and prints a weekly overdue list. (a) Decompose the problem into modules. (b) Identify one module that is used by more than one branch. (c) Explain two benefits of this decomposition.

  1. (a) Decomposition. Top level: record loan, record return, produce weekly report.
  2. Record loan refines to: validate the pupil ID exists and is not blocked; count the pupil's current loans; check the copy is not already on loan; if all three pass, write a new loan record with today's date and a due date 14 days later; otherwise output the reason for refusal.
  3. Record return refines to: find the loan record for this copy; mark it returned with today's date; if today is after the due date, compute days late and fine = daysLate × 0.10 and add it to the pupil's record.
  4. Produce weekly report refines to: scan all loan records; select those not returned whose due date has passed; sort by pupil name; format as a table.
  5. (b) Shared module. Both record return and record loan need "find the current loan record for a given copy ID", so write one function findLoanByCopy(copyID) and call it from both. Also "is this pupil blocked?" is used by record loan and could be used by the report.
  6. (c) Benefits. First, independent testing: daysLate and the fine calculation can be tested with two dates and no library at all, so a wrong fine is found in seconds rather than after a week of use. Second, localised change: when the school raises the fine to 20 cents, one constant in one module changes and nothing else is touched, so the change cannot break the report.
Marks: 3 for a decomposition with at least six named leaves that are individually meaningful; 2 for identifying a genuinely shared module (not "input" or "output"); 3 for two benefits each developed with a concrete consequence. "It makes it easier to debug" alone is worth 1; "…because the fine calculation can be tested with two dates and no file" is worth 2.
Worked example 2 IB-style · 6 marks · top-down design of a school timetable

Question. A school has 12 teachers, 20 classes and 30 rooms. Each class needs 30 lessons a week; each teacher can teach at most 22; some rooms are laboratories needed only by science classes. (a) State the decomposition into sub-problems. (b) State which sub-problem is the hard one and why. (c) Explain why the decomposition does not by itself solve the problem.

  1. (a) (i) model the data: a lesson is a triple (class, teacher, room, period); (ii) collect the hard constraints — no teacher in two places at once, no class in two places at once, no room double-booked, laboratories reserved for science; (iii) collect the soft preferences — a teacher should not teach more than four lessons in a row, PE should not be first period; (iv) generate an assignment of lessons to periods; (v) test the assignment against (ii); (vi) score it against (iii); (vii) repeat (iv)–(vi) and keep the best; (viii) output the timetable in a readable form.
  2. (b) Step (iv) is the hard one. There are 30 periods and 20 classes, so 600 lesson slots to fill; checking every possible arrangement is not feasible, so the generator must use a heuristic — most -constrained-first is the usual choice: place the class or teacher with the fewest remaining legal periods before the ones with many. Everything else on the list is bookkeeping.
  3. (c) Because decomposition splits the problem, it does not make the hard sub-problem disappear. It makes the hard part visible and isolated: steps (v) and (vi) can be written and tested first, so when you later improve the generator in (iv) you can prove immediately whether the new timetable is still legal. That is the real payoff — the other 90% of the problem becomes reliable, and only 10% stays difficult. An answer that says "decomposition makes it easier" scores 1; an answer that says "decomposition isolates the hard part so the rest can be verified mechanically" scores 3.
Answer: eight sub-problems, the generator is the hard one, and decomposition isolates rather than removes difficulty. Marks: 2 for the decomposition, 2 for identifying the generator with a reason tied to the size of the search space, 2 for the "isolates, does not solve" point.
Trap — "input, process, output" is not a decomposition

Every program on Earth can be described as input → process → output, so a three-box answer of that shape carries no information and examiners are instructed not to reward it. A real decomposition names the domain pieces — validate member, check availability, calculate fine — and leaves the reader able to guess what each one does without reading your code.

2 · Thinking logically

SL / HL

Intuition

Logic is the difference between "if it is raining I will take the bus" and a complete set of travel plans. The first sentence tells you nothing about what happens when it is sunny, or snowing, or raining but the buses are on strike. A program that is incomplete in that way does not crash — it quietly takes the wrong branch, which is far worse. Thinking logically is making the case analysis exhaustive (every input matches at least one case) and exclusive (no input matches two).

Formal idea: compound conditions

A condition is an expression that evaluates to true or false. Compound conditions combine simple ones with AND, OR and NOT, and the truth of the whole follows from the truth of the parts.

Three rules do almost all the work. NOT binds tightest, then AND, then OR — the same precedence as × before + in arithmetic — so use brackets whenever you mix them, for the reader's sake if not the machine's. De Morgan's laws let you push a negation inside: NOT (A AND B) is (NOT A) OR (NOT B), and NOT (A OR B) is (NOT A) AND (NOT B). And short-circuit evaluation means the second operand may never be evaluated: in (i < n) AND (A[i] = target), if i < n is false the array access never happens, which is exactly why that ordering prevents an out-of-range error. Put the guard first, always.

Connection

Boolean algebra in full — truth tables, logic gates, deriving an expression from a table and simplifying it — lives in A1 Computer fundamentals. Here you need only as much as controls an if. The link worth remembering: every condition in a program is a Boolean expression, and the CPU evaluates it with the physical gates you met in A1.

Decision tables

A decision table lists every combination of conditions and the action each combination should trigger. It is the best tool the syllabus gives you for a specification written in prose, because prose hides cases and a table cannot. The layout is fixed, and examiners expect it:

Rule 1Rule 2Rule 3Rule 4
Condition stub (each condition on its own row)Condition entries: Y, N or — (don't care)
Action stub (each action on its own row)Action entries: X marks the action to take

The two checks that turn a decision table from a drawing into an answer are completeness — with n yes/no conditions there are 2n rules, so check you have accounted for all of them, collapsing only those that genuinely share an outcome — and consistency — no two rules may have identical condition entries but different actions. Contradictions in the table are contradictions in the specification, and finding one is often exactly what the question is asking for.

Decision trees

The same information as a graph: each internal node tests one condition, each branch is an outcome, each leaf names an action. Trees are easier for a human to follow one case at a time; tables are easier to check for completeness. Convert table → tree when you are writing the selection; convert tree → table when you are auditing someone else's specification.

Worked example 4 IB-style · 7 marks · find and fix an incomplete specification

Question. Here is a specification handed to a programmer: "The program asks for a mark out of 100. Below 40 is a fail. Above 60 is a pass. Above 80 is a distinction. Validate the input." Identify three defects in this specification and rewrite it so that it is complete.

  1. Defect 1 — gaps. Exactly 40, exactly 60 and exactly 80 are not covered by any band: 40 is neither "below 40" nor "above 60", and 60 is neither "above 60" nor "below 40". The sets are not exhaustive.
  2. Defect 2 — overlaps. A mark of 90 is "above 60" and "above 80", so two rules match. If the conditions are written in ascending order the first one wins, so 90 is graded a pass and the distinction band is unreachable. The sets are not exclusive.
  3. Defect 3 — "validate" is undefined. What is a valid mark? Presumably an integer from 0 to 100, but the specification never says, so the programmer must guess, and whether 87.5 or "abc" or an empty entry is acceptable is undecided.
  4. Rewrite. "The program asks for a mark, which must be a whole number from 0 to 100 inclusive; any other entry produces the message 'Enter a whole number from 0 to 100' and the prompt repeats. A mark from 0 to 39 is a fail, from 40 to 59 a pass, from 60 to 79 a merit, and from 80 to 100 a distinction."
  5. Check. Every integer 0–100 now falls in exactly one band, and the boundaries 40, 60 and 80 are stated as belonging to the upper band. The corresponding test data are 0, 39, 40, 59, 60, 79, 80, 100 plus −1, 101, 45.5 and "abc".
Marks: 1 per named defect developed with an example mark, 2 for a rewrite that is both exhaustive and exclusive, 1 for the validation clause. The trick in these questions is to test the boundary values out loud: "what happens at exactly 40?" will find two of the three defects in about ten seconds.
Trap — ordering a chain of conditions

"Grade the exam: A above 80, B above 70, C above 60, otherwise fail." Written in descending order with a final catch-all it is correct. Written in ascending order — if mark > 60 then "C" first — a mark of 85 is caught by the first branch and graded C. The program runs, produces no error, and silently gives wrong grades to the best students. That is a logic error, not a syntax error, and it is the practical meaning of this strand. Rule of thumb: when bands are ordered, test them in one direction only, from the most restrictive to the least, and always end with else.

3 · Thinking ahead

SL / HL

Intuition

A good chef lays out every ingredient before turning on the stove. Thinking ahead is deciding in advance what the program needs, what it promises, and what could go wrong — because a decision made before coding costs seconds and the same decision made after deployment costs a rewrite.

Formal idea: inputs, outputs, preconditions, postconditions

Input / output specification

Inputs are everything the program needs before it starts: values typed by the user, files, sensor readings, the current date. Outputs are everything it must produce: printed text, a file, a screen, a signal to an actuator. Writing them as a list (an IPO chart) before designing is what stops you discovering halfway through that you never asked for the pupil's year group.

Preconditions and postconditions

A precondition is what must be true for a module to work correctly: the array must be sorted; the divisor must not be zero; the file must exist. A postcondition is what the module guarantees when it finishes, provided the precondition held. Preconditions are a contract: whose job is it to check? If every module checks everything, you have written the same code ten times; the disciplined answer is to check once, at the boundary, and state the precondition everywhere else.

How to use it — the pre-design checklist
  1. List the inputs. For each, write the type, the legal range, and where it comes from.
  2. List the outputs. For each, write the format the user needs — "a table sorted by surname", not "the data".
  3. State the preconditions for the whole program and for each module you intend to write.
  4. List the things that could go wrong: empty file, missing file, wrong type, zero, negative, duplicate, too many, none at all, run twice.
  5. Decide the behaviour for each: reject and re-prompt, use a default, or stop with a message. Any of the three is defensible; crashing is not.
  6. Ask what will change. The fine rate, the number of year groups, the file format. Put each in one place (a constant, a parameter, a configuration file) so it can change without surgery.
  7. Ask what is reused. Anything appearing twice becomes a parameterised module or a library function.

Caching and pre-fetching

A cache stores a computed or fetched value so that the next request for the same value is served from fast local storage instead of being recomputed or re-fetched. It is thinking ahead in its purest form: you predict what will be asked for and prepare it in advance. Pre-fetching goes one step further and fetches what you expect to need soon, before it is requested — a video buffer, the next map tile, the next page of results.

The trade is always the same: cache size and freshness against speed. Three questions decide whether a cache is a good idea — how expensive is the original operation, how often is the same value requested, and how quickly does the underlying value change? A cache whose contents go stale is worse than no cache, because it is confidently wrong, so caches need an invalidation rule: a time-to-live, or an explicit update whenever the source changes.

Reusable components

Thinking ahead also means writing the second program before you have finished the first. If a module is likely to be needed again, it should not know anything about the current problem: findMax(A, n) is reusable, findTopPupilInYearTen() is not. The mechanism is the same one you met in section 1 — parameters instead of hard-coded values — and the payoff is that the reused module is already tested.

Worked example 6 IB-style · 7 marks · how caching changes a design

Question. A weather app displays the current temperature for a city. Each display calls an API that takes 400 ms and is charged per call. The app refreshes the screen every second. (a) Explain the problem. (b) Describe how caching changes the design. (c) State one new problem the cache introduces and how to handle it.

  1. (a) One call per second per user is 86 400 calls a day per user, each billed, for data that changes every ten minutes. Ninety-nine per cent of the calls return a value the app already had. The cost is financial and the responsiveness is poor, because the user waits 400 ms for a number that did not change.
  2. (b) Insert a cache between the display and the API: the app asks the cache for the temperature; if the cache holds a value, it is returned immediately; if not (a cache miss), the API is called, the value is stored with a time stamp, and it is returned. A time-to-live of, say, five minutes means at most 288 calls a day instead of 86 400 — a reduction of about 99.7%, with the screen updating instantly.
  3. (c) The new problem is staleness: for up to five minutes the app shows a temperature that is no longer correct, and the user has no way to tell. Handle it by choosing the TTL from how fast the real value changes, by displaying the time of the reading next to it, and by providing a manual refresh that bypasses the cache.
  4. The general lesson: a cache trades correctness of freshness for speed and cost. An answer that names both sides of the trade and then says which side wins in this context is an evaluate answer; an answer that only lists the saving is a describe answer.
Answer: cache with a five-minute TTL; roughly 288 calls/day instead of 86 400; the new hazard is stale data, handled by TTL, a visible time stamp and a manual bypass. Marks: 2 for (a) quantified, 3 for (b) describing hit/miss and TTL, 2 for (c) naming staleness and a mitigation.
Exam technique — "state the precondition" is a free mark

Every binary search question, every array-processing question and every file question in this course has a one-mark precondition attached to it, and it is the same sentence shape each time: "the array must be sorted in ascending order", "the file must exist and be readable", "the divisor must be non-zero", "n must be at least 1". Learn the shape rather than the list: what would make this module fail, and who must guarantee it does not?

4 · Thinking concurrently

HL only

Intuition

Two people editing the same paragraph of a shared document: if both type at once, one person's work vanishes. Nothing is wrong with either person — the problem is that "open, edit, save" is not a single indivisible action, and their two sequences interleaved. That is the entire subject of concurrency in one sentence.

Formal idea: concurrency versus parallelism

ConcurrencyParallelism

The benefits are worth stating precisely. Throughput: while one task waits for the disk or the network, another uses the CPU, so the machine is idle far less of the time. Responsiveness: a user interface that keeps redrawing while a file loads feels instant even when it is not fast. Modularity: some problems are naturally a set of independent agents — one thread per network connection, one per sensor — and forcing them into a single sequence makes the code worse. Resource utilisation: a pipeline (fetch, decode, execute in A1) is concurrency inside a single processor.

The hazards

The price is that the order of operations is no longer fixed by the program text, and any reasoning that assumed an order becomes invalid. Four named failures follow.

Race condition
The outcome depends on the interleaving of two or more threads' operations on shared data. Two threads increment a counter; both read 7; both write 8; one increment is lost. The bug is real, it is not reproducible on demand, and it disappears when you add a print statement to debug it — a Heisenbug.
Critical section / mutual exclusion
The critical section is the part of a program that touches shared data and must not be executed by more than one thread at a time. Mutual exclusion is the property that guarantees it: at most one thread is inside. Enforced by a lock (mutex) or a semaphore: acquire before entering, release on leaving.
Deadlock
Two or more threads are each holding a resource the other needs and waiting for it, so none can proceed. The program does not crash and does not error — it simply stops making progress, often at 3 a.m. in production.
Starvation
A thread is permanently denied a resource it needs, not because of a cycle but because other threads keep being served first — an unfair scheduler, or a priority scheme that always favours the same jobs. Everything else makes progress; one unlucky thread does not.

The four Coffman conditions

Deadlock occurs if and only if all four of these hold simultaneously. They are worth memorising as a checklist, because a deadlock is prevented by breaking any one of them — and each gives you a named fix.

ConditionMeaningHow to break it
Mutual exclusion At least one resource cannot be shared; only one thread may hold it Make the resource shareable, or use copy-on-write so readers never block each other. Sometimes impossible — a printer really is exclusive
Hold and wait A thread holding one resource requests another and waits without releasing the first Acquire all resources at once, atomically; or require a thread to release everything before requesting more
No preemption A resource can only be released voluntarily; it cannot be forcibly taken Allow preemption: if a request is denied, the thread releases what it holds and retries later
Circular wait There is a cycle: T1 waits for a resource held by T2, T2 for one held by T3, …, Tn for one held by T1 Impose a global ordering on resources and require every thread to acquire them in that order. This is the practical, universal fix

Worked example 8 IB-style · 8 marks · a deadlock scenario with two locks, and two fixes

Question. Two bank accounts, X and Y. To transfer money a thread locks both accounts, moves the money, then unlocks both. Thread T1 transfers from X to Y and does lock(X); lock(Y); … unlock(Y); unlock(X). Thread T2 transfers from Y to X and does lock(Y); lock(X); … unlock(X); unlock(Y). (a) Explain how deadlock arises. (b) Show that all four Coffman conditions hold. (c) Give two different fixes.

  1. (a) Interleave: T1 locks X; T2 locks Y; T1 tries to lock Y and blocks because T2 holds it; T2 tries to lock X and blocks because T1 holds it. Neither can proceed, neither releases, so both wait for ever. Note it only happens if the interleaving is unlucky — the system can run correctly for days.
  2. (b) Mutual exclusion: a lock is held by one thread at a time. Hold and wait: T1 holds X while waiting for Y. No preemption: a lock cannot be taken from a thread; it is only released by its holder. Circular wait: T1 → Y → T2 → X → T1 is a cycle.
  3. (c) Fix 1 — lock ordering (breaks circular wait). Impose a global order, e.g. always lock the account with the lower ID first. Both threads then lock X before Y, so T2 simply waits at X until T1 finishes; no cycle can form. This is the standard fix: cheap, and provably correct.
  4. (c) Fix 2 — acquire both atomically (breaks hold and wait). Provide a single lockBoth(a, b) operation that either takes both locks or neither, so a thread never holds one while waiting for the other.
  5. (c) Fix 3 — timeout and retry (breaks no preemption, in effect). If T1 cannot get Y within 2 seconds it releases X, waits a random interval, and retries. This risks livelock if both threads keep backing off in step, which is why the wait is randomised.
Answer: deadlock by circular wait; all four conditions present; fix by imposing a global lock order, or by acquiring both locks atomically, or by timeout and retry. Marks: 2 for the interleaving, 3 for the four conditions each tied to the scenario, 3 for two fixes explained. Naming "lock ordering" without saying which order is only half a mark — say lower ID first.
Trap — concurrency buzzwords without a mechanism

"Use a semaphore to prevent the race condition" is worth one mark at best. The mark is for the mechanism: which operations are in the critical section, when the lock is acquired and released, and what happens to a thread that finds the lock taken. Likewise, do not write "deadlock is when two processes wait for each other" and stop — that is the definition, and the question almost always asks you to show the interleaving or name the condition you broke.

5 · Thinking abstractly

SL / HL

Intuition

A London Underground map is not London. It has no parks, no buildings, and the river is a blue line. Stations are evenly spaced even when they are not. Every one of those distortions is deliberate: the map throws away everything that does not help you decide which train to catch. That is abstraction — choosing what to discard so that what remains can be reasoned about.

Formal idea

Abstraction is representing the essential features of a problem while ignoring the irrelevant detail. Representation is the choice of how to encode what you kept: a graph, a table, a number, a queue. Generalisation is rewriting the solution so it works for every instance of the pattern rather than the one you started with — usually by replacing hard-coded values with parameters. Abstraction discards; generalisation re-parameterises; they are usually done together.

Abstraction is what makes problems tractable rather than merely smaller. A road network with speed limits, traffic lights, roadworks and weather cannot be solved by any algorithm; a graph with one number per edge can be solved in milliseconds by one you already know. The abstraction is not a simplification you apologise for — it is the step that turns an impossible problem into a solved one. Its price is that anything you discarded cannot affect the answer, so choosing badly produces a fast, elegant solution to the wrong problem.

Why abstraction is the highest-value strand

It is the one that recurs everywhere and the one that separates a 4 from a 7 in Paper 1. It is also the criterion the IA marks explicitly as abstraction, where the top band asks you to "demonstrate the development of an abstraction from the raw problem" — literally the before-and-after picture of what you kept and what you threw away. Practise stating it in one sentence of the form: "I model X as Y, keeping A and B, and discarding C because it cannot affect the answer." That sentence is three marks.

Worked example 9 IB-style · 6 marks · strip a concrete problem to its abstract model

Question. A delivery company has 8 vans and 60 parcels to deliver across a city. Each parcel has a weight and a destination; each van has a capacity; drivers must finish within an 8-hour shift. The manager wants the fewest vans used and the shortest total distance. Identify a suitable abstraction, stating what is kept and what is discarded.

  1. Keep. (i) A set of 60 delivery points with coordinates, or with a distance between every pair. (ii) The weight of each parcel, but only as a total per van, because only the sum matters against capacity. (iii) Van capacity as a single number. (iv) The shift length, as a maximum route duration.
  2. Discard. What each parcel contains; the van's colour, registration and service history; the driver's name and preferences; road surface and scenery; the shape of the streets between two delivery points — all replaced by a single number, the travel time.
  3. The model. A weighted graph: 60 nodes (delivery points) plus one depot node, edges labelled with travel time, plus a demand (weight) at each node and a capacity for each vehicle. The problem becomes the classical capacitated vehicle routing problem: partition the nodes into at most 8 tours starting and ending at the depot, each of total demand ≤ capacity and total edge weight ≤ 8 hours, minimising the sum of edge weights.
  4. Why it helps. The model is the same shape as a thousand other problems, so the whole literature of graph algorithms, heuristics and lower bounds applies immediately. Nothing about parcels or drivers needs to be rediscovered.
  5. The risk. Discarding traffic means the model assumes travel times are fixed. At 8 a.m. they are not. That assumption must be stated, and it is a limitation to discuss in an evaluation.
Marks: 2 for the model (weighted graph with capacities), 2 for a justified keep/discard list, 2 for a consequence — either the reuse of known algorithms or the stated limitation about fixed travel times.
Worked example 10 IB-style · 5 marks · the same abstraction in a second context

Question. A printer receives jobs from 40 computers and prints them in the order they arrive. (a) Name the abstract data type that models this. (b) Give a second, unrelated problem modelled by the same abstraction, and explain why the same operations are enough.

  1. (a) A queue — a first-in-first-out structure whose only operations are enqueue (add at the rear), dequeue (remove from the front), isEmpty and optionally peek. The printer does not care what is in a job, only which arrived first, so the entire content of every document is abstracted away.
  2. (b) Second context: breadth-first search on a graph (see B4). BFS needs to visit nodes in the order they were discovered, and that is the same FIFO discipline: enqueue each newly discovered node, dequeue the next node to explore. The queue operations are identical, and so is the code.
  3. (b) A third, if you want the extra mark: a keyboard buffer. Keystrokes must be processed in the order typed, so a FIFO queue again — and again the content of the keystroke is irrelevant to the structure.
  4. Why this is abstraction. The queue says nothing about printers, graphs or keyboards. It specifies behaviour — "the first thing put in is the first thing taken out" — and says nothing about arrays, linked lists or pointers, which is why the same ADT can be implemented three different ways and used in three unrelated programs. That separation of behaviour from implementation is the whole point.
Answer: a queue; also models BFS and a keyboard buffer, because all three need FIFO ordering only. Marks: 1 for naming the queue, 2 for a valid second context with the matching explained, 2 for identifying that the ADT specifies behaviour and hides implementation.

6 · Algorithm design and representation

SL / HL

Once the model exists and the problem is decomposed, you still have to express the solution. There are three notations, and each has a job: structured English for agreeing the design with a human, flowcharts for seeing control flow, and pseudocode for the precision that leads to code. Then there is the fourth thing, which is not a notation but the skill that earns the most Paper 2 marks: the trace table.

Structured English

Numbered steps in plain language, precise enough to be unambiguous but free of syntax. It is the right first draft, because you can show it to a non-programmer — your IA client — and they can tell you whether it is correct before you have written a line of code. The test of good structured English: every step is an instruction with a verb, and no step could be carried out two different ways by two different readers.

Flowcharts

Trap — flowchart arrows and decision labels

Three habits lose marks. (1) A decision diamond drawn with one arrow out instead of two. (2) Both branches labelled "yes" — or worse, unlabelled. Always check that every diamond has exactly two labelled exits and that every loop has a condition that can eventually become false.

Trap — MOD and DIV are not the same as / and %

DIV is integer division: it gives the whole-number quotient and throws away the remainder. MOD gives the remainder. So 17 DIV 5 = 3 and 17 MOD 5 = 2, and the identity a = (a DIV b) × b + (a MOD b) always holds. In Python these are // and %; in Java / on two ints is DIV and % is MOD, but / on a double is ordinary division — the classic source of "why is my average zero?" bugs.

Trace tables

A trace table records the value of every variable after each significant step. It is the only reliable way to answer "state the output of this algorithm" and the only reliable way to debug a loop. The method:

How to use it — build the table mechanically
  1. Make one column per variable plus one for output. Include loop counters, accumulators and any condition that changes.
  2. Write the initial values as the first row.
  3. Walk the algorithm one line at a time. Every time a variable changes, add a row.
  4. At a loop, evaluate the condition before each pass and stop when it is false. Record the condition's value as its own row if the question is about termination.
  5. Never do two steps in your head at once — that is where errors enter.
  6. Sanity-check at the end: does the final value make sense? If the algorithm counts something, count it by hand a second, different way.

Standard algorithms

Find maximum / minimum, count, sum, average

All four share one skeleton: initialise an accumulator, loop over the collection, update the accumulator, output it. The only thing that changes is the initial value and the update rule — and the initial value is where the bugs live.

// maximum of an array A of n values, indices 0..n-1
maxVal = A[0]                 // NOT 0 — see the trap below
loop for i = 1 to n - 1
    if A[i] > maxVal then
        maxVal = A[i]
    endif
next i
output maxVal
Trap — initialising the maximum to zero

maxVal = 0 is wrong whenever all the values could be negative: with the array [-5, -2, -9] the algorithm would report 0, a value that is not in the array at all. Always seed the accumulator with the first element and start the loop at index 1. Symmetrically, when finding a minimum, seed with the first element rather than 0. And when counting or summing, seed with 0 and divide by the count, never by a hard-coded constant.

Linear search

found = false
index = -1
i = 0
loop while (found = false) AND (i < n)
    if A[i] = target then
        found = true
        index = i
    endif
    i = i + 1
endwhile
output index

It examines elements one by one until it finds the target or runs out. It works on unsorted data, which is its main virtue. On average it examines half the elements; at worst all of them. Note the guard: i < n is tested before A[i] is touched, which is what prevents an out-of-range error on the last pass.

Binary search

low = 0
high = n - 1
found = false
loop while (low <= high) AND (found = false)
    mid = (low + high) DIV 2
    if A[mid] = target then
        found = true
    else
        if A[mid] < target then
            low = mid + 1        // discard the left half INCLUDING mid
        else
            high = mid - 1       // discard the right half INCLUDING mid
        endif
    endif
endwhile
Why Why binary search needs sorted data, and why it is O(log n)
  1. The algorithm's entire claim is: if A[mid] < target, then because the array is sorted, every element at index ≤ mid is also less than the target, so the target cannot be in the left half and we may discard it.
  2. That inference is only valid because of the ordering property. On unsorted data, "A[mid] is too small" tells you nothing at all about A[mid−1], so discarding half the array is unjustified and the algorithm can return "not found" for an element that is present. This is why "state the precondition" is a one-mark question that follows every binary search question.
  3. Why log2 n. Each comparison halves the search space: n → n/2 → n/4 → … → 1. After k comparisons the remaining space is n / 2k. The search ends when that reaches 1, so n / 2k = 1, giving 2k = n and k = log2 n.
  4. So 1 000 000 elements need at most about 20 comparisons, against 1 000 000 for a linear search in the worst case. That is the entire argument for sorting data you intend to search repeatedly.
  5. The catch: sorting costs O(n log n), so if you search once it is not worth sorting first. Sort once, search many times — that is when binary search pays.

What it means: the log behaviour is not a rule to memorise; it falls out of halving. When it is the wrong tool: on a small array, or on a linked list, where there is no random access to the middle element at all.

Bubble sort

swapped = true
loop while swapped = true
    swapped = false
    loop for i = 0 to n - 2
        if A[i] > A[i+1] then
            temp   = A[i]
            A[i]   = A[i+1]
            A[i+1] = temp
            swapped = true
        endif
    next i
endwhile

Each pass walks the array comparing adjacent pairs and swapping any that are out of order, so the largest remaining value "bubbles" to the end. The swapped flag is the optimisation: if a whole pass makes no swap, the array is sorted and the algorithm can stop early.

The same 6-mark question, answered badly and well

Question. "A supermarket self-checkout allows a customer to scan items, pay by card, and print a receipt. Two customers sometimes use adjacent machines at the same time and both are served from one shared queue of trolleys. Identify one problem with this arrangement and explain how it could be avoided. [6 marks]"

Worked example 18 Weak vs strong — where the six marks actually sit

Weak answer (2 of 6). "The problem is a race condition. Two people might take the same trolley at the same time, which would be a problem. To avoid it they should use synchronisation or a semaphore. This would make the system more reliable and prevent errors."

Diagnosis. The right term is named — that is the two marks — but nothing is explained. What is shared? What are the three steps? Which interleaving loses a trolley? Which operations are in the critical section, and when is the lock released? None of that is there, and "more reliable" is not a mechanism.

Strong answer (6 of 6).

  1. Name the hazard and the shared resource. "The trolley count is shared mutable state: each machine performs read the count, decrement it, write it back. These three steps are not atomic."
  2. Show the interleaving. "If machine A reads 3 and machine B reads 3 before either writes, both write 2. One trolley has been issued twice from a count that should now be 1, so a later customer is told a trolley is available when it is not."
  3. Name the property needed. "Read–modify–write must be mutually exclusive: only one machine may be inside that section at a time."
  4. Give the mechanism. "Guard the section with a lock: acquire the lock, read, decrement, write, release. A machine that finds the lock taken waits."
  5. Note the cost and check for deadlock. "The cost is that the second machine waits a few milliseconds, which is acceptable because the section is short. There is no deadlock risk here because only one lock is involved, so no circular wait can form."
Where the marks are: 1 for naming the race condition, 2 for the interleaving with concrete numbers, 2 for mutual exclusion with a lock acquired and released around the right operations, 1 for the cost or the deadlock observation. The single move that turns a 2 into a 6 is replacing the word "synchronisation" with the three steps it protects.
Exam technique — the two-minute plan for any scenario question
  1. Underline the data. Everything the scenario mentions that the system must remember becomes an input or a stored value.
  2. Say what is shared, sorted or required. One of those three words is nearly always the precondition the question wants.
  3. Decide the artefact. "Identify" → a list of nouns. "Construct" → a table. "Explain" → mechanism + consequence. "Evaluate" → criteria + both sides + verdict.
  4. Write in the scenario's vocabulary. Say "pupil", "loan", "trolley", not "user" and "record". Contextualised answers reach the top markband; generic ones do not.
  5. Answer the number of marks. Six marks means roughly three developed points or six identified ones. If you have written one sentence for a six-mark question, you have not finished.

8 · Testing: thinking ahead written down

Three kinds of error

TypeWhat it isWhen it appearsExample
Syntax errorThe code breaks the grammar of the languageAt compile time (Java) or immediately at run time (Python)Missing colon in Python; missing semicolon or unmatched brace in Java
Logic errorThe code runs but does the wrong thingNever reported — you find it by testingUsing > where >= was needed; the wrong loop bound; grading in ascending order

Syntax errors are the easy ones: the computer tells you. Logic and runtime errors are the ones your testing strategy exists to catch, and they are caught by choosing the right data.

Three kinds of test data

TypePurposeExample for "enter an age between 0 and 120"
Normal (valid)Show that it works for ordinary, expected input25, 47, 90
Boundary / extremeProbe the edges of the valid range, where off-by-one bugs live0, 120, and the just-invalid values −1 and 121; also 1 and 119
Erroneous / invalidCheck that bad input is rejected gracefully rather than crashing−5, 200, "twenty", an empty string, 3.7
Why Why boundary testing catches the off-by-one bug specifically
  1. An off-by-one error is a mistake at the edge of a range: < instead of <=, starting a loop at 1 instead of 0, or n - 2 instead of n - 1.
  2. Inside the range, all those mistakes give the same answer as the correct code, so normal data cannot detect them. A loop over 10 items behaves identically whether it runs to i < 10 or i <= 10 for the middle eight items — they differ only at the ends.
  3. Therefore the only inputs that can distinguish the two are the ones at the boundary, plus the first value just outside it.
  4. That is why a test plan without boundary data is not a test plan: it systematically misses the most common class of logic error in student code — and, for the same reason, the most common class of bug in real code.
  5. Practical rule: for a valid range a to b inclusive, test a−1, a, a+1, a middle value, b−1, b, b+1. Seven cases, and they cover virtually everything.

Practice

  1. State the output of this pseudocode.

    n = 5
result = 1
loop for i = 1 to n
    result = result * i
next i
output result
    Show the answer

    result = 1 × 1 × 2 × 3 × 4 × 5 = 120. This is 5! (5 factorial). Trace: 1, 1, 2, 6, 24, 120.

    Why this is worth knowing: if the loop were i = 0 to n, the first multiplication would be by 0 and the answer would be 0 — a single-character difference with a completely different result. Loop bounds are the most common single-mark error in Paper 2.

  2. Explain the difference between a syntax error, a logic error and a runtime error, giving an example of each.

    Show the answer

    A syntax error breaks the grammar of the language (missing colon in Python, missing semicolon in Java) and is reported before or immediately at run time.

    A logic error is valid code that computes the wrong thing (using > where >= was needed, or the wrong loop bound). It is never reported; only testing reveals it.

    A runtime error crashes during execution because of a specific situation (division by zero, index out of bounds, opening a file that does not exist). It only appears on the input that triggers it, which is why erroneous test data matters.

    Why this is worth knowing: the three categories tell you where to look. Syntax errors are found by the machine, logic errors by comparison with expected output, runtime errors by testing the edges. A testing strategy that only uses normal data catches none of the third kind.

  3. State the output of the following, and explain what the algorithm does.

    count = 0
loop for i = 1 to 20
    if (i MOD 3 = 0) AND (i MOD 5 = 0) then
        count = count + 1
    endif
next i
output count
    Show the answer
    1. The condition is true when i is divisible by both 3 and 5, i.e. by 15.
    2. Between 1 and 20, only 15 qualifies.

    Output: 1. The algorithm counts numbers in the range that are multiples of 15.

    Why this is worth knowing: AND means both conditions must hold. With OR, the count would be 6 + 4 − 1 = 9 (multiples of 3 or 5, remembering that 15 must not be counted twice). Mixing up AND and OR is the single most common error in compound conditions.

  4. Explain why binary search cannot be used on unsorted data, using a concrete example.

    Show the answer

    Take the array [9, 3, 7, 1, 5] and search for 1. Binary search computes mid = 2, sees A[2] = 7 > 1, and concludes the target must be in the left half — so it discards indices 3 and 4. But the target, 1, is at index 3, which was just thrown away. The algorithm reports "not found" for an element that is present.

    The inference "A[mid] > target, therefore everything to the right is also too big" is valid only because of the sorted-order property. Without it, the value at one position tells you nothing about any other position, so halving is unjustified.

    Why this is worth knowing: "state the precondition" follows almost every binary search question and is a free mark. The precondition is not a technicality — it is the assumption the halving inference depends on.

  5. A cinema gives a 20% discount to anyone aged under 16 or over 64, and a 10% discount to students who are not already eligible. Construct a decision table for the discount, and state one case the specification leaves unclear.

    Show the answer

    Why this is worth knowing: the R4 column shows what a decision table is for — forcing you to say what happens when nothing is true. And the ambiguity at exactly 16/64 is exactly the kind of gap an examiner rewards you for spotting, because real specifications are full of them.

  6. An array of 1024 sorted values is searched. State the maximum number of comparisons needed by (a) linear search and (b) binary search, and explain the difference.

    Show the answer
    1. (a) Linear search: 1024 in the worst case (the target is the last element or absent).
    2. (b) Binary search: log2 1024 = 10, so at most 11 comparisons in practice (ten halvings plus the final equality check that terminates the loop).
    3. The difference arises because binary search halves the search space at each step: 1024 → 512 → 256 → 128 → 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1.
    4. The precondition is that the data is sorted, and the cost of achieving that must be counted if the data is not already sorted.
    Why this is worth knowing: 1024 → 10 is the whole argument for sorting. But say the caveat too: sorting costs O(n log n), so binary search only pays when the data is sorted once and searched many times.

  7. Two threads run the following at the same time on a shared variable COUNT, initially 0. Each thread executes temp = COUNT; temp = temp + 1; COUNT = temp once. (a) State the correct final value. (b) Give an interleaving that produces a different value. (c) State the term for this situation and describe a fix.

    Show the answer
    1. (a) 2.
    2. (b) T1 reads 0; T2 reads 0; T1 sets temp = 1; T2 sets temp = 1; T1 writes 1; T2 writes 1. Final value 1.
    3. (c) A race condition — specifically a lost update, because read–modify–write is not atomic. Fix: place the three steps inside a critical section guarded by a lock or semaphore, so one thread completes all three before the other begins; then the result is 2 whichever interleaving occurs.
    Why this is worth knowing: the trace is the answer. Naming "race condition" without showing the interleaving scores about a third of the marks; the numbers are what convince the examiner you understand why the update is lost.

  8. Show the state of [7, 3, 5, 3] after each pass of bubble sort, and state the total number of comparisons.

    Show the answer
    1. Pass 1: 7,3 swap → [3,7,5,3]; 7,5 swap → [3,5,7,3]; 7,3 swap → [3,5,3,7]. (3 comparisons, 3 swaps.)
    2. Pass 2: 3,5 no swap; 5,3 swap → [3,3,5,7]. (2 comparisons, 1 swap.)
    3. Pass 3: 3,3 no swap. (1 comparison, 0 swaps.) No swaps, so stop.
    4. Total comparisons = 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 = n(n−1)/2 with n = 4.
    Why this is worth knowing: the two 3s are not swapped when they meet, which is what makes this variant stable — equal elements keep their relative order. Stability matters when you sort records by one field while preserving an earlier sort by another.

  9. State the time complexity of an algorithm whose running time is T(n) = 4n2 + 100n + 50, and justify your answer.

    Show the answer
    1. Big-O keeps only the fastest-growing term and drops constant multipliers.
    2. The terms are 4n2, 100n and 50. As n grows, n2 dominates n, and the constant 50 does not grow at all.
    3. So T(n) = 4n2 + 100n + 50 is O(n2).
    4. Check: at n = 1000, the 4n2 term is 4 000 000, the 100n term is 100 000, and the constant is 50 — the quadratic term is already 97% of the total, and the proportion grows.
    Why this is worth knowing: stating that the constants are dropped because they do not affect the shape of the growth is what earns the justification mark. Just writing "O(n2)" earns half.
