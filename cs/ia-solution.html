The computational solution (IA)

You find a real person with a real problem, you apply the computational thinking process to it, and you produce a working solution plus a document that shows every step of your thinking. That is 35 hours and 20 % of your HL grade, marked by your teacher and moderated by the IB. The single most important thing on this page is this: the marker cannot reward thinking they cannot see. A brilliant program with thin documentation scores 4 or 5; a modest program whose documentation shows deliberate abstraction, a real client, systematic testing and honest evaluation scores 7. Everything below is about making your thinking visible.

35 hours, same at SL and HL HL 20 % · SL 30 % Java or Python — pick one and stay in it
Why this is assessed this way

The old IA was called "the solution" and was largely a dossier about a product. The new one is called the computational solution and is explicitly built around the computational thinking process, because the IBO's view is that the program is the evidence, not the object of assessment. What is assessed is whether you can specify a problem someone actually has, decide what to keep and what to discard, design an algorithm that solves the abstracted problem, build it, prove it works, and judge it honestly. That is the same loop in B1; the IA is B1 with a client attached.

Choosing the problem: the two-question test

Almost every weak IA was decided in the first week. Use this test before you commit to anything:

Test 1 — "Can I demo it in three minutes?"

Without explaining anything, can you show a user doing the thing your solution does, from start to finish, in under three minutes? If a demo needs narration, your scope is wrong: either the problem is too big (you will run out of hours) or too diffuse (you have no clear single function to demo).

Test 2 — "Does it have a real user who is waiting for it?"

Name the person, out loud. If the answer is "me", "my teacher", or "students in general", you have no client, and the criteria that depend on a client — problem specification, success criteria, evaluation — have nothing to bite on. A real client is someone who will tell you that your first idea was wrong.

Trap — four ways the problem choice caps your mark
  1. No client. Without one there is no interview, no signed success criteria, and no external validation. The evaluation criterion asks for evaluation against success criteria; invented criteria are visibly invented.
  2. Solved problem. If a free app already does it perfectly, the marker asks why anyone needs yours. This is fine — say so, and explain what is different about this context (language, cost, offline use, the specific data).
  3. A game. Games are allowed but rarely score well: no real client, no real data, and evaluation collapses into "it was fun". If you must, make it an educational tool for a named teacher with measurable learning outcomes.
  4. Pure CRUD. Add, view, edit, delete with nothing interesting in the middle gives you almost nothing to write about in algorithm design. You need at least one algorithm worth a page: a search, a ranking, a scheduling rule, a forecast, a parse, an optimisation.

How to use it — pick in this order
  1. Shortlist five ideas where you already know a named person who has the problem. Access to a real client beats any amount of technical interest.
  2. Check the "draws on" column against what you will have covered by the end of DP1. If your project needs B4 and you are only starting B2, either change the project or change the timetable.
  3. Apply the two-question test to each. Kill any idea that fails it.

Criterion by criterion: what top-band work looks like

Confirm the wording yourself

The five strands below are those the new guide organises the IA around — problem specification, abstraction, algorithm design and implementation, testing and evaluation, documentation and reflection. The exact criterion letters, mark allocations and strand wordings are published in the current DP computer science subject brief and the IA criteria document on ibo.org; open them before you start and write the current wording at the top of your plan. What follows describes what the descriptors mean in practice, which does not change even when the marks do.

Criterion 1 · Problem specification

The question the marker is asking: does this student understand someone else's problem well enough to have specified it, rather than having invented a problem to fit a program they wanted to write?

ElementTop bandWeak
The problem statementOne paragraph, 100–150 words, naming the client, the setting, the current situation, and the measurable pain ("≈ 40 portions wasted per day, ≈ £260 per week"). "People find it hard to keep track of their homework." No client, no numbers, no setting.
Evidence of the problemInterview transcript extract, a photo of the current paper system, a sample of the existing spreadsheet, or a count you did yourself.Asserted: "this is clearly a problem".
Success criteria5–7 criteria, each measurable and testable, written with the client, dated and signed. "The forecast for a dish must be within 15 % of actual sales on 4 out of 5 school days."3 vague criteria invented by the student: "easy to use", "works well", "looks good".
Scope and limitsAn explicit "out of scope" list — what this solution will not do, and why.Absent, so the marker cannot tell whether you finished or gave up.
Justification of the solution typeWhy a program, rather than a spreadsheet or a change of process — one paragraph, honest ("a spreadsheet was tried and failed because …")."A computer program is the best way to solve this." Unargued.
Mistakes that cap this criterion
  • Success criteria that cannot be measured — "user-friendly" — make the evaluation criterion impossible to do well, because there is nothing to evaluate against.
  • Writing the specification after you built the program. The marker can tell: the specification describes features the program happens to have, including the accidental ones.
  • No client voice. Every criterion must be traceable to something the client said or agreed to.

Criterion 2 · Abstraction

The question: did the student decide what matters, and can they justify what they threw away?

ArtefactTop bandWeak
What is kept / discardedA table: for each real-world entity, the attributes kept, the attributes discarded, and one sentence of justification for each discard.A list of variables with types. That is a data dictionary, not abstraction.
The modelThe problem restated in abstract terms: "a dish is a tuple (name, category, lead time, price); a day is a sequence of demand values; the forecast is a weighted mean over the last k matching days."Restating the problem in the same words you started with.
DecompositionA structure chart or numbered decomposition into 4–8 sub-problems, each independently testable, with the dependency between them shown.A single flowchart of the whole program, 40 boxes deep.
Assumptions4–6 stated assumptions, each with the consequence if it is false. None stated, or "I assume the user is sensible".
Why this works Why abstraction is the criterion that separates 5 from 7
  1. Every other criterion can be satisfied by effort: you can write more tests, draw more diagrams, keep a longer record of tasks. Abstraction cannot be faked by volume, because it is a decision — you either justify discarding something or you do not.
  2. The reason it is assessed at all: the same abstraction reappears everywhere in the course — the layer model in A1, the E-R model in A3, feature selection in A4, the class in B3, the ADT in B4. A student who can justify one abstraction in their IA has understood the idea that runs through the whole syllabus, and the marker is looking for exactly that.
  3. The practical trick: after you choose what to keep, write one sentence for each discarded attribute beginning "I do not need X because …". Six of those sentences is a top-band abstraction section, and it takes fifteen minutes.
  4. When it is the wrong tool: do not over-abstract. If a detail genuinely affects the outcome (a dish's lead time affects whether you can order it at all), discarding it is an error, and saying so in the write-up is worth more than a tidy model. Honest complexity beats false simplicity.

Here is the same core algorithm written both ways, because your IA must be in one real language and because you should be able to read the other. This is the forecast function from the exemplar below — a weighted mean over the last k matching days.

Python

def forecast(demand, day_type, k=4):
    """Weighted mean of the last k matching days.
    demand: list of (date, day_type, portions)
    Weights: most recent matching day counts most."""
    matches = [d for d in demand if d[1] == day_type][-k:]
    if not matches:                 # no history: fall back to the mean
        return round(sum(d[2] for d in demand) / len(demand))
    total, weight_sum = 0, 0
    for i, row in enumerate(matches):   # i = 0 for the oldest match
        w = i + 1                       # 1, 2, 3, ... k
        total += row[2] * w
        weight_sum += w
    return round(total / weight_sum)

Java

public static int forecast(List< Demand > demand, String dayType, int k) {
    // collect matching days, then take the last k
    List< Integer > matches = new ArrayList<>();
    for (Demand d : demand)
        if (d.dayType.equals(dayType)) matches.add(d.portions);
    if (matches.isEmpty()) {                       // no history
        int sum = 0;
        for (Demand d : demand) sum += d.portions;
        return Math.round((float) sum / demand.size());
    }
    int from = Math.max(0, matches.size() - k);
    int total = 0, weightSum = 0;
    for (int i = from; i < matches.size(); i++) {
        int w = i - from + 1;                      // 1, 2, 3, ... k
        total += matches.get(i) * w;
        weightSum += w;
    }
    return Math.round((float) total / weightSum);  // cast BEFORE dividing
}
What markers actually reward here

They reward evidence of a decision. "I chose a weighted mean over a plain mean because the client said Monday's demand resembles last Monday more than last Friday; a plain mean over seven days gave a 31 % error on the test set and the weighted mean over four matching days gave 11 %" — that single sentence is worth more than three pages of code listing. Include the code in the appendix, and put the reasoning in the body of the document.

Criterion 4 · Testing and evaluation

The question: has the student proved the solution works, against criteria that came from someone other than themselves?

Mistakes that cap this criterion
  • Only valid data. A test table with six rows that all pass and all use normal input demonstrates nothing. Markers look for at least two rows that were expected to fail gracefully.
  • No expected column. "Input 120 → output 120 ✓" with no expected value written before the run is not a test; it is a record.
  • Bugs found but not described. If a test failed and you fixed something, say what failed, what the cause was, and what you changed. This is the most convincing evidence in the whole IA and students hide it out of embarrassment.
  • Evaluation written as a feature list. See below.

How to use it — the caption rule

Every diagram, mockup and screenshot gets a caption of one sentence saying what the reader should notice, not what the image is. "Figure 7. The forecast dialog, showing the 15 % tolerance band the client specified in criterion 4" is a caption. "Figure 7. Screenshot of program" is not — and a marker scrolling thirty uncaptioned screenshots learns nothing, so the work cannot be credited.

Using the client properly — the difference between a 5 and a 7

This is the highest-leverage part of the whole IA and the one most students skip. A client gives you three things you cannot invent: evidence that the problem is real, criteria you did not choose, and an external verdict.

  1. Interview 1 (week 1, 20 minutes, recorded or minuted). Ask: what do you do now? What goes wrong, and how often? How do you know when it has gone wrong? What would "better" look like, in numbers? What must the solution never do? Do not propose your solution in this meeting — you are there to hear the problem.
  2. Write the success criteria from their answers and send them back. Each criterion should be phrased so that it can only be answered yes or no with evidence.
  3. Get them signed — an email reply saying "yes, those are the right criteria" is enough and is better than a formal signature because it is dated and quotable. Screenshot it into the document.
  4. Interview 2 (week 10, 20 minutes). Demo it. Ask them to attempt a task themselves while you say nothing. Record what they struggle with — that is your best evaluation material.
  5. Ask for a written verdict against each criterion. Two sentences per criterion, in their words, is worth more than two pages of your own evaluation.

Evaluation that actually evaluates

An evaluation is not a list of improvements. It is a verdict on each success criterion, supported by evidence, followed by limitations that are the reason a criterion was only partly met.

Weak (generic)Strong (tied to a criterion)
"I would add a GUI if I had more time.""Criterion 5 (usable by staff with no training) was only partly met: the client completed the order task in 95 seconds without help, but twice pressed Enter at the prompt expecting a menu, which suggests a numbered menu would remove the ambiguity. This is a change to the input layer, not to the model."
"The program could be faster.""Criterion 2 (forecast within 15 % on 4 of 5 days) was met on 4 of 5 days; the fifth was the day after a public holiday, when the matching-day history contained no comparable day. The failure is a property of the data, not of the algorithm, and it would not be fixed by more efficient code."
"It works well and the client was happy.""The client's verdict on criterion 3 was 'it never under-cooks, which is the thing I care about' — but she also noted that the forecast is only useful if the previous week's sales were recorded correctly, which they sometimes are not. This is the most significant limitation, because it is outside the program's control."
"In future I would use a neural network.""A weighted mean was chosen over linear regression because with 30 days of history the regression overfits the two outliers; with a full term of data the regression would likely be better. That is the next thing I would test, and it is a consequence of the data volume, not of the design."
The three-sentence structure that always works

For each limitation: (1) what the criterion required; (2) the evidence that it was only partly met; (3) the specific change that would address it, with a reason why that change is not simply "more time". Then one sentence on what you would do differently in the process — that last sentence is the reflection the criterion asks for, and it must be about your decisions, not your feelings.

Trap — "cited" is not the same as "understood"

Crediting a block you cannot explain does not make it yours; the marker will ask you about it in the viva-style check many schools run, and unexplained code cannot be credited to the algorithm-design criterion anyway. Worked exemplar — OUTLINE ONLY

This is an outline, not a finished IA

What follows is invented and deliberately abbreviated: it shows the shape and the standard of each artefact, not their full length. A real submission would be several times longer at every step. Do not copy it — copying an outline gets you a project with no client, and the client is the part that earns the marks.

The problem

Client: Mrs Adeyemi, manager of the school canteen (410 students, one sitting, 12 dishes per day). Problem: she decides how much of each dish to cook by looking at the same day last week and adjusting by feel; she bins about 40 portions a day (≈ £260 of food per week) and runs out roughly once a fortnight. Existing process: a paper sheet and a spreadsheet that nobody updates.

Success criteria (agreed by email, 19 September)

  1. The tool must store each dish, its category and its lead time (days).
  2. Each evening it must show a forecast, in portions, for each dish for the next school day.
  3. The forecast must not be more than 15 % below the number actually sold, on at least 4 school days out of 5 — under-prediction is the failure that matters to the client.
  4. The manager must be able to enter the actual number sold in under 30 seconds for all 12 dishes.
  5. It must run on the canteen's existing Windows laptop with no internet connection.
  6. No dish may be deleted if historical sales reference it (the manager must not lose history by accident).

Abstraction — keep / discard

Real-world thingKeptDiscardedWhy discarded
Dishname, category, lead time (days), pricerecipe, ingredients, allergen list, photo The forecast is per dish; allergen data is already held by the school office and is a compliance matter, not a demand one.
Daydate, day type (A/B/C timetable), is-holidayweather, menu for other sittings, events The client said weather matters "some days", but there is no free offline weather feed and criterion 5 forbids internet — so the effect is absorbed by using only recent matching days.

Algorithm (pseudocode, one of four)

// Criterion 3: under-prediction is penalised, over-prediction is not.
// So after computing the weighted mean, add a safety margin equal to
// one standard deviation of the last k matching days.

METHOD forecast(demand, dayType, k)
    matches = all rows in demand where row.dayType == dayType
    matches = last k rows of matches
    if matches is empty then
        return mean of all rows            // cold-start fallback
    endif
    total = 0
    weightSum = 0
    for i = 0 to length(matches) - 1
        weight = i + 1                     // oldest match weighs least
        total = total + matches[i].portions * weight
        weightSum = weightSum + weight
    endfor
    mean = total / weightSum
    sd = standardDeviation(matches.portions)
    return round(mean + sd)                // asymmetric: bias upward
ENDMETHOD

Complexity note. The scan over demand is \(O(n)\) in the number of stored day-records; with one year of data \(n \approx 190\), so even a linear scan is instant and a more complex structure would be unjustified. The weighting loop is \(O(k)\) with \(k = 4\), i.e. constant. Dividing by weightSum rather than k is what makes the weights sum to 1.

Evaluation (extract, two of six criteria)

Criterion 3 — met. Over the ten-day trial the forecast was never more than 15 % below actual sales; on nine of ten days it was within 12 %. Under-prediction occurred zero times, which is the property the client cares about most, and it is a direct consequence of adding one standard deviation rather than rounding to the mean.

Criterion 4 — partly met. The manager entered twelve dishes in 95 seconds, not 30. Observation during interview 2 showed the delay was entirely at the "enter portions" prompt, where she pressed Enter expecting a menu. The data entry itself is fast. Limitation L2: the interface assumes the operator knows the prompt format. The fix is a numbered menu with a default value, which is a change to the input layer only — the model and the storage layer are unaffected, so this could be added without retesting the forecast.

Reflection. The most consequential decision was the asymmetric margin; I made it after interview 1, and if I had skipped that interview I would have optimised for absolute error and produced a tool that under-cooks once a week, which the client would have rejected. Next time I would write the test plan before the pseudocode, not after it — two tests in the table were impossible to run because I had not designed for injectable data.

Practice

  1. A student submits a technically excellent 900-line program with a 2-page document. Predict their mark and explain, in terms of the criteria, why the program cannot compensate.

    Show answer
    1. The IA assesses the computational thinking process demonstrated, and every criterion is evidenced by documentation: specification, abstraction, algorithm design, testing, evaluation and reflection.
    2. With two pages there is no client evidence, no signed success criteria, no keep/discard justification, no test table and no evaluation against criteria — so four of the five criteria have nothing to mark, regardless of code quality.
    3. The program is evidence for the algorithm-design and testing criteria only. A 900-line program with no documentation typically lands in the 3–5 range, and the marker has no route to award more.
    Rule of thumb: roughly half your 35 hours is documentation. If your record of tasks says otherwise, your plan is wrong.

  2. Rewrite this success criterion so that it is measurable: "The program should be easy to use."

    Show answer

    "A user who has not seen the program before shall complete the task enter the number of portions sold for all twelve dishes in under 60 seconds, without assistance, on the first attempt."

    Why this works: it names the task, the user's prior experience, the threshold, and the condition (no assistance). It can be tested and the result will be a number, which is what makes the evaluation possible. Note that the number should come from the client, not from you.

  3. Give three pieces of evidence that a student's specification was written after the program.

    Show answer
    1. The specification lists features that no client asked for and that are incidental to the problem (e.g. a settings screen, a colour theme).
    2. The success criteria are all met perfectly, with no borderline cases — real projects always have at least one partly-met criterion.
    3. The "out of scope" list corresponds exactly to features the program lacks, including ones a client would obviously have wanted, and no rationale is given for their exclusion.

  4. Explain why a test table containing only valid data cannot reach the top band, and state the four data types you must include.

    Show answer

    Valid-only testing shows the program works in the case it was written for; it says nothing about the boundaries, about invalid input, or about stress, which is where real defects live. Top-band testing anticipates failure — it is "thinking ahead" written down. Include valid/typical, boundary, erroneous (wrong type or out of range) and extreme data, with an expected outcome recorded before the test is run.

  5. A student writes: "A limitation is that I did not have time to add a GUI." Rewrite it as an evaluation point tied to a success criterion.

    Show answer

    "Criterion 5 (usable by staff with no training) was only partly met: the client completed the entry task unaided in 95 seconds against a target of 60, and the delay was entirely at the text prompt, where she pressed Enter expecting a menu. The limitation is therefore the input style, not the absence of a GUI; a numbered menu with a default value would address it and would not require changes to the model or data layer."

    Difference: the original names a missing feature; the rewrite names a criterion, gives evidence, locates the cause, and proposes a proportionate fix. Only the second is an evaluation.
