Command terms & exam technique

A large share of lost IB marks has nothing to do with not knowing the content. It comes from answering a different question from the one that was asked. This page is the vocabulary of exam questions and the habits that convert knowledge into marks.

Why command terms matter

"Describe the motion of the particle" and "explain the motion of the particle" look almost identical and are marked completely differently. The first wants what happens; the second wants why it happens, with a physical reason. Every mark you lose to a misread instruction is a mark you already earned and threw away. Underline the command term in every question before you write anything.

Command terms

TermWhat the examiner wants
StateGive a specific name, value or answer. No reasoning, no working.
DefineGive the precise meaning, ideally with the defining equation or condition.
ListA sequence of brief answers, no explanation.
CalculateObtain a numerical answer showing the working. Units required.
DetermineSame as calculate, but the route is yours to choose — often implies deriving an intermediate value first.
MeasureObtain a value from an instrument or a graph you have drawn.
DeriveManipulate a relationship to get a new one. Show every step — no leaps.
Show thatReach a given result, showing all the algebra. Because the answer is given, showing working is the whole point — an unsubstantiated answer scores zero even if you write the right line.
HenceUse the result you just found. If you cannot use it, you have probably made an earlier error — the examiner is pointing at the method.
DeduceReach a conclusion from information given or previously established. Short reasoning, not a full derivation.
ExplainGive a reason why. Cause and effect, referencing a law or principle. "Because… therefore…"
DescribeGive a detailed account of what happens. No causes needed.
OutlineA brief summary — the main features only.
AnalyseBreak into parts and examine the relationship between them; in the language subjects, the effect of authorial choices.
CompareGive similarities and differences — and for Paper 2, both must be in the same argument.
ContrastGive differences only.
DiscussOffer a considered, balanced review with a range of arguments, then a reasoned conclusion.
EvaluateMake an appraisal by weighing up strengths and limitations, with a judgement.
JustifyGive valid reasons or evidence to support an answer.
SuggestPropose a solution/hypothesis where there is no single right answer — but it must be physically or logically reasonable.
EstimateObtain an approximate value; an order-of-magnitude argument is often enough.
SketchDraw a graph showing the correct shape and labelled axes with key points (intercepts, maxima). Not to scale, but not wrong either.
PlotMark accurate data points on a graph with properly scaled, labelled axes.
ConstructProduce something to a stated specification (a diagram, a table, an argument).
CommentGive a judgement based on a given statement or result.
DistinguishMake clear the differences between two or more concepts.
To what extentConsider both the merits and the limits of an argument, and reach a balanced, qualified conclusion. Never answer with a flat yes or no.
The three most expensive misreadings
  1. "Show that" answered like "calculate". You write the final line and no algebra. The markscheme awards marks for the working, so you score zero for a correct answer. Always write the steps.
  2. "Explain" answered like "describe". You say what happens instead of why. In physics this costs you the entire second mark of nearly every two-mark explanation question.
  3. "Compare" answered as two separate descriptions. In Paper 2 this is the difference between a band 3 and a band 6. Similarity and difference must sit in the same sentence.

Mathematics: the calculator is a tool, not a strategy

Paper 1 has no calculator — prepare for it specifically

Paper 1 (30 %, 2 hours) is designed so that every question is doable by hand. That means exact values, surds, logs and standard angles. Practise with the calculator switched off from day one, or you will be slower than the paper in the real exam.

GDC skills that are worth marks

Numerical integration and area; equation solver (and knowing when it gives you only one root); normal cdf / inverse normal; binomial and Poisson cdf; regression and r; complex-number arithmetic and roots; matrices for systems of equations; graphing with a sensible window to locate intersections and turning points.

Answer exactly unless told otherwise

Unless the question says otherwise, give exact answers (in terms of π, e, surds, fractions) or three significant figures. Rounding an intermediate value and carrying it forward is the most common silent mark-loss in the whole paper.

Paper 3 strategy

Paper 3 reuses Paper 1/2 content in unfamiliar combinations. The difficulty is recognising the technique, not knowing it. Read the whole question before writing: part (a) is usually an easy "show that" designed to hand you the method for part (d).

Physics: the data booklet is on the desk

How to use the data booklet under time pressure
  1. Know its shape. You should be able to find any relationship within five seconds. Practise by opening it to a random page and naming the section.
  2. The booklet gives relationships, not judgement. It will not tell you which relationship to use, or whether the answer means anything. That is the skill being tested.
  3. Units are your checking tool. If your answer has the wrong units, the substitution was wrong — fix it before moving on.
  4. Paper 1B is data analysis. You are given real data with uncertainties and asked to process it. Practise: tabulate with units in headers, calculate percentage uncertainties, plot, find a gradient with its uncertainty, compare to a literature value.

Classify your errors — the pattern tells you what to study

Tag each entry: content (didn't know it), method (knew it, chose wrong route), arithmetic (slip), reading (misread the question), or time (ran out). After twenty entries the pattern is obvious. Content errors mean go back to the page. Reading errors mean slow down and underline. Arithmetic errors mean write more steps — the markscheme rewards them anyway.

Exam-day tactics

First five minutes

Read the whole paper. Mark the questions you know you can do and do those first. Confidence early changes how you perform on the hard ones.

One mark ≈ one minute

A six-mark question gets about six minutes. If you are past that, write down your best partial attempt and move on — partial credit is real credit and the next question may be free marks.

Never leave a blank

For "show that" questions, write the first line you are sure of. For explanations, write the relevant law. Marks are awarded for correct intermediate working even when you do not finish.

Check units, signs and significant figures

Three things, thirty seconds, at the end of every question. This is the cheapest mark recovery available anywhere in the exam.

Sanity-check every number

Is it the right order of magnitude? Is the sign what the physics says? A car at 4 000 m s⁻¹ and an electron at 2 m s⁻¹ are both obviously wrong, and both are handed in every year.

In the language papers, plan before writing

Ten minutes of planning in Paper 2 is not lost time — an unplanned comparative essay loses more marks to incoherence than to any lack of knowledge.

A note on grade boundaries

Grade boundaries move year to year and usually sit lower than students expect — typically 65–75 % for a 7 at HL in the sciences and maths, depending on the session. That means you do not need perfection; you need to bank the marks you can reliably get and stop bleeding easy ones. Aim for "no unforced errors" before you aim for the hardest question on the paper.
