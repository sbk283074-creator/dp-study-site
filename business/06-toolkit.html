The business management toolkit — the SL tools

The IB does not ask you to invent analytical frameworks. It publishes a prescribed list of fifteen tools, eight of which you must be able to use at SL — and "use" is the operative word. A candidate who can define SWOT analysis scores almost nothing; a candidate who runs it on the business in the stimulus, produces a filled-in grid, and then says what the grid means and where it misleads, scores the top band. This page treats all eight SL tools as procedures to be executed rather than definitions to be recalled.

Prescribed: 15 tools SL: 8 tools HL only: 7 extra Assessed at AO2 / AO4 HL only — skip: force field, Gantt, Hofstede, Porter generic, contribution costing, CPA, regression
Why this list exists

Before 2024 the syllabus said things like "analyse using appropriate tools and techniques". That is an unfair question, because the set of possible answers is unbounded — a candidate cannot know whether the examiner wants a SWOT, a Pest, a Boston matrix or a fishbone diagram, and a whole markband was decided by a guess. The current guide closes that: it names fifteen tools. So when a question now says "using an appropriate tool from the business management toolkit, analyse…", the word "appropriate" has a bounded answer set of fifteen, of which you only need eight. This is the single biggest free gift in the specification, and the cost of receiving it is that you must be able to actually operate each tool, not just name it.

What the toolkit is, and the golden rule

A tool is a structured way of converting messy information about a business into a small number of claims you can act on. Every tool on the list does the same three things: it forces you to look in specific places (a SWOT grid makes you check internal/external and helpful/harmful, so you cannot forget external threats), it compresses a case study into one page, and it produces an output that has to be interpreted — a quadrant, a number, a recommendation.

Notice what none of them do: none of them produce the answer by themselves. A Boston matrix that places a product in the "dog" quadrant has not told you to divest; it has told you where the product sits, and you still have to decide, using facts from the case, whether divestment is right. That is why the guide's assessment objectives treat tool use as AO2 (application) and AO4 (analysis / quantitative reasoning), never as AO1.

The golden rule of this whole page

The tool is the starting point of the answer, not the answer. A finished grid, matrix or tree is worth about a third of the marks; the remaining two-thirds sit in (i) the sentence that says what the output means for this business, (ii) the decision or recommendation you commit to, and (iii) the limitations you acknowledge. Write this at the top of your notes: draw → apply → conclude → limit. Four moves, every time, for every tool.

Why a physicist needs to be told this explicitly

You are coming from a subject where knowing the theory really is most of the battle. In physics, if you can state Faraday's law and set up the integral correctly, you have done the hard part; the arithmetic afterwards is routine. Business management inverts that. The toolkit is deliberately simple — a SWOT grid is a two-by-two table, and you mastered harder structures in year one of physics. The difficulty is that once the grid is drawn, the examiner asks a question that physics never asks: so what? And there is no formula for the answer.

So the transferable skill is not "learn eight frameworks". It is: given a business with a problem, decide which of eight frameworks extracts the most useful structure, run it, and defend both the output and the framework's blind spots. The last part — the blind spots — is what your physics training has not prepared you for, because in physics the model's domain of applicability is usually stated for you. Here you have to state it yourself, and doing so is worth marks.

Where this page sits

The toolkit is not a unit. It is a layer that sits across all five content units and is deployed inside them. Unit 1 (Introduction to business management) uses STEEPLE and SWOT; Unit 2 (Human resource management) uses decision trees and force-field thinking in change management; Unit 3 (Finance and accounts) owns the quantitative material; Unit 4 (Marketing) uses Ansoff, the BCG matrix and the four Ps; Unit 5 (Operations management) uses circular business models and decision trees. Common trap — HL contamination

The following seven tools are HL only and you will see them in HL textbooks, HL past papers, revision websites and YouTube: force field analysis, Gantt charts, Hofstede's cultural dimensions, Porter's generic strategies, contribution (make-or-buy, contribution costing, absorption costing), critical path analysis, and simple linear regression. HL only If you use one in an SL answer you will not be penalised for knowing it, but you will lose the marks that were allocated to the tool the question was fishing for, and you may run out of time writing about a framework the markscheme never mentions. Recognise them on sight so you can skip them.

Also not toolkit tools — do not double-count

Some things that feel like tools are syllabus content and are examined and taught on their own pages: ratio analysis and cash-flow forecasting and investment appraisal (payback period and ARR) live in Unit 3; break-even analysis lives in Unit 5; budgets are HL only. They are legitimate things to bring into an answer, but they are not "a tool from the business management toolkit" in the sense the examiner means, so never let one of them be your whole answer to a toolkit question.

Tool 1 · SWOT analysis

What it is. A four-box grid that sorts what you know about a business along two axes at once: internal versus external (is this about the business itself, or about the world it operates in?) and helpful versus harmful (does it help the business achieve its objectives, or hinder it?). The crossing gives strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats.

The question it answers: "Given everything in this case study, where does this business actually stand today?" It is a position audit, not a plan. That distinction matters, because the commonest misuse is treating a SWOT as if it were a strategy.

How to use it — the procedure
  1. Draw the grid with the axes labelled and keep the internal/external line straight. Strengths and weaknesses are internal — things the business controls: brand, patents, cash, skilled staff, location, capacity, culture, cost base. Opportunities and threats are external — things it does not control: demographic change, a rival's move, regulation, exchange rates, technology, input prices.
  2. Mine the stimulus in a fixed order. Work through the case once for internal facts and once for external facts, underlining as you go. Do not write into the boxes as you read — that is how points end up in the wrong quadrant.
  3. Write each point as a fact plus a consequence. "Strong brand" is a label; "brand ranked second in a national survey, allowing a 12% price premium over the market leader's own-label line" is a strength with a mechanism attached. Every box should contain at least two such points.
  4. Force a comparison against rivals. A strength is only a strength relative to competitors. "We have a website" was a strength in 1998 and is table stakes now. Where the case gives you a competitor, say explicitly whose strength is bigger.
  5. Rank, do not list. Pick the two or three factors that actually drive this case and say why they dominate the others. An unranked twelve-item SWOT signals that you have not decided anything.
  6. Go beyond the grid with matching (TOWS). The payoff from a SWOT comes from combining boxes: a strength paired with an opportunity is the basis of an offensive strategy; a weakness paired with a threat is a vulnerability that needs either fixing or avoiding. This is the step that turns a 3–4 mark grid into a 7–8 mark analysis.
  7. Close with a judgement and a limitation. One sentence: what should the business do, given this grid — and what does the grid not tell it?

Worked application: a Chinese EV battery firm considering Europe

Take a realistic composite — call it Shenjiang Power Co., a mid-sized Chinese manufacturer of lithium-iron-phosphate battery cells that currently sells almost entirely to domestic electric-vehicle makers. It is evaluating whether to build a plant in Central Europe to supply European carmakers. Run the grid.

Strengths (internal, helpful)Weaknesses (internal, harmful)
LFP cell chemistry know-how built over a decade, giving a cost per kilowatt-hour below most European cell start-ups. Established volume production experience — it already ships at scale to several domestic vehicle platforms, so the yield and quality learning curve has been climbed at home. No brand recognition with European vehicle buyers, who buy on safety certification and track record rather than price alone. Limited experience of EU-type labour relations and works councils. Balance sheet already committed to domestic capacity expansion, so new investment would need external finance. Almost no European after-sales or warranty infrastructure.
Opportunities (external, helpful)Threats (external, harmful)
European carbon regulation and fleet-emissions penalties are pushing carmakers towards electrification faster than local cell supply can grow, leaving a genuine supply gap. Local-content requirements mean a European-built cell may be treated more favourably than an imported one. Several European manufacturers are actively seeking a second source to reduce dependence on Korean and Japanese suppliers. Policy risk in both directions: local-content rules and possible trade measures on Chinese-linked battery supply can change with an election. European energy costs have been volatile and structurally higher than the firm's domestic costs, which attacks the cost advantage that is its main strength. Incumbent Korean and Japanese cell makers are simultaneously building European capacity, and a strong currency movement could wipe out the remaining cost gap.

Now the matching — the step most candidates skip. Pair S with O: the cost advantage plus the European supply gap plus local-content preference is a genuine offensive case for building. Pair W with T: no brand recognition combined with incumbents arriving at the same time is the real danger — the firm may build capacity into a market where it is the least-preferred supplier. That pairing does not say "don't build"; it says the plant is only defensible if it is built alongside a secured anchor customer, i.e. a long-term off-take agreement signed before construction starts. That is a conclusion, and it came out of the tool rather than out of the candidate's prior opinion.

What the marks depend on

In a 10-mark "conduct a SWOT analysis" question, roughly 2 marks sit on the grid being correct in structure and correctly sorted, 3–4 sit on the points being drawn from this stimulus and developed, 2–3 sit on the conclusion or matching, and 1–2 on limitations. If you draw the grid and stop, you have capped yourself. The phrase that signals you have moved to the top bands is short: "This matters because…" after each point, and "the grid therefore suggests…" at the end.

Limitations — what SWOT cannot tell you

  • It is static. It photographs the business on one day. In a fast-moving market, a SWOT done in January can be wrong by June — which is why examiners like to ask "how useful is a SWOT analysis to this business?" and expect you to say "less useful the faster the industry changes".
  • It does not weight or rank. Eleven boxes items of equal visual size imply eleven equally important factors, which is almost never true. Nothing in the tool tells you which factor dominates.
  • It is subjective. Two analysts given the same case study produce different grids, because "strength" is a judgement. That makes it usable for structuring thought but useless as evidence.
  • Classification is genuinely ambiguous. A skilled workforce is a strength; if those workers can be poached, the same fact is arguably a threat. Weak supplier power is an external opportunity, not a strength — candidates get this wrong constantly.
  • It generates options, not choices. SWOT will happily tell you that you have a strong brand and a growing market; it will not tell you whether to spend on advertising, on capacity, or on acquisition. That is what decision trees and investment appraisal are for.
Common trap

The single most common misuse is producing a list of generic points that would fit any business in any country — "good location", "motivated workforce", "competition is increasing". Those are worthless because they contain no information about this firm. The second most common is putting external facts in the strengths box: a growing market is an opportunity, never a strength, because the business does not own it. Third is writing a SWOT when the question asked for a decision — if the stem says "recommend", a SWOT can inform the answer but cannot be the answer.

Tool 2 · Ansoff matrix

What it is. A 2×2 that classifies growth strategies by two questions: is the product new or existing? and is the market new or existing? The four resulting cells are market penetration, product development, market development and diversification.

The question it answers: "In which direction should this business grow, and how much risk does that direction carry?" The risk gradient is the part that earns marks, and it is built into the structure: the further you move from what the business already knows — its product and its market — the less it knows and the higher the chance of failure.

How to use it — the procedure
  1. Fix the definitions of "product" and "market" for this case first. Is a new flavour a new product? Is a new province a new market? State your level of analysis — the same decision can sit in different cells depending on how you draw the boundaries, and saying so is a mark.
  2. Place the current position. Where is the business now, before any strategy is adopted? Usually penetration.
  3. Place each option under discussion in the correct cell, naming it — "launching the sugar-free variant in existing supermarkets is product development".
  4. Attach the risk statement to each cell, and rank the options by risk.
  5. Test each option against the business's actual resources — does it have the R&D, the cash, the distribution, the brand permission to do it? A quadrant does not tell you whether the firm is able to pursue a strategy, only how risky it is.
  6. Commit. Recommend one, justify it against the case's constraints, and say what would have to be true for a different cell to be better.

Worked application: a Chinese tea-drink chain

Take a realistic case of the kind the IB loves: a Chinese freshly-made tea drink chain — the category occupied by brands such as Mixue, Chayan Yuese and HEYTEA — with several thousand outlets in China, a low price point, and a franchise-heavy model. It is considering four growth options.

Conclusion, and how you get to it. The matrix alone does not pick a winner — it orders the options by how much the business has to learn. The chain's genuine asset is its supply chain and franchise management system, both of which are market-development assets: they travel geographically better than they travel across categories. So the analysis supports Southeast Asian expansion as the primary strategy, with the bottled line as a secondary, smaller bet, and it argues against the coffee chain. Then the limitation: the matrix says nothing about how fast to expand, how to finance it, or whether the franchisees can be recruited — those are Unit 3 and Unit 2 questions.

Limitations — what Ansoff cannot tell you

  • It ignores competition. Market penetration looks safe in the matrix, but if three rivals are penetrating simultaneously, a price war can make it the most destructive option available. The tool assumes the business's risk depends only on its own newness, which is false.
  • "New" is undefined. Is a reformulation a new product? Is an adjacent province a new market? Without stated boundaries the classification is arbitrary, and an arbitrary classification cannot carry a conclusion.
  • It says nothing about feasibility or finance. Diversification is risky, not impossible — and a well-financed firm with a strong balance sheet may rationally choose it. The matrix has no place to record "we can afford to be wrong".
  • It assumes growth is the objective. A business in crisis may need consolidation, retrenchment or divestment; none of those appear anywhere on the matrix.
  • Risk is assumed, not measured. The gradient is a heuristic, not a probability. If you need numbers, use a decision tree instead.
Common trap

Mislabelling market development as diversification (a common error when a business "enters a new country" — the market is new but the product is not, so it is market development), and calling any new product "diversification" when it is sold to existing customers (that is product development). The second trap is describing the four cells when the question asked you to recommend — the cells are vocabulary; the recommendation is the marks.

Tool 3 · STEEPLE analysis

Read this before anything else on this page

The prescribed tool is STEEPLE, with seven letters: Social, Technological, Economic, Environmental, Political, Legal and Ethical. It is not PEST and it is not PESTLE. If you write "PESTLE analysis" in an exam answer you have used a six-letter version of a seven-letter tool, you have silently dropped either the environmental or the ethical dimension, and — more damagingly — you have signalled to the examiner that you are working from a pre-2024 or non-prescribed source. Use the acronym as the guide gives it, spell out all seven letters, and use each letter as a heading in your answer. This is a memorisation point with a real mark attached to it.

What it is. A structured scan of the external environment, organised into seven categories. It is the external half of a SWOT analysis, done properly and in depth — which is why the two tools pair so naturally and why examiners often accept a STEEPLE feeding into a SWOT.

The question it answers: "What changes outside this business will affect it over the next few years, and do those changes represent opportunities to be seized or threats to be managed?"

How to use it — the procedure
  1. Write the seven headings down the page before you read anything. This converts the task from "think of factors" into "fill seven slots", which is much easier under time pressure and guarantees you do not miss the environmental or ethical dimension — the two most commonly omitted.
  2. Work through the stimulus marking every external fact with its letter. Anything the business does not control is external. Anything it does control is not a STEEPLE factor at all — park it for SWOT.
  3. For each factor, complete the chain: change → effect on this business → opportunity or threat. "Inflation is 6%" is not a point. "At 6% inflation, real household incomes fall, so demand shifts from the firm's premium line to its value line — a threat to margin and an opportunity for the value range" is a point.
  4. Classify each as opportunity or threat, and mark probability and impact. Not all factors are equal; say which two dominate.
  5. Identify interactions. STEEPLE factors rarely act alone: a technological change (automation) interacting with a legal change (minimum wage rise) can make a labour-intensive model uncompetitive far faster than either would alone. Interactions are top-band material.
  6. Conclude with the implication for strategy and state what the analysis cannot predict.

Limitations — what STEEPLE cannot tell you

  • It is overwhelming by design. Seven categories generate a very long list; without ranking and interaction analysis you produce noise. Length is not analysis.
  • It is only the external half. A business can face a benign environment and still fail because of internal weaknesses. STEEPLE must be paired with something internal, usually SWOT.
  • Data goes stale fast. Exchange rates, policy and technology shift within months, so a STEEPLE is a snapshot with a short shelf life.
  • Categories overlap. A carbon tax is environmental in purpose, political in origin and legal in effect. Where you file it matters less than that you trace its effect; do not waste exam time arguing with yourself about classification.
  • It does not quantify. "Threat" is qualitative. If you need magnitude, that is a statistics or decision-tree question.
  • It cannot predict discontinuous change. STEEPLE extrapolates visible trends; it did not predict a pandemic, and it will not predict the next Black Swan. Saying so is a legitimate evaluation point.
Common trap

Besides the PESTLE error: (a) filing internal facts as external — "our staff are well trained" is a strength, not a social factor; (b) listing a factor with no link to the business ("unemployment is 5%"), which earns nothing; (c) omitting the environmental and ethical dimensions entirely, which is the most common structural error in scripts; (d) treating it as a substitute for a SWOT when the question asked for one.

Tool 4 · Boston Consulting Group (BCG) matrix

What it is. A portfolio tool: instead of analysing a single product, it analyses a business with several products (or strategic business units), placing each one on two axes — market growth rate (how attractive is the market?) on the vertical, and relative market share (how strong are we in it?) on the horizontal. The four cells are stars, cash cows, question marks and dogs.

The question it answers: "Across our range of products, which should be funded, which should be milked for cash, which need a decision, and which should be dropped?" It is fundamentally a cash-flow allocation tool disguised as a diagram.

Get the axes right

The horizontal axis is relative market share — your share divided by the share of your largest competitor — not your share in absolute percentage terms. A 20% share is dominant if the leader has 25% and weak if the leader has 60%. The vertical axis is the rate of growth of the market, not the growth of your own sales. Getting either of these wrong destroys the whole analysis, and it is a favourite way for examiners to separate candidates.

How to use it — the procedure
  1. Define the unit of analysis. A product, a product line, or a strategic business unit? Say which, and be consistent.
  2. Get the two numbers for each product from the stimulus: relative market share (yours divided by the leader's) and the market's growth rate. If the case only gives you absolute share and a leader's share, compute the ratio — this is often where the quantitative mark sits.
  3. Decide where the axis divides high from low. Usually relative share of 1.0 (i.e. you are the leader) and a market growth rate compared with, say, GDP growth or a stated threshold.
  4. Plot each product as a circle sized by its share of total sales.
  5. Read the cash implication of each cell — this is the analysis: stars consume cash (they need investment to hold share in a growing market), cows generate it, question marks consume a lot and may return nothing, dogs tie up cash and management attention.
  6. Diagnose portfolio balance. A firm with only dogs and question marks has no internal source of cash; a firm with only cows is profitable today and dead in five years. Say which problem this business has.
  7. Recommend an action per product — build, hold, harvest or divest — and then state the limitations.

The strategic risk the matrix exposes is that the question mark (vehicles) is big enough in absolute cash terms to drain the cow faster than the star can replace it. That is a real, examinable insight and it emerges only because you asked about cash flows rather than about products. Limitations — what the BCG matrix cannot tell you

  • Only two variables. Market attractiveness and competitive strength are reduced to one proxy each. A high-growth market with brutal price competition and terrible margins looks attractive on the matrix — this is its most serious flaw and the one examiners reward most.
  • High share does not mean high profit. The matrix assumes an experience-curve link between cumulative volume and cost. In some industries — luxury goods, craft production, software with network effects — this simply does not hold.
  • It ignores synergies. A dog may be essential because it completes the range, brings customers in, or shares a platform with a star. Divesting on matrix logic alone can damage the rest.
  • Defining "the market" is arbitrary and changes the answer completely: a global share of 2% becomes a leading share of 40% if the market is redefined as "mid-price smartphones in one province".
  • It is static and assumes cash is the only constraint. It assumes cash is the scarce resource. A firm with plenty of cash but a shortage of engineers will misallocate using this logic.
  • Data problems. Reliable market growth and competitor share data is often unavailable, and for private companies or small firms it may be guesswork.
Common trap

Plotting absolute market share on the horizontal axis, and plotting the firm's own sales growth instead of the market's growth. The second most common error is treating the four labels as conclusions — writing "it is a dog, therefore divest" without asking whether the case gives a reason to keep it. And the third: forgetting bubble size, which is what makes the tool a portfolio tool rather than four separate judgements.

Tool 5 · The business plan

What it is. A written document that sets out what a new or changed venture will do, why it will work, who will run it, and what money it needs and when. Unlike the other seven tools, its output is not a diagram — it is a document, and that difference matters: the business plan is the one toolkit tool whose purpose is to persuade an external party, normally a bank, an investor or a grant panel.

The question it answers: "Is this venture coherent enough, and its numbers plausible enough, that someone should fund it?" Notice the word coherent — a business plan's real function is to force the entrepreneur to confront every part of the venture at once, so that contradictions surface. A plan that says "we will be the premium option" and then budgets for below-cost pricing has failed at its primary job.

Executive summary

Written last, read first. One or two pages: what the business does, why it will work, how much money is needed, and what the funder gets. Most readers decide here.

Business description and objectives

Legal form, location, mission, and SMART objectives. Links directly to Unit 1.

Market analysis

Target segment, market size and growth, customer profile, competitors. This is where STEEPLE and SWOT evidence belongs — a plan with market research in it is credible; one with assertions is not.

Marketing plan

The marketing mix extended: product, price, place, promotion, plus people, process and physical evidence for services. See Unit 4.

Operations plan

Premises, capacity, suppliers, production method, stock control, quality. See Unit 5.

Management and organisation

Who does what, their relevant experience, the organisational structure. Funders back teams more readily than ideas, because teams can change the idea.

Financial plan

Start-up costs, a cash flow forecast (monthly for at least twelve months), a break-even calculation, projected income statement and balance sheet, sources of finance and the amount requested. See Unit 3.

Risks and contingency

What could go wrong, its likelihood and impact, and what would be done. Omitting this is the clearest signal of an amateur plan.

How to use it — the procedure
  1. Start with the reader. A bank wants security and the ability to service debt; an equity investor wants growth and an exit; a grant panel wants jobs or environmental benefit. The same venture produces three different plans.
  2. Build it from evidence — market research, competitor observation, supplier quotes. Every number should be traceable to a source, and you should state the assumption behind each one.
  3. Make the financials hang together with the narrative. If you plan to sell 4,000 units in month one, the operations plan must show capacity for 4,000 units and the cash plan must fund the materials for 4,000 units. Internal consistency is the test.
  4. Forecast monthly for the first year, not annually. Annual figures hide the cash trough that kills new businesses — the phenomenon covered on the finance page.
  5. Include a break-even figure and state the margin of safety. It shows you understand how far sales can fall before the venture fails.
  6. Write the risk section honestly, with a named contingency for each major risk.
  7. Write the executive summary last, and keep it to one page.

Worked application: a student start-up seeking a bank loan

A realistic IB-scale case: two graduates want to open a specialty coffee and study-space business near a university campus in a Chinese second-tier city. They need ¥400,000: fit-out, equipment, three months' rent deposit, and working capital for the first two months of trading. Their plan contains the following.

Limitations — what a business plan cannot tell you

  • It is a forecast, and forecasts are wrong. The numbers are built on assumptions about demand, prices and costs that will not all hold. A plan that is right in every structural respect can still fail on one wrong assumption.
  • Presentation can substitute for substance. A glossy document with weak evidence can persuade, and a rigorous plan with poor writing can be rejected. This is a genuine weakness of the tool, not of the business.
  • It is expensive and slow to produce properly — a real constraint for a small entrepreneur, and a reason some businesses succeed without one.
  • It can create false confidence and rigidity. Founders fall in love with the plan and keep executing it after the market has told them otherwise.
  • It cannot predict competitor reaction. The plan assumes the market stays still while you execute. It rarely does.
Common trap

Treating the business plan as a list of section headings to memorise. In an exam, the marks are never for naming the eight sections; they are for saying why a funder wants each one and what happens to the venture if it is missing. The second trap is putting a plan's financial projections forward as if they were facts — always attach the assumption.

Tool 6 · Decision trees

What it is. A diagram of a decision under uncertainty. Squares are decision points (things the business chooses), circles are chance nodes (things the business cannot control), and branches carry probabilities and payoffs. Working from right to left, you calculate the expected monetary value (EMV) at each chance node and then choose the branch with the best expected value.

The question it answers: "Given several options and several possible futures with known (or estimated) probabilities, which option is worth most on average?" Note the italicised qualifier — it is where the entire evaluation of this tool lives.

How to use it — the procedure
  1. Draw left to right. Start with a decision node, add one branch per option, then add a chance node at the end of each branch with one outcome branch per possible future state.
  2. Put the probability on each chance branch. The probabilities leaving any single chance node must sum to 1. Check this explicitly — it is a mark and a common error.
  3. Put the net payoff at the end of each terminal branch, in money terms, net of any investment cost on that branch. Label the units.
  4. Roll back. At each chance node: EMV = Σ (probability × payoff). Write the EMV next to the node.
  5. Choose at each decision node the branch with the highest EMV, and cross off the others (literally draw a double line through them in the exam).
  6. Subtract any investment cost attached to a branch, if you have not already netted it off, and state the final net expected gain.
  7. Evaluate. The recommendation is conditional on the probability estimates. Test how wrong they would have to be to change the decision, and name what the tree ignores.

A full worked expected-value calculation

A manufacturer must decide whether to build a new plant or upgrade the existing one. Demand will turn out to be either high or low. The payoffs below are net profits over five years, already net of the investment cost on each branch. Probabilities come from the sales director's judgement and market research.

StepCalculationResult
1. EMV at the top chance node (build new plant)(0.6 × ¥42.0 m) + (0.4 × −¥8.0 m) = ¥25.2 m − ¥3.2 m¥22.0 m
2. EMV at the bottom chance node (upgrade existing)(0.6 × ¥18.0 m) + (0.4 × ¥6.0 m) = ¥10.8 m + ¥2.4 m¥13.2 m
3. Choose at the decision node¥22.0 m > ¥13.2 mBuild the new plant
4. Net expected gain over the next-best option¥22.0 m − ¥13.2 m¥8.8 m
The sentence that turns 4 marks into 8

Having computed ¥22.0 m, most candidates stop. The evaluation is this: the recommendation is conditional on the probability estimate of 0.6. Let p be the probability of high demand. Then build gives 42p − 8(1 − p) = 50p − 8, and upgrade gives 18p + 6(1 − p) = 12p + 6. Setting them equal: 50p − 8 = 12p + 6, so 38p = 14 and p = 0.368. Below a 36.8% chance of high demand, upgrading becomes the better decision. That single sentence is worth more than the whole calculation, because it tells the reader how robust the recommendation is — and because at p = 0.6 the decision is comfortable while at p = 0.4 it would flip. Sensitivity analysis like this is exactly what "evaluate" means in a 10-mark question.

Worked application: a Hong Kong–listed retailer choosing a store format

A regional convenience-store operator must choose between opening twenty large-format stores in new suburban districts or sixty small kiosks in existing dense districts. Large format: high payoff if a new residential district fills up, poor if occupancy is slow. Kiosks: modest but more certain. The tree gives an EMV advantage to the large-format rollout of, say, ¥8.8 m — but the board also knows that a bad outcome on the large format would breach a loan covenant, whereas the worst kiosk outcome would not. So the company may rationally reject the higher-EMV option. The tree's output is a recommendation, not a decision, and explaining that gap is the difference between applying the tool and being governed by it.

Limitations — what decision trees cannot tell you

  • Probabilities are usually estimates, often guesses. The output is only as good as the input, and the input is frequently a manager's opinion dressed as a number. This is the tool's central weakness.
  • Expected value ignores risk attitude. EMV treats a 1% chance of losing ¥100 m as equivalent to a certain loss of ¥1 m. For a small business, one of those is survivable and the other is not. The tree has no way to express "we cannot afford to be wrong".
  • It ignores non-quantifiable factors — brand damage, staff morale, strategic positioning, the reaction of competitors. These can dominate in reality and are invisible in the calculation.
  • Real decisions are sequential, and trees get complicated fast. With several stages the number of branches explodes, and the diagram stops being a simplification.
  • It assumes the probabilities are unaffected by the decision. In reality, entering a market can provoke a competitor response that changes the probabilities you were given.
  • Expected value is an average over repeats. Most strategic decisions are one-off; you will experience one branch, not the average.
Common trap

Probabilities from a single chance node that do not add to 1. Forgetting to subtract the investment cost, or subtracting it twice. Averaging the payoffs without weighting by probability (which is not an EMV at all). And the biggest one: writing "the business should build because EMV is higher" with no sensitivity analysis and no mention of risk attitude — that is a solid 4–6 and never a 9–10.

Tool 7 · Descriptive statistics

What it is. The set of techniques for summarising a body of data so that it can be understood and communicated: measures of central tendency (mean, median, mode), measures of dispersion (range, interquartile range, standard deviation), and the graphical forms — bar charts, pie charts, line graphs, infographics — that carry the same information to a reader who will not look at a table.

The question it answers: "What is this data actually telling us, how much spread is there behind that summary, and can we trust it?"

Why this is your easiest mark and your most dangerous one

You can compute every statistic on this page faster than most candidates can read the question. That is a trap, not an advantage, for two reasons. First, the arithmetic in an IB business question is usually worth one or two marks out of eight or ten; the rest sit in the interpretation. Second, because the arithmetic is easy, candidates do it and stop — producing "the mean is 33.3" and collecting one mark out of four. Train yourself to write, automatically, the sentence after the number: what does this mean for the business, and what does it hide?

The six statistics you must know, with the interpretation attached

StatisticFormulaWhat it tells the businessWhat it hides
Mean (arithmetic average) x̄ = Σx ÷ n, where Σx is the sum of all values and n is the number of values The "fair share" figure — useful for budgeting and forecasting because it uses every data point and totals reconcile: mean × n = total. Everything about shape. One extreme value drags it. A mean daily order count of 33 can be produced by steady trade or by one freak day plus nineteen quiet ones.
Median The middle value when the data is sorted. Position = (n + 1) ÷ 2; if that falls between two values, average them. The typical case. Because it ignores the size of extremes, it is the honest summary when data is skewed — which business data usually is. The total. You cannot reconstruct revenue from a median, so it is useless for budgeting.
Mode The most frequent value (or, for grouped data, the modal class). The most common single case — genuinely useful for inventory: "the most frequently ordered size". Almost everything. With many distinct values there may be no mode at all; with grouped data the mode depends on how you chose the class widths.
Range Range = largest value − smallest value A one-glance measure of spread — how much variation the business has to cope with. Whether the spread is normal or caused by one freak event. It is the least robust statistic on this page.
Quartiles and interquartile range Q1 = median of the lower half, Q3 = median of the upper half, IQR = Q3 − Q1 The spread of the middle 50% of observations — which is what operations actually has to plan for. Also gives an outlier test: values below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR deserve investigation. The tails — which is sometimes exactly the point, since the tails are where the problems are.
Standard deviation σ = √( Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ n )   population form;   s = √( Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) )   sample form Average distance of the data from the mean, in the original units. Small σ = consistent and predictable (good for staffing, stock and quality control); large σ = volatile and risky. Direction and shape. Two data sets can have identical σ and completely different distributions. And σ is not comparable between data sets with different means unless you scale it.
Population or sample? — be honest, not agonised

Your calculator has two buttons, usually labelled σn and σn−1. The first divides by n, the second by n − 1. The n − 1 version is the better estimate when your data is a sample from a larger population, which in business it almost always is. The IB's business management course does not expect you to defend the choice — it expects you to state which you used and to interpret the result. Do that and move on.

A worked data set, all the way through

A meal-delivery kitchen records the number of orders it fulfilled on each of twenty trading days:

Day1234567891011121314151617181920
Orders1215202122242526283031333536384042454895

Step 1 — sort it. The values above are already in ascending order; never skip this step in an exam, because the median, quartiles and range are all read off the sorted list.

Step 2 — the mean. Σx = 666 and n = 20, so x̄ = 666 ÷ 20 = 33.3 orders.

Step 3 — the median. With n = 20 (even), the median sits between the 10th and 11th values, which are 30 and 31, so the median = 30.5 orders.

Step 4 — the mode. Every value is different, so there is no single mode. Grouping is more useful: under 20 → 2 days, 20–29 → 7 days, 30–39 → 6 days, 40–49 → 4 days, 50 and over → 1 day. The modal class is 20–29 orders.

Step 5 — range. 95 − 12 = 83 orders.

Step 6 — quartiles and IQR. Lower half (first ten values): 12, 15, 20, 21, 22, 24, 25, 26, 28, 30 → Q1 = (22 + 24) ÷ 2 = 23. Upper half (last ten): 31, 33, 35, 36, 38, 40, 42, 45, 48, 95 → Q3 = (38 + 40) ÷ 2 = 39. So IQR = 39 − 23 = 16 orders. The upper outlier fence is Q3 + 1.5 × IQR = 39 + 24 = 63, so day 20's 95 orders is an outlier and should be investigated before any decision is based on it.

Step 7 — standard deviation. Using the n divisor: variance = (Σx² ÷ n) − x̄² = (27,988 ÷ 20) − 33.3² = 1,399.4 − 1,108.89 = 290.51, so σ = √290.51 ≈ 17.0 orders. Using the n − 1 divisor you would get about 17.5; state which you used.

The interpretation — this is the answer

Now write the paragraph that earns the marks. "Mean daily demand is 33.3 orders, but the median is 30.5 and the mean sits above the median because of a single exceptional day of 95 orders, which is above the upper outlier fence of 63 and should be checked — it is plausibly a bulk corporate order or a data-entry error rather than a repeatable day. The standard deviation of 17.0 orders is more than half the mean, so day-to-day demand is highly variable; the interquartile range of 16 shows that even the middle half of days spans 23 to 39 orders. The operational implication is that staffing and ingredient purchasing cannot be planned on the average: planning on the mean leaves the kitchen under-prepared on roughly half of all days. The kitchen should staff to around the third quartile of 39 orders, hold ingredient cover for that level, and re-forecast after confirming whether the 95-order day is repeatable."

Notice the structure: calculate → compare mean with median → flag the outlier → convert σ into a business statement → recommend an action that follows from the spread, not from the average. That is a top-band answer, and none of the arithmetic in it is harder than a square root.

The robustness demonstration. Suppose the 95 is confirmed as a data-entry error and the true value was 60. The mean falls from 33.3 to 31.4 and the standard deviation falls from 17.0 to about 11.7 — a 30% collapse in measured volatility from correcting one cell. The median stays at 30.5 and the quartiles do not move at all. That contrast is the single most useful thing to know about these statistics: the mean and standard deviation are sensitive to extreme values; the median and IQR are not. When a question gives you skewed data, reach for the median and IQR and say why.

Practice set A — statistics in context

  1. [AO4, 4 marks] A café records these daily sales figures (¥00) over eight days: 42, 38, 41, 39, 40, 37, 43, 160. Calculate the mean and the median, and explain which is the better summary for the café manager and why.

    Show answer
    1. Sort the data: 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 160.
    2. Mean: Σx = 37 + 38 + 39 + 40 + 41 + 42 + 43 + 160 = 440; n = 8; mean = 440 ÷ 8 = ¥5,500 (55 in ¥00 units).
    3. Median: n is even, so the median is the mean of the 4th and 5th values: (40 + 41) ÷ 2 = ¥4,050 (40.5 in ¥00 units).
    4. Which is better: the median, decisively. Seven of the eight days lie between 37 and 43, so the typical day is about ¥4,000; the single day of 160 is more than three times any other day and is almost certainly a private booking or a data-entry error. Because it is included in full in the mean, the mean is pulled to ¥5,500 — a figure that no actual day resembles and that would cause the manager to over-order stock and over-staff.
    Marks: 1 mean, 1 median, 1 for identifying the outlier, 1 for the recommendation with a business consequence. The interpretation marks are the last two.

  2. [AO4, 4 marks] Two suppliers deliver components. Supplier A's lead times have a mean of 6 days with a standard deviation of 0.8 days. Supplier B's have a mean of 5 days with a standard deviation of 4.2 days. Explain which supplier a manufacturer using just-in-time production should choose.

    Show answer
    1. Read the means: B is faster on average (5 days against 6).
    2. Read the spread: B's standard deviation of 4.2 days is enormous relative to its mean — most of B's deliveries will not arrive near day 5 at all, and some will be much later. A's spread of 0.8 days means nearly every delivery lands within a day or so of day 6.
    3. Apply to the business: just-in-time production holds minimal buffer stock, so a late delivery stops the line. What matters to JIT is predictability, not average speed.
    4. Conclusion: choose A. A JIT manufacturer can plan around a reliable 6 days; it cannot plan around an average of 5 that is sometimes 12. Quantitatively: on a normal approximation, roughly 95% of A's deliveries fall within about 4.4 to 7.6 days, while B's span roughly −3.4 to 13.4 days — i.e. an impossible negative lower bound, which itself tells you the distribution is skewed and B is unreliable.
    Marks: 1 for comparing means, 1 for comparing standard deviations, 1 for the JIT link, 1 for the committed conclusion. Note that "B is faster" alone scores nothing.

  3. [AO4, 4 marks] Eleven employees earn monthly salaries (¥000) of: 8, 8, 9, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 15, 18, 60. Determine Q1, the median and Q3, and hence identify any outliers using the 1.5 × IQR rule.

    Show answer
    1. n = 11 and the data is sorted. The median is the 6th value = ¥11,000.
    2. Lower half (the five values below the median): 8, 8, 9, 9, 10 → its median is the 3rd value = 9, so Q1 = ¥9,000.
    3. Upper half (the five values above): 12, 13, 15, 18, 60 → its median is 15, so Q3 = ¥15,000.
    4. IQR = 15 − 9 = ¥6,000. Lower fence = 9 − 1.5(6) = 0; upper fence = 15 + 1.5(6) = 24.
    5. Outlier: the highest salary of ¥60,000 lies above the upper fence of ¥24,000, so it is an outlier — presumably a director. No value falls below the lower fence.
    Marks: 1 each for Q1, median, Q3; 1 for the fences and the outlier identified. Adding "the median of ¥11,000 is therefore a fairer picture of pay than the mean of ¥15,727, which is distorted by the director" is the interpretive extra that pushes into the top band.

  4. [AO3, 4 marks] Explain two limitations of using descriptive statistics to support a strategic decision.

    Show answer
    1. Limitation 1 — they describe the past and assume stability. Every statistic here is computed from historical data and is only useful if the future resembles the past. In a market undergoing structural change — a new competitor, a regulation, a technology shift — the averages describe a world that no longer exists.
    2. Limitation 2 — they summarise away the information that matters. A mean and a standard deviation compress a distribution into two numbers, hiding its shape, its clusters and its extremes. If the business's problem lives in the tail — the handful of catastrophic late deliveries, the few customers who generate most of the complaints — the summary destroys the signal.
    3. Limitation 3 (alternative) — data quality. The output is only as good as the input: unrepresentative samples, recording errors and inconsistent definitions produce precise-looking nonsense.
    Marks: 2 marks per limitation, but only if each is explained with a consequence rather than merely stated. Give two, developed, rather than four in a list.

Tool 8 · Circular business models

What it is. A business model designed so that materials and products stay in use for as long as possible, are recovered at end of use, and are regenerated rather than discarded. The linear model is take → make → use → dispose; the circular model closes the loop so that "waste" from one stage becomes input to another. The IB recognises five distinct types.

The question it answers: "How can this business create and capture value while reducing its dependence on virgin material inputs — and how exposed is it if it does not?"

Reframe this before you revise it

It is a mistake to file this tool under "ethics" or "environment" and revise it last. A linear model is primarily a risk exposure, and the risk is commercial, not moral. If your revenue depends on buying a commodity and throwing it away, then you are exposed to the price of that commodity, to the politics of wherever it is mined, to any regulator who decides to tax or ban the resulting waste, and to the possibility that the asset you built to process it becomes worthless. That is a Unit 1 and Unit 3 argument in environmental clothing, and it is far more examinable than "it is good for the planet".

TypeWhat changesRealistic or real illustrationThe commercial logic
Circular supply The input: virgin, finite or risky materials are replaced by renewable, recycled or bio-based ones. A carpet manufacturer sourcing nylon recovered from discarded fishing nets instead of virgin polymer — the approach used by the flooring firm Interface through its Net-Works programme. Removes exposure to oil-linked input prices, and gives a procurement story large corporate customers actively ask for. Risk: the recycled feedstock may be less consistent in quality and its supply less predictable.
Resource recovery The end: products and materials are taken back and their value recovered rather than landfilled. Urban mining of lithium, cobalt and nickel from spent EV batteries — the business of specialist recyclers such as GEM in China; or automated disassembly of returned phones to recover aluminium, rare earths and gold. Recovered material is a second revenue stream and a hedge against input scarcity. But it requires reverse logistics — collecting, sorting and transporting used products — which is expensive and hard to run efficiently.
Product life extension The duration: products are repaired, upgraded, refurbished or resold so that each unit delivers value for longer. Outdoor clothing firms running trade-in and repair programmes, as Patagonia does with Worn Wear; modular phones designed so the camera and battery can be replaced; manufacturer-certified refurbished electronics sold with a warranty. Captures customers who cannot afford the new price, builds loyalty, and earns a second margin on the same unit. The obvious tension: selling longer-lasting products can reduce unit sales, so the firm must price accordingly.
Sharing The utilisation: the same physical asset serves more users, so fewer assets are needed in total. Car-sharing, bike-sharing and ride-hailing in Chinese cities — Didi, and the dockless bicycle schemes run by Meituan and Hello; tool libraries and peer-to-peer accommodation. Revenue per asset rises sharply. The cautionary case is instructive: dockless bike-sharing oversupplied cities, producing vast bicycle graveyards — proof that sharing only helps if utilisation actually rises, which requires matching supply to real demand.
Product service system The what is sold: the customer buys the outcome, not the object. The manufacturer keeps ownership and therefore wants the product to last. Rolls-Royce selling engine hours rather than engines ("power by the hour"); Michelin charging fleet operators per kilometre for tyres rather than per tyre; lighting sold as a service with the supplier retaining ownership of the fittings. Incentives align: the supplier now profits from durability and efficiency rather than from replacement sales. It builds long-term customer relationships and recurring revenue — but it moves cost forward, because the supplier funds the asset and is paid back over years.
How to use it — the procedure
  1. Map the current linear flow — where do materials come from, where do they go, and what is thrown away? Name the input and the waste stream.
  2. Identify which of the five types is available to this business. A business that sells a durable, high-value asset can consider product-as-a-service; one that sells a consumable consumed on contact cannot. Say which types are ruled out and why — that is analysis.
  3. Quantify the exposure you are removing. How much of the cost base is the material in question? How volatile has its price been? Is it sourced from a single country?
  4. Cost the circular alternative honestly — reverse logistics, collection, sorting, reconditioning, redesign, and the capital tied up in a service model are all real costs, and pretending otherwise is the difference between analysis and advocacy.
  5. Check the incentive alignment. In a product service system the supplier now bears the maintenance and replacement cost, so durability becomes profitable — say this explicitly, because it is the cleverest point available on this tool.
  6. Conclude and limit. Which type, what it costs, what risk it removes, and what it does not solve.

Worked application: an office-furniture manufacturer

A mid-sized manufacturer sells desks and office chairs to corporate customers, largely on a replace-every-ten-years cycle. Its inputs are steel, aluminium, engineered board and plastic; its products end up in landfill or low-value skip waste when an office is refitted. Apply the tool.

Limitations — what circular business models cannot tell you

  • Circular is not automatically cheaper. Collection, sorting, transport and reconditioning often cost more than virgin material, especially when oil prices are low. Recycling can consume more energy than it saves for some materials.
  • It does not remove demand for virgin input entirely — most circular systems still lose material each cycle and need topping up.
  • Customer behaviour is the binding constraint. Take-back only works if customers return products; sharing only works if assets are genuinely utilised. Both are outside the firm's control.
  • The cash-flow consequences can be severe. Product service systems convert an immediate sale into a long receivable, which can break a business with weak liquidity — the failure mode on the finance page, again.
  • It can be window dressing. A firm can adopt one small recycling initiative and market it heavily while 95% of its material flow stays linear. Identifying greenwashing is a legitimate evaluation point.
  • It does not address growth. Circular models are about resource productivity, not about finding new markets — that is Ansoff's job.
Common trap

Writing about circular business models as if they were purely environmental, and concluding "therefore it is good for the environment" — which earns almost nothing at AO3. Always connect to a commercial outcome: cost exposure, supply security, regulation, customer retention, recurring revenue. The second trap is describing "recycling" generically when the syllabus requires you to name the type; if you cannot classify it, you cannot analyse it.

Knowing eight tools is worth little if you cannot tell which one a question wants. The ninety-second selection routine
  1. Underline the command term — analyse, evaluate, recommend, to what extent. This tells you whether a conclusion is required (it always is).
  2. Circle the object — is the question about one product, a portfolio, an external environment, a decision, or data?
  3. Check for probabilities and numbers. Numbers plus probabilities means a decision tree; numbers without probabilities means statistics or Unit 3.
  4. Decide: is more than one tool appropriate? Often yes — STEEPLE then SWOT, or BCG then Ansoff. Using two tools well beats one tool at length, provided each is applied to the stimulus.
  5. Commit in the first sentence: "I will use a STEEPLE analysis because the question asks about factors outside the business's control." Naming your choice with a reason is a mark in itself.

Timing reality

A 10-mark Section B question should take about 20–22 minutes in Paper 1 or Paper 2. Budget it: two minutes selecting the tool and sketching its skeleton, ten to twelve minutes writing applied points, four minutes on the conclusion, three minutes on limitations and a re-read. Candidates who run out of time on the last question almost always lost it in the middle — writing eight applied points where four developed ones would have scored more.

Worked examples

A tool described is worth nothing in this course; a tool applied to the numbers in front of you is worth everything. Each example below takes a tool all the way to a decision — including the sentence about what the tool cannot tell you, which is the sentence that separates a 6 from a 9.

Worked example 1 A decision tree, start to finish · AO4 + AO3 · 10 marks

Question. Lianhe Retail must choose between opening a large store and a small store. The large store costs ¥6.0m to set up, the small one ¥2.5m. Forecast outcomes over five years:

OptionDemandProbabilityNet cash inflow over 5 years
LargeHigh0.6¥14.0m
LargeLow0.4¥3.0m
SmallHigh0.6¥7.0m
SmallLow0.4¥2.5m
  1. Calculate the expected value at each chance node. Large: (0.6 × 14.0) + (0.4 × 3.0) = 8.4 + 1.2 = ¥9.6m. Small: (0.6 × 7.0) + (0.4 × 2.5) = 4.2 + 1.0 = ¥5.2m.
  2. Subtract the cost of the decision at each decision node — not before. A very common error is to net the cost off before taking the expectation, which is fine here because both branches of a single option share the same cost, but it is wrong when they do not. Net gain, large = 9.6 − 6.0 = ¥3.6m. Net gain, small = 5.2 − 2.5 = ¥2.7m.
  3. Choose the higher net gain. Large store, expected net gain ¥3.6m against ¥2.7m — a ¥0.9m advantage.
  4. Show the probabilities sum to 1 at each node. 0.6 + 0.4 = 1.0 for both. If they do not, the expected value is meaningless and the whole calculation is wasted.
  5. Now do the analysis the markscheme actually rewards — the limitations applied to this decision. First, the probabilities are estimates. If the probability of high demand is 0.45 rather than 0.6, the large store's expected value becomes (0.45 × 14.0) + (0.55 × 3.0) = 6.3 + 1.65 = ¥7.95m, net gain ¥1.95m, and the small store wins. So the recommendation flips at a probability of about 0.5 — the decision is finely balanced and rests entirely on a number nobody knows. Second, expected value is an average over many repetitions; this business makes this decision once, so what actually happens is either ¥8.0m of profit or a ¥3.0m loss. Third, the model ignores risk: the large store can lose 6.0 − 3.0 = ¥3.0m, while the worst case for the small store is break-even, and a business that cannot survive a ¥3.0m loss should not take that risk however attractive the expected value.
  6. Conclude with a condition (AO3). On expected value, open the large store; but because the decision is sensitive to the probability of high demand and the downside is severe, the business should first test demand — for example with a pop-up store or by sub-letting part of the unit — before committing the full ¥6.0m.
Answer: expected values ¥9.6m and ¥5.2m; net gains ¥3.6m and ¥2.7m; choose the large store, conditional on testing demand and on being able to survive the ¥3.0m worst case. Marks: 2 for the two expected values, 2 for subtracting the decision-node costs, 1 for the choice, 2 for the sensitivity analysis showing the recommendation flipping, 3 for the limitations applied to this business and a conditional conclusion.
Trap — describing a tool instead of using it

The single most common way to lose marks on this page is to write an accurate description of a tool and never apply it. "A decision tree shows decisions, chance nodes and outcomes, and each branch has a probability" is knowledge, and it is worth one mark. "Because the probability of high demand is 0.6, the expected value of the large store is ¥9.6m, so after the ¥6.0m set-up cost the net gain is ¥3.6m" is application, and it is worth four. The test to apply to every paragraph you write on this page: does it contain a number, a named business fact, or a consequence? If not, it is not earning marks above AO1.

Worked example 2 The BCG matrix applied to a real portfolio · AO2 + AO3 · 8 marks

Question. A consumer-goods business has four divisions. Market growth and relative market share (its share divided by the largest rival's share) are given below. Classify each division, recommend what to do with each, and state two limitations of the analysis.

DivisionMarket growth p.a.Relative market shareRevenue ¥m
Fresh dairy2%0.4820
Plant drinks18%0.6240
Bottled water6%2.51,150
Snack bars22%1.8310
  1. Recall the two axes and the threshold. The vertical axis is market growth rate, with the high/low boundary conventionally set at about 10% a year; the horizontal axis is relative market share, with 1.0 as the boundary — above 1.0 means market leader.
  2. Classify each one by comparing against both thresholds. Fresh dairy: low growth, low share → dog. Plant drinks: high growth, low share → problem child (question mark). Bottled water: low growth, high share → cash cow. Snack bars: high growth, high share → star.
  3. Apply the standard prescription — and then qualify it. Bottled water should be milked: it generates the cash that funds everything else, and investment should be limited to defending the position. Snack bars should be invested in heavily to hold share while the market grows, so that when growth slows they become the next cash cow. Plant drinks is the real decision: high growth market but relative share of only 0.6 means the business is a follower in an attractive market, so it must either invest heavily to overtake the leader or withdraw — half-measures waste money. Fresh dairy is the divestment candidate: low growth and a weak position, with ¥820m of revenue tying up management attention for a poor return.
  4. Qualify using the actual numbers, because the standard prescription is often wrong. Fresh dairy contributes ¥820m of revenue — the second largest — and a dog that is cash-generative and requires little investment can be worth keeping, particularly if it shares distribution with the other divisions. Conversely, a "star" in a market growing 22% a year may be consuming cash faster than the cash cow can supply it, which is a liquidity problem rather than a portfolio problem.
  5. Limitation 1. The matrix rests on the experience-curve assumption that high relative share causes low unit costs, and therefore high profit. That is often true in manufacturing and often false in services, where a small specialist can be more profitable than a big generalist.
  6. Limitation 2. It reduces strategy to two variables. It ignores brand strength, quality, the competitive reaction, and whether the divisions are related at all — and "market growth" is itself hard to define, since the boundary depends on how narrowly the market is drawn. A business can place itself in any quadrant by choosing the definition of its market.
Answer: dairy = dog, plant drinks = problem child, water = cash cow, snack bars = star; milk the water, invest in snack bars, make a clear decision on plant drinks, and question divestment of dairy because of its ¥820m revenue and shared distribution. Marks: 2 for correct classification using both thresholds, 2 for a recommendation on each, 2 for qualifying the standard prescription with the figures, 2 for two explained limitations.
Worked example 3 Descriptive statistics on a real data set · AO4 + AO2 · 8 marks

Question. A business records monthly sales (¥000) for the first eight months of the year: 148, 162, 155, 171, 149, 205, 188, 162. Calculate the mean, median, mode and range, calculate the standard deviation, and comment on what the statistics do and do not tell the manager.

  1. Mean. Sum = 148 + 162 + 155 + 171 + 149 + 205 + 188 + 162 = 1,340. Mean = 1,340 ÷ 8 = 167.5 (¥167,500).
  2. Median — sort first, every time. Ordered: 148, 149, 155, 162, 162, 171, 188, 205. With 8 values the median is the mean of the 4th and 5th: (162 + 162) ÷ 2 = 162.
  3. Mode. 162 appears twice; all other values once. Mode = 162.
  4. Range. 205 − 148 = 57. The range is a crude measure because two extreme months determine it entirely.
  5. Standard deviation — build the table, do not shortcut. Deviations from the mean of 167.5: −19.5, −5.5, −12.5, +3.5, −18.5, +37.5, +20.5, −5.5. Squared: 380.25, 30.25, 156.25, 12.25, 342.25, 1,406.25, 420.25, 30.25. Sum of squares = 2,778. Variance (population) = 2,778 ÷ 8 = 347.25. Standard deviation = √347.25 = 18.6 (¥18,600).
  6. Interpret the spread in business language. With a mean of 167.5 and a standard deviation of 18.6, roughly two-thirds of months fall within ±18.6 of the mean, that is between about 149 and 186. The value 205 is two standard deviations above the mean and deserves an explanation: was there a promotion, a large one-off order, or a data-entry error? A sales figure that far from the mean should be investigated before it is used in a forecast.
  7. Compare the measures. The mean (167.5) is above the median (162) because of the high months, so the distribution is slightly right-skewed; for planning a typical month, the median is the safer figure to use.
  8. Say what the statistics cannot do — the AO3 mark. These are eight observations of one variable. They show that sales vary, but they say nothing about why, and they cannot distinguish a trend from noise: an upward drift from 148 to 162 would look identical to a genuinely rising business and to a stable one with a lucky July. Nor do they predict: the mean of the last eight months is not a forecast, particularly if the business is seasonal. Correlation, if calculated against advertising spend, would also not establish causation — higher sales and higher advertising may both be the result of a third factor, such as a competitor leaving the market.
Answer: mean 167.5; median 162; mode 162; range 57; standard deviation 18.6. Sales vary by about ±¥18,600 around a typical ¥160,000 month, and the ¥205,000 month should be investigated. Marks: 1 each for mean, median, mode and range, 2 for the standard deviation with the table of squared deviations, 2 for interpretation and for naming what the statistics cannot show.
Exam technique — every tool gets a limitation sentence

Markbands for extended responses reward explicit judgement and limitations, and on a toolkit question that means one sentence naming what the tool cannot tell you. Learn one limitation per tool and attach it to the business: "the BCG matrix ignores that dairy shares distribution with the other three divisions"; "the decision tree's probabilities are estimates, and the recommendation reverses at 0.5"; "correlation between advertising and sales does not establish causation". Generic limitations earn one mark; applied ones earn three.

Practice set B — toolkit questions

Answer these closed-book, then compare with the model solutions. In every case, write the conclusion and the limitation even when the question does not explicitly ask for them — that is the habit this page exists to build.

  1. [AO2, 6 marks] Changyu is a family-owned chain of eleven mid-priced restaurants in a Chinese provincial capital. It has a loyal local customer base, low staff turnover, and a head chef who is well known in the city. It has no delivery operation, no loyalty app, and its premises are all leased. Two national chains with delivery kitchens have opened nearby in the past year, and a new municipal regulation restricts kitchen extract systems in older buildings. Construct a SWOT analysis for Changyu and state which factor you consider most important.

    Show answer
    1. Strengths (internal, helpful): an established local reputation and a named head chef, which is a form of differentiation national chains cannot copy; low staff turnover, which preserves service quality and reduces recruitment and training cost; eleven years of trading data and supplier relationships.
    2. Weaknesses (internal, harmful): no delivery operation, so it cannot reach customers who now order in; no customer database or loyalty mechanism, so it cannot market directly or see repeat behaviour; all premises leased, so it has no property asset to borrow against and is exposed to rent reviews.
    3. Opportunities (external, helpful): the shift to delivery is also an opportunity — it can add a delivery kitchen without needing a new dining room; the national chains' arrival has grown overall category awareness in the district; a loyalty app would let it use the customer base it already has.
    4. Threats (external, harmful): the new chains compete on convenience and app reach; the extract regulation may physically prevent it from upgrading kitchens in older premises, which is a constraint rivals in new buildings do not face; rising food and labour costs.
    5. Most important factor — and why: the weakness of having no delivery and no customer data, paired with the threat of chains that compete precisely on those two things. The reputation is real but it defends the dining room, not the delivery order.
    6. Conclusion and limitation: the grid suggests Changyu should build a delivery operation and capture customer data before its dine-in base erodes. Limitation: the SWOT shows where it stands but not whether it can afford the investment, which is a cash-flow question the grid cannot answer.
    Marks: 2 for four correctly sorted boxes with stimulus-specific points, 2 for development (why each point matters), 1 for the prioritised factor, 1 for the conclusion. A grid of generic points scores 2–3.

  2. [AO2, 4 marks] For each of the following, state which cell of the Ansoff matrix it occupies and give one reason for your classification: (a) a Chinese smartphone manufacturer launching a smartwatch sold through its existing retail network; (b) the same manufacturer opening retail stores in Latin America; (c) a supermarket chain launching a discounted own-brand range to win customers from a discounter; (d) a railway operator launching a budget airline.

    Show answer
    1. (a) Product development. The market is existing (the firm's current retail customers) but the product is new — a smartwatch is a different product from a phone, even though it shares the brand and distribution.
    2. (b) Market development. The product is unchanged; the market is new geographically, with different income levels, regulations and competitor sets.

Note that the own-brand range is arguably a new product, which is exactly why you should state your definition — but the strategic intent is share gain in an existing market, so penetration is the better classification.

  3. [AO2, 6 marks] Conduct a STEEPLE analysis of a Chinese manufacturer of solar panels exporting to Europe. Identify one factor for each of the seven letters and classify each as an opportunity or a threat.

    Show answer
    1. S — Social: rising public concern about climate change and household adoption of rooftop solar → opportunity (demand growth).

  4. [AO4, 5 marks] A confectionery company has four product lines. Data: Line A — market growing at 1% a year, company share 8%, largest rival 16%.     Show answer
    1. Compute relative market share (own share ÷ leader's share): A = 8 ÷ 16 = 0.5; B = 4 ÷ 40 = 0.1; C = 30 ÷ 15 = 2.0; D = 45 ÷ 15 = 3.0.
    2. Line A: low growth, low relative share → dog. Action: harvest or divest, unless it completes the range.
    3. Line B: high growth, very low relative share → question mark, and a weak one at 0.1. Action: this is the hard decision — either invest heavily to gain share quickly or exit. Given a relative share of only 0.1 against a dominant leader, exit or a niche strategy is more realistic than a frontal challenge.
    4. Line C: high growth, high relative share → star. Action: build — fund it to defend leadership while the market grows, so it becomes the next cash cow.
    5. Line D: low growth, high relative share → cash cow. Action: hold and harvest — milk it to fund line C.
    6. Portfolio conclusion: the portfolio is workable — one strong cow funding one strong star — but it carries two dogs/question marks that consume management attention. The strategic risk is that line D's market is barely growing, so the cow will eventually dry up; line C must be converted into a cow before that happens.
    7. Limitation: the matrix says nothing about profitability — a high-growth market can be unprofitable — and the classification depends on how "the market" is defined.
    Marks: 2 for the four relative-share calculations (this is the quantitative mark), 2 for correct placement and appropriate action per line, 1 for the portfolio conclusion. Note that using absolute share instead of relative share would place C and D incorrectly.

[AO4, 6 marks] A business must choose between launching a new product (payoff ¥9 m if demand is high, −¥2 m if demand is low; probability of high demand 0.7) and extending an existing product (payoff ¥4 m if high, ¥1 m if low; probability of high demand 0.7). Draw the decision structure, calculate the expected monetary value of each option, recommend one, and comment on how sensitive the decision is to the probability estimate.

    Show answer
    1. Structure: one decision node with two branches; each branch ends in a chance node with two outcomes (high demand p = 0.7, low demand p = 0.3). Check: 0.7 + 0.3 = 1 at each chance node.
    2. EMV (launch): (0.7 × ¥9 m) + (0.3 × −¥2 m) = ¥6.3 m − ¥0.6 m = ¥5.7 m.
    3. EMV (extend): (0.7 × ¥4 m) + (0.3 × ¥1 m) = ¥2.8 m + ¥0.3 m = ¥3.1 m.
    4. Recommendation: launch, with a net expected gain of ¥5.7 m − ¥3.1 m = ¥2.6 m over the next best option.
    5. Sensitivity: let p be the probability of high demand. Launch: 9p − 2(1 − p) = 11p − 2. Extend: 4p + 1(1 − p) = 3p + 1. Equal when 11p − 2 = 3p + 1, so 8p = 3 and p = 0.375. Below a 37.5% probability of high demand, extending becomes preferable. At the estimated 0.7 the decision is comfortable; at 0.4 it would be marginal.
    6. Limitations: the EMV ignores risk attitude — a loss of ¥2 m might be unaffordable for a small firm even though the expected value is positive; the probabilities are estimates; and the tree omits qualitative factors such as brand fit and competitor reaction. Also, expected value is an average over many repeats, and this decision is made once.
    Marks: 1 for correct structure with probabilities summing to 1, 2 for the two EMVs, 1 for the recommendation with the net gain, 2 for the sensitivity analysis and limitations. Omitting the last part caps the answer at 4.

  6. [AO3, 6 marks] "A circular business model is simply a way for a business to be more environmentally friendly." To what extent do you agree with this statement?

    Show answer
    1. Agree in part (AO2): circular models do reduce waste and virgin-resource use, and for many firms that is part of the motivation and part of the marketing value.
    2. But the commercial argument is stronger (AO2): a linear model exposes a business to input price volatility, to supply insecurity where materials are geographically concentrated, and to regulation such as carbon pricing, packaging taxes or extended producer responsibility. Recovering materials and extending product life are hedges against those exposures — they reduce a risk, not just a footprint.
    3. And some circular models are chosen purely for commercial reasons (AO2): a product service system gives recurring revenue, closer customer relationships and an incentive to build durable products, and a manufacturer may adopt it with no environmental motive at all.
    4. Counter-argument (AO3): circular models are not automatically cheaper — reverse logistics, collection and reconditioning can cost more than virgin input, and service models tie up capital and convert an immediate sale into a long receivable.
    5. Judgement: agree only to a limited extent. Environmental benefit is usually a by-product rather than the defining feature; the defining feature is that materials and value stay in the system. A business that adopts circularity purely for appearances while 95% of its material flow stays linear is greenwashing, not circular.
    Marks: 2 for the explained "for" case, 2–3 for the developed commercial counter-case with examples, 1–2 for a committed judgement. One-sided agreement caps at 3–4.

  7. [AO3, 8 marks] Evaluate the usefulness of the SWOT analysis and the decision tree to a manager deciding whether to open a second factory.

    Show answer
    1. SWOT — useful for (AO2): it forces a structured audit of internal capacity (do we have the management bandwidth, the engineering skills, the cash?) and external conditions (is demand growing, are rivals also expanding?). It is quick, needs no data beyond what the manager already has, and produces a shared language for discussion.
    2. SWOT — limitations for this decision (AO3): it is static, unweighted and subjective, and above all it produces options rather than a choice. It cannot tell the manager whether the second factory is worth more than the alternative uses of the capital — which is precisely the question.
    3. Decision tree — useful for (AO2/AO4): it forces the manager to state probabilities and payoffs explicitly, produces an expected monetary value for each option, and makes the assumptions testable — a sensitivity analysis shows how wrong the demand estimate would have to be before the decision flips.
    4. Decision tree — limitations for this decision (AO3): the probabilities for a one-off, multi-year investment are estimates, often little better than guesses; the EMV ignores risk attitude and says nothing about whether the business could survive the bad branch; and it omits non-quantifiable factors such as the effect on staff or the strategic value of a second site.
    5. Judgement (AO3): the two tools do different jobs and are complements, not substitutes. SWOT is the better tool for framing the question and surfacing factors; the decision tree is the better tool for choosing between options once the factors are known. Used together — SWOT to identify what matters, a tree to value it, with the tree's probabilities challenged against the SWOT's threats — the manager gets a defensible decision. Used alone, SWOT is too vague to decide and the tree is too narrow to trust.
    6. What neither does: neither addresses financing — whether the firm can raise the money and at what cost is a Unit 3 question, and a cash-flow forecast would be needed before the recommendation could be acted on.
    Marks: 2 for each tool's usefulness applied to this decision, 2 for each tool's limitations, 2 for the comparative judgement. Describing both tools without applying them to the factory decision scores 3–4 at best.

  8. [AO2, 4 marks] An entrepreneur is seeking a bank loan for a new venture. Explain two sections of a business plan that the bank will scrutinise most closely, and why.

    Show answer
    1. The cash flow forecast. A bank is repaid out of cash, not profit, so it will examine the monthly forecast for the trough — the point at which the closing balance is lowest — and check that the loan is large enough to cover it plus a buffer. It will also test whether receipts are lagged realistically behind sales, because optimistic receivables assumptions are the commonest way a forecast is flattered.
    2. The security and management section. A start-up with no trading record has no unencumbered assets, so the bank will look at what the owners can offer as collateral and whether either founder has relevant experience. Lenders routinely say they back people rather than plans, because a capable team can change the plan.
    3. Acceptable alternatives: the market-research section (does demand actually exist, or is it asserted?) or the risk-and-contingency section (has the entrepreneur thought about what happens if the main assumption is wrong?).
    Marks: 2 per section, but only if the reason is given. Naming the eight sections of a business plan without explaining why a lender wants any of them scores 1–2.

  9. [AO2, 6 marks] Apply STEEPLE to a business that manufactures electric bicycles in a country that has just announced a subsidy for buyers and a tariff on imported batteries. Identify one factor from each of the six categories and state which two you consider most important.

    Show answer
    1. Social (1): rising concern about air quality and congestion makes cycling socially acceptable and even fashionable, expanding the market beyond enthusiasts.
    2. Technological (1): battery energy density and cost dominate the product's price and range; a competitor with better battery management can leapfrog this business whatever else it does.
    3. Economic (1): the subsidy lowers the effective price to the buyer, which raises demand, but it also makes demand dependent on government policy and may be withdrawn.
    4. Environmental (1): end-of-life battery recycling is becoming regulated, so the business may be required to take back and process used packs — a cost that must be designed in now rather than retrofitted.
    5. Political (1): the tariff on imported batteries raises the cost of the main component overnight, and the stability of that tariff is a political decision the business cannot influence.
    6. Ethical/Legal (1): product safety standards and liability for battery fires create legal exposure, and sourcing of the minerals used in cells raises ethical sourcing obligations that customers and regulators increasingly enforce.
    7. Which two matter most (1–2): the tariff and the subsidy, because both are external, both change the economics immediately, and neither is within the business's control — together they can turn a profitable product into a loss-making one in a single budget announcement. The correct response is to model both scenarios and to reduce dependence on imported cells.
    Marks: 1 for each of the six factors identified and applied to this business (a factor named in the abstract scores nothing), 1–2 for a justified choice of the most important two.

  10. [AO2, 6 marks] A business has four products. Product A: market growth 3%, relative share 0.5. Product B: market growth 15%, relative share 0.8. Product C: market growth 4%, relative share 3.2. Product D: market growth 16%, relative share 2.0. (a) Place each in the correct BCG quadrant. (b) Explain what the matrix suggests the business should do, and why that advice may be wrong for product A.

    Show answer
    1. Classification (2): A = dog (low growth, low share); B = problem child (high growth, low share); C = cash cow (low growth, high share); D = star (high growth, high share). The thresholds are about 10% growth and 1.0 relative share, and every classification depends on comparing against both.
    2. What the matrix suggests (2): milk C — it dominates a slow-growing market and should generate surplus cash with minimal new investment; invest in D to hold share so that it becomes the next cow when growth slows; make a clear decision on B, because a follower in a fast-growing market either invests enough to overtake the leader or should leave; and consider divesting A.
    3. Why that may be wrong for A (2): the matrix treats a dog purely as a cash drain, but a low-share product in a slow-growing market can still be highly profitable if it occupies a niche — a loyal specialist customer, a price premium, or a role that completes the range so that customers buy everything from one supplier. It may also share production and distribution with C, so removing it would raise the unit costs of the products that remain. Finally, A's low relative share may reflect a dominant competitor rather than weak performance; a business can be number two in a market with an 800% larger leader and still earn an excellent return.
    Marks: 2 for correct classification, 2 for the standard prescription for each quadrant, 2 for explaining why the prescription may be wrong for A, with at least one concrete reason.

  11. [AO4, 6 marks] A business records the following quarterly sales figures (¥000) over three years: 210, 245, 198, 302, 224, 260, 206, 315, 231, 268, 214, 324. (a) Calculate the mean and the range. (b) Identify the pattern the mean conceals and explain what the business should do about it.

    Show answer
    1. Mean (2): sum = 210 + 245 + 198 + 302 + 224 + 260 + 206 + 315 + 231 + 268 + 214 + 324 = 2,997. Mean = 2,997 ÷ 12 = 249.75 (¥249,750).
    2. Range (1): 324 − 198 = 126, or ¥126,000 — more than half the mean, which is a large spread and an immediate signal that a single average is misleading.
    3. The concealed pattern (3): the data is quarterly and plainly seasonal. Taking the quarters in order within each year: Q1 = 210, 224, 231; Q2 = 245, 260, 268; Q3 = 198, 206, 214; Q4 = 302, 315, 324. Every Q4 is the largest and every Q3 the smallest, and each series rises steadily year on year — so the business has both a seasonal peak in the fourth quarter and an underlying upward trend of roughly 5% a year. The mean of 249.75 hides both: it is higher than any Q1 or Q3 and lower than any Q4.
    4. What to do (1–2): deseasonalise before forecasting — compare each quarter with the same quarter a year earlier, which gives growth of about 10%, 6% and 8% for Q1–Q3 and about 4–7% for Q4 — and plan capacity, inventory and staffing around the Q4 peak rather than around the average. A forecast built on the annual mean would leave the business short of stock every fourth quarter.
    Marks: 2 for the mean with the sum shown, 1 for the range, 3 for identifying both the seasonality and the trend with evidence from the figures, 1 for a practical consequence.

  12. [AO3, 10 marks] Evaluate the usefulness of decision trees to a manager making a strategic decision.

    Show answer
    1. Define (AO1): a decision tree is a diagrammatic method of showing a sequence of decisions, chance events and outcomes, with probabilities and financial values attached, so that the expected monetary value of each route can be calculated and compared.
    2. Useful, applied (AO2): it forces the manager to be explicit about the alternatives, the probabilities and the payoffs — three things that are usually left vague in a meeting — and it makes the structure of the problem visible to everyone, including the points where a later decision depends on an earlier outcome. Expected value gives a single comparable number where otherwise there would be opinions, and it handles sequences: build a small unit now, and expand only if demand turns out high. It can be extended with sensitivity analysis to show how robust the recommendation is.
    3. Not useful, applied (AO2): the probabilities are usually subjective estimates, and where the recommendation is sensitive to them — as in the Lianhe example, where it reverses at about 0.5 — the tree gives spurious precision to a guess. Expected value is an average across many repetitions, but a strategic decision is made once, so the business experiences one branch, not the average. The tree also ignores risk attitude: a business that would be destroyed by the worst-case outcome should not follow expected value however large it is. And it excludes everything unquantifiable — brand damage, staff morale, competitor reaction — which is often exactly what decides strategic questions.
    4. Weigh (AO3): decision trees are strongest for repeatable, well-structured decisions where probabilities can be estimated from history — capacity additions, product launch decisions in a known category, bidding. They are weakest for genuinely novel strategic choices where there is no base rate to draw probabilities from. Their real value is often structural rather than numerical: the discipline of laying out the problem changes the discussion even when the numbers are soft.
    5. Limitations (AO3): the output depends entirely on the quality of the inputs, including the cost estimates, and a tree can become so complex that nobody can check it.
    6. Conclusion (AO3): decision trees are useful to a considerable extent as a structuring and sensitivity tool, but the expected values should be treated as one input to judgement rather than as the answer, and must always be presented alongside the worst case and the assumptions that would reverse the recommendation.
    Marks: 1–2 for the definition including expected value; 3–4 for one applied side; 5–6 for both sides with a weak conclusion; 7–8 for two developed applied sides with a judgement; 9–10 for those plus limitations and a conclusion that distinguishes structuring from deciding.

  13. [AO3, 10 marks] To what extent should a manufacturer of office furniture adopt a circular business model?

    Show answer
    1. Define (AO1): a circular business model designs out waste by keeping materials and products in use — through durability, repair, refurbishment, remanufacturing and leasing — rather than the linear take-make-dispose model.
    2. The case for, applied (AO2): office furniture is an unusually good fit. The products are durable, standardised and valuable at end of life: a steel-framed desk can be recovered, re-upholstered or resold, and the material cost saved is real. Leasing rather than selling — "furniture as a service" — aligns the manufacturer's interest with durability, because a product that lasts costs the manufacturer less, and it converts a one-off sale into recurring revenue. Customers, particularly large ones with environmental reporting obligations, are increasingly required to account for the waste they generate, so a take-back service is a competitive advantage. It also insulates the business from volatile raw-material prices.
    3. The case against, applied (AO2): the transition is expensive and the cash flow is worse before it is better: leasing delays revenue while the manufacturing cost is incurred immediately, and the business needs reverse logistics, inspection and refurbishment capacity — new skills and new premises. Demand for new furniture falls if refurbished units are resold at a discount, which cannibalises the existing product. There is a design tension too: products built for disassembly may be more expensive to make. And the model depends on customers co-operating — returning furniture in reasonable condition, at a time the manufacturer can use it.
    4. Weigh (AO3): the decisive issue is whether the business can finance the working-capital gap and whether customers value the service enough to pay for it. In a market of large corporate and public-sector buyers with sustainability targets, the answer is often yes; for price-sensitive small buyers it usually is not.
    5. Limitations (AO3): I cannot judge this without knowing the business's cash position, the residual value of its products after five years, and whether competitors already offer take-back. Regulation may also force the issue — extended producer responsibility rules would change the calculation entirely.
    6. Conclusion (AO3): the business should adopt circularity to a considerable extent but incrementally: start with take-back and refurbishment of the highest-value lines, where the material saving is clearest and the cannibalisation risk can be monitored, and offer leasing only where the business can fund the working-capital gap.
    Marks: 1–2 for the definition with examples of circular practice; 3–4 for one applied side; 5–6 for both sides with a weak conclusion; 7–8 for two developed applied sides with a judgement; 9–10 for those plus limitations and a phased, conditional recommendation.
