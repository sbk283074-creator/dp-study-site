Finance and accounts

Unit 3 is the unit where your mathematics is an unfair advantage — and also the unit where it can quietly cost you half the marks. Every calculation in this unit is worth roughly two marks; the other two sit in the sentence after it, where you say what the number means for this business, in this industry, at this moment, and what it cannot tell you. Learn the arithmetic in an afternoon. Learn to interpret and to criticise, and you will be answering the top markband questions that most candidates never reach.

3.1 Introduction to finance 3.2 Sources of finance 3.3 Costs and revenues 3.4 Final accounts 3.5 Profitability and liquidity ratios 3.7 Cash flow 3.8 Investment appraisal HL only — skip: 3.6 efficiency ratios, 3.9 budgets, NPV, depreciation methods
Why this unit exists

A business does not die because it is unprofitable. It dies because it cannot pay a bill on the day the bill is due. Those are two different failures, they have two different statements (the income statement and the cash flow forecast), and the IB deliberately puts both in one unit so that it can ask you why a company that made ¥5.8 million of profit went bust in month four. If you take one sentence from this page, take this one: profit is an opinion recorded over a period; cash is a fact recorded on a day.

Where this sits

Unit 3 is the numerical half of the course. Every other unit eventually asks "can we afford it?" — Unit 4 when it prices a product, Unit 5 when it chooses a production method, Unit 2 when it plans a restructure. If you can read a balance sheet you can answer questions in all of them. The tools that recur across units live on the toolkit page, and if you use any financial data in your research project you will be judged on exactly the interpretation skills practised here.

3.1 Introduction to finance: the difference between profit and cash

Start with a company that is doing everything right. Call it Hengli Display Ltd, a mid-sized Chinese manufacturer of display modules. In month 1 it wins a contract with a large electronics customer on 60-day payment terms. Sales grow from ¥3 million a month to ¥13 million a month inside six months. Materials cost 45% of sales value and are paid one month after purchase. Wages and overheads rise more slowly than sales. On paper — on the income statement, using the accruals principle — the business is a triumph:

The months 5 and 6 columns are the cruellest part of the table: the cash position was about to repair itself. Hengli did not fail because the business model was broken. It failed because of timing.

How to use it

In any question about a profitable business with a cash problem, run this four-step checklist out loud: (1) Is cash tied up in trade receivables — are customers being given (or taking) longer to pay? (2) Is it tied up in inventory — is stock building up faster than it sells? (3) Is it leaving as capital expenditure — a one-off asset purchase that does not appear in the profit calculation at all? (4) Is it leaving as drawings, dividends or debt repayment — which are not expenses? Each of those has a different fix, and naming the right one is what separates a 3–4 mark answer from a 7–8 mark answer.

What the finance function actually does

Strip away the jargon and the finance department has four jobs, and the syllabus is organised around them:

Raising finance

Getting the money in, in the right form and at the right time — 3.2. The wrong source (short-term money funding a long-term asset) is a classic cause of the failure in Figure 1.

Recording and reporting

Producing the final accounts so that owners, lenders and the tax authority can see what happened — 3.4. Recording is objective; the resulting profit figure depends on accounting conventions.

Controlling and planning

Forecasting cash, monitoring costs, and appraising investment — 3.7 and 3.8. This is forward-looking, unlike the accounts.

Measuring performance

Turning the accounts into ratios that can be compared with last year, with rivals, and with the cost of borrowing — 3.5.

Three distinctions examiners expect you to hold in your head

Profit
Revenue earned minus expenses incurred over a period, regardless of when money moved. Affected by non-cash items such as depreciation. A measure of performance.
Cash
Money actually available on a given day. Not affected by depreciation. A measure of survival.
Capital expenditure (capex)
Spending on non-current assets — a cash outflow now, an expense spread over years as depreciation. Buying a machine does not reduce this year's profit by the machine's price.
Revenue expenditure
Day-to-day spending — an expense in full, immediately (wages, materials, rent).
Insolvency
Being unable to pay debts when they fall due. A cash problem. A business can be insolvent while profitable and profitable while insolvent.
Working capital
Current assets − current liabilities. The buffer that lets a business pay suppliers before customers pay it. Negative working capital is not automatically fatal (supermarkets run on it) but it is fragile.
Common trap

Three answers lose marks here every session. (a) "The business made a loss" — no, it made a profit; that is the whole point. (b) "Reduce costs" — costs are already covered by revenue; the problem is timing, and cutting costs does not close a timing gap in month 4. (c) Listing every possible cause without ranking them — Section A marks sit on specific defined points, so pick the two causes the stimulus actually points at and develop each one.

3.2 Sources of finance

Finance is classified two ways at once, and examiners routinely ask you to hold both axes in a single sentence. By origin: internal (generated inside the business — retained profit, sale of surplus assets, tighter working-capital control, owners' own savings injected) versus external (from outside lenders or investors). By duration: short term (up to 1 year), medium term (1–5 years), long term (more than 5 years).

How to use it

The single most testable principle in 3.2 is matching: finance long-term assets with long-term finance, and short-term needs with short-term finance. Funding a factory with an overdraft is how Hengli-style failures start, because the bank can demand repayment on 24 hours' notice while the factory takes eight years to pay for itself. Whenever you recommend a source, say the matching sentence explicitly — it is a cheap, reliable mark.

Choosing between sources: the seven-factor framework

A 10-mark "recommend a source of finance" question is not asking which source you prefer. It is asking you to weigh these factors against the specific facts of the stimulus and then commit. Memorise the list, then apply only the three or four that the case actually turns on:

  1. Purpose and duration — is the money for working capital (short) or a new plant (long)? Matching applies here.
  2. Amount required — retained profit rarely funds a factory; a share issue is pointless for a ¥50,000 gap.
  3. Cost — total cost, not just the interest rate. Include arrangement fees, lost cash discounts, the factoring fee, and the opportunity cost of owners' capital.
  4. Risk and gearing — debt must be serviced whether or not profits appear; equity does not. Highly geared businesses are fragile in a downturn. (The gearing ratio itself is HL only.)
  5. Security — does the business own unencumbered assets a lender will accept? Start-ups often have none, which pushes them towards equity, leasing or personal guarantees.
  6. Control — equity dilutes ownership and decision-making; debt does not. For a family firm this can be decisive, and it is the factor candidates most often forget.
  7. Legal form and availability — a sole trader cannot issue shares; an unprofitable company cannot use retained profit; a business with a poor credit record may not get a loan at any price.
Worked example 1 IB-style · 6 marks · Section A

Question. Songlan Foods Ltd (the business used throughout this page) needs ¥2.4 million to buy an automated dumpling-folding line with an expected life of five years. It has ¥480,000 cash, strong retained earnings, a 6% long-term loan already outstanding, and shareholders who do not want to lose control. Recommend one source of finance, justifying your choice. [6]

  1. Identify the nature of the need (1 mark). This is capital expenditure on a non-current asset with a five-year life, so it should be financed with long-term finance — the matching principle. That rules out the overdraft and trade credit immediately, and saying so is worth a mark.
  2. Define/choose (2 marks). Recommend a five-year bank loan (medium-term debt) secured against the company's unencumbered premises, repayable over the asset's life.
  3. Apply (1 mark). Songlan already services a 6% loan and earns an ROCE of 13.9% (see 3.5), so borrowing at roughly 6% to fund an asset earning more than that is affordable — the return exceeds the cost of the finance.
  4. Develop — benefit (1 mark). Shareholders keep control, unlike a share issue, and the repayments are predictable, which helps cash-flow forecasting.
  5. Develop — limitation (1 mark). It raises gearing and the interest must be paid even if the new line underperforms; security over the premises puts an asset at risk if repayments are missed.
  6. Contrast (needed for the top band). Retained profit would be cheaper and avoids interest, but it would exhaust the cash buffer that the forecast in 3.7 shows is already thin — using it would create a liquidity problem to solve a profitability opportunity.
Answer: A five-year secured bank loan matched to the asset's life. The marks are in the matching sentence, the control sentence, and the honest admission that it increases gearing — not in the name of the source itself.
Common trap

Describing two sources and then picking one at random is a 3–4 mark answer. To reach 7–8 you must make the rejected option do work: "a share issue would avoid interest but the shareholders have stated they do not want to lose control, so the saving is not worth it." That is a judgement, and judgements are what the top markband pays for. Also: never say a source is "best" without saying what it is best for.

3.3 Costs and revenues

Cost classification is not a fact about a cost. It is a fact about a cost relative to a decision. That is the whole of 3.3, and it is the sub-topic where a mathematically strong student can score full marks by refusing to memorise "rent is fixed".

CategoryDefinitionSonglan Foods, year to 31 Dec 2027 (¥000)Behaviour when output rises
Fixed costsDo not change with output in the short runIndirect wages 3,300; rent and rates 1,240; administration 620; depreciation 860; fixed part of marketing 750; fixed part of distribution 180 → 6,950Total unchanged; fixed cost per unit falls
Variable costsChange directly in proportion to outputCost of sales (materials, packaging, direct labour) 13,100; variable marketing 500; variable distribution 300 → 13,900Total rises in proportion; variable cost per unit constant
Semi-variable costsA fixed element plus a variable elementMarketing 1,250 (750 + 0.42/unit); distribution 480 (180 + 0.25/unit) → 1,730Total rises, but less than in proportion

Three aggregates follow. At the actual output of 1,200,000 packs and net sales of ¥23,520,000:

  • Total cost = fixed 6,950 + variable 13,900 = ¥20,850,000.
  • Average cost (total cost per unit) = 20,850,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = ¥17.38 per pack. It falls as output rises because the ¥6.95 million of fixed costs is spread over more units — this is the numerical heart of economies of scale.
  • Marginal cost = the cost of one more pack = the variable cost per unit = 13,900,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = ¥11.58 per pack — provided the extra pack can be made within existing capacity.
Proof Why contribution matters more than average cost (and why marginal cost ≠ average cost)
  1. Revenue per unit (net of returns) = 23,520,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = ¥19.60.
  2. Variable cost per unit = 13,900,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = ¥11.5833.
  3. Define contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit = 19.60 − 11.5833 = ¥8.017.
  4. Total contribution = 8.017 × 1,200,000 = ¥9,620,000. Equivalently: revenue − total variable costs = 23,520 − 13,900 = ¥9,620,000. Same number, two routes.
  5. Profit before interest and tax = total contribution − fixed costs = 9,620 − 6,950 = ¥2,670,000, which is exactly the figure in the profit and loss account below. The identity is closed.
  6. Now the point. Average cost (¥17.38) is not marginal cost (¥11.58), because average cost includes an allocated share of fixed cost. If a customer offers ¥14 for an extra 100,000 packs, average costing says "we lose ¥3.38 each"; marginal costing says "we gain 14.00 − 11.58 = ¥2.42 each, so ¥242,000". Both statements are arithmetically correct. The right one depends on whether fixed costs actually change — and that is a question about the decision, not about the cost.

What it means: marginal/contribution reasoning answers "is this extra business worth taking?"; average/full-cost reasoning answers "is the business as a whole viable over the long run?". When it is the wrong tool: contribution reasoning breaks down as soon as the extra volume pushes you into a new fixed-cost step (a second shift, a new warehouse) — at that point the marginal cost jumps, and using ¥11.58 will lose you money.

Why the classification changes with the decision

Take one cost — the ¥3,300,000 of indirect wages at Songlan — through three different decisions:

Decision 1: produce 100,000 more packs this year

Capacity exists, no new supervisors needed. Wages are fixed. Marginal cost is ¥11.58 and the relevant test is contribution.

Decision 2: open a third shift next year

A shift supervisor and two QA staff must be hired. Those wages become variable with respect to the shift decision. The relevant cost is now ¥11.58 plus the new salaries.

Decision 3: close the Qingdao plant entirely

Almost everything becomes avoidable: rent ends, supervisors are made redundant, the machinery is sold. Over a long enough horizon, all costs are variable — which is why "fixed" always means "fixed over some stated time period and range of output".

And one that is neither

Depreciation of ¥860,000 is a fixed cost in the accounts and is never a cash flow. In a cash-flow forecast it does not appear. Confusing the two is the single most common numerical error in this unit.

Exam technique

When a question says "classify the following costs", it is almost always followed by "and explain your answer" or "state how the classification would change if…". Give the classification and the condition: "Rent is a fixed cost in the short run, because it does not vary with output; however, if output doubled the business might need a second warehouse, at which point rent becomes a stepped fixed cost." One extra clause, one extra mark.

3.4 Final accounts

Final accounts are a machine with three moving parts. The trading account works out gross profit; the profit and loss account takes gross profit down to net profit; the balance sheet is a photograph of what the business owns and owes on the last day of the year. They are joined by one number: the retained profit from the P&L is added into the equity section of the balance sheet.

The business is Songlan Foods Ltd, a manufacturer of premium frozen dumplings in Qingdao, selling to supermarket chains and restaurant groups. Figures in ¥000 for the year ended 31 December 2027. Every calculation on the rest of this page uses these numbers.

Proof Why cost of sales is "opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory"
  1. The trading account asks: what did it cost us to make the goods we actually sold this year?
  2. What we had available to sell = what we started with (opening inventory) + what we bought in (purchases, net of returns outwards, plus carriage inwards to get them to the factory).
  3. Whatever is left unsold at the year end (closing inventory) was not sold, so its cost must be taken out and carried forward as a current asset.
  4. Therefore: cost of sales = opening inventory + net purchases + carriage inwards − closing inventory.
  5. Sanity check the sign: if closing inventory is overstated, cost of sales is understated, so gross profit is overstated. That is exactly how an overvalued stock figure inflates profit — and it is a favourite examiner question.

What it means: inventory is a plug between two years' profits. When it is the wrong tool: service businesses have no inventory, so their "cost of sales" collapses to direct labour and direct expenses, and their gross margin means something quite different — never compare a manufacturer's gross margin with a consultancy's.

Proof Why the balance sheet must balance — and how retained profit links the two statements
  1. Every resource the business controls (an asset) was paid for either by the owners or by a lender. So by construction: assets = equity + liabilities. This is not a coincidence to be checked; it is the definition of equity as a residual.
  2. Check with Songlan: assets 22,440 = equity 13,636 + non-current liabilities 5,600 + current liabilities (2,240 + 380 + 584 = 3,204) = 13,636 + 8,804 = 22,440. ✔
  3. Equity = share capital 8,000 + retained earnings 5,636. Retained earnings is the running total of every year's profit that was not paid out as dividends.
  4. Link: opening retained earnings 4,586 + retained profit this year 1,050 = closing retained earnings 5,636. That ¥1,050 is the last line of the P&L and a line inside the balance sheet at the same time.
  5. Therefore anything that changes profit also changes the balance sheet. A revaluation of premises, a bad debt written off, an inventory error — each moves both statements, which is why a ratio that mixes a P&L figure with a balance-sheet figure (ROCE) can be distorted by either.

What it means: the balance sheet is a snapshot at midnight on 31 December; one large receipt on 30 December changes every liquidity ratio. When it is the wrong tool: never use a year-end balance to argue about what the business can pay in March.

3.5 Profitability and liquidity ratio analysis

Here is the attitude that earns the top band. A ratio is not an answer; it is a better question. Every ratio below has four parts in the markscheme: the formula (1), the calculation (1), the interpretation in context (1–2), and the limitation or comparison that shows you know the number is not the whole truth (1–2). Candidates who stop at the calculation get 2 of 4 or 3 of 6.

Exam technique

Write ratios in three strokes, always: calculate → compare → caveat. "Net profit margin is 11.4%" (calculate). "That is up from 9.8% last year and above the 8% typical of the frozen-food sector, suggesting the price rise held" (compare — to last year, to a rival, to the sector, to the cost of borrowing: pick the comparison the stimulus gives you). "However, the margin is measured before interest and it says nothing about whether the extra profit has actually arrived as cash — receivables rose sharply" (caveat). Three sentences, four marks, every time.

Gross profit margin

Formula: GPM = (gross profit ÷ revenue) × 100

The question it answers: "After paying for what we made or bought, how much of every ¥100 of sales is left to run the business and pay the owners?" It isolates the trading decision — buying and pricing — from the overhead decision. Put the sales figure on the bottom rather than cost of sales, because you want "per unit of revenue".

Songlan: (10,420 ÷ 23,520) × 100 = 44.3%.

What "good" depends on: the industry and the business model. A discount grocer runs on 20–25% and survives on volume; a luxury brand runs above 60% and survives on margin. Songlan at 44.3% is a premium branded manufacturer — the right comparison is other branded frozen-food producers (say 38–42%), not the supermarket that sells them.

Limitation: it says nothing about overheads, interest or tax; a business can have a beautiful gross margin and still be loss-making. It is also highly sensitive to the inventory valuation — overstate closing inventory by ¥1m and gross profit, and therefore GPM, rises by ¥1m.

Net profit margin

Formula: NPM = (net profit ÷ revenue) × 100

The question it answers: "Of every ¥100 of sales, how much survives everything — overheads, interest and tax?" This is the bottom-line efficiency test.

Songlan: using operating profit (PBIT), (2,670 ÷ 23,520) × 100 = 11.4%. Using net profit after tax, (1,750 ÷ 23,520) × 100 = 7.4%.

Common trap — which "net profit"?

The IB formula sheet says "net profit ÷ revenue", and different sources mean different things by net profit. At SL the safe move is this: use the profit figure the question supplies, state which one you used, and be consistent across the years you compare. Comparing this year's after-tax margin with last year's pre-tax margin is a genuine and common own goal. Most IB material uses profit before interest and tax, because it makes businesses with different levels of borrowing comparable — and that is the convention used on this page.

What "good" depends on: the sector's competitive intensity and overhead structure; whether the business is geared (interest eats margin); and the tax regime. A 5% net margin is excellent for a supermarket and terrible for a software firm.

Limitation: net margin can rise while the business gets weaker — by cutting marketing or training, by delaying maintenance, or by selling a division. It is a percentage, so it can improve while absolute profit falls (revenue down 30%, profit down 20% → margin up, business worse).

Return on capital employed (ROCE)

Formula: ROCE = (profit before interest and tax ÷ capital employed) × 100, where capital employed = total equity + non-current liabilities, equivalently total assets − current liabilities.

Songlan: capital employed = 13,636 + 5,600 = 19,236 (check: 22,440 − 3,204 = 19,236 ✔). ROCE = (2,670 ÷ 19,236) × 100 = 13.9%.

Proof Why ROCE uses profit before interest, and capital employed rather than equity
  1. The question ROCE answers is: "how hard is all the money invested in this business working, regardless of who supplied it?"
  2. Capital employed = equity + long-term debt = every long-term source of funds. If we used equity alone, a business funded mostly by debt would show a flattering return simply because the denominator is small.
  3. Interest is the reward paid to the lenders who supplied part of that capital. If we deducted it from the numerator, a business would appear to perform worse simply for choosing to borrow — but the borrowing decision is a financing decision, and ROCE deliberately measures the operating performance of the assets.
  4. So: numerator = return to all long-term providers of capital (profit before interest and tax); denominator = all long-term capital. Both sides include debt. The ratio is consistent.
  5. Consequence you can use in an exam: compare ROCE with the interest rate. Songlan earns 13.9% on capital and pays 6% on its loan, so borrowing is currently working in the shareholders' favour — the extra 7.9% accrues to them. This is the "gearing works for you" argument, and it drops straight into a 10-mark evaluation.
  6. Rearrangement worth knowing: if a new project earns less than existing ROCE, it dilutes overall ROCE even if it is profitable in absolute terms. That is exactly the situation in 3.8.

What it means: ROCE is the single best measure of how efficiently management uses the money entrusted to it. When it is the wrong tool: for a business with very old, fully-depreciated assets, capital employed is understated, so ROCE is flatteringly high — a genuine distortion when comparing an old firm with a newly-invested rival.

What "good" depends on: compare with (a) previous years — is performance improving?, (b) rivals in the same industry, (c) the cost of borrowing — ROCE above the interest rate means debt is working for shareholders, and (d) the risk-free alternative and the risk of the industry. 13.9% against a 6% loan is comfortable; 13.9% in a volatile industry may not compensate for the risk.

Limitation: it is based on net book values, which fall each year as depreciation is charged — so ROCE tends to drift upwards on an ageing asset base even when nothing improves. It uses historical cost, ignores inflation, and is a single-year snapshot.

Current ratio

Formula: current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities

The question it answers: "If every short-term debt fell due tomorrow, could we cover it from assets we expect to turn into cash within a year — and how many times over?"

Songlan: 6,460 ÷ 3,204 = 2.02 : 1.

What "good" depends on: the textbook target is 1.5–2, but the real answer is the business model. Supermarkets run below 1 quite safely because they sell for immediate cash and pay suppliers 30–60 days later — their suppliers finance their stock. A construction firm with long projects and stage payments needs far more. Also ask: how fast does the stock sell, and is there an unused overdraft?

Limitation: it treats inventory as if it were cash. Songlan's ratio of 2.02 looks comfortable, but ¥3,120,000 of it is frozen dumplings that may not sell, may be wasted, and cannot be used to pay wages. A high current ratio can actually signal poor management — too much idle stock or too lax credit control.

Acid test (quick) ratio

Formula: acid test = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities

The question it answers: the same as the current ratio but with the least liquid asset removed — "could we pay without selling a single item of stock?"

Songlan: (6,460 − 3,120) ÷ 3,204 = 3,340 ÷ 3,204 = 1.04 : 1.

What "good" depends on: around 1 is the textbook guide, but again depends on whether the business holds stock at all, on its credit terms, and on whether it has an agreed overdraft to fall back on — a facility is not on the balance sheet and yet it is real liquidity.

Limitation: it assumes trade receivables will pay in full and on time; a single large bad debt destroys it. And it ignores the timing of payments entirely — a business can show 1.04 and still be unable to pay next Friday.

Proof Linking the two liquidity ratios: how much stock can you hold before the acid test falls below 1?
  1. Write CA for current assets, INV for inventory, CL for current liabilities.
  2. Current ratio = CA ÷ CL. Acid test = (CA − INV) ÷ CL = (CA ÷ CL) − (INV ÷ CL).
  3. So: acid test = current ratio − (inventory ÷ current liabilities). The gap between the two ratios is exactly the inventory, expressed as a multiple of short-term debts. This is a quick mental check on any balance sheet you are given.
  4. Check with Songlan: 2.02 − (3,120 ÷ 3,204) = 2.02 − 0.97 = 1.04 ✔ — the same answer, and now you can see that inventory accounts for nearly a full point of the current ratio.
  5. Now use it as a decision tool. Acid test < 1 exactly when inventory > CA − CL, i.e. when inventory exceeds working capital. Songlan's working capital is 3,256 and inventory is 3,120, so it is ¥136,000 away from the acid test dropping below 1. That is a thin margin — one bad month of overstocking and the liquidity picture changes.
  6. Extension: this is why a business can "improve" its current ratio by buying more stock on credit (both CA and CL rise, but if CA/CL > 1 the ratio falls — check the direction!). Test it: CA 6,460, CL 3,204, ratio 2.02. Buy 500 of stock on credit: CA 6,960, CL 3,704, ratio 1.88. It falls. So equal increases to both sides always move the ratio towards 1 — a reliable exam insight.

What it means: the current ratio flatters any business that holds a lot of stock; the acid test strips that out. When it is the wrong tool: for a business with no inventory (a consultancy, a software firm) the two ratios are identical and the distinction is meaningless — say so rather than calculating both for the sake of it.

The tension worth an essay

Read the table again: every profitability ratio improved and every liquidity ratio deteriorated. That is not a coincidence — it is the most common pattern in a growing business, and it is the Hengli story in slow motion. Growth pushes up receivables and inventory (both current assets, so liquidity ratios should improve… until the business borrows short-term or pays out dividends to fund it). When you see margins rising and liquidity falling in the same set of accounts, say it out loud and then ask one question: where did the profit go? The answer is almost always in inventory, receivables, new fixed assets, or dividends — and a cash flow forecast will tell you which.

The general limitations of ratio analysis

  1. Historical. They describe the year that has ended, not the year that is coming. In a fast-moving market this is a serious weakness.
  2. No absolute standard. A ratio is meaningless without a comparison — last year, a rival, the sector, or the cost of borrowing. "The current ratio is 2.02" is worth one mark; "2.02, down from 2.45, because receivables grew faster than sales" is worth three.
  3. Different accounting policies. Two firms can use different depreciation methods, inventory valuation methods, or lease treatment, and produce different ratios from identical operations.
  4. Window dressing. Paying off creditors just before the year end, delaying a purchase, or offering a one-off discount to collect cash all improve the year-end snapshot without improving the business.
  5. Inflation. Historical-cost asset values understate replacement cost, which distorts ROCE in particular.
  6. One day, one number. The balance sheet is a snapshot; a seasonal business looks completely different on 31 December than on 30 June.
  7. Qualitative factors are invisible. Brand strength, staff morale, product quality, and a looming lawsuit do not appear in any ratio — and often matter more.

3.7 Cash flow

A cash flow forecast is the only forward-looking statement in this unit, and the only one that can save a business. It lists receipts (money in), payments (money out), net cash flow (in − out) for each period, and rolls a closing balance forward: closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow. Next month's opening balance is this month's closing balance. That single recursion is what makes the forecast a warning system.

How to build one

Five steps, in this order: (1) put the months across the top and decide the period (monthly is standard). (2) List every receipt — cash sales, receipts from credit customers (in the month they actually pay, not the month of sale), loans, asset sales, grants. (3) List every payment — materials (when paid), wages, rent, marketing, admin, tax, dividends, interest, capital expenditure, loan repayments. (4) Net off and roll the balance forward. (5) Read it: find the lowest closing balance and ask what happens if a large customer pays two weeks late. Exclude depreciation entirely — it is not a payment.

Question. Using the forecast above, (a) identify the month in which Songlan Foods faces the greatest cash flow problem and explain why [4]; (b) recommend two ways it could improve the forecast position, evaluating each [4].

Naming the two different causes is what earns the development marks here.
  3. (b) Recommendation 1 (2 marks): Arrange an overdraft facility before April, and negotiate staged payments on the new line (e.g. 30% on order, 40% on delivery, 30% after three months). Application: the supplier's staging alone turns April's −1,387 into roughly −667, leaving a closing balance near ¥780,000. Evaluation: it costs nothing extra if the supplier agrees, but a supplier may demand a higher price for deferred payment, and an overdraft must be arranged while the accounts still look strong — banks are reluctant once a forecast shows a deficit.
  4. (b) Recommendation 2 (2 marks): Defer or reduce the dividend. Application: cancelling the ¥600,000 dividend turns June's −39 into +561. Evaluation: it damages shareholder confidence and a listed company that cuts a promised dividend may see its share price fall; on the other hand it is the most flexible payment in the forecast because it is discretionary.

Answer: June (−¥39,000), caused by the dividend and interest coinciding with a receipts trough; April is the near-miss, caused by paying for a five-year asset in one month. The marks come from separating the two causes and from quantifying the effect of each recommendation — "it would improve cash flow" gets one mark, "it would add ¥600,000 to the June balance" gets two.
Common trap

Four errors dominate this sub-topic. (1) Putting depreciation in the payments column. (2) Putting the sale value of revenue in receipts rather than the cash actually collected — if the stimulus says 60-day credit terms, the January sale arrives in March. (3) Forgetting that the closing balance of one month is the opening balance of the next, so one arithmetic slip propagates through every column. (4) Recommending "increase sales" as a solution — in a cash flow question, selling more on credit usually makes things worse in the short term.

Note — cash flow forecast vs budget

They are not the same thing and the IB examines the difference. A cash flow forecast tracks only money in and out, and is about survival. A budget is a financial plan for revenue, costs and profit, usually departmental, used for control and variance analysis. Budgeting (including variance analysis) is HL only (3.9) — you do not need it.

3.8 Investment appraisal (SL)

Investment appraisal is the formal way of answering "should we spend this money?" At SL you have exactly two quantitative methods: payback period and average rate of return. HL only Net present value (NPV) is not in the SL syllabus — if you use it in an SL answer you will not be given credit for it, because the examiner cannot award marks for a method the programme does not teach at this level. Do not use it.

Payback period

Definition: the length of time it takes for the cumulative net cash flow to recover the initial cost of the investment.

Proof Interpolating payback to the month (and why you assume cash flows arrive evenly)
  1. Find the year in which cumulative net cash flow turns from negative to positive. Here: year 3, because cumulative is −880 at the end of year 2 and +20 at the end of year 3.
  2. At the start of year 3 the amount still unrecovered is ¥880,000. During year 3 the project generates ¥900,000.
  3. Fraction of year 3 needed = 880 ÷ 900 = 0.9778.
  4. Convert to months: 0.9778 × 12 = 11.7 months.
  5. Payback period = 2 years and 11.7 months (≈ 2.98 years).
  6. General form: payback = years before recovery + (amount still to recover ÷ net cash flow in the recovery year).
  7. Hidden assumption: that the ¥900,000 arrives smoothly through year 3. If it actually arrives as one payment in month 12, the true payback is 3 years exactly — which is why the examiner accepts "approximately 3 years" and why the assumption is worth a sentence in an evaluation.

What it means: payback measures risk and liquidity, not profitability — how long the money is at risk. When it is the wrong tool: when cash flows continue long after payback (payback ignores them entirely) or when two projects have similar paybacks but very different total returns.

Average rate of return (ARR)

Formula (IB form): ARR = (net return per annum ÷ initial cost of investment) × 100, where net return per annum = total net cash flow over the project's life ÷ number of years of use.

Proof Computing ARR from the cash flows, and why the initial cost is not double-counted
  1. Total net cash flow over the five years of income = 700 + 820 + 900 + 760 + 770 = ¥3,950,000.
  2. Subtract the initial cost of ¥2,400,000 to get the total net return (the profit the project makes over its whole life) = 3,950 − 2,400 = ¥1,550,000. Note this equals the final cumulative figure in the table above — a useful cross-check.
  3. Divide by the years of use to get the average annual net return: 1,550 ÷ 5 = ¥310,000 per year.
  4. Express as a percentage of the money tied up: ARR = (310 ÷ 2,400) × 100 = 12.9%.
  5. Why the initial cost appears in both places: it is subtracted once to measure the profit (step 2), and used once as the denominator to express that profit as a return on the investment (step 4). Both are necessary; using 3,950 ÷ 5 ÷ 2,400 instead would give 32.9% and would be wrong, because it treats the return of the original capital as profit.
  6. Decision rule: compare the ARR with a target rate, with the cost of borrowing, or with the ARR of an alternative project. Here: 12.9% versus a 6% loan rate (clearly worthwhile) but versus Songlan's existing ROCE of 13.9% (slightly dilutive).

What it means: ARR is the only SL method that gives a percentage return, so it is the one that lets you compare an investment with the interest rate. When it is the wrong tool: when the timing of cash flows matters, because ARR averages them and therefore treats ¥700,000 in year 1 exactly like ¥700,000 in year 5.

Worked example 3 IB-style · 10 marks · Section A, quantitative

Question. Songlan's board requires all capital projects to pay back within three years and to earn an ARR of at least 12%. Using the data above, calculate the payback period and the ARR, and recommend whether the new production line should be purchased. [10]

  1. Payback, method (1): accumulate net cash flow until it turns positive.
  2. Payback, working (2): −2,400 → −1,700 → −880 → +20. Recovery during year 3.
  3. Payback, calculation (1): 2 years + (880 ÷ 900) × 12 = 2 years 11.7 months.
  4. Payback, apply to the criterion (1): 2.98 years < 3 years, so it meets the payback criterion — but only just, and only if the year-3 cash flow arrives evenly.
  5. ARR, working (2): total net return = 3,950 − 2,400 = 1,550; average annual net return = 1,550 ÷ 5 = 310.
  6. ARR, calculation (1): (310 ÷ 2,400) × 100 = 12.9%.
  7. ARR, apply (1): 12.9% > 12%, so it meets the ARR criterion; it also comfortably exceeds the 6% cost of borrowing.
  8. Recommend with a caveat (1–2): yes, purchase — both criteria are met. But note the honest limitation the board should hear: the ARR of 12.9% is below Songlan's existing ROCE of 13.9%, so the project will slightly dilute overall return on capital even though it adds absolute profit; and both criteria ignore the fact that the cash flows are forecasts in an industry where raw-material costs move.
  9. Qualitative factors (needed for a full evaluation): the line may improve consistency and hygiene (fewer product recalls — a real risk in frozen food); it affects staff, who may need retraining or redundancies; automation changes the "people" and "process" elements of the marketing mix (see Unit 4) if it allows new pack formats; and forecasts of year-3-to-5 cash flows are far less reliable than year 1.
Answer: Payback 2 years 11.7 months; ARR 12.9%. Buy — both hurdles are cleared and the return exceeds the cost of borrowing by a wide margin. The top-band sentence is the one about dilution: 12.9% < 13.9% ROCE.

Limitations — learn these as pairs

Payback period — limitations

  • Ignores all cash flows after payback, so it can reject the most profitable project.
  • Ignores the timing of cash flows within the payback period.
  • Ignores the time value of money (¥1 tomorrow is not ¥1 today).
  • Gives no measure of absolute profitability — it is a clock, not a return.
  • The cut-off period is arbitrary and may encourage short-termism.

Average rate of return — limitations

  • Averages the cash flows, so it ignores timing entirely (this is the key contrast with discounted methods).
  • Ignores the time value of money.
  • Based on forecast cash flows over five years, which get less reliable each year.
  • It is a percentage, so it can favour a small project over a larger one that makes more total profit.
  • Usually ignores residual value sensitivity, and it uses accounting profit concepts rather than pure cash.

Why businesses still use both: payback is easy to understand, quick, and directly answers the question a cash-poor business actually cares about — how long before the money comes back. ARR gives a percentage that can be compared with the interest rate on a loan. Neither is theoretically ideal; both are practically useful, and the IB wants you to say precisely that.

Practice

  1. [AO2, 4 marks] Hengli Display Ltd made a profit of ¥2.65 million in month 4 and was declared insolvent in the same month. Explain how both of those things can be true.

    Show answer
    1. Knowledge (1–2): Profit is revenue earned minus expenses incurred over a period, calculated on the accruals principle — it counts sales when they are made, not when customers pay. Cash is money actually available on a given day.
    2. Application (1–2): Hengli sells on 60-day credit, so a large and growing share of its profit sits in trade receivables. Meanwhile wages, overheads and materials must be paid monthly. Every month that sales grow, the amount locked in receivables grows too.
    3. Development (1): The profit figure therefore represents value created but not yet received; the cash balance is negative because outflows occur before the matching inflows.
    4. Full-mark extra: note that depreciation is irrelevant here but capital expenditure on tooling (¥2m) is not — it is a cash outflow that never appears in the month's profit calculation. Insolvency is a cash test, so a profitable business fails it.
    Marks: 1–2 for defining the two terms, 1–2 for applying the credit-terms lag to Hengli, 1 for the conclusion that survival depends on cash timing, not profit.

  2. [AO3, 6 marks] A sole trader running a profitable small bakery with no savings and no assets to offer as security needs ¥400,000 to open a second shop, which is expected to be profitable for at least ten years. Recommend the most appropriate source of finance.

    Show answer
    1. Matching (1): a ten-year asset needs long-term finance, which rules out an overdraft or trade credit as the main source.
    2. Choose (1): a long-term bank loan (or a mortgage on the new premises if the lender requires security, which also solves the security problem since the property itself is the collateral).
    3. Apply — availability (1–2): the business is profitable, so a bank is likely to lend on the strength of the forecast cash flows; the new property provides security the trader currently lacks; control is not diluted, which matters to a sole trader.
    4. Develop — cost and risk (1): interest must be paid regardless of how the second shop performs, and monthly repayments reduce the cash available if trade is slow.
    5. Contrast (1): a business angel or a partner would bring equity with no interest and no security requirement, but the sole trader would give up part of the ownership and the profits — and retained profit is unavailable because past profits have already been drawn as drawings.
    Marks: marks are awarded for the matching sentence, the choice, two pieces of application (security, profitability, control), and the balanced limitation/contrast. Simply naming a bank loan without justification scores 1–2.

  3. [AO4, 6 marks] Songlan Foods is offered a one-off order for 100,000 packs at ¥14.00 each. Variable cost per pack is ¥11.58 and there is spare capacity. (a) Calculate the effect on profit of accepting the order. (b) State two non-financial factors that should be checked before accepting.

    Show answer
    1. Contribution per pack on the order = 14.00 − 11.58 = ¥2.42.
    2. Total contribution = 2.42 × 100,000 = ¥242,000.
    3. Fixed costs do not change (there is spare capacity), so profit increases by the full ¥242,000.
    4. Sanity check: the price is below average cost (¥17.38) but above marginal cost (¥11.58), so accepting adds profit. This is the whole point of marginal costing.
    5. Non-financial factors (2): (i) will existing supermarket customers discover the lower price and demand the same terms, or will it damage the premium brand positioning? (ii) can quality and food-safety standards be maintained on a rush order, and is there enough working capital to fund the extra materials before the customer pays?
    Answer: +¥242,000 profit — accept, subject to the brand and quality checks. Note that using average cost would wrongly suggest a ¥338,000 loss.

  4. [AO4 + AO2, 8 marks] A business has inventory ¥180,000, trade receivables ¥120,000, cash ¥20,000, trade payables ¥150,000 and a short-term loan ¥50,000. (a) Calculate the current ratio and the acid test ratio. (b) The business then buys ¥60,000 of inventory on credit. Recalculate both ratios and comment on what this shows.

    Show answer
    1. Current assets = 180 + 120 + 20 = ¥320,000. Current liabilities = 150 + 50 = ¥200,000.
    2. Current ratio = 320 ÷ 200 = 1.6 : 1.
    3. Acid test = (320 − 180) ÷ 200 = 140 ÷ 200 = 0.70 : 1.
    4. After buying ¥60,000 of stock on credit: CA = 380, CL = 260. Current ratio = 380 ÷ 260 = 1.46 : 1. Acid test = (380 − 240) ÷ 260 = 140 ÷ 260 = 0.54 : 1.
    5. Comment: both ratios fell. The current ratio fell because equal additions to both sides move the ratio towards 1 when it starts above 1. The acid test fell much more sharply, because the transaction added to inventory only — and inventory is exactly what the acid test excludes.
    6. Insight worth a mark: the acid test of 0.54 shows the business could not pay its short-term debts without selling stock, and the transaction made that materially worse while appearing to be a routine purchase.
    Answer: 1.6 : 1 and 0.70 : 1, falling to 1.46 : 1 and 0.54 : 1. The lesson: a purchase on credit can weaken liquidity even though it adds assets.

  5. [AO4 + AO2, 6 marks] Using Songlan's cash flow forecast, calculate the closing balance for March, April and May, and identify the month with the greatest risk. Assume the figures are as given in the 3.7 table.

    Show answer
    1. March: net cash flow = 1,996 − 2,101 = −105. Opening balance 1,563 → closing 1,458.
    2. April: net cash flow = 2,008 − 3,395 = −1,387. Opening 1,458 → closing 71.
    3. May: net cash flow = 2,112 − 1,720 = +392. Opening 71 → closing 463.
    4. Greatest risk: April, because the closing balance of ¥71,000 leaves almost no buffer — a single late payment from a supermarket chain would push it negative. (June is technically worse at −39, but April is the point at which the problem becomes visible and avoidable.)
    5. Cause: paying the full ¥2.4m for a five-year asset in one month. The fix is to phase the payments or to fund the asset with a matched loan, as argued in 3.2.
    Answer: 1,458 / 71 / 463 (¥000). April is the danger month.

  6. [AO4 + AO3, 10 marks] Two projects are available. Project A costs ¥1,000,000 and generates net cash flows of ¥400,000, ¥400,000, ¥300,000 and ¥200,000 over four years. Project B costs ¥1,000,000 and generates ¥100,000, ¥300,000, ¥500,000 and ¥900,000 over the same four years, with no residual value. (a) Calculate the payback period and the ARR for each. (b) Recommend one, justifying your choice.

    Show answer
    1. Project A cumulative: −1,000 → −600 → −200 → +100. Payback in year 3: 2 years + (200 ÷ 300) × 12 = 2 years 8 months.
    2. Project A ARR: total inflows = 400 + 400 + 300 + 200 = 1,300. Net return = 1,300 − 1,000 = 300. Average annual = 300 ÷ 4 = 75. ARR = (75 ÷ 1,000) × 100 = 7.5%.
    3. Project B cumulative: −1,000 → −900 → −600 → −100 → +800. Payback in year 4: 3 years + (100 ÷ 900) × 12 = 3 years 1.3 months.
    4. Project B ARR: total inflows = 100 + 300 + 500 + 900 = 1,800. Net return = 800. Average annual = 200. ARR = (200 ÷ 1,000) × 100 = 20.0%.
    5. Recommendation: choose B. It earns 20% against A's 7.5% and generates ¥800,000 of total profit against A's ¥300,000 — the payback method alone would have pointed to A, which is precisely the method's weakness.
    6. Balanced evaluation for the top band: but B's payback is 3 years 1 month against A's 2 years 8 months, and B's cash flows are heavily back-loaded (¥900,000 in year 4), so B is riskier: if the market changes in year 3, most of B's return never arrives. A pays back faster and suits a business that is short of cash. If the business has a strong cash position and can wait, choose B; if liquidity is tight, A may be the safer choice despite the lower return.
    Answer: A: 2 yr 8 mth, ARR 7.5%. B: 3 yr 1.3 mth, ARR 20.0%. Recommend B on return, A on risk — the balanced answer commits to one and explains the condition under which it would switch.

  7. [AO3, 10 marks] "Ratio analysis tells a manager everything they need to know about a business." To what extent do you agree with this statement?

    Show answer

    Structure for a 9–10 answer: a short judgement in the introduction, two developed arguments for, two developed against with limitations, and a committed conclusion that answers "to what extent".

    1. Judgement: ratios are a useful starting point for diagnosis but a poor substitute for understanding the business; they tell you what to ask, not what to do.
    2. For (AO2): they convert raw accounts into comparable numbers, allowing comparison with prior years, rivals and sector averages — ROCE of 13.9% against a 6% loan rate tells a manager immediately that borrowing is currently working. They are quick, cheap and objective.
    3. For (AO2): liquidity ratios give an early warning of insolvency, which no profit figure does; a current ratio falling from 2.45 to 2.02 while profit rises is exactly the signal a manager needs.
    4. Against — limitations (AO3): they are historical, based on a single day's balance sheet, distorted by inflation and by different accounting policies (depreciation and inventory valuation), and vulnerable to window dressing at the year end.
    5. Against — what they omit (AO3): they say nothing about brand strength, staff morale, product quality, competitor behaviour or a pending lawsuit. They can also conflict: cutting marketing raises net profit margin this year and weakens next year's revenue.
    6. Conclusion: agree only to a limited extent — ratio analysis is valuable for identifying questions and monitoring trends, but must be combined with cash flow forecasting and qualitative judgement before any decision is taken.
    Marks: a one-sided descriptive answer sits at 5–6; adding the limitations and a committed "to a limited extent" judgement with example moves it to 9–10.

  8. [AO2, 4 marks] Explain why the classification of a cost as fixed or variable depends on the decision being taken, using one example.

    Show answer
    1. Knowledge (1–2): a fixed cost does not vary with output over a stated time period and range of output; a variable cost varies directly with output. "Fixed" is therefore a statement about a particular time horizon and output range, not a property of the cost itself.
    2. Application (1–2): take supervisory salaries at a factory. For a decision about producing 10,000 extra units this month within existing capacity, they are fixed — no extra supervisor is needed, so the marginal cost of the extra units is the variable cost only. For a decision to add a third shift next year, an extra supervisor must be hired, so the same salaries are variable with respect to that decision. Over a long enough period, and in a decision to close the site entirely, all costs become avoidable.
    3. Development (1): therefore the correct classification is chosen after the decision is defined, not before — which is why managers use contribution (relevant) costing for short-term decisions and full costing for long-term viability.
    Marks: 2 for the definition with the period/range qualification, 2 for the worked example showing the same cost changing category.
