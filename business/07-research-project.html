The business research project (internal assessment)

This is the only piece of work in the whole Diploma that you choose, research, write and rewrite over eighteen months — and at standard level it is worth 30% of your final grade, more than the internal assessment in any other Group 3 subject you could have taken. It is also the component that most candidates throw away, not because it is hard but because they write the wrong thing: a description of a company instead of an argument about a decision. This page is written so that you never make that mistake. It covers what the task is, how to pick an organisation you can actually research, how to make one of the four key concepts do real analytical work, how to write a research question that evidence can answer, how to choose the three to five supporting documents, what each of the seven criteria is really asking for, and how to spend your 1,800 words so that none of them are wasted.

Why this component exists

The external papers test whether you can apply business management thinking to a business somebody else has chosen, under time pressure, with no choice of evidence. That is a genuinely useful skill and it is not what a manager does. A manager is handed a messy, specific problem in one organisation, decides what evidence would help, goes and gets it, applies two or three frameworks, weighs what the evidence cannot tell her, and commits to a recommendation in front of people who will be affected by it. The research project is the only assessment in the course that reproduces that sequence. Everything the course values — choosing rather than being told, applying rather than defining, weighing rather than listing — is concentrated in these 1,800 words, which is exactly why they carry the weight they do.

Trap — the internet is full of the wrong task

The guide that applies to you (first assessment 2024) replaced the old internal assessment entirely. The old SL task was a 1,500-word written commentary based mainly on secondary research, with no key concept anywhere in it. The old HL task was a 500-word research proposal plus a 2,000-word written report. Both are gone. There is now one task, the business research project, at both levels, marked with the same seven criteria and the same 1,800-word limit. A very large number of websites, tuition centres, YouTube channels and even some published workbooks still describe the old tasks: if a source tells you to write a "written commentary", to produce a "research proposal", to use a 1,500-word limit, or gives you a list of criteria that does not begin with integration of a key concept, it is describing a course that no longer exists. Check the criteria names on this page against whatever you are reading. If they do not match A–G below, close the tab.

Where this sits

The research project is not a separate skill bolted onto the course — it is the five units turned inward. Everything you can do in Unit 3, Unit 4 and Unit 5 becomes evidence here, and the tools you apply are the eight on the toolkit page. The subject-level map and the assessment arithmetic are on the business management index. One warning from the start: the discipline of writing a researchable question is the same discipline the extended essay demands, but the two projects must stay completely separate — see academic honesty below.

1. What the task actually is

Here it is honestly, in one paragraph. You choose one real organisation. You identify a real issue or problem it faces that is connected to something in the syllabus. You choose one of four key concepts — change, creativity, ethics or sustainability — write it on the title page, and use it as the lens through which you examine that issue for the whole project. You write a research question which may look forward ("should the organisation do X?") or backward ("how successful was the organisation's decision to do X?"). You attach between three and five documents that you selected yourself, published within three years of your submission, each no longer than five A4 pages, showing a range of views. You gather whatever additional primary and/or secondary research you need. Then you write a maximum of 1,800 words in which you apply business management tools and theories to the evidence, analyse and evaluate it through your lens, and reach a conclusion that answers your question. That is the whole thing. Moderators stop reading at word 1,800.

Two sentences in that paragraph decide almost everything about your mark. The first is "a research project" — not a report, not a case study, not a company profile. A report says what is the case; a research project asks a question and works towards an answer that was not obvious at the start. The second is "maximum 1,800 words". This is not a guideline with a tolerance. If your analysis section runs to 2,300 words and your conclusion begins at word 1,810, then your conclusion has not been read and cannot earn a single mark — which caps criterion E at zero and usually damages D as well, because the evaluation that D rewards is normally where you put it last. Budget for 1,700 and treat the last hundred as spillage.

One mark on the research project is worth more than one mark on either paper, and it is the only mark in the subject you can earn twice, because you can draft it, get feedback, and draft it again. Five marks — the difference between a project that describes and a project that evaluates, or between a project with a conclusion and a project without one — is six percentage points of your final grade. Grade boundary steps in Group 3 subjects are typically around seven to ten percentage points apart, so five marks on this project is most of a whole grade.

Now the other side of the arithmetic. The guide suggests roughly 20 hours of class time for the project. Twenty hours is a little over three school days. There is no other way in this subject to buy six percentage points with three days of work — but only if those three days are spent choosing well and then writing an argument, not spent writing a company history. And note the mirror image of that optimism: because the project is 30%, a rushed project is not a small problem. A 14/25 is worth 16.8 percentage points; a 21/25 is worth 25.2. That is an eight-point hole in your final grade that no amount of Paper 2 skill will fill.

The three free-est marks in the subject

Criteria F and G together are worth 4 marks — 4.8 percentage points — and they test nothing but discipline. G asks for exactly four things: a title page (carrying your key concept), an accurate table of contents, headings and sub-headings, and numbered pages. Miss any one and you drop to 1. F asks whether the project is organised appropriately: a reader can tell in ten seconds whether a project has a real structure or is one long scroll. Do this: after your final edit, regenerate the contents, re-paginate, and read the title page to check the key concept is still on it. Ten minutes, two marks, no intellectual effort required — and a remarkable number of candidates do not do it.

2. Choosing the organisation and the issue

Every strong project starts from a decision; every weak project starts from a company. When a candidate says "I want to do my IA on [famous multinational]", what they usually mean is that they want to write about a brand they admire — and what they then produce is a description of that brand's strategy, which scores Criterion C's bottom band and Criterion D's bottom band, because nothing has been applied to a question and no data has been used to weigh anything. The organisation is not the topic. The organisation is where the decision happens.

So the rule is: a real organisation you can get information about beats an impressive multinational you cannot. Not because big companies are forbidden — they are not, and plenty of excellent projects study listed firms — but because of a mechanical fact about this assessment. You have 1,800 words. That is about seven double-spaced pages. In seven pages you can examine one decision in one organisation with three or four pieces of evidence. You cannot examine "the marketing strategy" of anything. A large, well-documented company seduces you into a question that is too big, and then you spend your word budget on context instead of analysis. A small organisation with a sharp, specific decision fits the word limit like a key in a lock.

The viability test — three questions, all must be yes

Before you fall in love with an organisation, put it through this. If any answer is no, choose something else. This takes twenty minutes and saves you a term.

1. The information test

Can you already name, right now, two substantial, independent, published sources about this organisation dated within the last three years — and one source you could create yourself (an interview, a survey, a visit)? "Independent" means they are not the same text republished: a company press release and the news article that copies it are one source, not two. If you cannot name them, you are hoping, not planning.

2. The decision test

Can you state a specific decision the organisation faces or has just made, in one sentence, with a genuine yes and a genuine no? "Should it expand?" is not a decision, it is a mood. "Should it open a fourth site in Binjiang district in 2027, or add a second shift at the existing three?" is a decision. If you cannot name the alternative that was rejected, you have no evaluation to do, and criterion D has nothing to bite on.

3. The change test

Has something actually changed, or is something about to change? A competitor arriving, a cost doubling, a regulation landing, a founder retiring, a landlord not renewing, a customer concentrating, a technology dropping in price. If nothing is moving in this organisation, there is no issue to research — there is only a description waiting to happen. Change is not one of the four key concepts you may choose; it is a property that every viable issue has.

A fourth, softer question is worth asking too: the access test. Is there a human being you could plausibly contact — a manager, an owner, a former employee, a supplier, a regular customer — or a group of customers you could survey? You do not need primary research to pass. You do need it if your question asks about something that has never been written down, like staff morale, customer motives, or why a decision was really taken.

How to use it — the twenty-minute audit
  1. Write down five candidate organisations. Not one — five. Include at least one you have a personal route into (family business, part-time employer, a shop you use, a club's sponsor).
  2. For each, spend four minutes searching. Record: the two best published sources with their dates, and the single clearest decision you can see them facing.
  3. Score each organisation 0–3 against the three tests above.
  4. Choose the one scoring 3, not the one with the most famous name. If two score 3, choose the one where the decision is sharpest.
  5. Only then write a first research question — and expect to rewrite it four times.

3. The four key concepts: change, creativity, ethics, sustainability

You must choose exactly one of these four and state it on the title page. The choice is worth five marks on its own (criterion A) and quietly influences D and E as well, because the lens decides what counts as relevant evidence. It is also the single most mismanaged part of the task: the guide's descriptors move from "demonstrates knowledge of the key concept" (1 mark) through "describes the connection" (2) and "analyses the connection" (3) to "partially integrates" (4) and "effectively integrates … throughout" (5). Most candidates write the word once on the title page, once in the introduction, and then never again. That is 2 marks out of 5, and it costs six percentage points' worth of opportunity.

Change

Change treats the organisation as something in motion. It asks what is driving the movement, what is being left behind, how quickly it is happening, who gains and who loses, and what the organisation's capacity to absorb it actually is. A change lens never accepts "we will just do it" — it asks what stands in the way and what the transition itself costs in money, morale, capability and reputation. In business management terms, this lens reaches naturally into Unit 2's material on organisational change and resistance, into Ansoff (which is literally a map of how much change a strategy demands), and into decisions under uncertainty where you must weigh the cost of acting against the cost of waiting.

The distinctive move a change lens makes is to price the status quo. Most students analyse the proposed option and forget that not changing is also a decision with a cost. "Hanyu's current kiln schedule is not a neutral baseline — it is a commitment to a fuel cost structure that has already risen twice in three years" is a change sentence. It turns "do nothing" from the absence of a decision into the riskiest option on the table.

Creativity

Creativity treats the organisation as a place where new ideas either get generated or get suffocated. It asks where ideas come from in this business, whether there is slack (time, money, permission) to try them, what happens to a failed idea, and whether the option set on the table is unreasonably narrow. A creativity lens is the one that lets you legitimately invent an option the organisation has not considered — and then test it with the same tools you used on the real options. That is deeply satisfying to write and it scores well, because constructing a third way and then evaluating it is exactly what criterion D calls analysis.

Its danger is vagueness. "Be more creative" is not analysis. Creativity must land on a specific mechanism: an intrapreneurship budget, a staff idea scheme with a named owner, a partnership with a design institute, a pilot with a defined stop condition. If you cannot say who would do it, what it would cost and how you would know in six months whether it worked, you are writing about creativity as a mood rather than as a management decision.

Ethics

Ethics treats the organisation as a set of obligations to people who are not all in the room. It asks who is affected by the decision, who has power over it, whose consent was sought, what the organisation says it believes, and whether its behaviour matches. The crucial discipline here is that ethics is not the same as legality: if the law settles the question, there is no ethical dilemma to analyse and the lens collapses into compliance. Ethical analysis lives precisely where the law is silent or weak — a redundancy process that is legal but brutal, a sourcing decision that is legal but depends on a supplier no one audits, a marketing claim that is technically true and practically misleading.

The strongest ethical analysis is two-sided and costs something. If acting ethically is also free and easy, you have not found a dilemma; you have found a bargain. Write the cost honestly: "paying the audited supplier adds roughly 6% to unit cost, which at current volumes reduces gross margin by X — that is the actual price of the ethical position, and the question is whether the organisation can carry it." Then evaluate whether it can. That is criterion D work, done through a criterion A lens.

Sustainability

Sustainability is the most chosen and most misunderstood of the four. It is not a synonym for "environmental". In this course sustainability means meeting present needs without compromising the ability to meet future ones, and that definition has three legs: environmental (finite inputs, waste, emissions), social (the workforce and community the organisation depends on) and financial viability over time. A project asking whether a business can survive on its current cash cycle is a sustainability project even though nothing in it is green.

A sustainability lens asks: what input does this business model consume that will not always be available or affordable? What waste does it produce and who currently pays for it? Is the current position a path or a plateau? And — the question students forget — what would the circular alternative look like, because the toolkit's circular business model tool exists precisely to turn that question into structure.

Trap — choosing a lens your evidence cannot carry

Lucas, this one is aimed at you specifically, because a maths/physics mind will reach for the lens that makes the question interesting rather than the one the documents support. If every document you can find is about cost, a sustainability lens will be decorative and criterion A will sit at 2–3 no matter how earnestly you write about the planet. Match lens to evidence, and if you love a lens, go and find the document that makes it load-bearing. The reverse also happens: a candidate picks "ethics" because it sounds serious, and then discovers the only ethical content available is a corporate code of conduct PDF — which is a statement of intent, not evidence of practice, and gives you nothing to evaluate.

The habit that earns criterion A

A lens is integrated when the reader could not remove it without the project falling apart. Practically, that means your key concept appears in five places, doing work in each:

  1. On the title page — one of the four words, exactly as spelled in the guide. This is also part of criterion G.
  2. In the introduction — you define the concept in your own words and justify why it is the right lens for this organisation and this issue. One sentence of justification: "sustainability is the right lens because the decision is about an input (kaolin and gas) whose price and availability determine whether the business exists in ten years."
  3. At the head of every analysis section — a "lens sentence" that frames what the section is about to show. This is the single highest-value habit on the whole page.
  4. Inside the tools — the SWOT's opportunities are environmental, the decision tree's probabilities are about a carbon price, the investment appraisal includes the avoided waste cost. A tool run through the lens is what separates 4 from 5.
  5. In the conclusion and the limitations — the recommendation is qualified by the lens, and you state what the lens could not tell you. "A sustainability lens cannot by itself settle whether Hanyu can finance the conversion; that is why the payback calculation in section 3 matters."
How to check it — the highlighter test

When you think you are finished, use the highlighting tool on every occurrence of your key concept and its word family (sustainability / sustainable / sustain; ethics / ethical; creativity / creative; change / changing). Then read only the highlighted sentences in order. If they form a coherent argument that develops across the project, you are at 4–5. If they are three sentences in the introduction and one in the conclusion, you are at 2. If there are only two, you are at 1. If the highlighted word is not one of the four — "globalisation", "innovation", "corporate social responsibility", "digitalisation" — you are at 0, because the criterion explicitly awards 0 when the concept identified is neither change, creativity, ethics nor sustainability. Innovation is not creativity. Check the spelling.

Worked example 1 Writing lens sentences that earn criterion A

Situation. Beiyu Coffee, a nine-site specialty coffee chain, is deciding whether to close its two weakest sites and sell only through delivery platforms. The chosen lens is sustainability. Here is the same factual point written four ways.

  1. 0 marks (wrong concept): "This decision is an example of digitalisation, which is changing the coffee industry." — Digitalisation is not one of the four. Criterion A is zero.
  2. 1–2 marks (knowledge, then description): "Sustainability means meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Beiyu is considering closing two sites." — Definition present, connection asserted but not analysed. The two sentences could belong to different essays.
  3. 3 marks (analysis, in one place): "Read through a sustainability lens, the closure decision is really a question about which of Beiyu's two resource bases is more durable: the footfall of two high-street locations, which has fallen for six consecutive quarters, or the packaging-and-logistics intensity of a delivery-only model, which grows with every order and is currently unpriced." — Genuine analysis, but if this is the only such paragraph, integration is not "throughout".
  4. 4–5 marks (integrated): the paragraph above, plus a lens sentence opening each subsequent section ("The sustainability case for closure rests on the fixed cost of two loss-making sites…"; "The sustainability case against is that delivery packaging moves a cost from Beiyu's rent line onto a waste stream nobody in the transaction pays for…"), plus a decision tree whose probabilities are attached to a packaging levy, plus a conclusion that says "to a limited extent, and conditional on the packaging question — because a model that is cheaper this year and more wasteful in every year after is not sustainable in the sense this project has used the word."
What to copy: the mechanical pattern — one lens sentence at the head of each analysis section, the lens built into at least one tool, and the lens used to qualify the final recommendation. That pattern is worth about four marks, or nearly five percentage points.

4. Writing the research question

A research question is not a title and it is not a topic. It is the sentence that decides whether the next six months are easy or miserable, because it determines what evidence you need, which tools are relevant, what your conclusion must say, and whether criterion E ("explicitly answers the research question") is even achievable. Get it wrong and you will discover the problem in March, when it is too late to change organisation.

The guide's own examples are a good model of the register: "Should company Y change its manufacturing to outsourcing?" (change) and "How can airline X successfully target segment Y?" (sustainability). Both name an organisation, both centre on a decision or a problem, and both are small enough to answer. Notice also that they are plainly answerable either way — nothing in them presupposes the conclusion.

Seven tests for a research question

  1. The one-organisation test. Does it name exactly one organisation? "Should Chinese ceramic manufacturers adopt recycled clay?" is an industry question; you would be writing about an average firm that does not exist.
  2. The decision test. Is there a decision or a problem, not a topic area? "How does Hanyu manage quality?" has no decision in it. "To what extent should Hanyu accept a 4% yield loss in exchange for a recycled body?" does.
  3. The answerability test. Could you actually get the evidence in about twenty hours, from real documents and one primary method? If answering it requires Hanyu's internal management accounts and you have no route to them, it fails.
  4. The not-a-fact test. If the answer is a single piece of information that somebody has already published, it is a lookup, not a research question. "What is Hanyu's current export share?" is not a research question; it is a sentence in a document.
  5. The genuine-doubt test. Is there a real case on both sides? If you already know the answer, or if nobody sensible could disagree with you, there is nothing to evaluate and criterion D's top band is unreachable.
  6. The lens-fit test. Can your chosen key concept be applied to this question without contortion? Say your lens sentence out loud: "read through a sustainability lens, the question of whether Hanyu should…" If the sentence dies halfway, change the question or change the lens.
  7. The scale test. Can you answer it properly in 1,700 words? Count the evidence: if an adequate answer would need eight documents, four tools and three stakeholder groups, the question is too big. Split it.
How to use it — the question upgrade procedure
  1. Write the topic version first, deliberately: "Hanyu Ceramics and recycled clay."
  2. Turn it into a yes/no decision: "Should Hanyu use recycled clay?"
  3. Add a quantity and a date — vagueness is the enemy of evaluation: "Should Hanyu convert 30% of output to a recycled clay body by 2028?"
  4. Change should to to what extent should so that the answer can be balanced rather than binary.
  5. Add the tension that makes it interesting: "…given the yield loss and the energy cost of firing it?"
  6. Check it against the seven tests. Fix whichever one fails.

Six steps, about twenty minutes, and you will have a question your teacher can approve in one email.

Forward-looking or backward-looking

Both are permitted and each has a distinct risk profile. A forward-looking question ("should X do Y?") asks you to make and defend a recommendation. Its strength is that evaluation is unavoidable; its risk is that the future is unevidenced, so you must state your assumptions explicitly and test them. A backward-looking question ("to what extent was X's decision to do Y successful?") has the advantage that the outcomes already exist and can be measured; its risk is that you slip into narrating what happened rather than judging whether it worked, and you must therefore define "success" yourself and against explicit criteria before you can measure anything.

Whichever you choose, the top band of criterion D rewards "consideration of the assumptions underpinning the arguments". A forward-looking project makes those assumptions visible almost for free: write them down as a short list ("this recommendation assumes gas prices stay within X, that the EU buyer requirement is not delayed, and that yield loss can be held below 4%") and then attack your own list. That single habit is the most reliable route to 5 on D.

Connection — the extended essay

Every one of the seven tests above is a research-question test first and a business test second, and you will meet the same discipline in the extended essay. The difference is scale and purpose: the EE is 4,000 words, must be a question you genuinely cannot answer from existing literature, and is assessed on how you engage with a subject's methodology. The business research project is 1,800 words about a decision in one organisation and is assessed on whether you applied tools to evidence. Do not use the same question for both — see academic honesty.

5. The three to five supporting documents

The supporting documents are the spine of the project: criterion B judges your selection, criterion D judges how you use their data, and in practice they decide whether you have anything to say at all. They are attached to the project, not written into it, so the documents themselves do not consume your 1,800 words. What does consume words is anything you copy into the body — a pasted table, a long quotation, a transcript extract. Keep the documents as attachments and quote short fragments.

Why "a range of views" is assessed, and how to get it

Think about what the project is for. You are not being tested on your ability to find sources that agree with you; you are being tested on your ability to weigh evidence that points in different directions. Three documents that all endorse the organisation's plan give you nothing to weigh, so your "analysis" becomes summary, and criterion D stalls at 2–3 no matter how well you write. One good contradictory document is worth more than two more agreeing ones, because it forces the move that D's top band describes: examining the assumptions behind an argument.

A reliable way to build the set is to fill five roles, though with three or four documents you will merge some:

The internal view

Something the organisation says about itself: annual report, sustainability update, press release, website page, recruitment material. Invaluable, and never neutral. Its value is often as evidence of what the firm wants to be seen to believe, which you then test against the external evidence.

The external critical view

An NGO report, a union statement, a critical news investigation, employee reviews, a regulator's enforcement notice, a competitor's public claim. This is the document that lets you disagree — and disagreeing with a source is analysis.

The quantitative view

Government statistics, industry association data, published price series, market research with sample sizes. At least one document must give you numbers, because criterion D is about the "selection and use of data".

The contextual view

Regulation, a standard, a sector outlook, a demographic series. This is what stops your analysis treating the organisation as if it floats free of its environment.

The primary view you created

Your interview transcript or your survey results. Optional but powerful: it is evidence nobody else has, it is indisputably student-selected, and it is the cleanest way to produce a genuine range of views when the published material is one-sided.

The test before you commit

Make a table with columns: document, who produced it, what they want, what it lets me claim, what it lets me challenge. If the "what it lets me challenge" column has fewer than two real entries, go back and change one document.

Trap — five documents that are really one

The most common way to lose criterion B from 4 to 2 is a set of five documents that are the same event reported five times: a company press release, three news articles based on it, and a trade-magazine summary of the same story. That is one source with five URLs. It is also a silent academic-honesty problem, because you will appear to have five independent corroborations when you have one. Ask of each document: who produced this, and what would they lose if they were wrong? If the answer is the same twice, you have duplicates.

6. The criteria, one by one

The sections below use the official guiding questions and band language. Read them as an examiner would: each criterion is asking "how far up this ladder are you?", and the rungs are separated by very specific behaviours. The difference between bands is almost never effort. It is a habit.

Criterion A — Integration of a key concept (5 marks)

Guiding question: to what extent do you effectively integrate the analysis of the connection between the key concept and the organisation under study throughout the internal assessment?

The ladder: 1 — you demonstrate knowledge of the concept. 2 — you describe the connection between the concept and the organisation. 3 — you analyse that connection. 4 — you partially integrate the analysis. 5 — you effectively integrate it throughout. And 0 — the concept you identified is not one of the four.

What caps you in the middle. Two things. The first is the title-page-and-introduction problem: a definition and one analytical paragraph earns 3 at best, because "integration" is not the same as "presence". The second is attachment rather than integration — writing a paragraph about sustainability, then a paragraph about the decision, in which the sustainability paragraph could be deleted without changing the conclusion. If a reader could cut your lens paragraphs and still have a complete project, you have not integrated anything.

The habit that earns it. One lens sentence at the head of every analysis section, the lens built into at least one tool, and the lens used to qualify the final recommendation. Then run the highlighter test from section 3. If your highlighted sentences develop an argument rather than repeat one, you are at 4–5.

Criterion B — Supporting documents (4 marks)

Guiding question: do you select three to five relevant documents that address the research question in appropriate depth and breadth?

The ladder: 1 — one or two documents, or more than five, or documents of marginal relevance. 2 — three to five, generally relevant, but some lack depth. 3 — three to five, relevant and sufficiently in-depth. 4 — three to five, relevant, sufficiently in-depth, and providing a range of ideas and views.

What caps you in the middle. "Lack depth" is the killer at band 2, and it almost always means press releases, "About us" pages and undated web pages: thin, promotional, and containing no data. Band 3 is reached by relevance and depth but capped by sameness — four documents that agree. The step from 3 to 4 is a single decision: deliberately include a document whose conclusion you will argue against.

The habit that earns it. Build the five-role table from section 5 before you start writing, and write the "what it lets me challenge" column honestly. If a document earns its place only as background, it should not be one of your three to five — cite it in the bibliography instead.

Criterion C — Selection and application of tools and theories (4 marks)

Guiding question: to what extent do you effectively select and apply business management tools and theories that are relevant to the research question?

The ladder: 1 — limited selection and application, or the tools are not relevant. 2 — some tools selected and applied, relevance superficial. 3 — adequately selected and applied, but relevance not always clear. 4 — effectively selected and applied, with clear relevance.

This is the criterion where the difference between describing and applying decides two marks — 2.4 percentage points — and it is worth being completely concrete about it. Describing a tool means reproducing its structure. Applying a tool means putting this organisation's facts into it and then saying what the output means and where it misleads. A SWOT with four generic bullets is description. A SWOT whose "threats" box contains the specific buyer requirement from document 4, whose "weaknesses" box contains the yield figure from document 2, and which is followed by a sentence about which boxes conflict — that is application.

Worked example 2 Describing a tool vs applying it — criterion C bands 2 to 4

Question context. Beiyu Coffee is deciding whether to open a tenth site or to launch a home-subscription service. The candidate decides to use the Ansoff matrix.

Band 1–2 (description). "The Ansoff matrix is a tool developed by Igor Ansoff in 1957 which shows four growth strategies: market penetration, market development, product development and diversification. Beiyu could use market penetration by selling more coffee to existing customers, or diversification by doing something new." — This is a definition with a generic illustration. Nothing from the organisation appears, no option is placed in a cell with a reason, and nothing follows. Nothing here is worth more than AO1.

Band 3 (applied, but relevance not always clear). "Opening a tenth site is market penetration, because it is an existing product in an existing market. The subscription service is product development, because it is a new product for existing customers. Beiyu has used market penetration before when it opened its eighth and ninth sites." — Correct placement, organisation-specific, but the grids stop there. The reader is left asking "so what?" — and the tool has not influenced the conclusion, which is why the descriptor says relevance is "not always clear".

Band 4 (effectively applied with clear relevance). "Placing the two options on the Ansoff matrix explains why they carry such different risk. A tenth site is market penetration — existing product, existing market — and Beiyu's own data shows why that cell is now crowded: the two weakest existing sites trade below the level this option needs to clear its fixed costs, which is evidence that the Hangzhou market is close to saturation for this format. The subscription service is product development: new product, existing customers, and it draws on an asset Beiyu already owns (its 12,000-member WeChat base) rather than on a new lease. The matrix therefore does not answer the question, but it relocates it: the real choice is not between two growth options, it is between adding capacity in a saturating cell and adding a product in a cell where Beiyu has an unmatched asset. It also shows what the matrix cannot see — that product development here requires a packaging and delivery capability Beiyu does not have, which is why the recommendation is conditional."

Marks: the band-4 paragraph earns its marks in three places — correct placement with a reason, facts from the organisation's own documents used to make the placement meaningful, and a stated limitation of the tool. Note the closing move: naming what the tool cannot see is what turns a filled-in grid into analysis, and it is the same move that feeds criterion D.

How many tools? Two or three, applied properly, beat five listed. Every tool you add costs 100–150 words, and words are the scarcest thing you have. Choose tools that do different jobs: one to map the situation (SWOT, STEEPLE), one to weigh the decision (decision tree, investment appraisal, Ansoff), one if it is genuinely needed to handle the numbers (descriptive statistics). And never use a tool that does not change what you conclude — an unused tool is worse than no tool, because it advertises that you cannot tell the difference between decor and analysis. The eight SL tools and how to operate them are on the toolkit page.

Criterion D — Analysis and evaluation (5 marks)

Guiding question: to what extent do you effectively select and use data from the supporting documents in your analysis and evaluation of the research question?

The ladder: 1 — limited selection and use of data, no analysis or evaluation. 2 — superficial use of data, limited analysis. 3 — adequate selection with some analysis and evaluation. 4 — sufficient use of data, mostly effective analysis with some integration of ideas. 5 — effective use of data, thorough analysis and evaluation, sustained integration of ideas with consideration of the assumptions underpinning the arguments and implications.

What caps you in the middle. The word that separates band 3 from band 5 is assumptions. A band-3 project summarises documents accurately and then draws a conclusion from them: "Document 3 shows that 78% of comparable producers have not adopted recycled bodies, therefore adoption is risky." That is a conclusion, not an evaluation. A band-5 project asks what that 78% rests on: is the non-adoption a judgement about cost, or simply inertia and lack of capital? Does the survey's sample cover producers of the same size as Hanyu? Is the figure from before the buyer requirement was published? And then: if the assumption is inertia rather than cost, the 78% is not evidence against adoption at all — it is evidence that the first mover has an opportunity.

The habit that earns it. Every significant data point gets a four-move treatment, and you can write it in four sentences:

  1. Use it: what the number is, from which document, and what it says.
  2. Interpret it: what it means for this organisation's decision.
  3. Attack it: what assumption it rests on, or what is wrong with the sample, the date, the interest of the author.
  4. Imply it: if the assumption fails, what changes in your conclusion.

Moves 3 and 4 are the ones nobody writes. They are also the ones the descriptor names. Build a paragraph template and use it four or five times in the project and you will be writing at band 5.

Criterion E — Conclusions (3 marks)

Guiding question: are your conclusions consistent with the evidence presented, and do they explicitly answer the research question?

The ladder: 1 — inconsistent with the evidence, or superficial. 2 — some conclusions are consistent with the evidence. 3 — conclusions are consistent with the evidence and explicitly answer the research question.

What caps you in the middle. Three habits. First, the conclusion that never uses the question's own words — if your question is "to what extent should…", your answer must begin with a phrase like "to a limited extent" or "to a considerable extent", not with "in conclusion, Hanyu faces many challenges". Second, new material: any evidence or option introduced for the first time in the conclusion is by definition inconsistent with evidence presented, because it was not presented. Third, the balanced non-answer: "there are advantages and disadvantages either way and only Hanyu's managers can decide." That is band 1, because the whole point of the project is that you decide, on the evidence you gathered.

The habit that earns it. Four sentences, in this order: (1) a direct answer in the question's own vocabulary; (2) the condition attached to it ("…provided that the yield loss can be held below 4%"); (3) the single biggest uncertainty and what would change your mind ("if the buyer requirement is delayed beyond 2029, the recommendation reverses"); (4) the implication for the organisation beyond the decision itself. Then stop. A conclusion over 200 words is usually a second analysis section wearing a disguise.

Criteria F and G — Structure (2) and Presentation (2)

These are described in section 7 (structure) and section 1 (the four required presentation elements). The only thing to add here is the arithmetic: F and G together are four marks, which is 4.8 percentage points, which is more than criterion E and twice criterion G's apparent modesty suggests. They are also the marks most commonly lost through haste in the final week. Put a twenty-minute "G check" in your timeline and protect it.

Examiner's-eye summary

If a moderator read only your first and last pages, she could already place you roughly. A title page with the key concept on it, a research question that is a real question, section headings that name arguments rather than topics, and a conclusion that starts with "to a considerable extent…" — that project is already in the top half before she reads the analysis. A project with no key concept on the title page, a question ending in "…: a case study", headings called "Background", "Findings", "Conclusion", and a final paragraph beginning "overall, the company is doing well" — that project is in the bottom half no matter how good section 3 is.

What to cut when you are over

You will be over. Everyone is over, because drafting generously is the right way to write and cutting is the right way to finish. Cut in this order, top to bottom, and stop as soon as you are under 1,750:

  1. Company background. Anything a reader could get from the organisation's own "About us" page. Usually 120–200 words, and it earns nothing.
  2. Pasted tool output. A full SWOT grid rendered as a table or image costs 60–100 words of equivalent space and duplicates what the commentary says. Keep a two-sentence summary of the output and put the grid in an appendix only if your school allows one; otherwise let the prose carry it.
  3. Long quotations. Paraphrase and cite. A 40-word quotation that says what you could say in 12 words is 28 words of your budget spent on someone else's prose.
  4. The fourth tool. If you used four tools, the weakest one is nearly always droppable, and its removal usually improves the project by tightening the argument.
  5. Theory exposition. Define a theory in one sentence, not a paragraph. Nobody is marking your knowledge of the theory; criterion C marks your application of it.
  6. Repetition of the question. You do not need to restate the research question at the head of every section; a lens sentence does that job better.

Never cut: the lens sentences, the assumptions list, the limitations section, or the conclusion's conditions. Those four things are where criteria A, D and E live. A project of 1,800 words with a thin introduction and a fat limitations section will outscore a beautifully contextualised project whose evaluation was sacrificed to make room for history.

How to use it — the word-count discipline
  1. Write draft 1 to about 2,200 words on purpose. Trying to draft at 1,700 produces thin analysis.
  2. Cut in the order above. Do not cut by deleting sentences at random; cut whole units of content.
  3. Check how your school counts words in tables, figure captions and the bibliography. If there is any doubt, assume everything in the body counts, including numbers and labels inside tables and figures. Budget as if it counts and you will never be caught out.
  4. Leave the count in the document and on the title page. A moderator who has to count your words is already annoyed.

8. Research methods for SL

The task allows primary research, secondary research, or both, and there is no requirement to do primary work. But primary research is the most reliable way to lift criteria B and D at the same time: it is indisputably student-selected, it gives you data nobody else has, and it produces the "range of views" that B's top band asks for. It is also where academic-honesty risk concentrates, so do it properly.

Secondary research: what it is good for and where it fails

Secondary research — material already published by somebody else — will carry most of your evidence, and it should. Company reports and updates, trade press, industry association surveys, government statistics, regulatory documents, academic papers, NGO and union publications, analyst commentary: all legitimate, all citable. Its great strength is that someone else has already done the expensive work of gathering data; its great weakness is that every secondary source has an author with a purpose, and the purpose is rarely the same as yours.

Apply a standing three-question test to every secondary source before you rely on it: who produced it, what did they want to happen, and what method produced the number? A Chinese ceramics association survey, a buyer's supplier briefing and a company sustainability update can all state the same figure and mean three different things. That is not a reason to distrust them — it is the reason your analysis has something to do.

Running one short interview well

One 25–35 minute interview, done properly, is worth more to this project than ten hours of additional reading, because it gives you the two things documents never supply: the reason behind a decision, and the constraints that stop the obvious answer. Here is the whole procedure.

How to run it
  1. Ask in writing, with the question attached. State who you are, that it is for an IB internal assessment, how long it will take, how the data will be used and stored, and that the interviewee may withdraw or go off the record at any point. Keep the reply — that is your consent record.
  2. Choose the right person for the question. Do not interview the CEO about kiln yield. The maintenance supervisor knows the yield; the finance manager knows the payback; the sales manager knows what the buyer actually said.
  3. Write six to eight open questions, plus two or three planned follow-ups. Open means "what changed after the new kiln was installed?", not "was the new kiln an improvement?". A leading question produces a useless answer and advertises that you wanted a particular answer.
  4. Ask about numbers and about reasons. "How many?", "how often?", "compared with what?" — then "why do you think that is?" The second half is the half that becomes analysis.
  5. Record it only with permission, or take notes and write them up within 24 hours while you still remember what was said. Produce a clean transcript, send it to the interviewee to confirm, and attach it as a supporting document. If it came from a recording, treat it as your single audio-visual transcript.
  6. Quote two or three short fragments in the body and analyse each one. A quotation that is not followed by analysis is decoration.
  7. State the person's interest. "As production manager, she has an incentive to emphasise yield risk, so this evidence is treated as reliable on process and cautious on cost." That one sentence is a limitation, a strength and a piece of evaluation in fourteen words.

Running a small survey

A survey is appropriate when your question depends on what a group of people think, want or do — customers, staff, suppliers. Keep it small and clean rather than large and sloppy.

Design

Two or three classification questions (who are you — customer/non-customer, frequency, age band), three to five substantive questions, one open question. Use named scale points ("very unlikely, unlikely, neutral, likely, very likely") rather than an unlabelled 1–5. Never ask two things in one question ("how satisfied are you with our price and service?").

Sample

Thirty to fifty usable responses is plenty at SL — more important than the count is who they are. State your sampling method honestly ("convenience sample of customers at the Binjiang site on two Saturday mornings"), because that statement is itself a limitation you can then use. Never survey only your friends and then generalise.

Analysis

This is where a prescribed toolkit tool earns its place: use descriptive statistics — mean, median, mode, range, interquartile range, and a cross-tabulation splitting responses by customer type. A cross-tab is the cheapest real analysis available to you: "mean rating 3.8 overall, but 4.4 among weekly customers and 2.9 among occasional ones" is a finding, and it lets you argue for a targeted rather than a general response.

Reporting

Report the response rate, the sample size, the sampling method and the bias you can see. Put the raw tables in an attachment, and put the two or three summary statistics that carry your argument in the body. A survey whose limitations you have named is worth far more than one presented as objective truth.

Ethics of primary research — the non-negotiables
  • Informed consent before the interview or survey, in writing, with the purpose explained and the right to withdraw stated.
  • Anonymity where promised. "The production manager (name withheld at their request)" is fine; naming a junior member of staff criticising their employer is not.
  • No deception and no pressure. Do not pose as a customer to get an answer, and do not survey people who cannot comfortably refuse you.
  • No personal data in the submission. Names, phone numbers, ID numbers, photos of individuals: none of it goes to the IB.
  • Do not invent a single response. Fabricating even one questionnaire return is academic misconduct, not a shortcut, and it is the kind that ends a Diploma rather than losing a mark.
  • Store it and delete it. Keep the raw data until the result is confirmed, then delete what you no longer need.

How to handle a source that contradicts you

This is the moment that separates a band-3 project from a band-5 project, and it will happen to you: you will find a credible document that undermines the conclusion you were drifting towards. The instinct is to leave it out or to bury it. Both instincts cost marks. The correct response is a four-sentence move that criterion D's top band explicitly rewards — it asks for "consideration of the assumptions underpinning the arguments".

The contradiction move
  1. State it fairly. Give the opposing claim its full strength; a weakened opponent is obviously a straw man and reads as evasion. "Document 3 reports that 78% of comparable producers have not adopted recycled bodies, and gives cost as the main reason."
  2. Expose the assumption. "That evidence assumes non-adoption is a considered judgement about cost rather than inertia — the survey asked for reasons, not for a business case, and 61% of non-adopters gave no cost figure at all."
  3. Test it against your own data. "Hanyu's own yield trial (document 2) puts the loss at 3.4%, below the 5% threshold the association cites, which suggests the cost barrier is smaller for a producer of Hanyu's scale."
  4. Say what would change your mind. "If the 78% figure were broken down by firm size and showed that mid-sized exporters had tried and reverted, the recommendation would shift from 'convert 30%' to 'pilot one line for two quarters'. On the evidence available it does not, so the recommendation stands — with the pilot as a condition."

Notice what the last sentence does: it is a conclusion that has survived a challenge, with a stated condition. That is criterion E's top band and criterion D's top band arriving at the same time.

Worked example 3 Turning an interview answer into analysis — from quotation to criterion D

The raw material. You asked the production manager: "What changed after the trial runs with the recycled body?" She answered: "The reject rate went up, obviously — but not as much as I expected. We were at about seven per cent on the old body and it went to a bit over ten on the trial. The problem is it’s not steady. Some days it’s eight, some days it’s fourteen, and we can’t predict which."

Four ways to use that, in ascending order of mark:

  1. Not analysis (band 1–2): "The production manager said the reject rate went up from about 7% to a bit over 10%." — This is a paraphrase of a document. It is evidence, not analysis, and it earns nothing on its own.
  2. Weak analysis (band 3): "The reject rate rose from 7% to over 10%, which means the recycled body is less efficient and would cost Hanyu money." — A claim follows, but it is the obvious claim, and it ignores the second half of the quotation, which is the interesting half.
  3. Real analysis (band 4): "The rise in the reject rate from 7% to just over 10% is the number the decision turns on, and it is smaller than the industry association’s 5-percentage-point warning. But the more significant finding is the variance: a rate that moves between 8% and 14% and cannot be predicted is a different problem from a rate that is steadily 10%, because an unpredictable reject rate cannot be planned around — it affects delivery reliability to the two EU buyers, not just unit cost. Read through a sustainability lens, this is the point at which an environmental improvement creates a commercial risk that has nothing to do with the environment."
  4. Band 5: the above, plus the source’s interest and the implication — "As production manager she owns this number, so if anything she has an incentive to understate the improvement; that makes the 10% figure conservative. The implication is that the recommendation should be conditioned not on the average reject rate but on its variance, which is why the pilot in the conclusion is specified as two quarters rather than one: one quarter cannot distinguish an 8–14% band from a stable 10%."
What to copy: the third and fourth versions come from noticing that the most interesting thing an interviewee says is usually the thing they mention second. Listen for variance, unpredictability and reasons — not just levels — because variance is what creates risk, and risk is what gives you something to evaluate.

Run it against the seven tests: one organisation (yes); a decision with alternatives (yes — 30% conversion versus continuing as is, versus a pilot); answerable in twenty hours (yes — one interview plus four published documents); not a lookup (no single source answers it); genuine doubt (yes — the association survey says peers are not doing it, the buyer says they must); lens fit (yes — reading it through sustainability is natural, not forced); scale (yes — one decision, four pieces of evidence, roughly 1,700 words).

Step 5 — the analysis, section by section

Analysis 1: the situation and its drivers (350 words)

Lens sentence: "Through a sustainability lens, Hanyu's present position is not a stable baseline but a consumption path: it uses virgin clay at a rate set by its order book and gas at a rate set by its kiln schedule, and both prices have moved against it since 2025."

The section then establishes, from document 1 and document 5: current output split, the reject-and-waste rate, energy cost per firing, and the quarter-by-quarter energy price series. The circular business model tool is applied here — not described — by calculating what proportion of Hanyu's clay input the current reject stream could supply if it were recovered and reprocessed (an invented 9% in this illustration), and by identifying which of the five circular model types this represents. Then the first conflict: document 1 states an intention to "explore recycled content by 2028", while document 2 has the production manager describing the requirement as "soft" — so the firm's stated objective and its operational view already disagree, and the project says so.

Analysis 2: the options and the decision (400 words)

Lens sentence: "The sustainability case for converting is that it reduces exposure to two finite and volatile inputs at once; the case against is that firing a recycled body currently consumes more energy per usable piece, so the loop saves material and spends fuel."

Three options are set out with their alternatives named: full 30% conversion by 2028; a two-quarter pilot on one line; continuation with virgin clay. The decision tree is drawn over a single uncertainty — whether the buyer's recycled-content threshold becomes a condition of listing — with probabilities taken from document 4's phrasing ("intended", "from 2029") and from document 3's adoption data, and the basis for each probability stated in one clause. Expected values are calculated and then a sensitivity check is run: at what probability does the recommendation flip? The payback period on the kiln modification is calculated from the invented capital cost and the energy savings, with the provincial subsidy included and then removed, because removing it is the assumption most worth attacking. Then the assumptions list, stated explicitly: yield loss stays below 4%; gas prices remain within the 2025–2027 range; the buyer requirement is not delayed; the subsidy is received.

Analysis 3: the lens argument and stakeholders (350 words)

Here the sustainability lens does the work that no other lens could do. It asks who currently pays for Hanyu's waste stream (nobody — it is externalised), what happens to that cost if a landfill charge or a buyer requirement internalises it, and what a ten-year view does to a decision that looks marginal in year one. It also handles the strongest objection: if firing recycled clay uses more energy, then the environmental case is weaker than it appears, and the honest answer — that the environmental benefit depends on the grid's generation mix and is therefore uncertain — is stated rather than hidden. Stakeholders are brought in with costs attached: the 240 employees whose processes must change, the two EU buyers whose requirement drives the timing, the family owners whose capital is at risk, and the local community affected by both the waste and the employment.

Analysis 4: counter-evidence and limitations (200 words)

The association survey (document 3) is the counter-evidence and gets the full four-move treatment: stated at full strength, its assumption exposed, tested against Hanyu's own yield figure, and answered with the condition that would change the recommendation. Then the limitations, each as a fact about the evidence rather than an apology: the interview is one person, who owns the yield number; the buyer's briefing is a statement of intent, not a contract; the energy series is provincial, not Hanyu's actual tariff; the yield figure comes from a trial, not from full production.

Step 6 — the conclusion

To a considerable extent, Hanyu should move to a recycled-clay body — but as a two-quarter pilot on one line rather than an immediate 30% conversion, and conditional on holding the yield loss below 4%. The analysis supports conversion because the buyer requirement, the waste stream and the subsidy all point the same way; it stops short of full conversion because the single most influential number in the decision tree — whether the requirement becomes a condition of listing — is the least well evidenced. If document 4's 2029 date slips by two years, the expected value of immediate conversion falls below the pilot and the recommendation reverses. Beyond the decision itself, the implication is that Hanyu's dependence on two buyers is now a material risk in its own right, and diversification of the customer base deserves the same analytical attention that this project has given to the material. Illustrative conclusion — 194 words

Check what that conclusion does: it answers in the question's own words ("to a considerable extent"), it is consistent with the evidence presented (it uses only documents already discussed), it attaches a condition, it names the uncertainty that would reverse it, and it identifies an implication beyond the decision. It also makes the reader's disagreement possible, which is the mark of a real conclusion rather than a summary.

10. Academic honesty

Nothing here is a threat; all of it is protection. The research project is the component where academic honesty problems most often arise, and almost never through cheating in the crude sense. They arise through carelessness about the boundary between what you found and what you think about what you found.

What must be cited

  • Every fact, figure and claim that came from somewhere else. Output volumes, prices, adoption rates, requirements, dates, quotations.
  • Every supporting document, in the body where you use it and in the bibliography.
  • Paraphrase as well as quotation. Changing the words does not make the idea yours. "It has been argued that the buyer requirement is not binding (Document 4, 2026)" is a citation; dropping the brackets is not.
  • Data you collected yourself. "Interview with production manager, 12 March 2027" and "Author's survey, n = 43, April 2027".
  • Any framework or figure you reproduced, including the structure of a published analysis.

What counts as your own analysis

This is the part students underestimate. The following are yours, and no citation is needed for them, because nobody else produced them:

Selection

Which documents you chose, which pages you excerpted, which numbers you considered worth using. Three candidates given the same folder produce three different projects, and that difference is assessed under criterion B.

Application

Putting this organisation's facts into a tool and interpreting the output. The SWOT grid's structure is not yours; the filled-in grid and the sentence about which boxes conflict are entirely yours.

Judgement

Weighing two pieces of evidence against each other, deciding which assumption to adopt, stating what would change your mind. No source can do this for you.

Structure and argument

The order in which you make your points, the questions you ask of the evidence, the conclusion you reach and the conditions you attach to it.

The specific risks with supporting documents

Five ways documents cause academic-honesty problems
  1. Pasting extracts into the body. Long quotations from your documents are not your analysis, they consume your word count, and a project that is 40% pasted document has very little of you in it. Quote fragments; analyse them.
  2. Documents you did not select. The task requires student-selected documents. If your teacher hands the whole class the same three articles, the selection skill being assessed has been removed — and at moderation it looks like collusion. Use teacher-suggested sources to find your own documents; that is completely different and completely fine.
  3. Reusing a document across assessments. Using the same source in your extended essay and your business project is not automatically misconduct — a source is a source — but the analysis must be genuinely new, both must be acknowledged to both supervisors, and the two questions must be genuinely different. The failure mode is a business IA and an economics or business EE with the same organisation, the same documents, the same tools and slightly different wording. That is double submission, and it is a zero for both. See choosing your extended essay before you pick either topic.
  4. Misrepresenting a document's nature. Presenting a company press release as independent evidence, or an opinion piece as data, is a form of dishonesty even if the citation is technically present. Name what each document is: "Hanyu's own 2026 update (a promotional internal document)".
  5. Generative AI. If you use an AI tool to polish the language, generate ideas or summarise a source, acknowledge it in the same way you would acknowledge a tutor or a reference, and remember that the analysis has to be yours — an AI does not know your organisation's production manager or your survey results, and a project whose evidence is generic is a project that scores 2 on criteria C and D regardless. Check your school's current policy and follow it.

Collusion — the line

Discussing your project with classmates is good practice and is not collusion: comparing research questions, recommending a source, challenging each other's assumptions, reading each other's drafts for clarity. What crosses the line is shared work: writing analysis together, passing a document set around, or letting someone edit your argument. A useful test: if the sentence in your project would not exist without another candidate, it should not be in your project. When in doubt, ask your teacher — before you submit, not after.

Worked examples

The research project is assessed on whether you can produce knowledge that a textbook does not already contain. These three examples show the three places where projects are usually won or lost: the question, the analysis, and the conclusion.

Worked example 4 Turning a topic into a research question · AO3 · 6 marks

The situation. A student wants to study a chain of tea shops, Cháyuán, and writes: "How has Cháyuán grown and what are its marketing strategies?"

  1. Diagnose why the draft question fails. It is two questions joined by "and"; it can be answered entirely from the company's website and news articles; and it has no key concept, so it does not say what the student is trying to find out about. Under Criterion A this is a topic, not a research question, and it caps the whole project regardless of how good the writing is.
  2. Add a key concept. The four concepts are change, ethics, culture and strategy (creativity and innovation at some sessions). Cháyuán has recently started selling through delivery apps, which is a change. So: how has that change affected the business?
  3. Make it specific and arguable. Name the organisation, the concept, and the aspect being investigated: "To what extent has the introduction of third-party delivery platforms changed the customer experience and profitability of Cháyuán's city-centre branches?"
  4. Test the question against three checks. (a) Can it be answered with 1,800 words and the evidence you can realistically get? Yes — three branches, one manager interview, sales data. (b) Does it have more than one possible answer? Yes — delivery may add revenue but dilute the brand experience and take a 25% commission. (c) Would the answer be different for another business? Yes — for a café whose whole offer is staying in, delivery would matter much less. If all three are yes, the question is examinable.
  5. Explain why this question was chosen — the paragraph that earns Criterion A. "Delivery platforms now account for 31% of Cháyuán's city-centre revenue, up from 4% two years ago, and the founder has said publicly that in-store experience is the brand. These two facts are in tension, which makes the question worth asking; it is also small enough to answer with three branch visits and one interview, and large enough that the answer is not obvious."
  6. State what would make the answer wrong. A good project says what evidence would overturn its conclusion — for example, if delivery orders turn out to be incremental rather than substituting for in-store purchases, the concern about cannibalisation is unfounded.
Answer: "To what extent has the introduction of third-party delivery platforms changed the customer experience and profitability of Cháyuán's city-centre branches?" Marks: 2 for naming a specific organisation with a specific situation, 2 for embedding a key concept (change) rather than bolting it on, 2 for justifying the question in terms of tension, feasibility and non-obviousness.
Trap — the question that cannot fail

Students write questions that are safe: "How effective is X's marketing?" There is no way to be wrong, so there is nothing to find out, and the project becomes a description. The markscheme for Criterion A and C rewards a question whose answer could have gone either way. If you cannot imagine evidence that would contradict your expected answer, the question is not a research question. The second trap is the bolted-on concept: adding "from the perspective of change" to a descriptive question does not make it conceptual — the concept must be the lens through which the evidence is organised.

Worked example 5 The same evidence, analysed at two levels · AO3 · 8 marks

The evidence. Cháyuán's city-centre branch: revenue up 18% year on year; delivery orders up from 4% to 31% of revenue; gross margin down from 68% to 59%; in-store transactions down 22%; average in-store spend up 9%; one of the three baristas reassigned to packing delivery orders at peak times; a customer survey of 60 people in which 21 said they now visit less often because of queueing.

Version A — description (middle band, Criterion C).

Revenue at the branch increased by 18% and delivery orders rose from 4% to 31% of revenue. Gross margin fell from 68% to 59%. Fewer customers are coming into the store, and the survey showed that 21 out of 60 customers visit less often. In-store transactions fell by 22% while average spend rose by 9%.

This is accurate and worth almost nothing: every sentence is a fact from the source with no mechanism attached. It is what happens when a student runs out of time and pastes the data into prose.

Version B — analysis (top band, Criterion C).

The 18% revenue growth is not the good news it appears to be, because the composition of that revenue has changed. Delivery now accounts for 31% of sales and carries the platform's commission and packaging cost, which is the most likely reason gross margin fell nine percentage points to 59% — that is, revenue rose 18% while gross profit rose by only about 3% (1.18 × 0.59 against 1.00 × 0.68). Meanwhile in-store transactions fell 22%, so the added revenue is substantially substituting for, rather than supplementing, the core business: the branch is selling the same drinks through a more expensive channel. The 9% rise in average in-store spend is consistent with this — occasional customers, who buy one drink, have shifted to delivery, leaving a smaller base of regulars who buy more per visit. The mechanism is visible in the operations: with one barista reassigned to packing at peak times, the queue lengthens, and the survey's 21 respondents who say they visit less often because of queueing are the human form of the 22% transaction fall. Cháyuán is therefore trading its differentiating asset — the in-store experience the founder describes as the brand — for volume that a competitor with no such asset can match.

Why B is in the top band. Every number is still there, but each is attached to a mechanism ("which is the most likely reason", "the mechanism is visible in"), the figures are combined (1.18 × 0.59 against 0.68) to produce a new fact that appears in no source, and the paragraph ends with an evaluative point that follows from the chain. B also uses the tool — the operations detail and the survey — rather than quoting it.

  1. The transferable method. For every piece of evidence, ask three questions in order: what does this number show? Why did it happen — what mechanism connects cause to effect? And what does it mean for the research question? If you write only the first, you are describing; the second is analysis; the third is evaluation.
  2. Where students lose the evaluation mark. They write the analysis and then stop. The final sentence — "trading a differentiating asset for volume a competitor can match" — is what lifts the answer into the top band, and it must be argued, not asserted.
Marks: 2 for combining figures to produce something not in the source, 2 for explaining the mechanism behind each change, 2 for linking the evidence to the research question, 2 for an evaluative conclusion that follows from the chain rather than being stated separately.
Worked example 6 A conclusion, an evaluation and a reference list entry · AO3 · 6 marks

Writing the conclusion. The conclusion must answer the research question in the question's own words and must not introduce new evidence.

  1. Restate the question's structure. The question asks "to what extent", so the conclusion must quantify: "To a considerable extent, the introduction of delivery platforms has changed Cháyuán's city-centre business — but the change is a change in kind as much as in amount, and not all of it is beneficial."
  2. Give the supported answer in two or three sentences that mirror the analysis sections: revenue and reach have increased; profitability per transaction has fallen because of the commission and packaging cost; and the in-store experience that underpinned the brand has degraded because of queueing caused by reassigning staff.
  3. State the limitation of the conclusion. "This conclusion rests on one branch over eighteen months and a survey of 60 customers at one time of day; it does not establish that the same pattern holds in the suburban branches, where parking makes collection easier and delivery less attractive."
  4. Writing the evaluation (Criterion D). The evaluation is a separate, short section that judges the project, not the business. It should name: what the study could not establish (causality between the queue and the fall in visits — customers who already preferred delivery may also be the ones who complain about queues); what was done well (the triangulation of sales data, an interview and a survey, which is why the mechanism is credible); and what would be done differently (a larger, timed survey at both peak and off-peak, and a comparison branch without delivery).
  5. Referencing — the mechanics that cost marks in Criterion E. Every figure from a source gets an in-text citation, and the source list gives enough detail for someone else to find it. For an interview: Personal interview with the branch manager of Cháyuán, Wuyi Road, 14 March, 2027. For a report: China Tea Association, Instant Tea and Delivery Market Report 2026, Shanghai, p. 44. For a web page: Cháyuán, Annual Report 2026, accessed 3 February 2027, https://www.example.com/investors.
  6. Academic honesty — the line. Supporting documents (the interview transcript, the survey instrument, the raw sales table) go in the appendices and are not counted in the 1,800 words, but they must be referred to in the body and must be your own work. Paraphrasing a report's argument in your own sentence structure with a citation is fine; rearranging its sentences without one is not.
Marks: 2 for a conclusion that answers the question in its own terms with a quantified judgement, 2 for limitations and for an evaluation of the project's own method, 2 for referencing that is complete enough to be found and for correct treatment of supporting documents.
Exam technique — the 1,800-word limit is a design constraint, not a target

Projects that use all 1,800 words are usually descriptive, because description is cheap to write and analysis is expensive. A strong project is often 1,500 words. Use the word count where it earns marks — the analysis and the evaluation — and cut the introduction, the history of the company and anything that could be found on the first page of a search engine. As a rule: if a sentence contains a fact but no "because" or "therefore", question whether it belongs in the body at all.

Practice

  1. Diagnosing weak questions (1). Three candidates have submitted these research questions. For each, identify which of the seven tests it fails and rewrite it so that it passes.

    • (a) "How has Hanyu Ceramics been affected by rising energy costs?"
    • (b) "Should Chinese ceramic manufacturers use recycled materials?"
    • (c) "What is Beiyu Coffee's market share in Hangzhou?"
    Show answer
    1. (a) Fails the decision test and the scale test: there is no decision, only a cause, so the answer is a narrative ("first prices rose, then margins fell") with nothing to weigh. It also fails the not-a-fact test in spirit — the effect is measurable, so the question invites summary rather than judgement. Rewrite (change): "To what extent should Hanyu convert its second kiln to a heat-recovery system by 2028 rather than absorbing the rise in gas prices, given the provincial efficiency subsidy?"
    2. (b) Fails the one-organisation test outright — "Chinese ceramic manufacturers" is an industry, and the evidence would be about an average firm that does not exist. It also fails the answerability test at 1,800 words. Rewrite (sustainability): "To what extent should Hanyu Ceramics source 20% of its clay from reclaimed industrial ceramic waste by 2028, given the quality constraints of its export lines?"
    3. (c) Fails the not-a-fact test completely: the answer is a number that either exists in a document or does not, and there is no analysis to do. It also fails the lens-fit test. Rewrite (creativity): "To what extent should Beiyu pursue growth through a home-subscription product rather than a tenth site, given the saturation evidence from its two weakest locations?"
    Marks: in each case the credit comes from naming the specific test that fails — not from saying "it is too vague". Vagueness is a symptom; the test that fails is the diagnosis.

  2. Choosing the lens. Suyuan Fresh is a community fresh-produce delivery business with 35 staff and about 4,000 orders a week. It is deciding whether to replace its outsourced electric delivery fleet with a fleet it owns and employs directly. Recommend one of the four key concepts, justify it against the alternatives, and write the lens sentence you would put at the head of the first analysis section.

    Show answer
    1. Recommendation: change — with ethics as the runner-up. The decision is a change of operating model: assets move from a supplier to the business, staff move from a contractor's payroll to Suyuan's, and the transition itself (recruitment, vehicle purchase, route planning, maintenance) is where the risk sits. That is exactly what a change lens is for.
    2. Why not ethics? There is an ethical dimension — contractor riders versus employed drivers — and it is a legitimate choice. But if the documents you can find are about cost and control rather than about working conditions, the ethics lens will be asserted rather than evidenced, and criterion A will cap at 2–3. Choose ethics only if you have a document that speaks to the drivers' actual terms.
    3. Why not sustainability? It is tempting (electric vehicles, emissions) but the vehicles are electric in both options, so the environmental difference is small; the lens would be decorative.
    4. Why not creativity? Insourcing a fleet is a well-known move, not a novel one; asking "what option has nobody proposed?" does not fit the decision as stated.
    5. Lens sentence: "Through a change lens, the question is not whether owning a fleet is cheaper — the arithmetic in section 3 addresses that — but whether Suyuan has the managerial capacity to absorb a function it has never performed, and what the transition costs during the two quarters in which both the contractor and the new fleet are being paid."
    Marks: the justification against the alternatives is worth as much as the choice itself. A lens chosen without rejecting the other three looks like a guess; a lens chosen after rejecting three looks like analysis — which is what criterion A is.

  3. Criterion B — auditing a document set. A candidate submits five documents: (1) a company press release from 2019 announcing a new factory; (2) the company's 2027 sustainability report, all 84 pages of it; (3) a news article from 2026 that quotes the press release in (1); (4) a transcript of a 2026 podcast interview with the founder; (5) a 2026 government statistics release on regional wage levels. Identify every rule broken and state what mark criterion B would award.

    Show answer
    1. Document 1 is out of date — published more than three years before submission — and it is a press release, so it is thin on data anyway.
    2. Document 2 breaks the five-page rule: 84 pages is not a document, it is an archive. The candidate must excerpt the five pages that matter and cite the original.
    3. Document 3 is not independent of document 1 — it is the same story. The set therefore contains duplicate evidence presented as corroboration.
    4. Document 4 is the one audio-visual transcript, which is permitted — but only one is, so no other transcript may be added.
    5. Document 5 is recent, quantitative and independent. It is the strongest item in the set.
    6. Also missing: any document offering a different view. All five come from, or are sympathetic to, the company itself. There is nothing to disagree with.
    7. Mark: 1–2. Formally there are five documents so the count rule is satisfied, but one breaches the date rule, one breaches the length rule, two are duplicates, and the set provides depth without a range of views — which is precisely the "generally relevant but some lack depth" profile of band 2, dragged toward band 1 by the out-of-date item.
    The fix: drop document 1, excerpt document 2 to five pages, drop document 3, keep 4 and 5, and add an industry association report or a union/employee source that challenges the company's account.

  4. Criterion C — apply the tool. Suyuan Fresh's customer survey (n = 46) produced a mean delivery-satisfaction rating of 3.8 out of 5, a median of 4, a mode of 4, and a range of 1–5. Among the 19 customers who order weekly the mean is 4.4; among the 27 who order monthly or less the mean is 2.9. Write 120 words that apply descriptive statistics to this decision rather than describing them.

    Show answer

    Model answer. "The headline figure conceals the finding. The mean of 3.8 sits below the median of 4, which with a range of 1–5 indicates a cluster of very low ratings pulling the average down rather than a uniformly mediocre service — and the cross-tabulation locates that cluster: weekly customers rate delivery at 4.4 while occasional customers rate it at 2.9, a gap of 1.5 points that the aggregate mean completely hides. Read as a segmentation result, this says Suyuan does not have a delivery problem, it has an occasional-customer problem, and the operational implication is specific: the cause is most likely the delivery window offered to low-frequency orders, which are batched at the end of the day. The tool cannot tell us that — it can only show that the two groups are not experiencing the same service, and that a single, business-wide delivery promise would be the wrong response to a bimodal distribution."

    Marks: this earns band 4 because it does three things a description does not: it interprets the mean–median gap rather than reporting it, it uses the cross-tabulation to relocate the problem from "delivery" to "a segment of delivery", and it ends by naming what the statistics cannot establish. Note also that n = 46 is named, so the sample limitation is implicit rather than hidden.

  5. Word budget. A draft runs to 2,140 words: introduction 430, background to the industry 210, analysis 1 480, analysis 2 520, analysis 3 300, limitations 60, conclusion 140. Cut it to 1,700 and justify every cut.

    Show answer
    1. Delete the industry background in full (−210 → 1,930). It earns nothing under any criterion; context belongs inside the analysis, attached to the specific driver it explains. This is cut number one in the standing order.
    2. Cut the introduction from 430 to 200 (−230 → 1,700). Two sentences on the organisation, the issue, the lens with its justification, the question, one sentence on methods, one on structure. Everything else — history, mission, product range — goes.
    3. Protect analysis 1 and 2 at roughly 400 each (−200 total from analysis 1: 480 → 400; analysis 2 stays at 520 for now). But 520 is above the plan, so remove the fourth tool if one is present, or compress the tool output into two sentences of prose (−120 → 400). Running total: 200 + 400 + 400 + 300 + 60 + 140 = 1,500.
    4. Now spend, do not just save. The limitations section at 60 words is the real problem in this draft: it is where criterion D's "consideration of the assumptions" lives. Raise it to 200 (+140 → 1,640) and raise the conclusion from 140 to 200 (+60 → 1,700), because a 140-word conclusion cannot contain a direct answer, a condition, a reversal condition and an implication.
    5. Final allocation: introduction 200, analysis 1 400, analysis 2 400, analysis 3 300, limitations 200, conclusion 200 = 1,700.
    The lesson: the point of cutting is not to reach a number, it is to move words from the parts that earn nothing (background, long introduction) to the parts that earn criteria D and E (limitations, conclusion). Most candidates cut the wrong end.

  6. Handling contradiction. Your recommendation is that Beiyu Coffee should close its two weakest sites. Document 3, a 2027 survey of 210 independent coffee retailers, reports that 71% of operators who closed a site saw total revenue fall, and concludes that closures destroy more value than they release. Write the four-move paragraph that handles this.

    Show answer

    Model answer. "Document 3 reports that 71% of operators who closed a site saw total revenue fall, and on its face this is strong evidence against closure. The assumption behind it is that a closed site's revenue is transferable to the remaining sites — that the customers lost were customers who would have travelled. That assumption does not hold for Beiyu: document 1 shows the two sites draw 68% of their sales from within 800 metres, and the nearest surviving Beiyu site is 2.4 km away, well outside the walk-in catchment that document 2's survey data identifies as the source of sales. The survey's own composition points the same way — 210 independent single-site operators, for whom closing a site means leaving a market entirely, not consolidating within one. Tested against Beiyu's own catchment data, the 71% therefore measures something different from what is proposed here. The finding would change this recommendation only if Beiyu's two sites drew a material share of sales from customers who also use another branch; on the evidence available they do not."

    Marks: state the opponent at full strength (sentence 1), expose the assumption (sentence 2), test it against your own data, including a critique of the survey's sample (sentences 3–5), and state what would change your mind (sentence 6). That sequence is criterion D's top band written out as a recipe, and it is also why the conclusion that follows will satisfy criterion E's "consistent with the evidence presented".

  7. Academic honesty and the extended essay. Lucas is studying Business Management SL and is also considering a Business Management extended essay on the same organisation he has chosen for his research project. He plans to use the same five documents, the same interview, and the same SWOT and decision tree in both. Advise him, naming the specific risk and giving him a concrete rule.

    Show answer
    1. Name the risk: double submission. Submitting substantially the same work for two assessment components is academic misconduct under the IB's academic integrity policy, and the outcome is not a reduced mark — it is a failing condition for the components and potentially for the Diploma. Five identical documents plus an identical interview plus the same two tools is not "the same topic", it is the same project.
    2. The rule that keeps him safe: different question, different evidence, different analysis, both declared. If the EE asks a genuinely different question — a different decision, a different time horizon, a different theoretical framing at 4,000 words — and if the analysis is new, then sharing an organisation is fine and even efficient. Sharing the evidence base and the analysis is not.
    3. Practical separation: use the research project for the operational decision (the kiln, the yield, the payback) and the EE for a different question at a different level (for example the effect of buyer concentration on the strategies of mid-sized Chinese exporters, using Hanyu as one of three cases). Note that a three-case EE is also a stronger EE than a single-case one, which is a useful coincidence.
    4. Declare both. Tell both supervisors what the other component covers, in writing, before drafting. An undeclared overlap discovered at moderation is treated very differently from a declared one discussed in advance.
    5. Sequence them. Do the research project first — it is smaller, it is 30% of the SL grade, and it will teach him what evidence he can actually get. Then design the EE around what he learned, not around the same material.
    One more thing: even with different questions, the interview is primary data he generated. Using the same transcript in both is permissible only if both acknowledge it and both analyses differ. If in doubt, run a second interview for the EE with a different respondent about a different question — it costs an afternoon and removes the risk entirely.

  8. Criteria F and G. A candidate's project has: a title page with the title, the research question and the word count; a contents page listing four sections, two of which have been renumbered since it was generated; section headings "Introduction", "Analysis", "Analysis continued", "Conclusion"; and page numbers from 1 to 14. What does she score on F and G, and what exactly must she change?

    Show answer
    1. G: 1 mark, not 2. Three of the four required elements are present — title page, contents, page numbers — but the key concept is missing from the title page, and the contents is no longer accurate, so "an accurate table of contents" is also missing. G's descriptor is binary: all required elements present = 2, one or more missing = 1. She has also probably cost herself on criterion A, because the title page is where the concept is identified.
    2. F: 1 mark, not 2. "Analysis" and "Analysis continued" are not a structure; they are the absence of one. F asks whether the project is organised using an appropriate structure, and a reader cannot tell from the headings what each section argues or how the sections relate.
    3. Fixes, in order: add "Key concept: sustainability" (or whichever of the four she has used) to the title page; regenerate the contents after the final edit so the section names and page numbers match; rename the analysis sections after the arguments they make (for example "Why the buyer requirement, not the cost of clay, is the binding constraint" and "What the yield trial does and does not establish"); add sub-headings inside sections where the argument turns; then re-paginate and re-count the words.
    Cost of the oversight: three marks — 3.6 percentage points of her final SL grade — for about twenty minutes of work. This is why the G check is on the timeline.

  9. [AO1, 4 marks] State what a key concept is in the research project, name the four concepts, and explain what goes wrong when a project has none.

    Show answer
    1. Definition (1): a key concept is one of a small set of lenses — change, ethics, culture, strategy — through which the research question is framed and the evidence is organised.
    2. The four (1): change, ethics, culture, strategy.
    3. What goes wrong (2): without a concept the project becomes a company report — a description of what the business does, drawn from its own website, with no question that could have been answered differently. The student then has nothing to analyse, because analysis needs a tension, and the tension comes from the concept: change sets what the business was against what it is becoming; ethics sets what it does against what it ought to do; culture sets the formal organisation against the informal one; strategy sets intended against realised. Without that framing the project cannot reach the top bands of Criterion A or C however well it is written, and bolting the concept on at the end — "from the perspective of change" — does not help, because the concept must determine which evidence is collected in the first place.
    Marks: 1 for the definition, 1 for naming all four, 2 for explaining the consequence with the mechanism (no tension, therefore no analysis).

  10. [AO2, 6 marks] A student is investigating whether a family-owned bakery should open a second shop. She is considering (a) an interview with the owner, (b) a customer survey of 80 people outside the shop, and (c) published data on high-street footfall. Compare the three sources and recommend how she should combine them.

    Show answer
    1. Interview with the owner (2): a primary, qualitative source that gives access to exactly the things published data cannot — the owner's objectives, her appetite for risk, the terms of the lease, and the real reason she wants a second shop. Its weakness is that it is one person, with every incentive to be optimistic and to present her own history favourably; it cannot tell the student whether customers would visit a second location. It is also time-consuming and hard to repeat.
    2. Customer survey of 80 people (2): primary and quantitative, capturing the demand side the owner cannot see. Its weakness is sampling: 80 people outside one shop on one or two days will over-represent people who are already nearby, and if she asks "would you visit a second shop?" she will get answers, not behaviour. It is cheap and quick but only indicative.
    3. Published footfall data (2): secondary and quantitative, giving an objective, comparable baseline for the candidate location rather than the owner's impression. Its weakness is that it is not specific to this business: national or city-level footfall trends say nothing about whether this bakery's reputation travels to a new street, and the data may be a year old.
    4. Recommendation (2): use all three, because each answers a different part of the question and each has a weakness the others partly correct — this is triangulation, and it is what distinguishes a strong methodology section from a list. Concretely: use footfall data to identify the candidate street objectively, use the survey to test whether existing customers would travel to it, and use the interview to establish whether the owner's objectives and finances make the move sensible. She should also make the survey behavioural rather than hypothetical — ask where respondents currently buy bread and how far they travelled — because that produces evidence rather than opinion.
    Marks: 2 each for two sources compared with a strength and a weakness applied to this project, 2 for a justified recommendation that combines them with a reason.

  11. [AO2, 4 marks] A project cites a newspaper article claiming that a company's new factory "will create 500 jobs", and a trade union report claiming the same factory "will displace 900 workers elsewhere in the group". Explain how you would handle these two sources.

    Show answer
    1. Do not simply average them or pick one (1): the existence of a contradiction is the most useful thing in the sources, because it tells you that the number depends on who is counting and what is being counted.
    2. Interrogate the interest of each source (1–2): the newspaper article is likely to repeat a company press release, whose purpose is to present the investment favourably; the union has an interest in emphasising job losses, and its "900 displaced" may include natural attrition and redeployment rather than redundancies. Both are secondary, both are advocacy, and neither is evidence of what actually happens.
    3. Look for a third, disinterested source (1): the company's own annual report, a regulator's filing, or local government employment data — and prefer the source with the least to gain and the most specific methodology.
    4. Write the contradiction into the analysis (1): rather than resolving it, state it and use it: "the net employment effect is contested, with the company claiming +500 and the union −900; because both figures serve an interest, the project will rely on the group's own headcount disclosures, which show…" — and then note that the disagreement itself is evidence that the factory is a significant restructuring rather than a simple expansion.
    Marks: 1 for not treating either as fact, 2 for analysing each source's interest and methodology, 1 for handling the contradiction explicitly in the writing.

  12. [AO3, 10 marks] Evaluate the following research question: "Should Cháyuán Tea expand by opening more branches?" Rewrite it and justify your improvements.

    Show answer
    1. What is right (AO2): it names a specific organisation and a specific decision, and it is feasible — a student can find out about one company's expansion. Those two strengths should be kept.
    2. What is wrong (AO2): first, it is a yes/no question, so the answer can be given in one word and the project has nothing to develop; a research question should ask "to what extent" or "in what ways", which forces a reasoned answer. Second, it contains no key concept, so it does not say what the student is trying to find out about the expansion — it invites a description of the expansion rather than an investigation of it. Third, "more branches" is vague: how many, where, in what format, and over what period? Fourth, the question as posed can be answered largely from the company's own published plans, so it does not require original research.
    3. Rewrite (AO3): "To what extent is opening company-owned branches the most appropriate method of growth for Cháyuán Tea, given its dependence on in-store service quality?" This names the organisation, uses the concept of strategy (with change implied), sets growth by opening branches against the alternative of franchising or delivery, and introduces a real tension — the business's differentiator may be exactly what is lost when it grows through branches staffed by new people.
    4. Justify each change (AO3): "to what extent" forces a judgement with evidence on both sides; naming the alternative methods makes the question comparative, so the answer is not obvious; and the clause about service quality ties the question to something the student can actually investigate with one interview, a customer survey and branch-level data.
    5. Limitations (AO3): even the rewritten question depends on access to branch-level figures, and if the company will not provide them the question must be narrowed to one city or to a comparison of two branches. A question that cannot be answered with the available evidence is not better than a vague one.
    6. Conclusion (AO3): the original question is a topic dressed as a question and would cap the project in the middle bands; the rewritten version is narrower, comparative and conceptual, and would allow the student to reach the top bands provided the evidence is available.
    Marks: 1–2 for identifying what the question does well; 3–4 for two or more specific weaknesses explained; 5–6 for a rewritten question with a concept; 7–8 for justifying each change; 9–10 for that plus limitations and an assessment of what the original would have scored.

  13. [AO3, 6 marks] A student asks a friend who works at the company to write three paragraphs of "background on the company culture" and pastes them, lightly reworded, into the project without a citation. The paragraphs are accurate. Explain why this is a problem and what the student should have done instead.

    Show answer
    1. Name the problem precisely (2): this is collusion and plagiarism. Accurate content is not a defence: academic honesty is about the authorship of the work, not its truth. Rewording someone else's paragraphs and presenting them as your own analysis is plagiarism, and having another person contribute material to an assessed piece is collusion — two separate breaches, and the second is the more serious because it undermines the whole basis of the assessment.
    2. Explain the consequence (1–2): the project is not eligible for a grade; the marks that would have come from those paragraphs are removed, but the greater damage is that the surrounding work becomes suspect, and the student loses the ability to demonstrate Criterion C at all, because the analysis in those paragraphs is not theirs. Schools are required to report it.
    3. What should have been done instead (2–3): the friend is a legitimate source, not a co-author. The student should have interviewed the friend, recorded or noted the answers, cited the interview in the source list as a personal interview with the date, and then written her own analysis of what the friend said. Even better, she should have used the interview to generate primary evidence that no published source contains — what it actually feels like to work there — and combined it with the company's own published statements, comparing the internal view with the external one. If she wanted the friend's written words, they belong in an appendix and must be attributed.
    4. The general rule (1): other people provide evidence; you provide the writing and the analysis. If a sentence in your project was first written by someone else, it needs a citation — and if the analysis was first thought by someone else, it does not demonstrate your Criterion C at all.
    Marks: 2 for naming collusion and plagiarism and for rejecting the "it was accurate" defence, 1–2 for the consequence, 2–3 for the correct procedure including the interview as a citable primary source.
