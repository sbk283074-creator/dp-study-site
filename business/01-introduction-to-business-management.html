Introduction to business management

Unit 1 is where the whole course gets its vocabulary. Six sub-topics, twenty hours: what a business is, what legal forms it can take, what it is trying to achieve, who cares about it, how it grows, and what changes when it crosses a border. Why this unit exists

Before you can argue about what a business should do, you need to be able to say three things about it without hedging: what kind of thing it is (which decides who is liable and who is in control), what it says it wants (which gives you the standard to judge any decision against), and who is affected by the decision (which is where every good second argument comes from). Those are 1.2, 1.3 and 1.4. The other three sub-topics — what a business is, how it grows, what happens when it goes abroad — are the story around them. Unit 1 is not a warm-up you can skim; it is the frame the rest of the course hangs on.

1.1 What is a business?

A business is an organisation that combines the factors of production — land, labour, capital and enterprise — to produce goods or services that satisfy people's needs and wants, usually with the aim of earning a profit.

That sentence is worth learning more or less word for word, because nearly every 1.1 question is an invitation to unpack one clause of it. It is also the sentence students most often fail to produce. Asked to "define a business" for two marks, most people write something about making money or selling things, and get one mark at best. Notice what the definition does not say: nothing about size, nothing about shops, nothing about employees. A single student tutoring on weekends is as much a business as BYD.

Exam technique — unpacking a definition for marks

The definition is five clauses, and each clause can be developed into a sentence of its own. That is how a two-mark definition becomes a four-mark explanation.

  • "An organisation" — a structured arrangement of people and resources, not one person doing one task. Because it is an organisation, it has objectives, functions and (usually) a legal identity.
  • "combines the factors of production" — a business does not create value from nothing; it takes land, labour, capital and enterprise and organises them. If a stimulus hands you a list of resources, this is the clause that tells you what to do with the list.
  • "to produce goods or services" — output may be tangible (a phone, a bowl of noodles) or intangible (a haircut, insurance, a lesson, cloud storage). Writing "products" when you mean both is a small inaccuracy that costs marks in a definition question.
  • "that satisfy needs and wants" — the commercial test of an output is that someone will pay for it. Output nobody wants is not output, it is waste, and waste is a cost (Unit 5).
  • "usually with the aim of earning a profit" — the word "usually" is doing real work. It leaves room for social enterprises, cooperatives and state organisations that trade like businesses but exist for a different purpose. That exception is examined, which is why the word is in the definition.

Learn the one sentence, then learn the five clauses underneath it. In a 4-mark "explain what is meant by a business" question, the sentence is worth two marks and two developed clauses are worth the other two.

Needs, wants and the economic problem

People have needs — food, water, shelter, safety, and in most modern definitions a measure of healthcare and education: the things required to survive and to function. They also have wants, which are the particular forms those needs take: not shelter, but this flat in Chengdu, heated, near the metro, with fast internet. Needs are limited and broadly the same for everyone; wants are unlimited and specific to each person.

Resources are finite. That gap — unlimited wants, limited resources — is scarcity, and the economic problem that follows from it forces every society and every business to answer three questions: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. A business answers those three questions with its product decision, its operations method and its pricing and target-market choice, which is another way of saying that Units 4 and 5 are applied answers to the economic problem.

Because resources are finite, every choice has a cost measured in what was given up. That is opportunity cost: the benefit of the next best alternative forgone. It is the single most transferable idea in this unit, and it is the cheapest route to a genuine evaluation point anywhere in the course.

Common trap

Opportunity cost is not "the money spent". It is the next best alternative forgone. If Li Wei puts ¥2 million into twenty new tea stores, the cost of that decision is not ¥2 million — it is whatever that ¥2 million would have earned in its best alternative use (repaying the loan, upgrading training, opening in a different city). In an evaluation this is a free, genuinely sophisticated point, and almost nobody makes it.

Worked example 1 Opportunity cost — Mei's ¥200,000

Question. Mei has ¥200,000 of savings and a spare room she is not using. She is weighing three options. A: convert the room into a tutoring studio and run it herself, expecting ¥60,000 of profit in year one. B: leave the money in a deposit account at 3 % (¥6,000) and rent the room out (¥12,000) — ¥18,000, close to risk-free. C: put the money into a friend's bubble-tea franchise, expecting ¥55,000 but with far more risk and her capital locked in for three years. Identify the opportunity cost of option A.

  1. Do not start with the money spent. The ¥200,000 is the outlay, not the opportunity cost. The outlay is the same under A and C, which is exactly why it cannot be what distinguishes them.
  2. List the genuine alternatives and rank them. C gives ¥55,000, B gives ¥18,000. On expected value, C is the next best alternative to A — so the opportunity cost of A is ¥55,000, and A's ¥60,000 clears it by only ¥5,000.
  3. Then complicate it honestly. Expected value is not the only criterion. C locks up her money for three years and depends on a friend's business, while B is nearly risk-free and leaves her time free. If Mei judges C too risky or too illiquid to be a real alternative, the next best genuine alternative becomes B, and the opportunity cost of A falls to ¥18,000.
  4. Conclusion with a condition. The opportunity cost of the tutoring studio is ¥55,000 if a risk-adjusted view is taken and ¥18,000 if the risky option is excluded — and that range is the point. A rational decision needs the ¥5,000 surplus to be worth both the risk and the loss of her own time, which is an argument, not an arithmetic result.
Answer: ¥55,000 on a straight expected-value view, ¥18,000 if the franchise option is excluded as too risky — and the fact that the answer moves with an assumption is what makes this evaluation rather than arithmetic. Why this scores: the definition is AO1; the ranking of alternatives is AO2; the argument that the answer depends on whether C counts as a genuine alternative is AO3.

The factors of production

To produce anything you need four inputs. IB examiners like these because they are a ready-made four-mark list, but the marks only come if each one is attached to the business in the stimulus.

Land
All natural resources: the site, the raw materials, water, minerals, energy sources, and increasingly the "environmental" inputs a business draws on — clean air, water, fish stocks. The payment for it is rent.
Labour
Human effort, physical and mental, including the skill, training and judgement of the people involved. The payment is wages and salaries. Note that labour is not interchangeable: the cooks in a restaurant are the reason customers return, which is why a business's labour is often its hardest resource to replace.
Capital
The man-made aids to production: machines, tools, vehicles, buildings, software, and the money used to buy them. The payment is interest. Keep two meanings apart — capital as stuff (the ovens, the delivery vans) and capital as money (the finance used to buy the stuff). Unit 3 uses the second meaning almost exclusively.
Enterprise
The willingness of someone to combine the other three, take the risk, and make the decisions. The reward is profit. Enterprise is the factor that is not paid a fixed amount: rent, wages and interest are contractual, and the entrepreneur gets whatever is left — including, in a bad year, nothing at all.

Why enterprise is the interesting one

Land, labour and capital are bought: their owners have contracts and are paid whether the business succeeds or not. The entrepreneur has no such guarantee, and is paid last — after rent, wages, interest and suppliers. That ordering is not a technicality, it is the reason profit exists. Profit is the price of uncertainty. Once you see that, a whole set of questions changes shape: "should the business pay higher wages?" is not only an ethics question and not only a cost question, it is a question about who is bearing the risk and whether the return compensates for it. This is also the cleanest answer to "why do entrepreneurs exist?" — because somebody has to make decisions under uncertainty and accept being paid last for doing it.

The transformation process and added value

A business takes those inputs, does something to them, and produces an output that is worth more than the inputs were. That is the transformation process, and the difference is added value.

How to calculate and use added value

Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in inputs (materials, components, ingredients). Write the formula, substitute, and then — this is the step students skip — say what the number means.

  • Per unit: a bowl of noodles sells for ¥18; ingredients and packaging cost ¥4; added value = ¥14 per bowl.
  • In total: 12,000 bowls a month → total added value = 12,000 × ¥14 = ¥168,000. (Check: revenue ¥216,000 − ingredient cost ¥48,000 = ¥168,000.)
  • Then subtract everything else: rent ¥30,000, wages ¥90,000, utilities ¥12,000 = ¥132,000. Profit = 168,000 − 132,000 = ¥36,000.
  • Say what it means: the business keeps about 21 % of the value it creates. Anything that raises the price, lowers the bought-in cost, or raises volume flows straight through to profit at that ratio — which is why a ¥1 rise in ingredient cost cuts profit by ¥12,000, a third of the whole profit.

Two ways to increase added value, and they are examined: raise the price by branding, design, service or differentiation (customers pay more for the same inputs), or reduce the cost of bought-in inputs by buying in bulk, switching suppliers or cutting waste. The first is a Unit 4 answer; the second is a Unit 5 answer. Both are 1.1 answers.

Proof Where added value comes from — and why it is not profit
  1. Take one unit of output. Its price is what a customer will pay; the bought-in inputs (materials, components, ingredients) have a cost. Added value per unit = price − direct cost of bought-in materials.
  2. Example: a bowl of noodles sells for ¥18. The ingredients — flour, meat, vegetables, packaging — cost ¥4. Added value per bowl = ¥18 − ¥4 = ¥14.
  3. But the business also has costs that are not in the ingredients: rent, wages, electricity, equipment, delivery apps' commission. These must be paid out of the added value. If those come to ¥132,000 a month and the shop sells 12,000 bowls, added value is ¥168,000 and profit is ¥36,000.
  4. So: added value is the pool; profit is what is left in the pool after everything else. Added value measures how much value the business creates; profit measures how much it keeps. A business can create enormous added value and still make a loss, and that is the most common way growing businesses fail.
  5. When this is the wrong tool: added value says nothing about cash. A business can add value on paper while customers pay slowly and suppliers demand payment quickly — which is why cash flow, not added value, is what kills businesses (Unit 3).

Sectors of industry

Businesses are grouped by the stage of the chain they sit in: primary (extracting raw materials — farming, mining, fishing, forestry), secondary (manufacturing and construction), tertiary (services — retail, transport, banking, education, tourism, healthcare) and quaternary (information and knowledge — software, research, data services, consulting, financial analysis). A smartphone passes through all four: mined minerals, assembled components, sold through a retail network, and supported by software, cloud services and the data analysis behind the app store. Most of the value — and most of the profit — is no longer in the first two.

The pattern of sector shift. As economies develop, employment and output move from primary to secondary, then from secondary to tertiary and quaternary. The mechanism is not mysterious: productivity rises faster in farming and manufacturing than in services, so food and goods get cheaper relative to incomes, and households spend a shrinking share of their income on them and a growing share on services, health, education and digital products. China is the large, fast example: in the late 1970s roughly seven in ten workers were still in the primary sector, whereas today the figure is around one in five and services are the largest single employer, with a quaternary layer — software, platform businesses, research — growing on top. Britain and the United States went through the same transition over a much longer period, and the political aftermath of deindustrialisation in both is a reminder that the shift creates losers as well as winners.

Precision note

Treat the exact percentages above as indicative — the pattern is the examinable point, not the decimal. If your teacher or textbook gives you precise figures, use those. And keep two distinctions clean: sector is the stage of the chain (primary to quaternary), while industry is a group of businesses making similar things — "the airline industry" is entirely within the tertiary sector. Also note that a single business can operate in several sectors at once; an oil company is primary when it extracts and tertiary when it runs its own retail forecourts.

Why businesses exist: profit, risk and reward

The standard answer is that businesses exist to satisfy needs and wants by combining the factors of production, and that they take the risk of being wrong in exchange for profit. That is true and incomplete. Some organisations exist to create social or environmental value — social enterprises trade like a business but exist primarily to achieve a social purpose and reinvest most of their surplus in it. Others are owned by the state and pursue public policy objectives alongside commercial ones. Keeping this in view matters exam-wise too: at SL, one of the Paper 2 Section B options can be based on a social enterprise.

The profit motive

Profit is the signal that a business has created more value than it consumed, the reward that compensates the owner for risk, and the cheapest source of finance for the next investment (retained profit). Remove it and you remove the reason anyone takes the risk.

Risk and reward

The entrepreneur is paid last and only if there is anything left. Higher risk demands a higher expected return, which is why a speculative venture must promise more than a deposit account — and why a large share of new businesses fail.

Why not everyone is an entrepreneur

Most people prefer a contractual, predictable income to a residual, uncertain one. That is the real content of the word "enterprise": a willingness to be the residual claimant.

Why some businesses refuse profit maximisation

Survival, independence, a comfortable living, a social mission, or growth ahead of profit. "All businesses maximise profit" is the most common wrong sentence in Unit 1 — see 1.3.

The four business functions

Every business, whatever its sector, has to do the same four things, and those four things are Units 2 to 5:

Human resources (Unit 2)

Get the right people, organise them, lead them, pay and motivate them, communicate with them.

Finance and accounts (Unit 3)

Find the money, control the costs, record what happened, and work out whether the business is solvent and profitable.

Marketing (Unit 4)

Find out what customers want and design the product, price, distribution and promotion around it.

Operations (Unit 5)

Actually make it and get it to the customer, at the right quality, in the right quantity, at the right cost.

Connection

The four functions are not departments you study separately — they are four descriptions of the same business, and every decision you take in one of them is a decision in the others. A price cut is a marketing decision (Unit 4), a contribution-per-unit decision (Unit 3), a capacity decision (Unit 5) and, if it changes the sales team's commission, a motivation decision (Unit 2). The IB examines the arrows between the boxes, not the boxes.

Businesses and other organisations

The boundary of "business" is a favourite place to set an exam question, so know where the line falls. The test is not whether an organisation sells things — plenty of non-businesses charge for what they do — but who owns it, who gets the surplus, and what it is ultimately for.

Worked example 2 Apple — added value by design, not by factory

Question. Explain, using an example, what added value means and why a business might try to increase it.

  1. Define. Added value is the difference between the price a customer pays and the cost of the bought-in materials and components that went into the product.
  2. Apply. Apple does not manufacture most of its own products — assembly is contracted to manufacturers such as Foxconn, whose large Zhengzhou complex is the biggest single iPhone assembly site. What Apple owns is the design, the operating system, the chip architecture, the brand and the retail and app-store ecosystem. Those are the parts of the chain where the difference between cost and price is largest.
  3. Develop. Because the added value is concentrated in design, software and brand rather than in assembly, Apple can keep a large share of the final price even though it does not own the factories. That is what lets it fund research and development and still earn high margins — and it is also why the arrangement is criticised: the assembly work, with its lower added value and harder conditions, sits with suppliers.
  4. Sanity check / second side. Concentrating added value upstream also creates dependency: when Zhengzhou was badly disrupted in late 2022, iPhone supply was hit, which showed that owning the design does not remove the operational risk of a single-site supply chain.
Answer: added value is the excess of price over bought-in cost, and Apple increases it by owning the high-value stages of the chain — with the trade-off that the remaining stages become a concentrated operational risk. Marks: definition (AO1) 1; application to a named business's actual structure (AO2) 1–2; development of the consequence (AO2) 1–2; the second side is what lifts this from a 3 to a 5 on a 6-mark question.

1.2 Types of business entities

A business's legal form decides three things that come up in almost every case study: who is liable when it fails, who controls it, and how easily it can raise money. Everything else in this sub-topic is detail hanging off those three questions.

How to use this section — the six questions

Every legal form can be described by answering the same six questions, and every comparison or recommendation question in 1.2 is really asking you to work through them. Learn them as a checklist and you will never run out of things to say:

  1. Liability — if the business fails owing money, how much can the owners lose? Just what they put in, or their house as well?
  2. Finance — where can the money come from, and how much of it? Savings and bank loans only, or shares sold to investors and the public?
  3. Control — who makes the decisions, and can the founders keep making them as the business grows?
  4. Continuity — what happens if an owner dies, retires, sells up, or falls out with the others? Does the business survive them?
  5. Disclosure and tax — who gets to see the accounts, and is profit taxed once (as company profit) or twice (company profit then personal income)?
  6. Cost and complexity — how much does it cost to set up and to keep running, in money, paperwork and time?

Notice that the questions are in tension: what you gain on one you usually pay for on another. That tension is the evaluation. A legal form is never "better" in the abstract — it is better for a business with a particular situation, and that is the sentence examiners are waiting for.

1. Sole trader (sole proprietor)

A sole trader is a business owned, financed and controlled by one person, who keeps all the profit and carries all the risk. There is no legal distinction between the owner and the business: the business is not a separate legal person, it is the owner trading under a name.

  • Formation. Minimal. In most jurisdictions you register the business name with a local or national authority — in China, the individual-business-household form registered with the market-regulation authority — and, if you are taking on staff, register for payroll and social insurance. It can be done in days for a small fee and no legal advice.
  • Finance. The owner's own savings, money from family and friends, retained profit, supplier credit, and bank borrowing — but the bank will usually want security, and security means the owner's personal assets, typically the family home. A sole trader cannot issue shares: there is no one to issue them to.
  • Liability. Unlimited. If the business fails with debts it cannot pay, creditors can pursue the owner's personal assets — savings, car, and in the worst case the house. This is the single biggest drawback of the form and the reason most growing businesses eventually leave it.
  • Control. Complete. Every decision, every hour, every yuan of spending is the owner's. For someone whose product is their own judgement — a chef, a designer, a tutor — that is a real advantage, and it is why many profitable sole traders refuse to incorporate.
  • Tax and disclosure. The simplest treatment of all: business profit is treated as the owner's personal income and taxed once, at personal rates, with no separate company return. There is no obligation to publish accounts; only the tax authority sees the numbers.
  • Continuity. None. The business legally ends when the owner stops, retires, dies or sells — the premises and equipment can be sold, but the business itself does not survive the person.
  • Cost and complexity. The lowest of any form. No annual filings, no board, no audit, no company secretary.

Evaluation. This is the right choice when the business is small, personal and low-risk: the product depends on the owner's own skill, capital needs are modest and can be met from savings, customers pay promptly, and the owner's objective is independence and a living rather than rapid expansion — a tutor, a freelance designer, a single-site noodle shop, a market trader. It is the wrong choice when the business takes on borrowing it could not repay from the owner's own assets, signs long leases or large supply contracts, operates in a field where customers might sue (food safety, childcare, construction), or wants to bring in outside investors — because none of those are available to it. It depends above all on how much debt the business intends to carry and how replaceable the owner's personal assets are: unlimited liability is a terrifying phrase for a homeowner with a big loan and an almost fictional one for a student with no assets and no borrowing.

2. Partnership

A partnership is a business owned and run by between two and twenty-or-so people (the cap varies by jurisdiction) who share the profit, the workload and the risk. In an ordinary partnership the firm is still not a separate legal person: the partners are the business.

  • Formation. Easy and cheap, but the important document is the deed of partnership (partnership agreement). It should set out how much capital each partner contributes, how profit and loss are shared (which need not be equally, and often should not be, if contributions differ), how decisions are made and disputes settled, what happens when a partner wants out or a new partner joins, and what happens on death or retirement. Without one, most jurisdictions impose a default of equal profit-sharing and equal say — which is rarely what the partners actually intended.
  • Finance. Better than a sole trader, because each partner brings capital and each can borrow. That pooled capacity, plus complementary skills, is the main reason partnerships exist.
  • Liability. In an ordinary partnership, unlimited — and, worse, joint and several. Joint and several liability means a creditor who cannot recover from one partner can pursue any of the others for the whole debt. You are not merely liable for your own share; you can be pursued for your partner's mistakes. This is why partnerships are common among people who trust each other professionally and why professional firms have pushed for limited forms.
  • Variants. A limited partnership has at least one general partner with unlimited liability and one or more limited partners whose liability is capped at what they invested — provided they do not take part in management. It is the standard structure for venture-capital and private-equity funds. A limited liability partnership (LLP) goes further and gives all partners protection from the negligence of the others, which is why it is the usual form for large law and accountancy practices. Both variants exist to fix the joint-and-several problem; the SL syllabus only needs you to know they exist and why.
  • Control. Shared. Fast decisions among two trusted people; slow and painful ones when they disagree. Disagreement between partners is the classic failure mode of this form, well ahead of running out of money.
  • Tax and disclosure. Usually taxed like a sole trader: each partner is taxed personally on their share of the profit, so there is no separate company tax. Accounts are private.
  • Continuity. Fragile. An ordinary partnership technically dissolves when a partner dies, retires or leaves, unless the deed provides otherwise — which is exactly why the deed exists.

Evaluation. A partnership is the right choice when the business needs more capital or a wider range of skills than one person has, when professional trust is high, when the partners' reputations are the product (doctors, architects, lawyers, accountants, design studios), and when the work is hard to standardise and therefore hard to manage at arm's length. It is the wrong choice when large amounts of external capital are needed, when the activity carries high litigation or accident risk, or when the partners have materially different appetites for risk or different time horizons — a partner who wants to reinvest and a partner who wants to draw an income will destroy the business eventually. It depends on whether the joint-and-several risk is acceptable given what the partners know about each other, and on whether they have done the unglamorous work of writing a deed before they need it.

3. Private limited company (Ltd)

A private limited company is incorporated: it is a separate legal person, distinct from the people who own it. It can own property, sign contracts, sue and be sued in its own name, and continue indefinitely. Ownership is divided into shares held by shareholders, and the company is run day to day by directors appointed by those shareholders. The defining restriction is in the name: it cannot offer its shares to the general public.

Why incorporation is the hinge of Unit 1

Incorporation does three things at once, and all three are examined. It creates separate legal identity — the company, not the owner, owns the assets and owes the debts. It creates limited liability — the most a shareholder can lose is the amount unpaid on their shares. And it creates perpetual succession — the company continues to exist when owners change, so shares can be sold, gifted or inherited without the business dissolving. One legal step, three consequences; say all three and you have answered most "explain the advantages of incorporation" questions. Notice when it happens: at incorporation, not at any particular size.

  • Formation. Documents (a constitution or articles of association, and details of directors, shareholders and registered office) are filed with a companies registry and a certificate of incorporation is issued. In China the equivalent is the limited liability company, registered with the market-regulation authority with a stated registered capital. It takes longer and costs more than registering a sole trader, but it is a routine, well-understood process.
  • Finance. Much wider. Share capital can be raised from a known group — founders, family, business angels, venture-capital funds — and banks lend more readily to a company with a legal identity, audited accounts and assets in its own name. What it cannot do is advertise shares to the public or list them on an exchange.
  • Liability. Limited. Shareholders lose what they invested and no more. The catch, which textbooks understate, is that lenders to small companies routinely require personal guarantees from the directors, so in practice the founder of a small Ltd has often pledged the family home anyway — the protection is real but thinner than the theory.
  • Control. Shareholders vote, normally in proportion to shares held, and appoint the directors; in a small company the same people are usually both. Control is preserved as long as the founders hold more than 50 % (and comfortably so at 75 %+, which is what special resolutions typically require). The moment outside investors take a large stake, control becomes something that has to be negotiated in a shareholders' agreement rather than assumed.
  • Tax and disclosure. The company pays corporation tax on its profits, and shareholders then pay personal tax on dividends or on director salaries they take out — the "double taxation" point, which is real but often manageable because a well-advised owner takes a mix of salary and dividends. Annual accounts must be filed with the registry and are, in most jurisdictions, public: competitors, suppliers, customers and employees can see a good deal more than a sole trader would ever reveal.
  • Continuity. Perpetual succession. The company survives the departure, retirement or death of any shareholder or director. Shares pass to heirs or are sold, and the business continues — which is what makes it possible to build something intended to outlast its founder.
  • Cost and complexity. Materially higher: registration fees, annual returns, statutory registers, a company secretary in some jurisdictions, possibly an audit, and the ongoing time cost of compliance. For a one-person business with no borrowing this can be pure overhead.

Evaluation. A private limited company is the right choice when the business needs external capital from investors, carries meaningful commercial or legal risk, signs significant contracts or leases, wants to survive its founder, or plans to grow beyond what personal savings can fund — which describes most businesses at the point where they stop being a lifestyle and start being an asset. It is the wrong choice when the business is tiny, debt-free and unlikely to grow, when privacy about earnings matters more than access to capital, or when the compliance cost outweighs the benefit — and note that incorporating does not by itself make a business more profitable or better managed. It depends on the growth and risk plans of the owners: the form is bought for its optionality, and if you never intend to exercise that option you have paid for something you do not use.

4. Public limited company (PLC)

A public limited company is an incorporated company that may offer its shares to the public, usually by listing them on a stock exchange. All PLCs have limited liability; not all of them are listed, but the whole point of the form is the option to go public. Taking that step — a flotation or initial public offering (IPO) — means publishing a prospectus, having the shares underwritten by banks, and meeting the exchange's continuing obligations.

Underwriting, legal, accounting and marketing fees typically take a percentage of the funds raised, and the process consumes months of senior management time.

Going public is the right choice when the business needs more capital than private investors or banks will supply, when it wants to make acquisitions using shares, when the original owners want to realise some of their wealth, or when the credibility of being listed is commercially valuable in its own right. It is the wrong choice when the owners' central objective is control and a long time horizon — which is precisely why some very large, very successful companies deliberately stay private — when the business has no use for a large slug of capital, or when its earnings are volatile enough that public scrutiny would be a permanent distraction. It depends on whether the capital raised can be invested at a return above its cost: listing to fund growth that earns more than the market demands is value-creating; listing because it is what successful companies do is expensive theatre. BYD's listing funded capital-intensive factories at home and abroad — a genuine use for public capital — while Huawei's decision not to list preserves its freedom to spend on research over decades without justifying it every quarter. Same industry, opposite choices, both defensible.

5. Franchise

A franchise is not a legal form of ownership so much as a contractual arrangement, which is why it sits in both 1.2 and 1.5. The franchisor owns a brand and a proven way of operating, and licenses a franchisee to run an outlet using it. The franchisee supplies most of the capital and manages the outlet; in return they usually pay an initial franchise fee, an ongoing royalty (a percentage of turnover, not of profit), a contribution to national marketing, and often a commitment to buy from approved suppliers.

  • For the franchisee. Lower risk than starting from nothing — the concept has been tested, training and systems are provided, and the brand brings customers from day one. The price is autonomy: hours, layout, suppliers, prices and even the menu are largely dictated, and the fees are payable whether or not the outlet makes money.
  • For the franchisor. Growth funded by other people's capital, with owner-managers who are far more motivated than any salaried branch manager. The price is control: consistency across hundreds or thousands of independently run outlets is a permanent managerial problem, and every quality failure anywhere in the network is a failure of the brand.
  • Where it works. Where the operating model can be written down — fast food, coffee and tea chains, courier depots, tutoring centres, hotels — and where local knowledge (sites, staff, relationships) makes an owner-operator better than a salaried one.
  • Where it fails. Where quality depends on individual judgement that cannot be standardised, where the brand is too young to carry a fee, or where unit economics are too thin to support both a royalty and a franchisee's living.

Evaluation. Franchising is the right choice when the constraint on growth is capital or speed rather than capability: it lets a business secure sites and market coverage far faster than it could fund them itself, which is exactly the situation Mixue was in as it built a network of tens of thousands of outlets at low price points. It is the wrong choice when the constraint is quality — if the brand promise depends on craft or on people you cannot recruit and train fast enough, franchising multiplies the problem at the speed of the rollout. It depends on the strength of the system behind the brand: franchise agreements, training, supplier control and inspection are what hold consistency together, and a business that franchises before it has them is selling its reputation to the highest bidder.

6. Joint venture

A joint venture (JV) is a business arrangement in which two or more organisations create a new, jointly owned entity to pursue a specific project or market, while each parent remains independent. It sits between a merger (which permanently combines two firms) and a strategic alliance (which is cooperation without a new entity). Ownership, investment, risk, control and profit are shared according to an agreement.

  • Why firms do it. To enter a market where a local partner brings knowledge, licences, relationships or political acceptance — historically important in China, where foreign firms in several industries were required to work with a domestic partner; to share the enormous cost and risk of a project that neither could fund alone; to combine complementary capabilities, such as one firm's technology and another's distribution; and to move faster than either could alone.
  • What it costs. Profits are shared rather than kept. Decisions are slower, because they must be agreed. Cultures and management styles can clash badly. And the partner you teach is a competitor you have created — technology and know-how leakage is a real and common outcome.
  • How it usually ends. With a pre-agreed exit: one partner buys the other out, the venture is sold, or it is wound up. Ventures that begin without that clause are the ones that end badly.

Evaluation. A joint venture is the right choice when the opportunity is real but neither firm can reach it alone — a market that requires local knowledge or a licence, a project whose cost exceeds one balance sheet, or a capability gap that would take years to build. It is the wrong choice when the firm's competitive advantage is precisely the knowledge it would have to share, when the partners' objectives diverge (one wants market share, the other wants short-term profit), or when the same outcome could be bought outright. It depends on partner compatibility and on the contract: a clear agreement on funding, control, intellectual property and exit converts a JV from a gamble into a tool.

7. Cooperatives, social enterprises and the not-for-profit boundary

These forms exist because "make a profit for outside owners" is not the only reason to organise economic activity. Get the boundary right, because the syllabus explicitly asks you to distinguish for-profit from not-for-profit organisations.

Cooperative

Owned and democratically controlled by its members, who may be its customers (a retail or consumer co-op), its workers (a worker co-op), or its suppliers (an agricultural marketing co-op). The rule that distinguishes it is one member, one vote — control follows people, not capital. Surplus is returned to members in proportion to their use of the cooperative, or reinvested. It is trading, and it aims for a surplus, but the surplus serves the members rather than outside shareholders, so it is best described as member-benefit rather than straightforwardly for-profit.

Social enterprise

Trades commercially like any business, but exists primarily to achieve a social or environmental purpose, and reinvests most of its surplus in that purpose. Some are structured as ordinary companies with a locked social mission; others are not-for-profit and cannot distribute surplus at all. The purpose, not the legal form, is what makes it a social enterprise — which is why the category cuts across the others and why a Paper 2 option may be built on one.

Charity / NGO

Not-for-profit. Governed by trustees, funded by donations, grants and sometimes trading income, with every yuan of surplus applied to the cause. Distributing surplus to owners is exactly what would disqualify it. Some charities run trading arms that look and behave like businesses, which is where students get confused.

State-owned enterprise (SOE)

Owned wholly or mainly by the state, typically in utilities, transport, energy, banking and telecommunications, and expected to serve policy objectives alongside commercial ones. In China this is not a fringe category — it is central to the economy. An SOE can be profitable, listed, and still be carrying employment, regional-development or strategic objectives that a private firm would not.

For-profit or not? The quick answer

For-profit: sole trader, partnership, private limited company, public limited company — surplus belongs to the owners. Member-benefit: cooperative — surplus belongs to the members, on a one-member-one-vote basis. Purpose-locked, may be either: social enterprise — check whether its constitution permits any distribution to owners. Not-for-profit: charity or NGO — no distribution at all. If a question asks you to classify an organisation, this is the sentence to write, and you should follow it with the consequence: a not-for-profit can survive without profit but not without funding, while a for-profit can survive without funding but not without margin.

Evaluation. A cooperative is the right choice where the members' problem is market power rather than capital — small farmers pooling to reach buyers, savers pooling to borrow — and it works best when members share a clear, narrow interest. It is the wrong choice when decisions need to be fast or when large external capital is required, because one-member-one-vote and non-distributable reserves make outside investors indifferent to it. A social enterprise is the right choice when the social purpose is the objective and a commercial income makes the mission sustainable rather than grant-dependent, and the wrong choice when trading activity would distort the mission — chasing revenue can quietly become the objective. Both depend on whether the people running them can hold the commercial and the social objectives in tension without letting either disappear.

Worked example 4 Three real structures: Huawei, BYD and Haier

Question. Explain why two businesses in the same industry might choose different legal forms.

  1. Huawei. Founded in Shenzhen in 1987 and grown into one of the world's largest telecoms-equipment makers, Huawei is not publicly listed. It describes itself as employee-owned, with shares held through a holding company whose equity is held by an employee representative body. Choosing not to list means no obligation to publish detailed accounts, no quarterly pressure from outside shareholders, and freedom to take a very long view on research spending — a real advantage when your main product is infrastructure that governments buy on trust. The cost is a much narrower pool of capital, and it also makes the ownership structure harder for outsiders to evaluate, which has fed suspicion in some markets.
  2. BYD. Started in the mid-1990s as a rechargeable-battery maker and moved into cars, then into electric vehicles, BYD is a public limited company whose shares trade on stock exchanges, which is how it funds capital-intensive factories at home and, more recently, plants announced in Hungary, Brazil, Thailand and Turkey. Listing bought it the capital; the price is public disclosure, market scrutiny, and exposure to investor sentiment.
  3. Haier. Began in Qingdao in the mid-1980s as a collective enterprise with a quality crisis famous enough that its boss reportedly had defective refrigerators smashed in front of the workforce. It evolved into a listed multinational and bought GE Appliances in 2016 — the same firm, three different structures over forty years, which is exactly what "evolution" in 1.5 means.
  4. Compare and conclude. The difference is not size — all three are large. It is about what each owner wanted to protect: control and long horizons (Huawei), access to capital for factories (BYD), and a route out of state ownership into global markets (Haier).
Answer: legal form follows control, capital and time horizon, not size — Huawei stays private to protect long-term control, BYD lists to fund factories, and Haier changed form as its objectives changed. Marks: two businesses explained in context (2 × 2), plus a comparative conclusion that answers "why different" (1–2).
Common trap

Three mistakes recur. (1) "Limited liability means the owner loses nothing." It means the owner loses their investment — and in practice banks often require personal guarantees from directors of small companies, so the protection is thinner than the textbook suggests. (2) "A PLC is bigger than a private limited company." Legal form is not size; there are private companies larger than listed ones. (3) "Sole trader means no employees." A sole trader can employ hundreds; the word describes ownership, not scale.

Connection

Legal form reappears in Unit 3 as the gate on sources of finance: a sole trader cannot issue shares and a private limited company cannot sell them to the public, so the growth options in 1.5 are partly determined by the entity chosen in 1.2. It also reappears in 1.6 — "multinational" is a geographic label, not a legal one, so an MNC can be any of these forms.

Decision framework: how to choose, and when to change

Choosing a legal form is not a matter of picking the "best" one. It is a matter of matching the form to the constraint the business actually faces. Work through these five questions in order; each one eliminates half the options.

  1. How much capital does the business need, and from whom? If savings and a modest secured loan are enough, the simple forms are fine. If money must come from outside investors, you need shares, which means a company. If it must come from the public, you need a PLC.
  2. How much risk does the activity carry, and what personal assets are exposed? Food safety, childcare, construction and anything involving large contracts raise the value of limited liability sharply. A student with no assets and no borrowing has almost nothing to protect; a homeowner signing a five-year lease has a great deal.
  3. How much control are the owners willing to give up? Every external source of money comes with someone else's opinion attached. If control is the objective, stay private and grow from retained profit.
  4. What is the time horizon? A business the owner intends to sell or pass on needs continuity, and therefore incorporation. A business intended to fund a lifestyle for fifteen years and then close does not.
  5. What does the compliance cost buy? Filing, disclosure and audit costs are worth paying when they unlock capital, limit risk or allow succession. Otherwise they are overhead.
How to answer a "recommend a legal form" question

Do not describe the options and then pick one. Use this shape, which is short enough to write in eight minutes and hits all three assessment objectives:

  • Decide in the first sentence. "I recommend X." One mark for committing.
  • Two reasons tied to facts in the stimulus. Not "Ltds have limited liability" but "the founders are about to sign a five-year lease and take a bank loan, so limited liability matters here".
  • One reason the alternative was rejected. This is what makes it a decision rather than a description: "a partnership would be cheaper, but joint and several liability is unacceptable given…".
  • One condition. "This recommendation holds as long as…; if the business instead plans to raise capital from the public within two years, a PLC would be right."

Four sentences, four functions, full marks. Students lose these questions by writing eight sentences about what each form is.

Five scenarios: what would you recommend, and why?

Worked example 5 Scenario 1 — A student starting a tutoring business

Situation. Lucas, in his first year of the Diploma, tutors two younger students for two hours a week at ¥150 an hour, with no premises, no stock, no borrowing and no staff.

  1. Recommendation: sole trader.
  2. Reason one — the exposure is trivial. There are no leases, no inventory, no employees and no borrowing, so there is almost nothing for unlimited liability to attach to. Limited liability is insurance; do not buy insurance for a risk you do not have.
  3. Reason two — the compliance cost is disproportionate. Registration, annual filings and possibly an audit would consume time and money out of all proportion to a few thousand yuan a month of income.
  4. Reason three — control and simplicity. There is one decision-maker and the tax treatment is the simplest available.
  5. What would change the answer. The day he signs a lease for a studio, hires other tutors, takes a loan, or brings in a partner to fund expansion, the answer becomes a private limited company — because that is the day the risk and the capital requirement both arrive.
Answer: sole trader, because with no assets, debts or staff the protection of incorporation is worth nothing and its cost is real. Why this is worth knowing: it is the standard starting case, and the conditional last line is what makes it an evaluation.
Worked example 6 Scenario 2 — A family opening a restaurant

Situation. Two siblings and their parents put ¥600,000 of family savings into a restaurant. They sign a five-year lease costing ¥22,000 a month, fit out the premises for ¥300,000, and take a ¥150,000 bank loan. Food safety and a customer slipping on a wet floor are both real risks.

  1. Recommendation: private limited company, with the family as shareholders and the two working siblings as directors.
  2. Liability is now a real risk, not a theoretical one. Five years of lease liability plus a bank loan plus the possibility of a food-poisoning or accident claim is exactly the situation unlimited liability is designed to be unbearable in. Incorporation caps the family's loss at what they put into the company.
  3. Continuity matters because the family is the business. If one sibling wants out in three years, a company can survive that — shares are reallocated or bought — whereas a partnership would technically dissolve.
  4. The honest counter-argument. The bank will almost certainly require personal guarantees from the directors for a loan to a newly formed company with no trading record, so the protection is partial in year one. That does not make the recommendation wrong; it means the family understands exactly what it is still on the hook for. And a sole trader would attract the same guarantees anyway.
  5. Why not a partnership? It would be cheaper to run, but joint and several liability means the parent who is least involved could still be pursued for the whole debt — an unacceptable structure for a family.
  6. Condition. If the concept works and they want to open five more sites, the capital constraint returns: either take outside investors into the company, or franchise the format.
Answer: a private limited company, because leases, borrowing and liability risk make unlimited liability dangerous, and because the family needs a structure that survives one member leaving. Marks: recommendation 1; two reasons applied to the facts 2 × 2; the honest point about personal guarantees is the AO3 development.
Worked example 7 Scenario 3 — A tech startup wanting outside investment

Situation. Three graduates have built an app with 40,000 users and no revenue. A venture-capital fund has offered ¥4 million for 25 % of the business, on condition the founders stay for four years and the fund gets a seat on the board.

  1. Recommendation: incorporate as a private limited company and issue new shares to the fund.
  2. The form is forced by the finance. A venture fund cannot invest in a partnership — there are no shares to buy and the liability would be unlimited. Only a company can issue the equity the deal requires.
  3. What they gain. ¥4 million of risk capital that does not have to be repaid, plus governance, contacts and credibility.
  4. What they give up, honestly stated. A quarter of the business, a board seat, and a say in strategy. At 75 % the founders still control ordinary decisions, but they no longer control everything, and future funding rounds will dilute them further. The fund's objective — an exit within five to seven years — is now partly their objective too.
  5. The alternative they rejected. Bank borrowing or a smaller, slower rollout funded from revenue would preserve full ownership, but at 40,000 users and no revenue there is nothing for a bank to lend against, and slow growth in a platform market usually means losing it.
  6. Condition. This is right if the money can be turned into users and revenue fast enough to justify the dilution; it is wrong if the founders' real objective is a comfortable owner-managed business, in which case they should not take the money at all.
Answer: a private limited company, because only a company can issue the shares a venture investor requires — and the price is control and a share of future profits. Why this is worth knowing: "needs outside investment" is the cleanest of all the triggers for incorporation.
Worked example 8 Scenario 4 — A manufacturer considering going public

Situation. A profitable, family-controlled manufacturer of industrial pumps has revenue of ¥900 million, no debt, and an order book that requires a ¥500 million factory. The bank will lend ¥200 million. The founding family owns 100 % and has run the business for thirty years.

  1. The recommendation is not obvious, and the reasoning is more valuable than the answer. Arguments for a flotation: it raises the ¥300 million the bank will not, funds a factory that the order book justifies, gives the family partial liquidity after thirty years of reinvestment, and creates a share price that can be used to acquire competitors.
  2. Arguments against, developed. The company has no debt and is profitable, so the cheapest option — retained profit plus the ¥200 million bank facility plus a slower build — preserves 100 % family control and avoids flotation costs, disclosure and quarterly pressure entirely. Going public permanently changes the objective: the family would be running the business partly for outside shareholders who did not spend thirty years building it, and a business with a long product-development cycle fits quarterly reporting badly.
  3. A third option most students miss. Sell a minority stake to a private-equity investor or a strategic partner. This raises the missing capital without listing, at the cost of a private investor's influence — and keeps the disclosure burden and short-term pressure away.
  4. Judgement with a condition. Listing is justified if the factory can be built and filled fast enough that the return on the capital exceeds its cost, and if the family is genuinely willing to accept outside scrutiny. It is not justified simply because the company is large — legal form is not a reward for size. If the family's overriding objective remains control, the private placement or the slower build is better even if it means growing more slowly.
  5. What I cannot know. The actual cost of the flotation, the valuation the market would put on the business, and whether the order book is contracted or merely forecast — and all three would change the recommendation.
Answer: it depends on whether the family values growth or control more, and on whether the new capacity earns more than the capital costs — with a private minority stake as the middle way. Why this is worth knowing: this is the exact shape of a 10-mark question, and the middle option is what most candidates never see.
Worked example 9 Scenario 5 — Two partners who fall out

Situation. Two friends set up a design studio as an ordinary partnership three years ago, splitting profit 50:50 with no written agreement. One now wants to take a bank loan and hire four people; the other wants to keep the studio small and draw a steady income. They agree on nothing.

  1. Diagnose the structural cause, not the personalities. Ordinary partnerships make decisions jointly, and without a deed the default is an equal say. A 50:50 split with no deadlock-breaking mechanism is a structure that can only work while the partners want the same thing. The disagreement was predictable the day they set up.
  2. The liability point makes it urgent. Liability is joint and several. If the expansionist partner signs a lease or a loan, the cautious partner is liable for all of it — and cannot prevent it. That is the real danger of the form.
  3. Options, with consequences. (a) Write a deed now, setting out capital contributions, a different profit split reflecting effort, and a decision or deadlock procedure — cheap, and it fixes the governance but not the liability. (b) Incorporate as a private limited company with a shareholders' agreement and different share classes or voting arrangements, which caps liability and lets the cautious partner be bought out. (c) One buys the other out, valuing the goodwill — often the only durable answer once trust has gone. (d) Dissolve.
  4. Recommendation. Convert to a private limited company with a shareholders' agreement, and use it to execute a buy-out of whichever partner wants to leave. Incorporation imposes the governance they failed to write for themselves and limits the exposure that currently makes each of them liable for the other.
  5. Condition. If they can still agree on a future, option (a) is cheaper and sufficient; if they cannot, no structure will save the business and a clean buy-out is better than a slow collapse.
Answer: incorporate, because the partnership's joint and several liability plus equal control is the mechanism turning a disagreement into a threat to both of them. Why this is worth knowing: it links 1.2 to 1.5 — the business has outgrown the form that suited it at the start.

Common exam traps on legal form

Common trap

Five mistakes account for most of the marks lost on 1.2. Learn them and say the correction out loud.

  • Confusing size with legal form. "Sole trader" describes ownership, not scale: a sole trader can employ hundreds of people and turn over millions. "PLC" describes the right to sell shares to the public, not size: there are private companies larger than most listed ones. Never infer a legal form from how big a business looks, and never use "big" as a reason for incorporating.
  • "Limited liability means the bank can never touch your house." It means shareholders lose their investment. In practice, banks lending to small companies routinely require directors' personal guarantees, so the founder has often pledged the house anyway. The protection is real, but partial — and saying so is a genuinely sophisticated point.
  • "A PLC is simply a better Ltd." It is a different trade, not an upgrade. Listing buys capital and liquidity; it costs control, privacy, money and time, and it imports short-term pressure. Plenty of firms that could list choose not to, and they are not being timid — they are protecting a long time horizon.
  • "Unlimited liability means you lose everything." In practice it means you lose everything you have. A creditor cannot take assets that do not exist: a sole trader with no savings, no property and no borrowing has almost nothing to lose, which is why unlimited liability is a serious risk for a homeowner with a lease and nearly irrelevant for a student with a laptop. The right sentence is always "the significance of unlimited liability depends on the owner's personal assets and the business's debts".
  • Describing when asked to recommend. If the command term is recommend, justify or evaluate, a list of features scores at the bottom of the level. Decide in the first sentence and spend the rest of the answer on why the alternative was rejected and what would change your mind.
Common trap

Three more, on the non-standard forms. (1) "A franchise is a small business." The franchisee's outlet usually is; the franchisor may be a listed multinational with tens of thousands of outlets. Franchising is a growth method, not a size. (2) "A joint venture is a merger." It is not: the parents remain independent, and the venture is usually created for a defined purpose and period. (3) "A cooperative is a charity." No — it trades, aims for a surplus, and returns it to its members. Not-for-profit means no distribution to owners; a cooperative distributes to members.

How to write the AO3 evaluation for legal-form questions

The reason 1.2 is worth twenty hours of your attention is that it is the first place in the course where you are asked to recommend. That is an AO3 skill, and AO3 has a shape you can learn.

How to build an evaluation: because → therefore → it depends on

Every developed argument in business management is three moves, though you will not write them as headings:

  1. State the mechanism — "because". Not the feature, the mechanism. "Because incorporation gives the company a separate legal identity…"
  2. State the consequence for this business — "therefore". "…therefore the founders are not personally liable for the five-year lease they are about to sign, so the most they can lose is the ¥200,000 they have invested." Numbers from the stimulus, consequences for this business.
  3. State the condition that would reverse it — "it depends on". "…but this only holds while the bank does not require personal guarantees; if it does, the protection is much thinner."

Two "because → therefore" chains, one for each side, plus a judgement that says what the answer depends on, plus one honest limitation. That is a 9–10. What students actually write is a list of features, which is a 4–5, because description is AO1 and the top levels are reserved for consequence and condition.

Worked example 10 Model answer — a 10-mark response, with the reasons it scores

Question. Kay and Tomas run a successful bakery as an ordinary partnership. A supermarket chain has offered a contract that would double their output, but it requires new equipment costing ¥800,000 and delivery within three months. Evaluate whether they should change their legal form.

Introduction and decision. I recommend that they incorporate as a private limited company. The current form was right when the business was small, but the contract changes both the capital requirement and the risk, and a partnership is suited to neither.

Argument one — finance, because → therefore. Because a partnership cannot issue shares, the ¥800,000 can only come from the partners' own savings or from borrowing. Therefore, if they have, say, ¥200,000 between them, they must borrow ¥600,000 — and they must do it within three months, against personal security, at a time when the equipment is not yet earning anything. A private limited company, by contrast, could bring in an outside investor for part of the equipment cost, or offer shares to a supplier, and would find banks more willing to lend to a company with a legal identity of its own.

Argument two — liability, because → therefore. Because ordinary partnership liability is unlimited and joint and several, each of them is liable for the whole debt and for the other's decisions. Therefore the ¥600,000 is not really shared: if the supermarket contract is cancelled after four months, either partner can be pursued for all of it, and a home or a car is at stake. Incorporation caps the loss at the capital invested and, just as importantly, separates the two of them.

The case against, developed honestly. Incorporation is not free. There are registration and ongoing filing costs, annual accounts become available to competitors and suppliers — including, in principle, the supermarket chain that is setting their prices — and profit may be taxed twice, once as corporation tax and again when it is taken out as dividends. There is also a real chance that the bank requires personal guarantees anyway, in which case the liability advantage is smaller than the textbook claim. And none of this makes the bread better: the contract still has to be delivered.

Judgement, with the condition. On balance the recommendation holds, because the two factors that have changed — the size of the borrowing and the risk attached to it — are exactly the two factors a partnership handles worst. But it depends on the financing route: if the equipment can be leased rather than bought, or the supermarket can be persuaded to fund or part-fund it, the capital requirement shrinks and remaining a partnership becomes defensible for longer. It also depends on whether the contract is genuinely contracted rather than a letter of intent — borrowing ¥600,000 against a deal that can be cancelled is the real risk here, not the legal form.

Limitation. I do not know their personal assets, their existing borrowings, the terms of the supermarket contract or their tax position, and each of those would affect how strong the argument is. In particular, if the contract allows cancellation at short notice, the recommendation changes from "incorporate and invest" to "do not invest at all until the contract is firmer".

Why this scores in the top band.

  • AO1 (knowledge): unlimited and joint and several liability, inability to issue shares, incorporation and separate legal identity, disclosure and double taxation — all used accurately rather than listed.
  • AO2 (application): every point is attached to a number or a fact from the stimulus — ¥800,000, three months, the supermarket's power over price, the possibility of leasing.
  • AO3 (analysis and evaluation): each argument is a chain (because → therefore) rather than a feature; the counter-argument is developed in its own right rather than dismissed; the judgement names the conditions under which the answer would change; and the final paragraph identifies what the stimulus does not say.

A version of this answer that correctly describes partnerships and companies but never says what Kay and Tomas should do is worth roughly half.

Answer: incorporate as a private limited company, because the contract changes the capital requirement and the liability exposure simultaneously — and the recommendation weakens if the equipment can be leased or the contract is not firm. Marks: knowledge 2–3, application 2–3, analysis and evaluation 3–4, with the conditions and the limitation paragraph separating 9–10 from 7–8.
Exam technique

If a 1.2 question appears inside a Paper 1 case study, the stimulus will almost always contain a trigger — the business is taking on a loan, signing a lease, being offered investment, planning to franchise, considering a listing, or an owner is retiring or dying. Find the trigger before you write anything, and let it choose the form for you. Then spend your words on the trade-off the trigger creates, not on describing the form. A candidate who spots that "the founder's daughter does not want to take over the business" is a continuity question has effectively been given the marks.

1.3 Business objectives

An objective is a specific, measurable target that a business sets in order to move towards its aims. It sits inside a hierarchy, and the hierarchy is worth knowing because it lets you place any statement a case study makes:

Vision — the future it wants→ Mission — why it exists→ Aims — general goals→ Objectives — measurable targets→ Strategy — the plan→ Tactics — the actions

Objectives are usually described as needing to be SMART: specific, measurable, achievable (or agreed), relevant and time-bound. That is worth knowing, but the exam value is in the why: a vague objective cannot tell a manager which of two options to pick, and the whole point of an objective is to act as a decision criterion.

What businesses actually aim for

Survival

The first objective of almost every new business. Most new ventures do not make it past the first few years, and in that period the target is not profit but staying solvent.

Profit

The reward for risk, the source of retained finance, and the measure most owners use. But "maximise profit" is rarely the literal objective — see the trap below.

Growth and market share

Growth in revenue, outlets, employees or output; market share is growth relative to competitors. Often pursued ahead of profit, especially in new markets.

Social, ethical and environmental objectives

Fair wages, reduced emissions, community investment, ethical sourcing. For a social enterprise these are the primary objective, not an add-on.

Shareholder value

For listed companies, the pressure to raise the share price and pay dividends is a genuine constraint on what management can do — and a frequent source of conflict with long-term investments.

Personal objectives of the owner

Independence, a comfortable income, a business to pass on, a particular lifestyle. This is why many profitable small firms refuse to grow.

Objectives change

Objectives are not fixed. They change with the age of the business (survival → profit → growth → consolidation), its size (a listed company has objectives a sole trader does not), its ownership (an SOE carries policy objectives; a family firm may prioritise control over growth), its sector, and the external environment (a recession shifts the objective from growth to survival; new regulation creates a compliance objective). They also change because stakeholders push them (1.4).

Conflicts between objectives

This is the part that earns evaluation marks. Objectives collide constantly:

  • Short-term profit vs long-term growth. Cutting the training budget raises this year's profit and damages the capability the business needs to grow.
  • Profit vs ethics. A cheaper supplier with poorer labour standards raises margin and risks the brand and the licence to operate.
  • Growth vs quality and control. Opening outlets faster than you can staff them trades the brand for the network.
  • Market share vs margin. Cutting price wins share and destroys the gross profit margin that funds everything else.
  • Shareholder returns vs employee pay. The classic, and the one Paper 1 loves.
Worked example 11 Unilever — when the objective itself is the story

Question. Explain why a large business might set objectives other than profit maximisation.

  1. Define the issue. Profit maximisation assumes a single, unambiguous goal. Real listed companies serve shareholders whose horizons differ, customers who punish reputational damage, regulators, and employees — so the objective has to be a compromise.
  2. Apply. Unilever, the consumer-goods group behind brands such as Dove and Knorr, has publicly tied part of its strategy to sustainability targets and has argued that its purpose-led brands grow faster than the rest of its portfolio. Whether or not you accept that claim, the structure of the objective is clear: environmental and social performance is stated as a business objective, not as charity.
  3. Develop. There are three reasons this is rational rather than sentimental. Customers increasingly choose on these grounds, so there is a revenue argument. Employees and graduates prefer to work for such firms, so there is a recruitment and retention argument (Unit 2). And regulation and investor expectations increasingly price in environmental risk, so there is a cost-of-capital argument (Unit 3).
  4. Second side. It also creates real tensions: sustainability commitments can require investment with a long payback, which conflicts with shareholder pressure for short-term returns — and Unilever has faced exactly this debate, including criticism from some investors that its sustainability push distracted from performance, alongside decisions such as announcing the separation of its ice cream business to sharpen the portfolio.
Answer: objectives beyond profit are set because customers, employees, regulators and investors all constrain what "success" can mean — and because they conflict with short-term profit, which is the interesting part. Marks: this shape (definition, application, development, tension) is what 6–8 mark AO2/AO3 questions reward.
Worked example 12 A restaurant's first three years — how objectives change

Year 1: survival. The objective is to reach the end of the year with cash in the bank. Decisions are judged by one question: does this keep us open? That is why the owner discounts rather than invests in decor, and why cash flow matters more than profit.

Year 2: profit and stability. With a customer base, the objective becomes a target level of monthly profit and lower staff turnover. Now the owner may invest in training, because the payoff is within reach.

Year 3: growth or not. The owner can open a second site — growth, more profit, more risk, less control, and the need for managers she trusts — or keep one site and take more money out. Both are rational; which is right depends on her objectives, not on the numbers alone. And a good number of owners choose the second, which is why "businesses want to grow" is a claim you should always challenge.

Point of the example: "what the business wants" is a variable, and identifying what stage it is at is the cheapest way to produce a developed, applied answer.
Common trap

"All businesses aim to maximise profit" is the single most common wrong sentence in this unit. Plenty of businesses aim to survive, to grow share, to serve a community, to provide the owner with a comfortable living, or to achieve a social mission — and even profit-seeking firms rarely maximise, because maximising this year's profit would mean starving training, maintenance and research. Use "profit" as one objective among several that must be traded off, and you will sound like the markscheme.

Connection

Objectives are the bridge between 1.3 and 1.4: stakeholders are, in effect, groups with competing views about what the objective should be. They also drive Unit 2 (targets motivate — or demotivate — people) and Unit 3 (objectives become budgets and ratio targets). In TOK terms, an objective is the assumption you smuggle into an argument; naming it is a genuine critical-thinking move.

1.4 Stakeholders

A stakeholder is any person or group with an interest in, or affected by, the activities and decisions of a business. The word is deliberately wider than shareholder: shareholders own the company, stakeholders are everybody the company touches. That distinction is the foundation of half the evaluation questions in the course.

Internal stakeholders

Owners and shareholders, managers, employees. They are inside the organisation and are directly affected by its decisions — and they have the most frequent influence on them.

External stakeholders

Customers, suppliers, lenders and banks, government and regulators, the local community, pressure groups and, in most treatments, competitors. They are outside the business but can still constrain it severely.

Mapping stakeholders: the power/interest grid

Naming stakeholders is trivial; deciding which ones matter for a particular decision is the skill. The standard tool is a two-by-two grid that sorts stakeholders by how much power they have over the decision and how much interest they have in it.

Conflict, and what managers can actually do

Stakeholder objectives conflict because the business has only one pool of money and one set of decisions. If it goes to wages, it cannot go to dividends; if it goes to cheaper suppliers, it may not go to quality. Conflicts are not a management failure, they are the normal condition — which is why the interesting question is never "who is right" but "how was it handled".

The standard responses are consultation and communication (talk before deciding, and explain after), negotiation and compromise (a partial outcome each side can accept), prioritisation (deciding in advance which stakeholders matter most for this decision), and, in the best case, reframing the decision so a trade-off becomes a joint gain — for example, a training programme that raises both wages and productivity. What does not work, and what examiners see constantly, is pretending the conflict does not exist.

Worked example 13 Apple, Foxconn and Zhengzhou — a stakeholder conflict with consequences

Question. Discuss the conflicts of interest that can arise between a multinational and the suppliers in its supply chain.

  1. Identify the stakeholders. Apple's shareholders want margin and reliable supply; its customers want low prices and new products on time; Foxconn wants volume and predictable orders; the assembly workers want pay, hours and conditions; the local government in Zhengzhou wants employment and tax revenue.
  2. Explain the structural cause. Apple designs and sells the product but contracts the assembly, so the cost pressure it faces is transmitted to a supplier whose own margin is thin. The supplier's rational response is to keep labour costs flexible and to concentrate production where it is cheapest and most scalable — which is how a single very large site becomes the bottleneck for a global product.
  3. Develop the consequence. Late in 2022, disruption at the Zhengzhou complex affected iPhone supply. That single event exposes the whole stakeholder web: customers waited, Apple's sales were constrained, workers were caught in the middle, and the local authority had to manage the fallout. It also illustrates the counter-argument to "efficiency is always good" — concentration buys scale and cost, and pays for it with fragility.
  4. Second side and judgement. It is too simple to call this exploitation. The jobs are often better paid than the alternatives available to the workers, the technology and skills transfer is real, and the host region gains an industrial base it could not have built alone. The defensible conclusion is about balance and transparency: the arrangement is beneficial but fragile, and the fragility is concentrated on the stakeholders with the least power.
Answer: the conflict is structural — the MNC transmits cost and volume pressure to a supplier whose workers bear the flexibility, and the same concentration that delivers efficiency also creates the risk that hits everyone. Marks: stakeholder identification is AO1; the causal chain is AO2; the "fragility" conclusion and the balanced judgement are AO3.
Common trap

A stakeholder list is not an analysis. "The stakeholders are customers, employees, suppliers, government and the local community" is one mark and nothing more. The marks come from saying what each one wants in this decision, why those wants conflict, who has the power, and what the business should do about it. If you catch yourself writing a list, stop and add a verb to every name.

Connection

Stakeholders reappear everywhere. In Unit 2 they become employees (motivation, communication, and the HL only industrial-relations material you can skip). In Unit 3 they become lenders and shareholders reading the accounts. In Unit 4 they become customers. In TOK and the EE, stakeholder conflicts are the cleanest available example of a knowledge question with real ethical weight: whose evidence counts, and who gets to decide?

1.5 Growth and evolution

Growth means getting bigger — more revenue, more outlets, more output, more employees, more market share. Evolution is the broader idea: as a business grows it changes what it is. A one-person operation with a van becomes a company with a warehouse, a manager, a bank loan and a payroll; that company becomes a group with divisions, then perhaps a multinational. The structure, the objectives and the problems all change on the way.

Two routes: internal and external

RouteWhat it isWhy choose itWhat it costs you
Internal (organic) growth Expanding using the business's own resources: more output from existing sites, new sites, new products, more staff, selling online. Controlled, financed out of retained profit, keeps the culture and the quality standards, no integration risk. Slow. In a market where competitors are taking the best locations or the best people, slow can mean locked out.
External growth Growing by combining with another organisation: merger (two agree to combine), acquisition / takeover (one buys control of another, which may be contested), joint venture (a new jointly owned entity), strategic alliance (cooperation without merger), franchising (growth funded by someone else's capital). Fast, buys market share, brands, technology, patents, distribution or a route into a new country in one move. Expensive, risky, and the failure is usually cultural rather than financial. Integration is where value is destroyed.
Common trap

A merger is not a takeover. In a merger two businesses agree to combine as roughly equal partners; in an acquisition one takes control of the other, sometimes against the wishes of its board. Case studies often use the soft word "merger" for what is really a takeover, and noticing that is a genuinely sharp AO2 point — it tells you who has the power afterwards and therefore whose culture will win.

Economies of scale — and their limits

Economies of scale are the reductions in average cost that come from being bigger. The internal ones are worth being able to name and explain: purchasing (bulk discounts), managerial (specialists are affordable at scale), financial (bigger firms borrow more cheaply), marketing (the same advertisement reaches more customers), technical (large-scale equipment is more efficient per unit), and risk-bearing (diversification). There are also external economies of scale that come to a whole industry in one place — a pool of skilled labour, specialist suppliers, shared infrastructure — which is a large part of why electronics assembly clusters in particular Chinese cities.

Then it turns. Diseconomies of scale are the rises in average cost that come from being too big: communication slows and distorts as layers are added, coordination becomes a full-time job, decision-making drifts away from the customer, and workers in a large organisation can feel like a number, which raises turnover and lowers productivity. The mechanism is always the same — more people between the decision and the customer.

Why grow, why stay small, and why growth fails

Reasons to grow

Higher profits in absolute terms; economies of scale; larger market share and the market power that comes with it; diversification so one failure is not fatal; survival when competitors are consolidating; prestige and managerial ambition; and, for listed firms, the share price that growth supports.

Reasons to stay small

A small or local market; a niche where personal service is the product; flexibility that a large competitor cannot match; the owner's own objectives; lack of finance; and the diseconomies that arrive before the economies do.

Why growth fails

Cash: growing businesses run out of it while waiting for customers to pay (overtrading). Control: the founder can no longer see what is happening. Quality: standards do not survive faster hiring than training. Fit: an acquisition where the cultures do not combine. And planning: growing into a market that turns out to be smaller than the forecast.

The lifecycle view

Businesses move through stages — start-up, growth, maturity, then decline or renewal — and the objective that is right at one stage is wrong at another. "Evolution" in the sub-topic title is partly this: the business that survives is the one that changes shape as the stage changes.

Worked example 14 Three growth stories: BYD, Mixue and Haier

Question. Compare two methods of growth using real examples.

  1. Organic, capability-led: BYD. It began in the mid-1990s making rechargeable batteries, used that expertise to move into cars, and then into electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles — each step built on a capability it already had. That is internal growth funded largely from its own operations, and its advantage is coherence: the battery knowledge is the reason the vehicle business is competitive. Its cost is speed: building factories, supply chains and overseas plants takes years.
  2. Franchise-led: Mixue. The iced-tea and ice-cream chain grew to tens of thousands of outlets, overwhelmingly franchised, at low price points, and listed in Hong Kong in 2025. Franchising solves the capital problem that limits organic growth — each outlet is funded by someone else — and it is fast enough to secure sites ahead of rivals. Its cost is control: consistency across tens of thousands of independently run outlets is a permanent managerial problem, and every quality failure is a brand failure.
  3. Acquisition: Haier. Buying GE Appliances in 2016 gave a Qingdao firm an established American brand, distribution and customer base in one transaction — something organic growth could not have delivered in a decade. The risk is the classic one: integrating a foreign business with its own culture, management habits and labour relations.
  4. Compare. All three are growth, but they buy different things: BYD buys capability, Mixue buys speed and coverage, Haier buys market access and brand. The right method depends on what is scarce — capability, capital or time — and on what the business's objectives actually are.
Answer: the method follows the constraint; comparing them on that basis — capability, capital, time — turns a list into an analysis. Marks: comparison needs explicit points of contrast (cheaper/faster, more/less control), so avoid describing each method separately and calling it a comparison.

1.6 Multinational companies (MNCs)

A multinational company is a business with operations — production or service delivery — in more than one country. The key test is operational, not commercial: a firm that merely exports is an exporter, not an MNC; a firm that owns or controls production, or delivers the service, in another country is a multinational. When it builds or buys productive assets abroad, that is foreign direct investment (FDI).

Why go abroad at all

Four motives explain most cases, and being able to name the motive is what turns "they went to China" into an analysis:

Market-seeking
To reach customers who are there — because the market is large, growing, or protected by tariffs that make exporting expensive. Starbucks entering mainland China in the late 1990s was market-seeking: the customers were in China.
Resource-seeking
To secure raw materials, components, or labour — often cheaper, sometimes simply unavailable at home.
Efficiency-seeking
To lower cost by locating each stage where it is cheapest or most productive, which is how a single product ends up designed in one country, made from components from five, and assembled in a sixth.
Strategic asset-seeking
To acquire brands, technology, patents, talent or distribution that the firm cannot build quickly itself — much of the motive behind cross-border acquisitions.

Global or local? The glocalisation question

An MNC faces a standing tension: standardise everything and gain scale and consistency, or adapt to each market and gain relevance. Most successful MNCs do a version of both — global brand and systems, local product and format — which is what glocalisation means. Starbucks in China sells tea-based drinks and seasonal products tied to local festivals alongside its global core; McDonald's runs a recognisably global system with local menu items; global consumer-goods firms such as Unilever sell international brands with formulations, pack sizes and prices adapted to local habits and incomes. The interesting question is never "should they adapt?" but "which parts can they standardise without losing the customer?"

Impact on the host country

Potential benefitsPotential costs
Capital investment when domestic savings are insufficient; job creation, and jobs with training attached. Profits may be repatriated rather than reinvested, so the accounting gain exceeds the local one.
Transfer of technology, management know-how and skills, which raises the productivity of local suppliers and workers. Local firms can be crowded out, unable to match the MNC's capital, brand or scale.
Tax revenue, infrastructure that the MNC helps build or makes viable, and access to export markets through its distribution network. Dependence: if the MNC leaves, a whole local supply chain can collapse with it. Tax arrangements may also be negotiated aggressively, reducing the revenue gain.
Higher standards: competition and the MNC's own procurement requirements can raise quality, safety and environmental practice across the local industry. Labour and environmental standards can be driven down if the MNC is choosing the location precisely because regulation is weak; cultural homogeneity is a further, softer cost.

And on the home country: jobs may be lost to offshoring, but the firm gains cheaper inputs, market access and profits that can be invested at home. Both columns are real, which is exactly why MNC questions are evaluation questions.

Worked example 15 BYD abroad, Starbucks in China — two directions of the same phenomenon

Question. Evaluate the impact of multinational companies on a host country.

  1. Inward FDI — Starbucks in China. Market-seeking: it entered mainland China in the late 1990s and built thousands of stores, bringing a service format, training systems and supply-chain standards, employing locally and paying local tax. The demonstration effect is real — the modern Chinese coffee-shop market, in which Luckin Coffee later expanded extremely fast and overtook Starbucks in China by store count, was shaped partly by what Starbucks proved was possible.
  2. Outward FDI — BYD building plants abroad. Chinese MNCs now play the same role elsewhere. BYD has announced or built vehicle plants in Hungary, Brazil, Thailand and Turkey — market-seeking (selling inside tariff barriers and next to customers) and, in time, efficiency-seeking. Host governments compete for these plants with tax and infrastructure offers, which shows how much they value the jobs and the supply chain that follow.
  3. Both sides, developed. For: investment, jobs, skills transfer, tax, competition raising standards, and — for BYD specifically — the transfer of electric-vehicle manufacturing capability into new markets. Against: profits may flow back to the MNC's home country; dependence on a single employer is dangerous when conditions change; local competitors may be squeezed; and the investment is mobile, so the bargaining power sits with the MNC.
  4. Judgement with conditions. The impact depends on what the host country brings to the bargain. Where local suppliers are capable, the MNC's procurement builds an industry; where they are not, the MNC imports its own supply chain and the local gain is mostly wages. Where the state negotiates technology transfer, local content rules and training obligations, the benefits are far larger than where it only offers tax holidays.
  5. Limitation. "Impact" cannot be judged without a time horizon: the costs of disruption arrive immediately, and the benefits of skills and supply-chain development arrive over a decade or more.
Answer: the impact is conditional on host-country capability and on what the government negotiates, not on the MNC alone. Marks: notice that the 9–10 shape here is not a longer list of positives and negatives — it is a judgement about the condition under which the impact is positive.
Common trap

Three. (1) "MNC" is not a synonym for "large company" — a business with one factory abroad qualifies, and a huge domestic firm does not. (2) Offshoring (moving your own operations abroad) is not outsourcing (paying someone else to do them); they overlap and are frequently confused. (3) Do not assume the answer is either "MNCs exploit host countries" or "MNCs develop host countries". The markscheme rewards the conditional answer, and the condition is usually about host-country capability and government negotiation.

Exam technique

Worked example 16 Added value and opportunity cost · AO4 + AO2 · 6 marks

Question. Beishan Roasters buys green coffee beans for ¥48 per kilogram, roasts them, and sells the roasted beans to cafés for ¥136 per kilogram. One kilogram of green beans yields 0.82 kg of roasted beans. The roastery could instead rent its spare floor space to a catering business for ¥9,000 per month. In a typical month it roasts and sells 3,400 kg of green beans, and pays rent and salaries of ¥71,000.

  1. Work out output first, not revenue. 3,400 kg of green beans × 0.82 = 2,788 kg of roasted beans sold.
  2. Sales revenue. 2,788 × ¥136 = ¥379,168.
  3. Direct cost of materials. 3,400 × ¥48 = ¥163,200.
  4. Added value. Added value = sales revenue − cost of bought-in materials = ¥379,168 − ¥163,200 = ¥215,968 per month.
  5. Added value is not profit. Subtract the other costs: ¥215,968 − ¥71,000 = ¥144,968 operating profit before the opportunity cost is considered.
  6. Now the opportunity cost. The spare floor space has a next-best use worth ¥9,000 per month. Economic profit = ¥144,968 − ¥9,000 = ¥135,968.
  7. Interpret. Added value per kg sold = ¥215,968 ÷ 2,788 = ¥77.47. That is the value the roasting step creates per kilogram and it is the number that justifies the business existing: if a competitor could buy roasted beans for less than ¥136 − ¥77.47, the roastery would have no customers.
Answer: added value ¥215,968 per month (¥77.47 per kg); operating profit ¥144,968; economic profit after opportunity cost ¥135,968. Marks: 1 for converting green to roasted kg, 1 for correct revenue, 2 for added value (formula + substitution), 1 for separating added value from profit, 1 for the opportunity-cost adjustment and its interpretation.
Trap — added value is not profit, and opportunity cost is not a cost

Two errors dominate this topic. First, students subtract all costs from revenue and call the answer added value. Added value only deducts the cost of bought-in goods and materials; wages, rent and depreciation are paid out of added value. Second, students treat opportunity cost as a cash item and put it in a profit and loss account. It is not: no money leaves the business. It appears only in the reasoning — "compared with the next best use of these resources, this decision earns ¥9,000 less than the accounts suggest". An examiner who sees opportunity cost inside a cash flow forecast will penalise it twice: once for the accounting error and once for the misclassification.

Worked example 17 Legal form and what it actually costs · AO4 + AO2 · 6 marks

Question. Two engineers are setting up a business. They estimate the following for the first year: revenue ¥1,900,000; bought-in materials ¥820,000; wages ¥540,000; other running costs ¥310,000. They need ¥1.2m of start-up finance and the bank will lend ¥400,000 with the owners' homes as security. One proposal is to trade as a partnership; the other is to incorporate as a private limited company. Calculate the first-year profit in each case and explain, with figures, how the two forms differ in the worst case where the business fails with debts of ¥1.2m and assets of only ¥700,000.

  1. Profit is the same in both cases. Legal form does not change the arithmetic: ¥1,900,000 − ¥820,000 − ¥540,000 − ¥310,000 = ¥230,000.
  2. The difference is who carries the shortfall. On failure, the gap is ¥1,200,000 − ¥700,000 = ¥500,000 that creditors cannot recover from the business.
  3. Partnership (unlimited liability). The partners are personally liable for the ¥500,000. The bank can pursue their homes, so the ¥400,000 secured loan is only partly the issue — the remaining unsecured debt also falls on them personally. Each partner is usually jointly and severally liable, meaning a creditor may pursue either partner for the whole amount, not just a share.
  4. Private limited company (limited liability). The company is a separate legal person. The shareholders' loss is limited to the capital they paid for their shares. If they each paid ¥5,000, the most they lose is ¥5,000 each — unless they gave personal guarantees, which banks routinely demand for small-company loans. With a personal guarantee over the ¥400,000 loan, the protection is partly undone: that is the detail examiners reward.
  5. The financing arithmetic. They need ¥1.2m and the bank lends ¥400,000, leaving ¥800,000 to find. A partnership cannot sell shares, so it must come from owners' savings, a further loan, or a new partner. The company can issue 800,000 ¥1 shares — but only to people the directors are willing to have as shareholders, and the existing owners' percentage control falls accordingly. If they issue to an outside investor who takes 40%, they raise ¥480,000 and retain 60% control plus 60% of future dividends.
  6. Judgement. Incorporation costs money and disclosure: annual accounts are filed and become public, and administrative costs run to several thousand per year. It is worth it here because the business is borrowing against the owners' homes and the downside is personal bankruptcy. For a tiny, low-risk venture with no borrowing, the cost of incorporation can exceed the benefit.
Answer: profit ¥230,000 in both cases; the forms differ only in risk and in how the remaining ¥800,000 can be raised (partnership: savings, loans, new partners; Ltd: share issues, at the cost of diluted control and public disclosure). Marks: 2 for the profit calculation in both cases, 2 for the liability contrast with the ¥500,000 shortfall named, 2 for the financing consequence and a supported conclusion.
Worked example 18 AO3 model answer · "To what extent should growth be the main objective?" · 10 marks

Question. To what extent should Beishan Roasters, a profitable family-owned business with 22% of its regional market, make growth its main objective?

Step 1 — decide the verdict before you write. Markband 9–10 requires a supported judgement. "It depends" with no commitment is 5–6. I will argue: growth should be an objective, justified up to the point where the family's control and the business's cash position are at risk, but not the overriding one.

  1. Define the terms in the question (AO1). Growth can mean revenue growth, market share growth, or growth in the scale of operations; the answer differs for each. I will treat it as growth in revenue and capacity.
  2. Argue the case for growth, applied (AO2). With 22% of the regional market the business has scale to exploit: spreading fixed costs such as the roastery lease and the ¥71,000 of monthly overheads over more kilograms lowers unit cost, which either widens the ¥77.47 of added value per kg or allows a price cut that takes share from competitors. Growth also gives the second generation of the family something to do and makes the business a more attractive borrower.
  3. Argue the case against, applied (AO2). Growth consumes cash before it produces it: more green-bean inventory, more receivables from cafés that pay in 60 days, and new equipment. A business with ¥144,968 of monthly operating profit can become insolvent while profitable if it grows too fast — that is the profit-versus-cash trap in Unit 3. Growth also risks the objective the family actually holds: for many family firms survival and independence rank above size, and taking an outside investor to fund growth changes control permanently and irreversibly.
  4. Weigh them against each other (AO3). The decisive comparison is between the return on the capital growth requires and the cost and risk of that capital. Internal growth funded from retained profit keeps control and is low risk, though slower. External growth — taking a loan or an investor — is faster but adds a fixed obligation or a permanent claim on the business. For a business whose main asset is a local reputation built on relationships with a few dozen cafés, growth by acquisition in another city buys very little of that reputation, so the case for external growth is weaker here than it would be for a standardised product.
  5. State the limitations of the analysis (AO3). I cannot judge this properly without knowing the capacity of the existing roastery, the payment terms the cafés demand, the family's willingness to lose control, and whether competitors would respond to a price cut by cutting theirs.
  6. Conclude with a conditional judgement (AO3). Growth should be pursued, but as a constraint-satisfying objective rather than the main one: grow at a rate the business can fund internally, and treat any growth that requires outside capital or threatens family control as a different decision that must be argued separately.
Marks: 1–2 for the definition of growth; 3–4 for one developed side with application; 5–6 for both sides but with a weak or missing judgement; 7–8 for two developed applied sides with a judgement; 9–10 for those plus explicit limitations and a conclusion that follows from the chain of reasoning rather than being appended to it.
Exam technique — the conclusion is not a summary

A conclusion that restates what you have already argued earns nothing above the band you are already in. A conclusion earns marks when it resolves a tension you set up: "X is stronger than Y because …, provided that …". Two sentences. Write it as an answer to the question in the question's own words — if the question says "to what extent", your conclusion says "to a limited extent", "to a considerable extent" or "fully", and then says why.

Practice

  1. [AO1, 4 marks] State two reasons why businesses exist, and give one example of each.

    Show answer

    Any two of: (1) to satisfy needs and wants by combining the factors of production — for example a bakery turning flour, labour, ovens and the owner's risk-taking into bread that people want to buy; (2) to create added value and earn a profit as the reward for risk — for example a business buying components and assembling them into a product sold for more than the parts cost; (3) to provide employment and income — a logistics firm employing drivers; (4) for a social enterprise, to achieve a social or environmental purpose while trading — for example a business that trains and employs people excluded from the labour market.

    Where the marks come from: one mark per reason, one mark per example that actually illustrates the reason. Naming a business without explaining the link earns the second mark only if the link is obvious.

  2. [AO2, 4 marks] Explain the difference between added value and profit, using a named example.

    Show answer

    Added value is the difference between the selling price and the cost of the bought-in materials or components: for a bowl of noodles sold at ¥18 whose ingredients cost ¥4, added value is ¥14. Profit is what remains after all other costs — rent, wages, utilities, equipment, commission — have been paid out of that added value, so a shop selling 12,000 bowls a month generates ¥168,000 of added value and, if other costs are ¥132,000, a profit of ¥36,000. The distinction matters because a business can create substantial added value and still make a loss, if its overheads are larger than the value it adds — which is common in fast-growing businesses that hire and rent ahead of sales.

    Where the marks come from: 1 for the definition of added value, 1 for the definition of profit, up to 2 for a numerical example that shows the difference and explains why it matters.

  3. [AO2, 4 marks] Explain two reasons why the owner of a small restaurant in China might choose to remain a sole trader rather than set up a limited company.

    Show answer

    Control and simplicity. A sole trader makes every decision personally with no shareholders, no board and no statutory reporting, which matters a great deal to someone whose product is their own judgement about food. Incorporating brings formalities, filings and disclosure that cost time and money disproportionate to a single-site business.

    Objective fit. If the owner's objective is a comfortable living, independence and flexibility rather than rapid expansion, the advantages of a company — access to external capital, continuity beyond the owner — are not worth having, because she does not intend to raise capital or sell the business.

    Development to add: the obvious counter-argument is unlimited liability, so the decision is only sensible while the business has few debts and no large contracts; if she signs a lease for a second site or takes on significant borrowing, the limited company becomes the safer form.

    Where the marks come from: up to 2 marks per reason — one for the reason, one for developing it into a consequence for this business. The counter-argument is what converts a 3 into a 4.

  4. [AO2, 4 marks] Distinguish between a private limited company and a public limited company.

    Show answer
    • Shares: a private limited company cannot offer its shares to the general public; a public limited company can sell shares on a stock exchange, which is the main reason for choosing that form.
    • Capital: being listed gives access to a far deeper pool of finance for expansion, at the cost of dilution of the founders' control and exposure to a takeover.
    • Disclosure: a public company must publish detailed accounts and disclose material information, so competitors, customers and employees see far more than they would of a private company.
    • Pressure: listed companies are judged quarterly by investors, which can push management towards short-term results; private companies can take a longer view.
    • What does not differ: both have limited liability and separate legal identity — size is not the distinction.

    Where the marks come from: the command term is distinguish, so each point must be stated as a contrast. Two clear contrasts with development is 4; describing each company separately earns 2 at most.

  5. [AO2, 4 marks] Explain how two objectives of the same business can conflict, using a real or realistic example.

    Show answer

    Take a franchise-led drinks chain whose founder has publicly committed to being "the most trusted" brand in her region while also targeting rapid outlet growth.

    The conflict: rapid growth requires opening outlets faster than the business can recruit and train staff, so service and product consistency fall; but consistency is precisely what "most trusted" depends on. Each objective can only be pursued at the expense of the other, because both draw on the same scarce resource — trained people and managerial attention.

    The development: the conflict cannot be resolved by working harder; it has to be resolved by choosing, or by sequencing — grow first in markets where consistency cannot damage the existing brand, and fix training before growing in the home market. Alternatively the business must admit that its stated objective has changed from trust to scale.

    Where the marks come from: 1 for identifying two objectives that genuinely compete, 1–2 for explaining the mechanism of the conflict in context, 1 for the consequence or resolution.

  6. [AO4 + AO2, 6 marks] A noodle shop sells 12,000 bowls a month at ¥18. Ingredients cost ¥4 per bowl. Rent is ¥30,000, wages ¥90,000 and utilities ¥12,000 a month. (a) Calculate the added value per bowl and the total monthly added value. (b) Calculate monthly profit. (c) Comment on the effect of a 25 % rise in ingredient costs.

    Show answer
    1. Added value per bowl = ¥18 − ¥4 = ¥14.
    2. Total added value = 12,000 × ¥14 = ¥168,000 a month. (Check: revenue ¥216,000 − ingredient cost ¥48,000 = ¥168,000.)
    3. Other costs = 30,000 + 90,000 + 12,000 = ¥132,000, so monthly profit = 168,000 − 132,000 = ¥36,000.
    4. Ingredient cost rises 25 %: ¥4.00 → ¥5.00 per bowl. Total ingredient cost becomes ¥60,000, so added value falls to ¥156,000 and profit falls to ¥24,000.
    5. Comment: profit falls by ¥12,000, which is a 33 % fall in profit from a 25 % rise in one input cost. That is the point — a business with a high ratio of fixed costs to profit is highly geared to small cost changes, so it should either hedge ingredient supplies, negotiate longer fixed-price contracts, or raise prices; and whether it can raise prices depends on how price-sensitive its customers are.
    6. Limitation: this assumes volume stays at 12,000 bowls. If the business raises prices to protect margin and customers buy fewer bowls, the fall in profit could be larger still.

    Where the marks come from: 1 for added value per unit, 1 for total added value, 1 for profit, 1 for the recalculation after the cost rise, and up to 2 for a comment that explains the disproportionate effect and what should be done about it.

  7. [AO3, 10 marks] To what extent should BYD continue to grow by building vehicle plants in other countries?

    Show answer

    For, developed. Building plants abroad converts a Chinese manufacturer into a genuine multinational and solves three problems at once. It gets inside tariff and trade barriers, which matter enormously as electric-vehicle trade becomes politically sensitive; it puts production next to customers, cutting shipping cost and delivery time; and it reduces dependence on a single domestic market where price competition is intense. It is also a market-seeking move that turns an export business into a local one, which makes the brand more acceptable to local buyers and governments. BYD's own history supports the logic: it has repeatedly moved into adjacent products by building on a capability it already had, from batteries to vehicles.

    Against, developed. Overseas plants are capital-intensive, slow to reach full output, and expose the business to exactly the risks that organic domestic growth avoids: unfamiliar labour markets and labour relations, different regulatory and environmental standards, currency exposure, and political risk if relations between the host country and China deteriorate. Capacity built on forecasts of electric-vehicle demand is a bet on a market that has already seen sharp swings, and fixed costs in a new plant do not fall when sales disappoint. There is also a capability limit: managing plants in several countries is a different business from managing them in one, and it is a common way that successful manufacturers stumble.

    Judgement. To a considerable extent, but sequenced and hedged rather than simultaneous: prioritise markets where tariffs make local production essential, start with assembly rather than full-scale manufacturing to keep the fixed-cost commitment reversible, and use joint ventures or local partners where labour and regulatory unfamiliarity is greatest. Where the domestic market still offers growth, that growth is cheaper.

    Limitations of what I know. I do not have the actual capacity, cost or expected payback figures for these plants, nor the detail of any local subsidies, and both would change the judgement; nor do I know the terms of any host-government agreements.

    Where the marks come from: the same shape as every 10-marker — two developed sides with mechanisms rather than lists, a conditional judgement that answers "to what extent", and an explicit acknowledgement of what the stimulus does not tell you. A version that simply lists the benefits of FDI is a 5–6.

  8. [AO3, 10 marks] Evaluate the view that multinational companies bring more benefits than costs to the country that hosts them.

    Show answer

    Benefits, developed. An MNC brings capital that may not be available domestically, creates jobs, and — crucially — transfers technology, management practice and skills, which raise the productivity of the whole local supply chain rather than just the MNC's own workers. Tax revenue and infrastructure follow, competition raises standards among local firms, and access to the MNC's global distribution network can turn local suppliers into exporters. For a developing host economy the demonstration effect is often as valuable as the investment itself.

    Costs, developed. Profits may be repatriated rather than reinvested, and tax arrangements and transfer pricing can reduce the local revenue gain to far less than the headline investment suggests. Local firms may be crowded out by a competitor with superior capital, brand and scale. The jobs created can be low-skilled and vulnerable, and if the MNC's motive was efficiency-seeking it may leave when costs rise, taking a dependent supply chain with it. There are also environmental and labour-standard risks where the location was chosen precisely because regulation is weak.

    Judgement. The view is defensible but only under conditions, so the honest answer is "it depends, and here is what it depends on". Where the host country has capable suppliers and a government that negotiates technology transfer, local-content requirements, training obligations and credible tax terms, the benefits dominate. Where it has neither, the gain is largely wages and the leverage belongs to the MNC.

    Limitations. Costs are immediate and benefits are spread over a decade or more, so the answer changes with the time horizon; and the balance differs by industry — assembly operations transfer less than research-intensive ones.

    Where the marks come from: developed arguments on both sides (2 × 3–4 marks available), a judgement that addresses "the view" rather than sitting on the fence, and the limitations paragraph that separates 9–10 from 7–8. Adding one named host country and one named MNC pushes the application marks from partial to full.

  9. [AO1, 4 marks] State the four factors of production, the payment each one earns, and identify which factor bears the risk of the business failing.

    Show the answer

    Land — all natural resources, paid rent. Labour — human effort and skill, paid wages and salaries. Capital — man-made aids to production and the money to buy them, earning interest. Enterprise — the willingness to organise the other three and take decisions, rewarded with profit.

    Enterprise bears the risk. Rent, wages and interest are contractual: they are owed whether the business succeeds or not. The entrepreneur is paid last, out of whatever is left, and in a bad year that is nothing. That is why profit is the price of uncertainty rather than a reward for greed — and why a business that cannot earn a profit above the return on a deposit account has no reason to exist.

    Why this is worth knowing: the four factors are the fastest four marks in Unit 1, and "which one carries the risk" is the question that separates rote learning from understanding. It also gives you a ready-made structure for any "how does this business create value?" question.

  10. [AO2, 4 marks] A furniture maker buys wood, fittings and fabric for ¥320 per chair and sells each chair for ¥980. Rent, wages and other overheads come to ¥480,000 a month and output is 1,000 chairs. (a) Calculate the added value per chair. (b) Calculate monthly profit. (c) Explain one way the business could increase its added value, and one reason that way might fail.

    Show the answer
    1. Added value per chair = ¥980 − ¥320 = ¥660.
    2. Total added value = 1,000 × ¥660 = ¥660,000. Profit = 660,000 − 480,000 = ¥180,000 a month.
    3. To increase added value: raise the price by branding and design — selling the same chair as a designer piece at ¥1,400 would raise added value to ¥1,080 per chair and add ¥420,000 to monthly profit if volume held.
    4. Why it might fail: the price rise assumes customers will pay it, which depends on brand strength and on what competitors charge. If volume falls by more than about 45 % as a result, total added value falls rather than rises — and building the brand costs money up front, so profit may drop before it rises.
    5. The alternative route: negotiate cheaper bought-in inputs. Cutting materials to ¥280 adds ¥40 per chair with no risk to volume, but cheaper materials may damage the quality the brand depends on — which is the same trade-off from the other side.
    Why this is worth knowing: added value has only two levers — price up or bought-in cost down — and every method in Units 4 and 5 is one of them. Being able to say which lever a proposal pulls, and what it risks, is the whole of the analysis.

  11. [AO2, 4 marks] Distinguish between unlimited liability and limited liability, and explain what "joint and several liability" adds to the risk of a partnership.

    Show the answer

    Unlimited liability means the owners are personally responsible for the debts of the business without limit: creditors can pursue personal assets — savings, vehicles, and in the worst case the family home — once business assets are exhausted. Sole traders and ordinary partners have it.

    Limited liability means the most a shareholder can lose is the amount they have invested (strictly, the amount unpaid on their shares). The company's debts are the company's, because incorporation has made it a separate legal person. Shareholders of Ltds and PLCs have it.

    Joint and several liability makes the partnership case worse than mere unlimited liability: each partner is liable for the whole debt, not only a proportionate share. If one partner cannot pay, or has left the country, the creditor can recover everything from the other. So you are not only exposed to your own decisions, you are exposed to your partner's.

    The development that earns the higher marks: unlimited liability in practice means losing what you have, which is why it is severe for a homeowner with a lease and close to irrelevant for a student with no assets; and limited liability is often weakened in practice by directors' personal guarantees demanded by banks.

    Why this is worth knowing: liability is the single most examined feature of legal form, and the two qualifications — joint and several, and personal guarantees — are the points that distinguish a level-3 answer from a level-1 one.

  12. [AO2, 6 marks] Explain three reasons why a successful and profitable partnership of architects might choose to become a private limited company.

    Show the answer

    Liability. Architecture carries professional-negligence risk on projects worth far more than the practice's own assets. Under joint and several unlimited liability, one partner's error on a large project can cost the others their personal assets. Incorporation caps the loss at the capital invested, and professional indemnity insurance works alongside a corporate structure more cleanly than alongside an unlimited one.

    Capital. A growing practice wanting to open a second office, invest in design software and take on larger commissions needs more finance than the partners can provide or borrow personally. A company can issue shares to new directors or outside investors; a partnership cannot issue shares at all.

    Continuity and succession. An ordinary partnership technically dissolves when a partner leaves, retires or dies, which is disruptive for a practice with multi-year commissions and for clients who need to know who is responsible for their building in ten years' time. A company has perpetual succession: shares transfer, the entity continues, and retiring founders can be bought out over time.

    The counter-argument to acknowledge: incorporation brings filing costs, public accounts that competitors and clients can read, possible double taxation of profit, and the loss of the privacy and informality that made the partnership pleasant. For a two-person practice with no borrowing and no ambitions, that is a real price to pay.

    Why this is worth knowing: "why change form?" questions are really trigger questions. Name the trigger — liability, capital, continuity — and the form follows; then acknowledge the cost of the change and you have both sides.

  13. [AO2, 4 marks] Explain two disadvantages to the franchisor of growing by franchising rather than by opening company-owned outlets.

    Show the answer

    Loss of control and brand risk. The franchisee owns and manages the outlet, and the franchisor can specify the system but cannot stand in the kitchen. Every outlet that cuts a corner on hygiene, understaffs at peak times or treats customers badly damages a brand the franchisor owns — and at tens of thousands of outlets, monitoring is a permanent, expensive problem rather than a solved one. One viral video about one outlet is a problem for all of them.

    Shared economics and dependence on franchisees. The franchisor earns a royalty, typically a percentage of turnover, rather than the whole profit of the outlet. If the format is very profitable, the franchisor keeps the smaller share; if franchisees are unprofitable, they default, close, or litigate, and the network shrinks at exactly the moment the brand needs coverage. The franchisor has also traded the ability to extract profit for the speed of expansion, which is only a good deal if speed was the binding constraint.

    A third, if you need one: franchisees are independent business people with their own objectives, so getting them to accept a change — a new menu, a refit, a delivery partnership — requires negotiation rather than instruction.

    Why this is worth knowing: most candidates can give the advantages of franchising and freeze on the disadvantages. The core disadvantage is always the same idea: you have sold control of the thing that carries your name.

  14. [AO3, 6 marks] Chen runs a profitable delivery business as a sole trader, employing four drivers. She has been offered a two-year contract with a large catering group that would require her to buy three refrigerated vans for ¥450,000. Recommend whether she should remain a sole trader or incorporate as a private limited company.

    Show the answer

    Recommendation: incorporate as a private limited company.

    Reason one — the risk has changed shape. Because the contract requires her to take on roughly ¥450,000 of borrowing or lease commitments against a two-year contract, therefore the consequence of the catering group cancelling or paying late is that she personally owes the money. As a sole trader her personal assets — including her home — are available to creditors. Incorporation caps the loss at her investment.

    Reason two — finance and credibility. Because a company has a separate legal identity and can be required to file accounts, therefore lenders and large corporate customers take it more seriously; a two-year contract with a national catering group may even require her to be a company as a condition of the deal.

    The other side, honestly. Incorporation costs money and time in filings and accounts, her accounts become visible to competitors and possibly to the customer who sets her prices, and the bank may still require a personal guarantee, so the liability protection is partial in the first year or two. If she can lease the vans instead of buying them, the borrowing — and therefore the argument — shrinks considerably.

    It depends on: whether the contract is genuinely firm for two years. If it is cancellable at one month's notice, the correct advice is not "incorporate" but "do not commit to the vans on these terms" — the legal form is secondary to the contract risk.

    Why this is worth knowing: this is the model shape for a 6-mark recommend question — decide, two developed chains, the counter-argument, and a condition that would reverse the decision.

  15. [AO3, 8 marks] Evaluate the view that a business entering a foreign market should always use a joint venture with a local partner rather than setting up its own subsidiary.

    Show the answer

    For the joint venture. Because a local partner brings market knowledge, relationships with distributors and regulators, an existing workforce and sometimes a licence the foreign firm could not obtain alone, therefore the entrant moves faster and avoids the expensive mistakes of misreading a market — mistakes that are the usual reason market entry fails. Risk and cost are shared, which matters greatly for a capital-intensive plant. In several industries and countries a local partner has historically been a legal requirement rather than a preference.

    Against it. Because the venture is jointly owned and jointly controlled, therefore profits are shared, decisions are slower, and strategy has to be negotiated with a partner whose objectives may differ — typically a partner who wants short-term profit while the entrant wants market share. More seriously, the partner you teach is a competitor you have created: technology, supplier relationships and know-how leak, and a number of foreign entrants have found that their local partner eventually became their strongest rival. Culture clash between two management teams is the most common cause of joint-venture failure, ahead of commercial disagreement.

    Judgement with conditions. The view is too strong — "always" cannot survive contact with a case study. A joint venture is right when the market is genuinely hard to read, when local relationships or regulation are decisive, when the capital requirement exceeds what one firm should risk, and when the firm's advantage is not the thing it would have to share. A wholly owned subsidiary is right when the firm's advantage is proprietary technology or a tightly controlled brand standard, when it can afford to learn slowly, and when it wants the whole of the profit.

    Limitation. The answer also depends on the time horizon: joint ventures are often a step rather than a destination, and a firm may use one to learn a market and then buy out its partner — which is why the exit clause matters as much as the entry agreement.

    Why this is worth knowing: "always" and "never" in a question are an instruction to write a conditional judgement. Rejecting the absolute in the first or last sentence is the cheapest AO3 mark on the paper.

  16. [AO3, 10 marks] Evaluate the view that every business which grows large enough should become a public limited company.

    Show the answer

    The case for. Because a listed company can sell shares to the public, therefore it can raise sums no group of private investors or banks would supply, which is what funds factories, research programmes and acquisitions — BYD's listed status is what allows it to finance capital-intensive vehicle plants at home and abroad. Listing also gives the original owners liquidity, creates a share price that can be used as currency to buy other companies, raises the firm's profile with customers and lenders, and imposes governance and audit discipline that often improves decision-making.

    The case against, developed. Because outside shareholders buy both capital and influence, therefore the founders lose control: decisions must be justified to people who did not build the business, and if enough shares end up in public hands the company can be taken over against the board's wishes. Because results must be published frequently, therefore management faces quarterly pressure that favours short-term results over investment with a long payback — the classic short-termism objection, and a serious one for businesses whose advantage is research carried out over decades. There are direct costs too: flotation fees, ongoing listing and compliance costs, and the disclosure of strategy, margins and risks to competitors.

    The decisive point about size. The view confuses size with legal form. Legal form is not a reward for getting big; it is a tool for raising capital and limiting risk. There are private companies larger than most listed ones, and some very large, very successful firms stay private deliberately to protect a long time horizon and to avoid publishing accounts — Huawei is the standard example, and the trade-off is visible: freedom to spend on research for decades, in exchange for a much narrower pool of capital.

    Judgement. The view is largely wrong as stated. Listing is justified when the business has a use for very large amounts of capital that will earn more than the cost of that capital, when the owners want an exit or an acquisition currency, or when the credibility of being listed is commercially valuable. It is not justified merely because the business is large, and it is positively harmful for owners whose central objective is control and long-term investment.

    Limitations. I cannot judge this without knowing the firm's capital requirements, its objectives, the cost of flotation in its market and the valuation the market would place on it. The answer also varies by jurisdiction: in some markets disclosure rules and minority-shareholder protections make listing more or less attractive than in others.

    Why this is worth knowing: this question is designed to catch the "PLC is a bigger, better Ltd" misconception. Naming that misconception and correcting it is worth more than any list of advantages of listing.

  17. [AO1, 2 marks] State the difference between a need and a want, and give one example of a product that meets a need in one market and a want in another.

    Show answer
    1. Knowledge (1): a need is something required for survival — food, water, shelter, clothing; a want is something desired but not necessary for survival, and wants are shaped by culture, income and marketing.
    2. Application (1): bottled water is a need in a city where tap water is unsafe and a want in a city where it is safe and free — same product, different classification, which is why a business must identify which market it is selling into before it sets a price.
    Marks: 1 for the distinction, 1 for an example where the classification changes with context rather than with the product.

  18. [AO2, 4 marks] A sole trader with annual revenue of ¥640,000 and total costs of ¥590,000 is offered a salaried job at ¥78,000 a year. Calculate her annual profit and explain, using the figures, why the profit figure alone does not tell her whether to stay in business.

    Show answer
    1. Calculate (AO4, 1): profit = ¥640,000 − ¥590,000 = ¥50,000.
    2. Bring in the opportunity cost (AO2, 1–2): the salary she gives up is ¥78,000, so her economic profit is ¥50,000 − ¥78,000 = −¥28,000. On this basis the business destroys value compared with the next best alternative, even though it makes an accounting profit.
    3. Develop (AO2, 1): the comparison is not purely financial. Running the business may give her independence, flexible hours, and the option value of an asset she could sell; the salaried job may offer security and paid leave but no upside. Therefore profit answers "is the business viable", while opportunity cost answers "is it worth her time" — two different questions.
    Marks: 1 for the calculation of ¥50,000, 2 for identifying and applying the ¥78,000 opportunity cost, 1 for a developed comment on non-financial factors or on the meaning of the negative economic profit.

  19. [AO2, 4 marks] Yongjia Shoes increases output from 40,000 to 90,000 pairs a year. Its total fixed costs rise from ¥1.8m to ¥2.1m, and variable cost per pair falls from ¥62 to ¥49 because it can now buy leather in bulk. Calculate the average cost per pair before and after the expansion, and state what this shows.

    Show answer
    1. Before: total cost = ¥1.8m + (40,000 × ¥62) = ¥1.8m + ¥2.48m = ¥4.28m. Average cost = ¥4.28m ÷ 40,000 = ¥107 per pair.
    2. After: total cost = ¥2.1m + (90,000 × ¥49) = ¥2.1m + ¥4.41m = ¥6.51m. Average cost = ¥6.51m ÷ 90,000 = ¥72.33 per pair.
    3. Separate the two effects (the point of the question): fixed cost per pair falls from ¥45.00 to ¥23.33 — that is the pure economies-of-scale effect. Variable cost per pair falls from ¥62 to ¥49 — that is purchasing economy from bulk buying. Total unit cost falls by ¥34.67, of which ¥21.67 comes from spreading fixed costs and ¥13.00 from the lower input price.
    4. Interpret: if the market price is ¥95, the business was loss-making before (¥107 − ¥95 = ¥12 loss per pair) and profitable after (¥22.67 contribution per pair). Scale alone changed the sign of the profit.
    Marks: 1 each for the two average-cost calculations, 1 for separating the fixed-cost effect from the purchasing effect, 1 for an interpretation that uses the result.

  20. [AO3, 10 marks] To what extent do the costs of a multinational company operating in a developing country outweigh the benefits to that country?

    Show answer
    1. Define and scope (AO1): an MNC is a business with operations in more than one country; the "host" country is the one receiving the investment. Costs and benefits must be compared over the same time horizon and for the same stakeholder group.
    2. Benefits, applied (AO2): direct job creation, and the multiplier effect as wages are spent locally; transfer of technology and working practices that raise the productivity of local suppliers; tax revenue on profits and payroll; and the demonstration effect — local firms copy a successful entrant. For a country with high youth unemployment, even a factory paying modest wages is a net gain in the short run.
    3. Costs, applied (AO2): MNCs can use transfer pricing to shift profit to low-tax jurisdictions, so the taxable base is smaller than the activity suggests; they may drive local competitors out of business before raising prices; they may bring in expatriate managers so that the well-paid jobs do not go to locals; and a plant that is footloose can leave when wages rise, stranding the workers who relocated for it. Environmental standards may also be lower than at home.
    4. Weigh (AO3): the benefits are front-loaded and visible; many of the costs are back-loaded and invisible. The net effect therefore depends on host-country policy: a government that requires local sourcing, enforces environmental standards and restricts profit repatriation captures far more of the benefit than one that competes to offer the lowest tax and the weakest regulation.
    5. Limitations (AO3): the balance differs by sector — an extractive MNC with no local suppliers generates a much smaller multiplier than a manufacturer with a local supply chain. I also cannot generalise from one country to another.
    6. Conclusion (AO3): the benefits outweigh the costs to a considerable extent where the host government has the capacity to regulate and the MNC has a reason to stay, but where the investment is footloose and the state is weak, the costs dominate — which is why the answer depends less on the MNC than on the country receiving it.
    Marks: 1–2 for definition/context; 3–4 for one side developed with application; 5–6 for both sides with a weak conclusion; 7–8 for two applied sides plus a judgement; 9–10 for that plus limitations and a conclusion that resolves the tension rather than repeating it.

  21. [AO2, 6 marks] Lumen Lens Co, a private limited company, has three shareholders who also work in the business and wants to raise ¥6m to build a second factory. Its retained profit is ¥900,000 and its bank will lend a further ¥2m. Explain two advantages and two disadvantages of bringing in an outside shareholder, and recommend what it should do.

    Show answer
    1. The gap (AO2): ¥6m − ¥900,000 − ¥2m = ¥3.1m still to find. That number should open the answer, because it rules out the internal route on its own.
    2. Advantages of an outside shareholder (2 marks): no interest is due and no repayment date is set, so cash flow is protected while the factory is being built; and a good investor brings contacts, discipline and credibility with suppliers and future lenders.
    3. Disadvantages (2 marks): dividends are a permanent claim on future profits, so the cost of the capital is not zero even though the cash cost this year is; and control is diluted — if the new shareholder takes more than 50%, the founder-directors can be outvoted on strategy, and if the three working shareholders disagree with a passive investor about whether to take profit out or reinvest it, the business has a conflict it cannot resolve internally.
    4. Recommendation (2 marks): a timed, staged response fits best. Issue enough shares to raise the ¥3.1m now, but negotiate a shareholders' agreement that fixes dividend policy and reserves strategic decisions to the working directors, and include a buy-back option so control can be recovered once the second factory is generating cash. If the investor will not accept those terms, the alternative is to phase the factory — build half now and fund the rest from the profit the first half generates — which is slower but keeps ownership intact.
    Marks: 1 for quantifying the ¥3.1m gap, 2 for two developed advantages, 2 for two developed disadvantages, 1 for a justified recommendation that weighs the alternatives rather than simply picking one.
